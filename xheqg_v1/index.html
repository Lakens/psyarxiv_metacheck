Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

Abstract

Dominant theories of consciousness (Higher-Order, Global Workspace, Integrated Information and Predictive Processing theories) locate its neural substrate in cortical association areas. I argue that this convergence reflects the technical accessibility of cortical tissue rather than a demonstrated sufficiency of cortical circuits, and I present the Thalamocortical Mnemonic Model of Consciousness (TMMC), which assigns the thalamus a constitutive role in selecting and sustaining conscious content. Content is selected by competition at the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) among thalamic channels, under four selection pressures with identifiable anatomical sources: salience, prediction, value and mnemonic coherence. The mediodorsal nucleus (MD) sustains prefrontal activity once content has arrived, the hippocampus generates episodic endogenous content, the nucleus reuniens coordinates its arrival in prefrontal cortex, and the amygdala biases the competition directly. Building on earlier constructive accounts, the TMMC holds that all conscious content is a configuration of learned cortical patterns; its contribution is the mechanism that selects among them. The model distinguishes two partially dissociable state variables, the level, L(t), and the content, C(t), of consciousness, and three operating modes (exogenous, endogenous and free) whose transitions reflect coupled thalamocortical processes on multiple timescales. Three falsifiable predictions are derived: thalamic precedence over prefrontal cortex for spontaneous thought, accelerated loss of maintained content without delayed generation after MD damage, and thalamus-to-cortex theta-band flow before perceptual switches in binocular rivalry. A further test asks whether the selection pressures dissociate. The TMMC offers a mechanistic alternative to corticocentric models and a research program for thalamocortical consciousness science.

1. Introduction

The neuroscience of consciousness has entered a period of productive instability. After three decades during which theoretical progress was largely decoupled from direct experimental arbitration, adversarial collaborations, in which proponents of competing theories agree in advance on preregistered tests (Melloni et al., 2021), have begun to subject the leading frameworks to genuine empirical stress (Cogitate consortium et al., 2025). The results have been uncomfortable for the dominant consensus: the largest preregistered test of Global Workspace Theory against Integrated Information Theory found evidence inconsistent with the signature predictions of both frameworks when applied to the same data. This empirical pressure does not yet constitute a paradigm collapse, but it does open conceptual space for frameworks grounded in different anatomical and mechanistic premises. The TMMC is a proposal developed in that space. It does not begin from a dissatisfaction with phenomenological description, nor from a preference for subcortical anatomy as a matter of principle. It begins from a specific diagnosis: the dominant theories share a structural assumption -cortical sufficiency for conscious experience -that has been inherited from methodological constraints rather than established by evidence, and that assumption has systematically prevented integration of an accumulating body of thalamic evidence that is, on its own terms, mechanistically compelling.

The identification of prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex as the principal neural correlates of consciousness rests on a convergent literature spanning functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), electroencephalography (EEG), single-unit recordings in non-human primates (NHP), and intracranial electrophysiology in humans (Dehaene & Changeux, 2011; Lamme, 2006). The technical basis for that convergence, however, is not neutral. fMRI is systematically disadvantaged for subcortical structures by three compounding factors: reduced signal-tonoise ratio at depth due to distance from surface receiver coils, susceptibility artifacts arising from air-tissue interfaces near the brainstem and medial temporal structures, and disproportionate physiological noise -cardiac and respiratory -in deep brain regions (Forstmann et al., 2017; Logothetis, 2008). Scalp EEG faces a complementary limitation: its source localization problem is formally ill-posed, and sensitivity falls sharply with source depth, rendering it effectively insensitive to signals originating in thalamic nuclei and other subcortical structures (Baillet, 2017). Single-unit microelectrode recording in behaving non-human primates has been concentrated predominantly in prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex, as reflected in the anatomical coverage of major neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) reviews (Boly et al., 2013), driven by experimental tradition and surgical accessibility rather than by evidence that these are the only areas of mechanistic interest.

The cumulative effect of these three technical constraints is a literature that accurately describes what is happening in cortex during conscious experience but is structurally unable to evaluate what is simultaneously happening in the thalamus -and has therefore systematically underestimated thalamic contributions not because evidence against them exists, but because the evidence for them was not collected. The corticocentric consensus is not an inference from a body of evidence that exhausted subcortical explanations and found them wanting. It is the outcome of a sampling process skewed toward cortical tissue, combined with a theoretical tradition that treated subcortical contributions as "enabling" rather than "constitutive" -a distinction that was never mechanistically justified. The TMMC takes the position that this distinction must be earned, not assumed, and that the available evidence does not support it.

In parallel, a distinct empirical literature has begun to implicate the thalamus not only in arousal, where its role has long been accepted (D. A. McCormick & Bal, 1997; Steriade, 1996), but in determining what enters consciousness. Stimulation of the central lateral nucleus restores wake-like cortical activity and behavioral responsiveness under anesthesia (Redinbaugh et al., 2020); consciousness-related activity in human intralaminar and medial thalamus precedes that of prefrontal cortex during perception (Fang et al., 2025); TRN subnetworks gate specific sensory channels according to task demands (Wimmer et al., 2015); and stimulation of the human MD alters reportable experience (Pantis et al., 2026). These findings, reviewed in Section 2.5, call for a model in which the thalamus is a constitutive node of the circuit that generates conscious content rather than a valve that enables or suppresses cortical processing.

The four major theoretical families -Higher-Order Theories (HOT), Global Workspace Theory (GWT), Integrated Information Theory (IIT), and Predictive Processing (PP) -each contribute genuine insights, and each, on its own terms, leaves the thalamus underspecified: unlocated (HOT), unassigned a mechanism (GWT), uninterpreted despite formal inclusion (IIT), or given a role in inference without a mechanism of selection (PP). The shared limitation, however, is architectural rather than framework-specific: all four inherit a corticocentric topology in which thalamic activity is downstream of, or parallel to, rather than upstream of and constitutive of, the cortical dynamics that generate consciousness. This shared blind spot defines the conceptual gap the TMMC attempts to fill.

The thalamocentric tradition in consciousness research has a clear, if minority, history. Penfield and Jasper's work on the centrencephalic system proposed that consciousness depends on subcortical integration, not on any single cortical region (Penfield & Jasper, 1954). Llinás and colleagues proposed the most developed thalamocentric theory to date: that consciousness is the intrinsic oscillatory activity of thalamocortical loops, with 40 Hz thalamocortical resonance underlying the temporal binding of conscious content (Llinas et al., 1998; Llinás, 2002; Llinás & Paré, 1991). The TMMC inherits this foundational intuition. In parallel, the TRN itself has a history as a candidate gating structure. Crick's searchlight hypothesis proposed that the TRN controls attentional enhancement of selected thalamic channels (Crick, 1984); Newman and Baars placed reticular-thalamic gating at the entrance to the global workspace (Newman & Baars, 1993); and Min proposed a TRN-mediated networking model in which conscious perception emerges from the integration of first-order and higher-order thalamocortical signals across the reticular sheet (Min, 2010), with supporting human magnetoencephalography (MEG) and fMRI evidence (Min et al., 2020; Seo et al., 2022). Most recently, Whyte et al. (2024) proposed that activity-dependent recruitment of the TRN implements a divisive-normalization-like process that segregates conscious contents, allowing the dominant thalamocortical coalition to suppress its competitors. Crabtree (2018) proposed that open-loop TRN circuits select between competing messages in different thalamic nuclei according to their salience or relevance, a form of internuclear attentional selection. These accounts establish the TRN as a plausible site of gating and competition, but they leave largely unspecified what sets it: which signals, from which structures, bias selection at a given moment. They also address perceptual rather than endogenous content.

The TMMC extends this body of work in three respects. The first is the mechanism of content selection, specified as distinct selection pressures with identifiable anatomical sources integrated at the TRN. The second is the origin of endogenous content, with the hippocampus as a constructive simulation engine whose output reaches prefrontal cortex directly, coordinated there by the nucleus reuniens (RE). A third element of the framework, the claim that what is selected is always a dynamic configuration of learned cortical patterns and never a raw input, has substantial precedent. Llinás's own account of intrinsically generative thalamocortical activity already implies it, and it was made explicit by Damasio's model of multiregional retroactivation and Edelman's characterization of consciousness as a "remembered present" (Damasio, 1989; G. Edelman, 2001). The TMMC's contribution to this third element is not the mnemonic-content claim itself but its mechanistic specification: competition at the TRN under distinct selection pressures determines which stored configuration is reconstructed at a given moment, a selection problem that these earlier accounts raise without resolving. The selection mechanism, the endogenous content source and the mechanistic specification of mnemonic structure are the TMMC's contributions relative to its intellectual lineage. The present paper presents the conceptual architecture of the model, its two-dimensional state space, its three operating modes, and three falsifiable predictions with experimental protocols, together with a test of the selection pressures.

In this paper, "consciousness" is used in a functional sense: a content is conscious when it is available for report, reasoning and the voluntary control of action (Block, 1995). In sleep, where action is suspended, availability for later report stands in for this criterion. The level and content of consciousness are defined in the same functional terms, as the variables L(t) and C(t) (Section 5.1). This differs from uses of the term that refer to the experiential dimension of consciousness as distinct from its contents (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026). The TMMC addresses which content becomes conscious and through which circuits; it takes no position on why this is accompanied by experience (Section 7.4).

This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides a critical review of the dominant frameworks and the thalamic evidence they fail to integrate. Section 3 describes the fivecomponent architecture of the TMMC and its four selection pressures. Section 4 develops the mnemonic thesis and its taxonomic implications. Section 5 formalizes the L(t)/C(t) state space and the three operating modes. Section 6 derives three falsifiable predictions and a test of the selection pressures with specified protocols and contrasts with existing models. Section 7 discusses open problems, relationships to adjacent frameworks, and the research program the TMMC generates.

2. Dominant frameworks and their shared structural limitation

The four major theoretical frameworks in the neuroscience of consciousness -Higher-Order Theories, Global Workspace Theory, Integrated Information Theory, and Predictive Processing -differ substantially in their ontological commitments, explanatory targets, and empirical signatures. What they share is an anatomical premise: the neural substrate of conscious experience is located in cortical association areas, principally in prefrontal and posterior parietal regions, with subcortical structures assigned at most an enabling or modulatory role. This premise is rarely stated as an explicit axiom; it operates instead as a background assumption embedded in the choice of experimental paradigms, the interpretation of neural correlates, and the architecture of the proposed mechanisms. The goal of this section is to make that assumption explicit, evaluate the evidence that supports it, and identify the body of thalamic evidence it systematically excludes. The point is not that corticocentric theories are wrong in what they affirm, but that they are incomplete in what they structurally cannot accommodate. The review concludes by characterizing the specific conceptual gapa mechanistic account of how thalamic architecture determines the content, not merely the level, of conscious experience -that the TMMC is designed to address.

2.1 Higher-Order Theories

HOTs hold that a mental state is conscious if and only if it is represented by a higher-order state -a representation of a representation -that renders the first-order state available to the subject as their own (Lau & Rosenthal, 2011; Rosenthal, 2002). The framework's core contribution is the observation that phenomenal access is not uniform: identical first-order stimuli can be processed with or without conscious awareness depending on the availability of higher-order representation, which explains a range of masking, inattentional blindness, and change blindness phenomena (Lau & Rosenthal, 2011).

The structural limitation is twofold. First, HOT locates the higher-order representation in prefrontal cortex -specifically in lateral PFC -on the basis of neuroimaging and lesion evidence that is largely correlational (R. Brown et al., 2019). This localization has been directly challenged: patients with substantial PFC damage can retain normal conscious access under some conditions (Boly et al., 2017; Odegaard et al., 2017), and the metacognitive deficits produced by PFC lesions are dissociable from conscious access itself. Second, and more fundamentally for the present argument, HOT provides no account of how the thalamus contributes to or arbitrates the availability of higher-order representation. The thalamus is absent from the theory's mechanism. Evidence from human thalamus is difficult for HOT to accommodate (Fang et al., 2025). Thalamic activity preceding PFC would not by itself trouble the theory, which could read it as first-order input. The difficulty is that the divergence between seen and unseen trials, the neural signature of consciousness itself, appeared in intralaminar and medial thalamus before lateral PFC. If the higher-order representation in PFC constitutes the conscious state, that divergence should arise there first.

2.2 Global Workspace Theory

GWT proposes that consciousness arises when information is broadcast broadly across a network of specialized processors via a global workspace -a distributed set of long-range cortical neurons, densest in prefrontal and parietal areas, that amplifies and disseminates locally processed content (Dehaene & Changeux, 2011; Mashour et al., 2020). GWT's core contribution is operational: it generates testable predictions about the signature of conscious access -late, widespread cortical activation and a non-linear ignition threshold -that have been replicated in paradigms designed to probe conscious versus non-conscious processing using EEG and MEG (Del Cul et al., 2007; Sergent et al., 2005), though the specificity of these signatures has been challenged by preregistered adversarial testing (Cogitate consortium et al., 2025).

Two limitations are relevant here. First, the "ignition" metaphor describes a cortical broadcasting event without specifying what determines whether a given representation reaches ignition threshold. The thalamus is a natural candidate for this gate -it is anatomically positioned to control what reaches cortical amplification. Early workspace formulations linked the workspace to thalamic and reticular gating (Newman & Baars, 1993), but the contemporary global neuronal workspace relocated the mechanism of access to longrange cortical ignition (Dehaene & Changeux, 2011; Mashour et al., 2020), with subcortical structures treated as inputs rather than as constitutive elements of selection. Second, the largest preregistered empirical test of GWT against IIT -the Cogitate consortium studyfound that the late-ignition signature predicted by GWT was not reliably observed in occipital cortex as the theory requires, weakening the specificity of GWT's signature claim (Cogitate consortium et al., 2025). This result does not falsify GWT, but it does indicate that the theory's mapping onto neural architecture is less precise than previously assumed and opens the question of what anatomical mechanism actually determines workspace access.

2.3 Integrated Information Theory

IIT identifies consciousness with integrated information (Φ), a formal measure of the causal power of a system above and beyond the sum of its parts (Albantakis et al., 2023; Tononi et al., 2016). IIT's core contribution is the most ambitious of the four frameworks: it proposes an intrinsic, substrate-independent definition of consciousness that in principle could be applied to any physical system and generates a rich set of structural predictions about the architecture of conscious systems.

The limitations are of two types. Empirically, IIT predicts that posterior cortical areascharacterized by highly integrated, densely recurrent architecture -should be the primary substrate of conscious experience, with the cerebellum (despite its large neuron count and predominantly feedforward structure) contributing minimally (Tononi et al., 2016). The Cogitate study found that the early, sustained posterior cortical activity predicted by IIT as the signature of conscious content was not reliably differentiated from non-conscious conditions (Cogitate consortium et al., 2025), weakening IIT's empirical support. Theoretically, IIT's treatment of the thalamus is paradoxical: the thalamocortical system is explicitly cited as a high-Φ complex and therefore a locus of consciousness (Tononi et al., 2016), yet the theory offers no mechanistic account of how thalamic architecture shapes the specific content of experience. The thalamus appears in IIT as part of the integrated complex, not as a selective operator. This distinction -between participating in an integrated system and actively determining its content -is precisely what the TMMC proposes to formalize.

2.4 Predictive Processing

PP frameworks propose that the brain is a hierarchical generative model that continuously predicts its own sensory inputs and updates its representations in proportion to prediction error (Clark, 2013; Friston, 2010; Hohwy, 2013). PP is the most anatomically articulated of the four frameworks: it assigns distinct functional roles to cortical layers (superficial layers carry prediction errors upward; deep layers carry predictions downward) and has been extended to account for attention, action, and interoception within a unified free-energy minimization scheme. Its core contribution is providing a principled computational language for describing top-down and bottom-up interactions -a language the TMMC partially adopts.

The limitation relevant to the present argument is that PP, despite its anatomical specificity at the cortical level, leaves the thalamus underspecified as a computational node. In the canonical PP hierarchy, the thalamus functions as a first-order relay that passes sensory prediction errors into the cortical hierarchy (Friston, 2010). Some extensions of PP have assigned the thalamus a role in precision-weighting, modulating the gain on prediction error signals (Kanai et al., 2015). Others treat higher-order thalamus as a Bayesian observer that updates cortical beliefs from contextual signals, with mediodorsal-like inputs setting the gain of recurrent cortical input rather than driving cortical neurons (Rikhye, Wimmer, et al., 2018). These accounts give the thalamus a computational role in inference but do not specify how one hypothesis is selected over its competitors; the TRN appears in them only as a possible route by which corticothalamic input enhances or suppresses thalamic predictions. PP's answer to which hypotheses achieve conscious access, precision weighting, is specified computationally but not anatomically. The TMMC proposes the TRN as its thalamic implementation.

2.5 Thalamic evidence the frameworks do not integrate

Against the theoretical background just described, a body of empirical evidence has accumulated that collectively establishes the thalamus as a causally active, anatomically specific node in the generation of conscious content. I summarize the key lines here; Section 3 provides the mechanistic integration.

Causal efficacy and temporal precedence.

Electrical stimulation of the central lateral (CL) thalamic nucleus in anesthetized macaques restores wake-like cortical activity and behavioral responsiveness at anesthetic doses that otherwise maintain unresponsiveness (Redinbaugh et al., 2020). This is interventional rather than correlational evidence: activating a single thalamic nucleus is sufficient to shift the global state of the cortex. Using simultaneous stereo-EEG (sEEG) recordings from thalamic nuclei and prefrontal cortex during near-threshold visual detection, Fang et al. (2025) found that consciousness-related activity emerged earlier and more strongly in the intralaminar (CM, Pf) and medial (MDm) nuclei than in ventral thalamic nuclei or lateral PFC (mean divergence onsets ≈ 252 vs. ≈ 280 ms after stimulus), and that theta-band phase synchronization and phase-amplitude coupling were directed from these nuclei to PFC. Temporal precedence does not by itself establish causation, and posterior cortex was not sampled; the findings are nonetheless inconsistent with the view that thalamic activity merely follows prefrontal ignition, and are compatible with higher-order thalamus gating the transfer of posteriorly generated content to frontal targets. Whether thalamic activity is needed to sustain conscious content, and not only to precede it, is addressed by Prediction 2, which tests whether MD damage accelerates the loss of maintained content while sparing its generation.

Computational architecture of the TRN.

The TRN consists exclusively of GABAergic neurons that inhibit thalamic nuclei but do not project to cortex, placing it in an anatomically ideal position to control cortical access (Jones, 2007; Pinault, 2004). In behaving mice, TRN subnetworks causally gate specific sensory channels according to task demands (Wimmer et al., 2015) and the nucleus comprises distinct subnetworks connected with first-order and higher-order thalamic nuclei (Y. Li et al., 2020; Martinez-Garcia et al., 2020). Section 3.1 reviews this evidence and its limits; the computation by which the TRN favors one channel over others remains unresolved.

Thalamic contributions to state and content.

Any framework that assigns the thalamus a role in conscious content, and not only in arousal, must specify how thalamic circuits for content relate to those for state. In mice, the same matrix-layer 5 circuit that supports the waking state also sets detection thresholds (Suzuki & Larkum, 2020; Takahashi et al., 2020), and core thalamocortical loops have been proposed to maintain specific contents over time (Whyte et al., 2024). This synthesis supports a thalamic contribution to content, but through mechanisms partly shared with state rather than through separate substrates (Section 5.1).

Mediodorsal nucleus and conscious experience in humans.

Direct evidence that thalamic activity is not merely enabling but causally consequential for conscious experience comes from stimulation studies: electrical stimulation of the mediodorsal nucleus (MD) in awake human patients produces reportable changes in conscious experience in the large majority of stimulations, rather than merely permissive or arousal-gating effects. In Section 3.3 I provide details on the character of these changes and their bearing on MD's proposed contentnonspecific sustainer role (Pantis et al., 2026). None of the dominant theories accommodates this evidence within its core mechanism: it is noted, reframed as modulatory, or left outside the mechanistic account. What is missing is a framework that assigns the thalamus a constitutive role in determining conscious content, specifies the anatomical components and operations of that role, and generates predictions that discriminate it from corticocentric accounts. The TMMC is a proposal to fill that gap.

3.1 Thalamic reticular nucleus

The TRN is a thin sheet of GABAergic neurons that envelops the dorsal thalamus and receives collaterals of virtually all corticothalamic and thalamocortical axons as they pass through it (Jones, 2007). Its defining anatomical property -GABAergic neurons that project exclusively to dorsal thalamic nuclei, never to cortex -places the TRN in a structurally ideal position to control what information reaches cortical amplification without itself participating in the cortical representation. It does not select what enters consciousness; it eliminates what does not. This is the sense in which the TMMC designates it as a negative-selection operator: the content that becomes conscious is the residue of the TRN's inhibitory field, not its target. Inhibition is not, however, purely suppressive: in mouse midline thalamus, transient TRN inhibition followed by its release produced rebound excitation that promoted a behavioral decision, whereas sustained inhibition impaired it (Ma et al., 2024). Negative selection describes the TRN's action on losing channels; its effect on a winning channel may depend on the timing of inhibition. In the monkey MD, moreover, about 60% of TRN boutons synapse on GABAergic local circuit neurons rather than on relay neurons (Tai et al., 1995), so TRN activity there may disinhibit, as well as inhibit, the relay neurons that project to prefrontal cortex.

How the TRN implements content-selective rather than uniform inhibition is not yet established, and the TMMC's proposal should be read with that limitation in view. Three lines of evidence constrain it. First, the TRN gates specific channels causally in behaving animals: in a cross-modal attention task, visual TRN neurons tracked which modality the mouse was about to select under a task rule, optogenetic activation or silencing of these neurons shifted performance in the predicted direction, and silencing abolished the differential inhibition of the lateral geniculate nucleus between attended and unattended trials (Wimmer et al., 2015). Second, the TRN is not homogeneous. It contains genetically and physiologically distinct subnetworks, including a core population connected with first-order nuclei and a shell population connected with higher-order nuclei (Y. Li et al., 2020; Martinez-Garcia et al., 2020), as well as populations whose activity is differentially linked to sensory and limbic targets (Halassa et al., 2014). Third, inhibition within the TRN has sources of different spatial reach.

In mouse slices, pallidal input inhibited every recorded TRN neuron, whereas intra-reticular inhibition was sparse and arose mainly from somatostatin-expressing neurons (Cho et al., 2025, preprint). This combination of diffuse and selective inhibition is of the kind a selection operator would require, but slice data do not show how the circuitry operates in vivo. The TMMC therefore treats channel-specific gating by the TRN as established, and the computation by which the TRN ranks competing channels as an open question that the selection pressures described below are meant to frame rather than resolve.

3.2 Selection pressures

The TMMC treats the TRN as a site of biased competition (Desimone & Duncan, 1995) relocated from cortex to thalamus. Two anatomically distinct classes of input converge on it (Fig. 1). Bids arrive from below, as collaterals of thalamocortical axons that branch in the reticular sheet on their way to cortex (Jones, 2007; Pinault, 2004); each bid is a copy of what a thalamic channel is currently sending to cortex. Biases arrive from above and from the side: from layer 6 corticothalamic axons, which branch in the TRN before reaching their thalamic targets (Bourassa et al., 1995); from the amygdala (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2012); and from prefrontal cortex, both directly (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2012) and via the basal ganglia (Nakajima et al., 2019). Two corticothalamic pathways bypass the TRN: layer 5 axons, which drive higher-order nuclei (Bourassa et al., 1995; Reichova & Sherman, 2004; Sherman & Guillery, 1996), and a layer 6b population that innervates higher-order nuclei selectively (Hoerder-Suabedissen et al., 2018). Frontal cortex is an exception. In mice, layer 5 pyramidaltract neurons of frontal cortex give off collaterals in the anterior TRN on their way to the thalamus, basal ganglia and brainstem, whereas those of sensory cortices rarely do, and the TRN neurons they drive inhibit frontal-related thalamic nuclei, including the MD and intralaminar nuclei, in a feedforward and lateral manner (Hádinger et al., 2023). A comparable projection was first proposed for primate prefrontal cortex on morphological grounds (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2006), and in prelimbic cortex it targets shell-type TRN neurons that innervate the midline thalamus (Ma et al., 2024). The TMMC draws one rule from this anatomy: an input defines a selection pressure only if it reaches the TRN and acts on specific channels. Inputs that bypass the TRN shape the bids rather than biasing the competition among them. Each pressure is defined by the input class it uses and the comparison it supports.

Salience (r_sal) is carried by bids from first-order nuclei. A single first-order relay copy carries intensity and change for its own channel, but salience is relational: a stimulus stands out only against its competitors. The TMMC therefore proposes that salience is computed in the TRN by comparison across first-order bids. TRN neurons connected with first-order nuclei form a central, or core, population with strong bursting and rapidly depressing inputs suited to discrete sensory events (Y. Li et al., 2020; Martinez-Garcia et al., 2020). Burst firing at stimulus onset, which drives the TRN strongly, has been proposed as a thalamic "wake-up" signal (Sherman, 2001). Of the four pressures, r_sal has the weakest direct support as a distinct TRN computation. It is established that the TRN causally gates first-order channels. That the TRN ranks first-order bids against one another is a TMMC hypothesis.

Prediction (r_pred) arises from the interaction between layer 6 biases and the bids of the channel they target. The same layer 6 axon excites relay neurons directly and inhibits them through the corresponding TRN sector (Bourassa et al., 1995; Crandall et al., 2015). Varela et al. (2024) propose that this push-pull arrangement imposes an expectation template: relay neurons representing expected input are depolarized into tonic mode, while those representing unexpected input are hyperpolarized into a burst-ready state, so that an unexpected driver input triggers a burst. On this account, the comparison between expectation and input takes place in the thalamocortical-TRN microcircuit, and its outcome reaches the TRN as a change in the bid: a burst-amplified bid when input violates expectation. The mechanism has been tested only in single-cell simulation. Independent evidence is consistent with parts of it: corticofugal feedback modulates burst occurrence during behavior (Ortuño et al., 2014), layer 6 ensembles regulate the behavioral impact of sensory deviants (Voigts et al., 2020), and TRN neurons show change-detection responses (Yu et al., 2009). r_pred is signed rather than a pressure that favors mismatch. The net effect of corticothalamic feedback on relay neurons switches from suppression to enhancement as layer 6 firing increases (Crandall et al., 2015), and valid expectations make conscious access faster and more likely (Meijs et al., 2018; Melloni et al., 2011; Pinto et al., 2015). The TMMC therefore treats r_pred as signed gain control: confirmed expectations facilitate access of the expected content, and strong violations capture access through amplified bids. The two halves of r_pred also differ in their dependence on awareness: stimulus-evoked prediction errors are generated even for stimuli that do not reach awareness, whereas expectations take effect only when the predictive cue has itself been consciously perceived (Meijs et al., 2018). If this asymmetry holds for the corticothalamic template, r_pred is recursive across gating cycles: content selected on one cycle sets the layer 6 template against which input on the next is compared. This recursive reading is a TMMC interpretation; the cited evidence is behavioral and corticalelectrophysiological, and does not address thalamic circuitry. Layer 6 feedback is not confined to first-order nuclei. In the visual system, a subset of layer 6a neurons projects to first-and higher-order thalamus together with the shell TRN (Katzner et al., 2025). The TMMC proposes that the same interaction operates on higher-order channels, as an expectation about which loop should be active. This would extend r_pred from sensory input to endogenous content.

That extension is a TMMC hypothesis.

The account yields a test that separates r_pred from the other pressures (Section 6.4).

Coherence (r_coh) is carried by bids from higher-order nuclei. Higher-order nuclei such as the pulvinar and posterior medial nucleus are driven by layer 5 pyramidal neurons (Reichova & Sherman, 2004; Sherman & Guillery, 1996), whose output depends on coupling between the apical dendrite, which receives feedback and contextual input, and the soma, which receives feedforward input (Larkum, 2013). This coupling is state-dependent. Anesthesia decouples layer 5 neurons, and so do inactivation of higher-order thalamus and blockade of metabotropic glutamate and cholinergic receptors (Suzuki & Larkum, 2020). Coupling is therefore a property of the loop rather than of cortex alone: higher-order thalamus contributes to layer 5 coupling and is driven by its output. A higher-order relay copy reaching the TRN thus samples the reverberation of a cortico-thalamo-cortical loop, that is, the extent to which feedforward input and stored context are converging on the same cortical representation. Layer 6b, which bypasses the TRN and provides purely excitatory drive to higher-order nuclei, has been proposed to sustain such loops (Hoerder-Suabedissen et al., 2018; Zolnik et al., 2026). In TMMC terms it would strengthen higher-order bids without itself constituting a pressure. The TMMC proposes that loop reverberation is the physiological expression of mnemonic fit: a candidate that matches a learned pattern recruits coincident apical and basal input, couples layer 5, and drives its higher-order relay. This link between higher-order relay output and mnemonic fit is a TMMC hypothesis; it has not been measured. TRN neurons connected with higher-order nuclei form a distinct shell, or edge, population whose slower, less depressing inputs drive more persistent firing (Y. Li et al., 2020; Martinez-Garcia et al., 2020), a profile suited to signals integrated over time. Mnemonic coherence in this sense is representational and should not be confused with oscillatory coherence between signals, such as the theta coherence between hippocampus and prefrontal cortex discussed for RE (Section 3.5). A loop can be synchronized without matching any stored pattern, though the two likely correlate in practice.

Value (r_val) is carried by biases that weight channels by relevance rather than by stimulus properties. Affective relevance arrives directly from the amygdala through strong excitatory terminals on TRN neurons (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2012; Section 3.6). Because these terminals excite TRN neurons, which inhibit their targets, the amygdala can favor a channel only by driving the TRN sectors of its competitors; whether its terminals select those sectors has not been established. Goal relevance arrives from prefrontal cortex by two routes. In mice, it is relayed through the basal ganglia (Nakajima et al., 2019): engaging this pathway lets internally generated goals suppress the task-irrelevant sensory modality and filter background noise within a modality. The pathway contains two inhibitory steps, so prefrontal drive disinhibits the TRN and increases its suppression of goal-irrelevant channels. The TRN end of this route has been shown causally (Wimmer et al., 2015; Section 3.1). In primates, posterior orbitofrontal and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices also project directly and widely to the TRN, including caudal sectors, where their terminals overlap those from temporal association cortices (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2006 , 2012), so goal and value signals can reach the competition without the basal ganglia relay.

Novelty and unexpectedness. Separating r_pred from r_coh distinguishes three cases. A familiar configuration in its expected context produces strong coherence with confirmed expectation, the case in which r_pred facilitates rather than captures access. A familiar configuration in an unexpected context produces a strong violation with strong coherence. A novel configuration produces a strong violation with coherence that holds for its parts but not for the whole (Section 4.1). The distinction between parts and whole does not require a second coherence signal. Each higher-order bid indexes the coupling of one loop: coherence of the parts is coupling in loops through the cortical areas that represent the elements, and coherence of the whole is coupling in loops through higher-tier areas that represent the configuration as a whole. This mirrors the distinction between novelty and surprise in the learning literature (Barto et al., 2013) and yields a graded prediction. Both violation cases should engage the core TRN through amplified first-order bids, but shell engagement should be weaker in the novel case than in the familiar-unexpected case, because the loop representing the configuration as a whole is not coupled. The mapping of parts and whole onto loops at different cortical tiers is a TMMC hypothesis.

Competition, not intersection. The content that survives TRN selection is not the content that satisfies all four pressures. It is the candidate with the greatest combined weight under the current biases (Fig. 2). The weights differ across operating modes (Section 5.2). A novel stimulus can win with weak coherence, and dream content can win with weak salience and prediction signals. The outcome of this competition at each gating cycle defines the pinhole (Section 3.7). Coherence is measured before selection, not after. Because loop coherence is also characteristic of the conscious state, r_coh risks circularity: the pressure that selects for consciousness cannot be the outcome of selection. The TMMC therefore defines r_coh as the coherence of candidate loops before selection, partial and competing, and defines selection as the TRN-mediated amplification of one candidate at the expense of the others. Because higher-order thalamus itself contributes to coupling, pre-selection coherence is partly set by the thalamus. This is consistent with the TMMC's position that coherence is a loop property. The ordering is testable: higher-order collateral drive to the shell TRN should rise in the winning channel before conscious access and fall in the losing channels.

Relation to prior proposals. Min (2010) proposed that conscious perception arises from integrating first-order and higher-order signals across the TRN. The TMMC specifies the higher-order signal as an index of layer 5 loop coupling and assigns it a distinct TRN population. Dendritic Integration Theory (DIT) places layer 5 coupling and higher-order thalamus at the center of conscious processing (Aru et al., 2020). In its account, a content becomes conscious when feature-specific input to the basal compartment of layer 5 neurons matches contextual input to the apical compartment, which can include semantic and episodic knowledge, while the neurons are coupled, a state controlled by higher-order thalamus. The TMMC reads this match as the mnemonic fit that higher-order bids carry to the TRN (r_coh). What it adds is competition: DIT proposes that dendritic integration occurs in parallel across the cortical sheet, and does not specify how matching candidates compete for access (Section 3.7).

Scope.

Three limits apply. First, the coherence reading fits best the higher-order nuclei, and subdivisions, whose driving input comes mainly from layer 5. The pulvinar is not uniform in this respect: its inferior and ventrolateral subdivisions also receive direct input from the superficial superior colliculus, relayed to area MT (Berman & Wurtz, 2010), and this tectopulvinar route supports visually guided saccades without awareness after V1 lesions (Kinoshita et al., 2019). The MD and RE also receive substantial non-cortical drive (ventral pallidum and amygdala for the MD; hippocampus and subiculum for RE), so relay copies from these nuclei and subdivisions are mixed signals and are not given a pure r_coh reading. Second, the frontal layer 5 projection to the anterior TRN (Hádinger et al., 2023; Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2006) is a bias, not a bid. Which pressure it carries is unresolved. TRN neurons driven by it track the magnitude of synchronous frontal activity (Hádinger et al., 2023), which resembles the loop signal behind r_coh, whereas in prelimbic cortex it carried outcomedependent information about an upcoming decision (Ma et al., 2024), closer to r_val. Prefrontal projections that reach the caudal, sensory sectors (from areas 46, 13 and 9) are treated here as r_val (Value, above). The two cannot yet be separated by cortical layer, because single prefrontal axons in the TRN carry both the small boutons typical of layer 6 and the large boutons attributed to layer 5 (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2006).

What prefrontal layer 6 input to the TRN carries is also unspecified, so the assignment of layer 6 input to r_pred applies to sensory cortex. Third, the core/shell division has been characterized in mice; whether primate TRN shows the same organization has not been established.

3.3 Mediodorsal nucleus

The MD has extensive reciprocal connections with prefrontal cortex, is preferentially connected with medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and has been identified as a subcortical node of the default mode network (DMN) (Alves et al., 2019; Mengxing et al., 2023). Using 7T fMRI and dynamic causal modeling, Harrison et al. (2022) found dissociable roles for the MD and the basal forebrain (BF) in modulating DMN cortex. The MD was engaged during self-focused cognition, where its excitatory influence on DMN regions outlasted the cortical DMN response. BF responses at the onset of task and rest blocks preceded changes in mPFC, consistent with a role at transitions between internal and external states. During self-focused cognition, BF also exerted a strong inhibitory influence on the MD. The authors relate this to GABAergic BF projections to the thalamus (R. E. Brown & McKenna, 2015) but do not establish the mechanism. In TMMC terms, these results suggest BF as a switching signal at mode transitions and the MD as a sustainer of prefrontal activity once engaged. Two limits apply: the external condition was a lexical judgment task rather than perceptual engagement, and dynamic causal modeling of BOLD signals estimates effective connectivity, not synaptic interaction.

The mechanistic substrate for the MD's sustaining role comes from rodent optogenetic studies.

In mice holding one of two attentional rules across a sub-second delay, MD suppression largely spared rule encoding at cue presentation but diminished the maintenance of the rule representation in prefrontal cortex, and MD dependence increased with delay length (Schmitt et al., 2017). MD neurons active during the delay did not distinguish between the rules, and their activity depended on prefrontal input. The MD acted by amplifying functional connectivity among prefrontal neurons: its activation increased the firing of fast-spiking interneurons and the connectivity of regular-spiking neurons without changing their firing rates, and amplified intracortical responses supralinearly without driving them (Schmitt et al., 2017). With delays of 10 and 60 s, inhibiting MD terminals in medial prefrontal cortex impaired spatial working memory only at the 60-s delay, and diminished the firing of delay-elevated prefrontal neurons whose activity peaked in the middle and late delay while largely sparing early-peaking ones; these neurons showed no spatial tuning (Bolkan et al., 2017). Spatially tuned prefrontal neurons depended instead on ventral hippocampal input during encoding, a double dissociation between a hippocampal route that delivers content and an MD route that sustains prefrontal activity (Bolkan et al., 2017). The authors conclude that local prefrontal circuits may suffice to maintain representations at short timescales but require MD amplification as representations decay or task demands increase. MD delay activity is also lower in dimensionality than its prefrontal afferents (Wolff & Halassa, 2024). In TMMC terms, the MD is a content-nonspecific amplifier of prefrontal activity: it sustains the conditions under which cortical content persists rather than storing that content.

Content-nonspecificity is a property of this sustaining role as characterized in rodents, not of the MD as a whole. In mice, MD activity encodes task context and is required for switching between the prefrontal representations that context selects (Rikhye, Gilra, et al., 2018). In primates, MD delay activity carries spatial and stimulus information (Tanibuchi & Goldman-Rakic, 2003; Watanabe & Funahashi, 2004), and lateral MD neurons relay direction-specific corollary discharge to the frontal eye fields, whose inactivation prevents receptive-field remapping (Sommer & Wurtz, 2006). These differences may reflect species, the MD subdivision recorded, or task demands (Parnaudeau et al., 2018). The MD's effect on prefrontal cortex is also not only amplifying. In mice, one genetically distinct MD projection amplifies prefrontal activity through VIP interneurons and is required when task inputs are sparse, whereas another suppresses it through PV interneurons and is required when inputs are dense but conflicting; MD neurons encoded input conflict rather than the choice, which was represented in prefrontal cortex (Mukherjee et al., 2021). The TMMC's claim is restricted to the sustaining role of the MD-prefrontal loop.

The MD's role in the TMMC is therefore downstream of, and agnostic to, the source of the content it sustains. Because MD activity in this sustaining role does not carry item content (Bolkan et al., 2017; Schmitt et al., 2017; Wolff & Halassa, 2024), the MD cannot discriminate whether the prefrontal activity it amplifies originated in sensory input or in hippocampal content coordinated by RE. Consistent with this, consciousness-related MD activity is present in perception, where it precedes that of lateral PFC (Fang et al., 2025). The MD's importance is set instead by how much external drive the content has. Content under continuous input is partly held up by that input, whereas endogenous content has none and depends on MDmediated sustainment. The association of the MD with internally focused cognition (Harrison et al., 2022) follows from this difference in drive, not from a specialization of the MD for endogenous content (the endogenous mode; Section 5.2.2). Prediction 2 (Section 6.2) tests this account.

Direct electrical stimulation of the MD in awake neurosurgical patients produced reportable changes in experience in 89% of stimulations (Pantis et al., 2026). The changes were mostly visceral, somatosensory and affective (diffuse bodily sensations and shifts in emotional tone) rather than specific representational content. This fits a content-nonspecific role, in which stimulation alters the gain or affective coloring of ongoing experience. Affective and bodily coloring is itself experiential content, however, and may reflect the MD's limbic afferents from the amygdala and ventral pallidum; the stimulation data do not separate these possibilities.

3.4 Hippocampus

The hippocampus is included as the source of one class of endogenous content, not of endogenous content in general. The basis is the episodic simulation hypothesis: the hippocampus constructs scene-based representations that serve both retrieval of the past and imagination of the future (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007; Schacter et al., 2007). Lesion evidence limits this assignment. Patients with selective bilateral hippocampal damage after autoimmune limbic encephalitis mind-wander as often as controls. Their spontaneous thoughts shift from episodic, scene-based and temporally displaced content toward semantic, verbal and presentoriented content, while remaining as self-referential (C. McCormick et al., 2018). Hippocampal damage thus changes the character of endogenous content without abolishing its generation, and the TMMC assigns the hippocampus the episodic, scene-based subtype.

The sharp-wave ripple (SWR) is a candidate mechanism by which this content reaches prefrontal cortex and the loops that sustain it. SWRs are high-synchrony CA3→CA1 events that compress and transmit sequence representations to downstream targets (Buzsáki, 2015; Carr et al., 2011; Joo & Frank, 2018). These sequences can be prospective as well as retrospective: before a novel track is explored, place-cell sequences that later match its layout occur during rest (Dragoi & Tonegawa, 2011), and during pauses in an open-field navigation task, ripple-associated sequences depict the path the rat will take next to a remembered goal, even from start-goal combinations it has not experienced before (Pfeiffer & Foster, 2013).

SWRs are temporally coupled with DMN activity in macaques and humans (Kaplan et al., 2016; Norman et al., 2021). Iwata et al. (2024) recorded hippocampal activity for 9-15 days in 10 patients, with hourly experience sampling. SWR rates in the five minutes before a report were higher when thoughts were more vivid, less focused on the external task and more visually imaginable, and were more strongly related to these features than to physical activity.

The authors note that the data are correlational and the window broad, so SWRs may set a mode that favors self-generated thought rather than encode its content. Both readings are compatible with the TMMC: the hippocampus supplies candidate content, and thalamocortical selection determines which candidate becomes conscious.

The source of non-hippocampal endogenous content, the semantic and verbal mindwandering that persists after hippocampal damage, is not specified by the current architecture (Section 4.3). One anatomical candidate is the frontal layer 5 projection to the TRN (Hádinger et al., 2023; Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2006). Its effect on thalamic targets can be facilitatory: through rebound in mice (Ma et al., 2024), and possibly through disinhibition in the primate MD (Tai et al., 1995). That this route lets frontally generated content engage MD-mediated sustainment is a TMMC hypothesis.

3.5 Nucleus reuniens

The RE is a major source of thalamic input to the hippocampus and the principal thalamic node linking the hippocampus and mPFC (Dolleman-van Der Weel et al., 2019). The mPFC has no direct projection to the hippocampus and reaches it indirectly, including through RE (mPFC→RE→HC); a parallel HC→RE→mPFC route is also documented (Dolleman-van Der Weel et al., 2019). This position defines RE's place in the TMMC. Hippocampal content reaches mPFC directly: spatially tuned prefrontal neurons depend on ventral hippocampal input, whereas MD input sustains prefrontal activity without spatial tuning (Bolkan et al., 2017; Section 3.3). Because this projection bypasses the thalamus, hippocampal content arrives in prefrontal cortex unselected. In the TMMC it becomes conscious only if the MD-prefrontal loop that sustains it wins the competition at the TRN, through MD bids to the TRN (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2012) and the frontal layer 5 collaterals that bias the anterior TRN (Section 3.2).

That selection of hippocampal content acts on its sustaining loop rather than on its route of arrival is a TMMC hypothesis. The subiculum also projects to the anterior thalamic nuclei (Aggleton et al., 2010), but RE is the thalamic nucleus positioned to coordinate hippocampal and prefrontal activity.

RE does not generate the rhythms that organize hippocampal output. Hippocampal theta depends on medial septal pacing together with intrinsic hippocampal and entorhinal generators (Buzsáki, 2002), and sharp-wave ripples arise within the CA3-CA1 circuit (Buzsáki, 2015). RE's contribution is coordination. It is required for hippocampal-prefrontal theta coherence during spatial working memory (Hallock et al., 2016). Its inactivation reduces this coherence and impairs the retrieval of extinction memories (Totty et al., 2023). It also initiates, rather than follows, transient beta synchrony between the two structures during memory retrieval (Jayachandran et al., 2023). In the TMMC, RE and the MD play complementary roles rather than forming successive stages of a relay. RE coordinates the arrival of episodic, hippocampally generated content in mPFC (Section 3.4), and the MD sustains the prefrontal activity that represents it, whatever its source (Section 3.3). The temporal order of RE coordination, MD engagement and prefrontal activity during endogenous events has not been measured. Prediction 1 (Section 6.1) tests whether the thalamic lead seen in perception (Fang et al., 2025) holds for endogenous content.

3.6 Amygdala

The amygdala is a component of the selection system because it projects directly to the TRN. In rhesus monkeys, the basal, basomedial and cortical amygdalar nuclei send excitatory projections to the TRN, a pathway not previously described in any species (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2012). The terminals are concentrated anteriorly (about 70%) but extend into central and posterior sensory sectors. They have the highest proportion of large, proximal boutons, the configuration most effective at driving postsynaptic neurons, of any TRN afferent characterized, including that from the MD. Their field overlaps those of MD and posterior orbitofrontal afferents in anterior TRN. The authors propose that this convergence lets amygdalar, prefrontal and MD input override the comparatively weak, small-bouton input from sensory cortex in these sectors. The pathway makes the amygdala a source of biases, alongside layer 6 cortex and the prefrontal-basal ganglia route.

The amygdala also acts indirectly. It enhances the encoding of emotionally salient memories through noradrenergic and glucocorticoid modulation of hippocampal plasticity (McGaugh, 2004). Hippocampal and amygdalar reactivations are coordinated during NREM sharp-wave ripples after emotional learning (Girardeau et al., 2017). An amygdala→MD→posterior orbitofrontal pathway (Timbie & Barbas, 2015) gives emotional relevance access to the loop that sustains content. These routes bias which content is generated and sustained, but the case for the amygdala as a component rests on the direct TRN projection.

3.7 The pinhole

The limited capacity of consciousness, its serial and focal character relative to the parallel processing of the brain, is a central explanandum for any mechanistic theory. The TMMC accounts for it through the pinhole: at each gating cycle, competition at the TRN admits only a few winning configurations, so the states that can be stabilized at a given moment occupy a manifold of much lower dimensionality than the full cortical representation space. The selection pressures determine where this manifold lies; the competition, through TRN inhibition of losing channels, determines how small it is (Fig. 2A). Two substrates could support competition between channels. The first is open-loop connectivity, through which TRN neurons driven by one channel inhibit relay neurons of others. TRN feedback to relay neurons is mainly open-loop and produces lateral inhibition (Crabtree, 2018; Pinault, 2004), and in rat slices, activating neurons in one thalamic nucleus inhibits neurons in another through the TRN, within specific groups of nuclei that include higher-order and intralaminar nuclei (Crabtree & Isaac, 2002). The second is inhibition within the TRN. In rodents, chemical synapses between TRN neurons are largely lost after the second postnatal week, while electrical coupling persists (reviewed in Crabtree, 2018), and intra-reticular inhibition in slices is sparse (Cho et al., 2025, preprint). Open-loop connectivity is therefore the better-supported substrate, although the internuclear evidence comes from rat slices and has not been tested for the MD. Single TRN neurons can also innervate more than one thalamic nucleus (Hádinger et al., 2023; Pinault et al., 1995). That TRN-mediated competition imposes a capacity limit of this kind is a TMMC hypothesis. Whyte et al. (2024) also propose that the thalamus lowers the dimensionality of conscious content, but through the convergence of many cortical inputs onto far fewer thalamic neurons, so that dimensionality is fixed by connectivity. In the TMMC it arises from competition, varies with the pressure weights, and holds within a single gating cycle.

This cycle-level claim must be kept distinct from the complexity of thalamocortical dynamics measured over extended windows, which is reliably higher in conscious states. EEG signal complexity falls under propofol anesthesia (Schartner et al., 2015); the complexity of fMRI functional connectivity tracks the level of consciousness across sedation and disorders of consciousness (Varley, Craig, et al., 2020; Varley, Luppi, et al., 2020); and the perturbational complexity index shows the same relationship (Casali et al., 2013). The two are compatible.

A system that reselects a different low-dimensional configuration on each 125-250 ms cycle (Section 3.8) traces a highly differentiated trajectory across many cycles, which a windowed measure registers as high complexity. On this account, the collapse of complexity under anesthesia reflects a failure of content cycling, not a change in the dimensionality of any one state. This is a TMMC hypothesis; no study has measured TRN-mediated content cycling under anesthesia.

The account yields a test. Dimensionality computed in windows time-locked to the phase of the gating cycle should show successive low-dimensional configurations, each differing from the last, rather than continuously high-dimensional activity. IIT, which attributes complexity to the causal structure of the system as a whole, does not predict this cycle-locked structure. A formal treatment, deriving trajectory-level complexity from cycle-to-cycle occupancy of the manifold, is part of the research program described in Section 7.5.

3.8 Rhythmic gating and thalamic pacing frequency

The TMMC proposes that selection is sampled rhythmically rather than continuously. Its temporal unit is the TRN gating cycle, provisionally 125-250 ms (about 4-8 Hz). At each cycle the weighting of the four pressures is updated and C(t) changes discretely, so changes in content are not visible at shorter timescales. That this rhythm originates in TRN-dorsal thalamus loops and propagates to cortex is a TMMC hypothesis; the range is set to the theta band, which current directional evidence favors (see below), and would need revision if the rhythm proved to be alpha. The proposed rhythm is distinct from hippocampal theta, which depends on septal pacing and hippocampal and entorhinal generators (Section 3.5); whether the two are phase-coupled during endogenous content generation is an open question.

Rhythmic sampling itself is well supported. Detection of near-threshold stimuli depends on the phase of ongoing oscillations at stimulus onset, with effects in both the alpha and theta ranges (Mathewson et al., 2009; VanRullen, 2016). Phase dependence is compatible with a cortical origin in either band, however. The TMMC's specific claim concerns direction.

On direction, the evidence differs by band. For alpha, human intracranial recordings during quiet wakefulness show cortical alpha bursts preceding pulvinar bursts. Corticothalamic Granger causality exceeds the reverse in nearly 90% of channel pairs, and thalamic alpha phase is predicted by cortical high-gamma activity rather than the converse (Halgren et al., 2019). This favors a cortical origin of alpha at rest. For theta, rhythmic stimulation of the ferret lateral posterior/pulvinar complex at theta (about 5 Hz) but not alpha (15-16 Hz, the ferret alpha band) frequency improved sustained attention and reduced omissions. The effect was mediated by increased thalamocortical and corticocortical theta coupling, and thalamus-tocortex theta drive rose with attentional demand while the reverse direction did not (Huang et al., 2024). In macaques performing spatial attention, information flow between the pulvinar and frontoparietal cortex ran from pulvinar to cortex during attentional engagement and reversed during disengagement (Fiebelkorn et al., 2019). In humans, theta-band (2-8 Hz) phase synchronization and phase-amplitude coupling were directed from intralaminar and medial thalamic nuclei to prefrontal cortex during near-threshold visual detection (Fang et al., 2025). This is the only directional theta evidence from human thalamus in a conscious-access task.

Three limits apply to this evidence as a whole. First, none of these studies recorded the TRN: the animal work targeted the pulvinar/LP complex, and the human work intralaminar and medial nuclei. Second, Huang et al. show that imposed theta is sufficient to improve behavior, not that endogenous theta originates in the thalamus. Third, in Fang et al. the band was defined broadly and evoked responses were not removed before phase analysis, so stimuluslocked transients may contribute to the directional estimate. Within these limits, the evidence suggests a dissociation by band: alpha runs from cortex to thalamus at rest, and theta from thalamus to cortex during engagement. Theta is therefore the better-supported candidate for a thalamically originating selection rhythm. Prediction 3 (Section 6.3) tests this in a contentselection paradigm. Note. Only the five components developed as dedicated architectural elements in Section 3 are listed. Other bias sources, layer 6 corticothalamic feedback and the prefrontal-basal ganglia route, are specified within the selection pressures (Section 3.2). Ascending neuromodulatory systems are treated as setting the state of the competition (Section 5.3).

4. The mnemonic thesis

The mnemonic thesis states that all conscious content has mnemonic structure. As noted in Section 1, the claim has precedent in Damasio's multiregional retroactivation (Damasio, 1989) and Edelman's "remembered present" (G. Edelman, 2001); the TMMC's contribution is the mechanism that selects which stored configuration is reconstructed at a given moment (Section 3).

Three clarifications are essential. First, the thesis does not assert that consciousness is recall: the activation of a mnemonic pattern in the context of current sensory input is not the same operation as deliberately retrieving a past episode. The patterns are activated, updated, and recombined in real time; their mnemonic character refers to their origin as learned representations, not to their functional deployment as memories. Second, the thesis does not assert that all conscious content requires hippocampal involvement: the cortical pattern repertoire is largely independent of the hippocampus once consolidated, and perceptual experience can draw on that repertoire without engaging hippocampal episodic generation. Third, and most importantly, the thesis does not deny the causal role of the external world in shaping conscious experience. The world constrains which mnemonic configurations are activated -via r_sal and r_pred -and the repertoire itself is built through genuine interaction with the world across development and continuously revised thereafter.

4.1 The cortex as mnemonic substrate

Cortical circuits store information in synaptic weights modified by experience. Hebb (1949) proposed that such modifications bind neurons into cell assemblies that can be reactivated as a unit, and attractor network models later showed formally how a stored pattern can be retrieved from partial input (Hopfield, 1982). The mnemonic thesis draws the consequence for experience: the configurations selected into the thalamocortical loop are learned patterns at every level, from primary sensory to conceptual. Even in V1, basic tuning properties are consistent with efficient coding of natural image statistics (Simoncelli & Olshausen, 2001), and contextual modulation is reshaped by task-relevant top-down signals during perceptual learning while basic receptive-field tuning remains largely stable (W. Li et al., 2004).

Perception, imagery and memory-guided recall draw on shared cortical representations at several levels of the visual hierarchy. In category-selective cortex, imagery of faces and places activates the regions defined by perception of the same categories, with 84-92% of imagery-active voxels falling within them, although imagery responses are weaker (O' Craven & Kanwisher, 2000). In V1 and V2, encoding models of low-level feature tuning, trained only on responses to photographs, identify which memorized scene a participant is recalling, with accuracy approaching that for perception in well-tuned voxels (Naselaris et al., 2015). In parieto-occipital EEG, patterns evoked by perceiving and imagining the same object crossclassify in the alpha band, with a shared geometry best explained by high-level visual features (Xie et al., 2020). Transcranial magnetic stimulation of medial occipital cortex impairs imagery and perception to a comparable degree (Kosslyn et al., 1999). At the conceptual level, distributed patterns in heteromodal semantic hubs are better explained by experiential and sensorimotor structure than by taxonomic or distributional structure (Fernandino et al., 2022). Under the mnemonic thesis this convergence is expected: perception, imagery and recall reactivate the same stored patterns through different afferent routes.

When the TRN weighs r_coh, it does not compare the current state with an abstract template; on the TMMC's proposal it reads, through higher-order relay output, how strongly a candidate configuration is stabilized by the cortex's learned attractor landscape. Configurations within that landscape can be sustained by re-entrant thalamocortical activity because the cortex has the machinery to sustain them. This also clarifies how a genuinely novel stimulus can become conscious under a thesis that makes all content mnemonic. Novelty is a property of the configuration, not of its elements: a six-legged mammal is unfamiliar as a whole but is composed of learned parts (bodies, legs, fur, gait), each of which is highly coherent with the repertoire. Such a stimulus produces a strong prediction violation (r_pred) with weak coherence for the configuration as a whole (r_coh), and what becomes conscious is the bestfitting composition of learned elements, the same constructive operation that underlies imagination (Hassabis & Maguire, 2007). The experience of novelty is, on this account, the experience of a composition that the repertoire supports in its parts but not as a whole. This compositional account of novelty is a TMMC proposal.

4.2 Completion and categorical perception

Perceptual completion shows the same principle where feedforward input is absent. The visual system fills in scotomas, the blind spot and occluded regions with vivid percepts. The proposed mechanism is not isomorphic spreading but activation of feature-tuned neurons in the deep layers of V1 by horizontal and feedback input (Komatsu, 2006). Laminar recordings at the macaque blind spot show a corresponding signature, low-beta LFP in superficial layers and multiunit activity concentrated in layer 5, present only under filling-in conditions (Saito et al., 2023). Categorical perception can be read as attractor completion (Damper & Harnad, 2000).

In macaque inferior temporal cortex, responses to stimuli morphed toward a stored exemplar converge over time onto a single profile, whereas responses to morphs toward an image with no stored representation remain graded; the authors' attractor model reproduces this asymmetry only when one member of the pair is a stored memory (Akrami et al., 2009). An attractor model of active categorization yields the same convergence (Volpi et al., 2014).

4.3 A taxonomy of conscious content

The mnemonic thesis generates a natural taxonomy of conscious content types, distinguished not by their phenomenological qualities but by the source, pathway, and constraint conditions of the mnemonic activation that produces them. Four types are identified, corresponding to the three operating modes of the TMMC (Section 5.2) and their subdivisions.

Conscious perception is the activation of cortical patterns by ascending sensory input, with selection weighted toward r_sal and r_pred. The sense of seeing the world directly reflects high coherence between input and a repertoire built from past contact with it. Expectation acts in both directions (Section 3.2): a valid expectation favors the expected configuration when input is weak or ambiguous (Meijs et al., 2018; Melloni et al., 2011; Pinto et al., 2015), and input that confirms current experience requires no new selection, consistent with repetition suppression as fulfilled expectation (Summerfield et al., 2008). Violations capture access in two forms: a familiar configuration in an unexpected context (strong r_pred, strong r_coh) and a novel configuration (strong r_pred, with r_coh high for the parts only; Section 4.1).

Thought is the goal-directed reconfiguration of mnemonic patterns, with selection dominated by r_val and r_coh. Its content comes from the cortical semantic repertoire and, for episodic and scene-based components, from the hippocampus, coordinated by RE; in both cases the MD sustains it in prefrontal cortex. The generator of its non-episodic content is not specified by the current architecture (Section 3.4). Its tendency to follow inferential rather than purely associative sequences reflects the bias of r_val toward content that serves current goals.

Imagination is the synthesis of novel configurations from the cortical repertoire, decoupled from immediate sensory input and from strict goal-directedness. It shares its generative routes with thought but operates under relaxed r_val, permitting combinations that serve no immediate goal. Following Hassabis and Maguire (2007), it uses the same computation as episodic retrieval and differs in the novelty of the configuration. An imagined chimera is no more coherent as a whole than a dreamed one, yet it is not taken for reality, so the distinction cannot rest on r_coh. Reality monitoring appears to weigh several cues, none decisive alone (Dijkstra et al., 2022), among them the strength of activity in sensory cortex (Dijkstra et al., 2025; Dijkstra & Fleming, 2023) and the control under which content is generated. In TMMC terms, imagined configurations lack the support of first-order sensory bids and are generated under voluntary prefrontal control; in dreaming, configurations are vivid while prefrontal control is reduced (Section 5.2.3), so both cues fail and the dream is experienced as real. This mapping of reality-monitoring cues onto TMMC components is a TMMC proposal.

Dreaming is the operation of the hippocampal simulation engine under strongly attenuated sensory correction (r_sal and r_pred suppressed) and without the anchoring of current goals (r_val driven mainly by the amygdala). It is as vivid as perception because it uses the same cortical patterns and thalamocortical re-entry. The mnemonic thesis predicts the form of dream bizarreness. Dream reports seldom replay whole episodes but often contain fragments of waking experience (Fosse et al., 2003), and their bizarre elements are mainly incongruous combinations, vague elements and discontinuities rather than elements without precedent (Revonsuo & Salmivalli, 1995). This is high coherence of the parts with low coherence of the whole, the signature of a novel stimulus without a sensory violation. In waking, such a configuration is checked against input through first-order r_pred; in the free mode that check is attenuated, so it can win the competition and, for the reasons given above, is experienced as real. This reading of dream bizarreness is a TMMC proposal.

These four content types are not discrete categories but regions of a continuous space defined by the relative dominance of the four selection pressures and the source of the content vectors. Their boundaries are not fixed: a perceptual episode can shade into imagination as sensory input becomes ambiguous; thought can become dream-like as motivational constraint relaxes. 4.4 Relation to adjacent positions

Three adjacent positions warrant comparison. The thesis is closest to predictive processing, in which perception is inference under a learned hierarchical generative model (Friston, 2010; Hohwy, 2013; Rao & Ballard, 1999). A learned generative model is a mnemonic repertoire in the sense used here. The thesis takes a position on a question PP leaves open, whether conscious content corresponds to confirmed predictions or to the errors that falsify them (Pinto et al., 2015): conscious content is always a learned configuration that has won the competition at the TRN, and prediction errors are not content but one of the pressures that bias selection.

Configurations that lose the competition are attenuated rather than silenced and can still guide well-practiced behavior without becoming conscious (Section 7.1). The thesis also extends the generative account to endogenous content, with a specified source, the hippocampus, whose arrival in prefrontal cortex RE coordinates, for content not elicited by sensory input. The anatomical separation of prediction error from repertoire fit, which distinguishes the TMMC from PP's single inference, is discussed in Section 7.3.

Some theorists have argued that consciousness requires the activation of memory representations (Lamme, 2003; Tulving, 1985). The mnemonic thesis is stronger: there is no conscious content that is not a configuration of learned patterns. It also specifies a mechanism, thalamocortical selection, which turns the claim into an empirical hypothesis.

Constructivist theories in the tradition of Helmholtz and Gregory hold that perception is hypothesis-driven: the brain constructs a representation of the world by testing hypotheses against sensory evidence (Gregory, 1980). The mnemonic thesis shares this view and specifies where the hypotheses come from, a learned repertoire, and how one is chosen, competition at the TRN.

4.5 One open problem: volition

The mnemonic thesis, as stated, leaves one problem unresolved: the operationalization of volition. The taxonomy presented above distinguishes thought from imagination on the basis of goal-directedness: thought is constrained by active goals (high r_val), imagination operates with a relaxed motivational constraint. This distinction requires that the concept of a goaland by extension, the concept of volition -be operationalized in a way that is independent of the phenomenological report of intending to think about something. This operationalization is currently underdeveloped in the TMMC framework. I can specify the neural substrate that mediates r_val (direct prefrontal projections to the TRN and the prefrontal-basal ganglia-TRN pathway) and describe its computational function (biasing selection toward content that serves active drives), but I cannot yet give a fully mechanistic account of where goals come from or how they are maintained independently of the conscious content they shape.

5. Two partially dissociable dimensions and three operating modes

>Two partially dissociable dimensions and three operating modes

5.1 L(t) and C(t)

The distinction between the level of consciousness and the content of consciousness is one of the oldest conceptual tools in consciousness research, predating modern neuroscience. Its appeal is phenomenological: a patient emerging from anesthesia is conscious at an increasing level long before specific contents become organized; conversely, detailed and vivid conscious contents occur during REM sleep, when the sleeper is behaviorally unresponsive to the environment. Whether the two depend on separable neural circuits, however, has remained an open question. The status of this distinction has been sharpened, but also complicated, by recent synthesis. Reviewing cellular, systems-level and computational evidence, Whyte et al. (2024) argue that matrix neurons, through their interaction with layer 5 pyramidal neurons, support wakefulness and set the threshold at which content enters consciousness, whereas core neurons help maintain specific contents. The same mechanism therefore sits on both sides of the level/content distinction: coupling of layer 5 pyramidal neurons depends on higher-order thalamic input and collapses under anesthesia (Suzuki & Larkum, 2020), and the circuit that supports the waking state also sets detection thresholds (Takahashi et al., 2020; Whyte et al., 2024). Dendritic Integration Theory builds on this convergence: coupling of the apical and somatic compartments of layer 5 pyramidal neurons, controlled by higher-order thalamus, sets the conscious state, and within that state, contents become conscious through the integration of feature-specific and contextual input (Aru et al., 2020). The TMMC therefore treats L(t) and C(t) as partially dissociable and distinguishes them by the range over which they act.

The TMMC formalizes the level/content distinction through two state variables. L(t) is the level of consciousness at time t: how readily thalamocortical loops can couple, regardless of which channel they carry (the range-of-action criterion is developed below). Global thalamocortical coherence is a candidate measure of L(t), not its definition. High L(t) corresponds to the waking state, characterized by tonic firing of thalamocortical neurons and low-amplitude, fast cortical activity. Low L(t) corresponds to deep NREM sleep and anesthesia, characterized by burst firing of thalamocortical neurons and large-amplitude slow oscillations (D. A. McCormick & Bal, 1997; Steriade et al., 1993) and, under anesthesia, by decoupling of layer 5 pyramidal neurons (Suzuki & Larkum, 2020). L(t) is not behavioral arousal. In REM sleep, responsiveness to the environment is low, but thalamocortical neurons fire in a wake-like tonic mode (Steriade et al., 1993) and cortical responses to transcranial magnetic stimulation regain a wake-like spread and differentiation (Massimini et al., 2010), so L(t) is high, although whether layer 5 neurons are coupled in REM sleep was raised as an open question by Aru et al. (2020); what distinguishes REM is the weighting of the selection pressures (Section 5.2.3). Nor need L(t) be uniform across cortex: in both NREM and REM sleep, dream reports follow a local reduction of low-frequency activity over posterior cortex (Siclari et al., 2017). The TMMC therefore treats L(t) as regionally variable; this is a TMMC hypothesis.

L(t) is a continuous variable, not a threshold: there is no single value below which consciousness abruptly disappears, but a gradient of states with different capacities for content selection. C(t) is the content of consciousness at time t, defined as the vector of active mnemonic states in the thalamocortical circuit at each moment -the specific configuration of the cortical pattern space that has survived TRN selection and achieved re-entrant stabilization. C(t) is a highdimensional vector in the sense that it encodes structured, multimodal representations; but its effective dimensionality at any given moment is constrained by the pinhole to a lowdimensional subspace -a per-cycle constraint that is compatible with the high dynamical complexity observed across extended time windows, as described in Section 3.7. C(t) is updated at the timescale of the TRN gating cycle (Section 3.8). Contents can nonetheless persist across many cycles. Content under continuous input is held in place partly by that input, whereas content that must outlast its drive depends on MD-mediated sustainment of prefrontal activity (Section 3.3; Prediction 2, Section 6.2).

Partial dissociation requires a criterion for telling the two variables apart. The TMMC distinguishes them by their range of action. L(t) corresponds to diffuse, largely channelnonspecific control of how readily any candidate loop can couple, the role proposed for the central lateral thalamus (Redinbaugh et al., 2020). C(t) is fixed by which candidates win the competition at the TRN, under the channel-specific coherence signal carried by higher-order nuclei (Section 3.2). That this difference in range separates level from content is a TMMC hypothesis. It predicts that a change in level should shift detection thresholds across channels together, whereas a change in selection should alter the relative access of competing channels. Clinical states illustrate partial dissociation rather than independence. Both the vegetative state/unresponsive wakefulness syndrome and the minimally conscious state (MCS) show substantially reduced L(t) relative to healthy consciousness, with a residual connectivity gradient distinguishing the two, but the MCS patient shows residual fluctuations in C(t) -the 'minimal, inconstant yet definite behavioural signs of awareness' that define the MCS diagnosis -essentially absent in the vegetative state (Di Perri et al., 2014).

As with the pinhole, the present paper treats L(t) and C(t) as conceptual state variables defined by their neural substrates and functional roles. A full mathematical treatment -defining L(t) precisely as a function of loop-coupling readiness, measurable for instance through thalamocortical coherence spectra and C(t) as a projection operator on the cortical state space -is part of the research program described in Section 7.5. The conceptual definitions are sufficient for the operating mode framework developed below.

5.2 Three operating modes

Within the space defined by L(t) and C(t), the TMMC identifies three operating modes that characterize the dominant source and constraint structure of conscious content. These modes are not discrete states but attractors in the state space -regions to which the system tends to return when perturbed -and transitions between them are continuous rather than abrupt. The modes are defined by the content source that drives C(t). Each has a typical weighting of the selection pressures and a typical level of sensory correction and motivational constraint (Table 4), but the weights can shift transiently within a mode, as when an emotionally relevant stimulus captures access (Fig. 2B).

5.2.1 Exogenous mode

In the exogenous mode, C(t) is dominated by ascending sensory input. Selection in the posterior, sensory sectors of the TRN is weighted most heavily toward r_sal and r_pred acting on first-order channels, and the content of experience closely tracks the structure of the external environment. r_pred is not exclusive to this mode: layer 6 feedback also reaches higher-order nuclei (Section 3.2), so expectations can bias endogenous content as well. Its first-order form, however, compares expectation with sensory input and therefore operates fully only when sensory input is present. This is the mode that the existing literature has studied most thoroughly -virtually all experimental paradigms in the neuroscience of consciousness use exogenous stimuli to induce or probe conscious experience. Even in the exogenous mode, however, the mnemonic thesis applies: what is consciously experienced is not the raw sensory signal but its interpretation through the lens of the cortical pattern repertoire. The thalamus in exogenous mode is not a passive relay: through r_pred, cortical expectations determine whether a given input is facilitated, attenuated as already known, or allowed to capture access as a violation. The exogenous mode is distinguished not by the absence of top-down influence but by the dominance of the sensory signal over the internally generated content vectors from the hippocampal simulation engine.

5.2.2 Endogenous mode

In the endogenous mode, C(t) is dominated by internally generated content: episodic and scene-based content from the hippocampus, coordinated with mPFC via RE, and semantic and verbal content whose source the current architecture does not specify (Section 3.4), both sustained in prefrontal cortex by the MD. The dominant selection pressures are r_coh and r_val: content is favored to the extent that it is mnemonically coherent (consistent with the learned cortical pattern space) and motivationally relevant (consistent with active goals and drives), while r_sal and r_pred carry less weight than in the exogenous mode. This mode corresponds to the phenomenology of spontaneous thought, deliberate imagination, voluntary memory retrieval, and prospective planning -the rich inner life that occupies a substantial portion of waking experience but has been systematically neglected by experimental paradigms that control external stimulation.

Aphantasia, the marked reduction or absence of voluntary imagery, affects about 1% of the population in its extreme form. Everyday functioning and performance on most perceptual and memory tasks are largely preserved, although subtle differences are reported, including slower visual processing and weaker face recognition, and the richness of autobiographical memory and of imagined scenes is reduced (Zeman, 2024). The converse dissociation also occurs: in a review of 55 cases, vivid imagery was repeatedly preserved after severe or complete bilateral destruction of primary visual cortex, whereas imagery loss in anatomically informative cases was never confined to V1 (Zago et al., 2026). Perceptual and voluntary access to the visual repertoire can therefore fail largely independently. A further dissociation bears directly on the operating modes. Most people with lifelong aphantasia report visual dreams, and many report hypnagogic imagery (Zeman, 2024), so the visual repertoire that cannot be accessed voluntarily in waking is accessed in sleep. Zeman attributes this to the distinct neurochemical and regional state of REM sleep. The current mechanistic proposal for congenital aphantasia locates the deficit in reduced coupling between a fusiform imagery node and frontal regions engaged by voluntary imagery (Liu & Bartolomeo, 2025; Takamura et al., 2026), a cortico-cortical account with no thalamic or hippocampal component. In TMMC terms, preserved dream imagery fits a free mode in which prefrontal control of content is reduced (Section 5.2.3), so dream content does not depend on the frontal route that fails in aphantasia. This reading is a TMMC proposal. Whether fusiform-frontal coupling itself depends on MDprefrontal loops is an open question.

The endogenous mode is also highly prevalent: spontaneous thought occurs in nearly half of experience samples taken during daily life (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010), and it becomes more frequent as external task demands decrease (Smallwood & Schooler, 2015). The TMMC therefore treats it as the mode toward which the system tends when external demands are low; whether it is a default in a stronger, architectural sense is not established. Primate anatomy suggests where in the TRN the two modes could be separated. Posterior and central TRN sectors receive sensory cortical and thalamic input together with widespread projections from the amygdala, posterior orbitofrontal cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, whereas the anterior sector receives projections exclusively from prefrontal and limbic cortices, including anterior cingulate cortex and frontal polar area 10, together with strong amygdalar and MD input. On this basis, Zikopoulos & Barbas (2012) propose that posterior TRN serves attention to external stimuli and anterior TRN attention to internal stimuli. In mice, TRN neurons projecting to sensory nuclei are more active during quiescence and those projecting to limbic nuclei during active exploration (Halassa et al., 2014). Segregation alone would not produce competition between the modes, because sectors that inhibit only their own targets do not interact. Inputs associated with internal attention do reach posterior sectors: amygdalar terminals extend into central and posterior sensory sectors, and posterior orbitofrontal and dorsolateral prefrontal projections reach them as well (Zikopoulos & Barbas, 2006 , 2012). Through these inputs, and through TRN neurons with more than one target (Section 3.7), internal-mode sources could suppress sensory channels (Fig. 2C). The reverse, exogenous drive suppressing endogenous channels through the TRN, has no comparable anatomical support. Three caveats apply: the primate proposal is inferred from tract tracing, not physiology; its "internal stimuli" are interoceptive signals and drives rather than spontaneous thought; and the rodent finding concerns arousal state, not the exogenous/endogenous distinction.

The endogenous mode is the one most distinctively addressed by the TMMC relative to existing frameworks. Mind-wandering episodes are associated with increased activity in the DMN and decreased activity in the dorsal attention network (DAN) (Fox et al., 2005; Smallwood & Schooler, 2015). The TMMC interprets the DMN-DAN competition as the mesoscale expression of the balance between endogenous and exogenous modes. First-order sensory channels supply the drive for the exogenous mode; RE coordinates and the MD sustains content for the endogenous mode; and TRN sectors could arbitrate between them, most clearly when internal-mode sources suppress sensory channels. The relative dominance of the two modes reflects the current balance of the selection pressures.

5.2.3 Free mode

The free mode corresponds to states in which the hippocampal simulation engine operates with sensory correction strongly attenuated (r_sal and r_pred weak and largely decoupled from content) and without stable motivational constraint from behavioral goals (r_val dominated by amygdalar priority signals rather than by prefrontal goal representations). REM sleep is the canonical instance. During REM, thalamocortical neurons return to a tonic, wakelike firing mode and cortical cholinergic tone is high (Steriade, 2004; Steriade et al., 1993), supporting the vivid cortical activity that characterizes dreaming, while prefrontal regulation of goal-directed content is reduced, consistent with the reduced dorsolateral prefrontal and inferior parietal activity reported during REM (Hobson et al., 2000; Tononi et al., 2024). Sensory input is not abolished but strongly attenuated and largely decoupled from conscious content: external stimuli are still processed and can be incorporated into dreams (Dement & Wolpert, 1958), responsiveness differs between phasic and tonic REM (Ermis et al., 2010), and lucid dreamers can perceive and respond to external cues in real time (Konkoly et al., 2021).

The free mode is theoretically important for several reasons. First, it demonstrates that the thalamocortical mechanism of consciousness does not require sensory input: the same architecture that generates conscious experience in the exogenous and endogenous modes generates the phenomenologically rich experience of dreaming with external stimulation strongly attenuated. This supports the TMMC's claim that the thalamus is not a sensory relay but a content-selection system that can operate on internally generated content. Second, the emotional intensity of dreams, disproportionate to their narrative coherence, is consistent with amygdalar priority signals operating without the moderating influence of first-order r_pred and prefrontal r_val. Bilateral basolateral amygdala lesions leave dream recall intact but produce shorter, simpler dreams with a more positive and less negative emotional tone (Blake et al., 2019), consistent with the amygdala shaping rather than generating dream content. In four patients with selective bilateral hippocampal damage, dreams were reported after fewer awakenings than in ten controls, and one patient reported none (Spanò et al., 2020). The dreams the other three reported contained fewer episodic details but no fewer semantic details, and did not differ significantly in rated vividness or complexity. Hippocampal damage thus degraded dreaming most clearly in its episodic, scene-based content, which fits the hippocampal contribution assumed in Section 3.4; with four patients, whether the hippocampus is required for dreaming as such remains open. Third, certain dissociative states and psychedelic experiences share characteristics with the free mode -unconstrained content generation with reduced sensory anchoring -and may represent partial free mode activations during waking (Carhart-Harris et al., 2016).

5.3 Multiple timescales of mode-relevant thalamocortical dynamics

Transitions between operating modes are continuous. The TMMC proposes that they reflect the joint state of several thalamocortical, hippocampal and neuromodulatory processes, with characteristic timescales from tens of milliseconds to hours. These processes are coupled but only partially ordered: some are cleanly scale-separated, some overlap, and one has no established timescale. I indicate below where scale separation holds and where it does not.

TRN gating cycle (~125-250 ms; Section 3.8).

This is the timescale at which the selection pressures are integrated and C(t) is updated. Transitions at this scale are changes of content within a mode, not changes of mode. The TMMC does not specify how TRN-admitted content is expressed in cortex. A plausible route is the established dissociation between gamma-band feedforward and alpha/beta-band feedback signaling across visual areas (Bastos et al., 2015; Jensen et al., 2015; Van Kerkoerle et al., 2014). That this dissociation is how TRN-admitted content is expressed cortically in all three modes is a TMMC extension that the cited studies do not test.

Hippocampal sequencing: two state-dependent channels. Theta-coupled sequences (~100-300 ms cycle) order hippocampal content during active exploration, attention and REM sleep (Buzsáki & Moser, 2013; Hasselmo & Stern, 2014). SWR-paced injection (events of ~50-150 ms, occurring in bouts) operates during immobility, quiet wakefulness and slow-wave sleep, when theta is absent (Buzsáki, 2015; Carr et al., 2011; Section 3.4). Because theta and SWRs occur in largely non-overlapping behavioral states, they are alternative channels gated by state, not successive stages of one process.

DMN/DAN competition: two coupled timescales.

The anticorrelation between default-mode and dorsal-attention networks is infraslow in BOLD, below 0.1 Hz (Fox et al., 2005). Most of its energy lies at periods of tens of seconds, with an EEG alpha-power correlate over the same timescale (Chang et al., 2013). Intracranial recordings show a faster component: task-evoked activation of attention and salience network nodes precedes DMN deactivation by hundreds of milliseconds, a lag fMRI cannot resolve (Kucyi et al., 2020). This fast component was observed during externally oriented attention and was attenuated at rest. It is the more plausible substrate for rapid exogenous engagement by a salient event, and the infraslow anticorrelation is its aggregate signature in BOLD. The fast component overlaps the TRN gating cycle and theta, so no scale separation is claimed. Whether it also drives self-initiated switches to the endogenous mode is not established.

MD-mediated set updating (timescale unresolved).

The MD is necessary for shifting to a previously irrelevant stimulus dimension, but not for reversals within a dimension (Ouhaz et al., 2022). In primates, prospective, response-related coding during working-memory maintenance emerges in the MD before prefrontal cortex (Watanabe & Funahashi, 2012). No evidence fixes the duration of MD-mediated switching in spontaneous cognition, so this process is not placed on the timescale axis.

Cholinergic tone (minutes).

High cholinergic tone, characteristic of attentive waking and REM sleep, supports tonic firing of thalamocortical neurons. It does so by depolarizing them directly and by hyperpolarizing TRN neurons, which disinhibits them (Steriade, 2004). In waking, high acetylcholine biases cortical circuits toward afferent input and suppresses intrinsic and feedback-driven activity (Hasselmo & McGaughy, 2004). This is difficult to reconcile with REM, where high tone accompanies paradigmatically endogenous content. One resolution is that cholinergic tone amplifies thalamocortical transfer in whichever mode is otherwise dominant: exogenous in attentive waking, endogenous in REM, when sensory drive is attenuated. Basal forebrain influence is also not only cholinergic. GABAergic basal forebrain projections target the TRN and the MD (R. E. Brown & McKenna, 2015), and the basal forebrain inhibited the MD during self-focused cognition (Harrison et al., 2022; Section 3.3). The net effect of the basal forebrain on endogenous content therefore cannot be inferred from cholinergic tone alone. Both points are unresolved. Whether cholinergic tone is elevated in the MD and RE during endogenous engagement has not been tested.

Arousal and circadian cycle (hours).

The slowest timescale sets the envelope of L(t) and the availability of the free mode. Sleep architecture alternates REM with NREM slow oscillations that support hippocampal-cortical memory consolidation (Diekelmann & Born, 2010). Cholinergic tone is nested within, and constrained by, this cycle; this is the one clean scale separation in the set.

Taken together, these processes do not form a nested hierarchy. A person performing a perceptual task can experience intrusions of spontaneous thought within a high-arousal waking state. This reflects several coupled processes operating together, not a cascade in which each level frames the one below.

5.4 Clinical and individual difference implications

The L(t)/C(t) framework describes disorders of consciousness by the thalamic circuits on which each variable depends. Beyond the partial dissociation between the vegetative and minimally conscious states (Section 5.1), it attributes the intermittency of content in the minimally conscious state to partial function of TRN selection and MD sustainment. This circuit-level attribution is a TMMC hypothesis; current clinical imaging does not test it. Lockedin syndrome, with preserved L(t) and C(t) and disrupted motor output, is consistent with the preserved default-mode network integrity (Roquet et al., 2016) and the near-normal perturbational complexity and white-matter integrity (Bodart et al., 2018) reported in these patients. Because all current theories expect consciousness to be preserved in locked-in syndrome, this case does not discriminate the TMMC from its alternatives.

In healthy individuals, the habitual balance between exogenous and endogenous modes should track resting functional connectivity between the MD and core DMN nodes. Because the MD sustains content that lacks external drive, stronger coupling should lengthen endogenous episodes and so increase the share of experience spent in spontaneous thought and imagery. This expectation is not developed as a formal prediction, because the duration of spontaneous episodes cannot yet be measured without the measurement interfering with them.

6. Falsifiable predictions

Each prediction below is meant to be specific enough to test, to discriminate the TMMC from existing frameworks, and to fail under a stated outcome. For each, I give its derivation from the architecture, a protocol feasible with current methods, and the discriminating contrast: what a corticocentric account would predict instead. A prediction that existing models also make gives no support to the TMMC, so the contrast is the main criterion.

The three predictions span temporal, lesion and oscillatory levels of analysis and different experimental modalities, reducing the risk that confirming evidence reflects a single methodological artifact. A further test addresses the selection pressures themselves (Section 6.4). The hypothesis that winning the TRN competition under adequate L(t) is sufficient for access (Section 7.1) is not developed as a separate prediction. Testing it requires channelresolved measures of higher-order collateral drive to the shell TRN, which current methods do not provide, and in animals, report can only be approximated by detection (Section 6.4).

6.1 Prediction 1: Thalamic temporal precedence extends to endogenous content

Prediction: During spontaneous thought, consciousness-related activity in medial and intralaminar thalamic nuclei will emerge before that of prefrontal cortex, with theta-band coupling directed from thalamus to prefrontal cortex, extending to endogenous content the precedence shown for conscious perception (Fang et al., 2025).

Derivation. In perception, consciousness-related activity emerges earlier in intralaminar and medial thalamus than in lateral PFC, with theta-band coupling directed from thalamus to PFC (Fang et al., 2025; Section 2.5). In the TMMC, this precedence reflects thalamic control over which content is stabilized in frontal cortex, and it should not depend on where the content comes from. For the MD, this implies an ordering within its sustaining role (Section 3.3). Content can reach prefrontal cortex without the MD, but the MD amplifies prefrontal connectivity without driving it (Schmitt et al., 2017), so its engagement should precede, not follow, the prefrontal divergence that marks content becoming conscious. That MD amplification precedes and contributes to this divergence is a TMMC interpretation of the result of Fang et al. (2025). If thalamic precedence is a property of the selection architecture, and not a consequence of the thalamus lying on the ascending sensory pathway, it should also hold for internally generated content.

Protocol. Neurosurgical patients with simultaneous sEEG contacts in the hippocampus, the medial and intralaminar thalamus, and lateral and medial PFC are recorded during extended rest with periodic thought probes (Christoff et al., 2009). Event onset is anchored to hippocampal sharp-wave ripples rather than to a report. Ripple rates rise during vivid, visually imaginable and task-unrelated thought (Iwata et al., 2024), and ripples are coordinated with default-mode activity during recollection (Norman et al., 2021), which makes them report-free markers of candidate endogenous events. Probes serve only to classify the preceding interval as containing spontaneous thought or not, so no report interrupts the events being timed. The design mirrors that of Fang et al. (2025): ripples take the place of stimuli, and intervals with and without reported thought take the place of seen and unseen trials. Consciousness-related activity is the difference between ripple-locked responses in the two classes of interval. Directional coupling between thalamic and prefrontal contacts is assessed in the theta band with phase-based directed measures and transfer entropy. The prediction concerns direction only: thalamus-to-PFC coupling should exceed the reverse. No lag is predicted: the lead of about 28 ms observed for perceptual content (Section 2.5) gives no basis for an estimate for endogenous content. Pupil diameter or an EEG vigilance index is recorded throughout and entered as a covariate, because the intralaminar nuclei are also arousal structures.

Four limits apply. First, a ripple marks a candidate event, not a conscious one. Because each probe labels a whole interval, ripple-locked responses in thought-present intervals mix conscious and unconscious events, which reduces the sensitivity of the contrast; shorter intervals between probes reduce this dilution at the cost of more frequent interruption. Second, ripples index the episodic, scene-based content that the TMMC assigns to the hippocampus (Section 3.4). Semantic and verbal thought, which persists after hippocampal damage (C. McCormick et al., 2018), is not covered. Third, in the epileptic hippocampus, physiological ripples coexist with pathological high-frequency oscillations that cannot be reliably distinguished by waveform; in the only study linking ripples to spontaneous thought, the relationship did not appear to arise from epileptic activity (Iwata et al., 2024). Interictal event rates are therefore modeled alongside ripple rates for each interval, so that a difference between thought-present and thought-absent intervals can be attributed to ripples rather than to epileptic activity. Fourth, RE coordination of episodic content (Section 3.5) is not tested by this design, because RE is not a standard target of clinical depth electrodes; the RE-specific version of this test requires recordings in animals.

Discriminating contrast.

Neither GWT nor HOT has committed to a temporal order of thalamic and prefrontal activity for spontaneous thought. Both, however, locate the event that makes content conscious in prefrontal cortex: ignition of the global workspace (Mashour et al., 2020) or the formation of a higher-order representation (R. Brown et al., 2019). Extended to endogenous content, both lead one to expect consciousness-related prefrontal activity to precede or coincide with thalamic engagement. A thalamus-to-PFC lead after ripples in thought-present intervals is predicted by the TMMC and not by these accounts. A PFC-tothalamus lead would count against the TMMC's claim that thalamic gating precedes access for endogenous content. Anchoring on ripples puts the hippocampus first by design, as the TMMC expects for a content generator upstream of selection (Section 3.4), so the test concerns the order of thalamus and PFC after the ripple. Because no report is timed, the report-preparation confound that affects frontal activity in report-based paradigms (Frässle et al., 2014), including that of Fang et al. (2025), is reduced, although the end-of-interval probe still requires a report.

6.2 Prediction 2: MD disruption accelerates the loss of maintained content without delaying its generation

Prediction: Disrupting the mediodorsal nucleus will accelerate the loss of internally maintained content without delaying its generation, and without impairing access to continuously driven perceptual content.

Derivation. In rodents, the MD sustains prefrontal activity without carrying its content and becomes necessary as delays lengthen (Section 3.3; Bolkan et al., 2017; Schmitt et al., 2017).

In the TMMC, its importance is set by how much external drive the content has. Content held after its cue is gone has none, so MD disruption should accelerate its loss while leaving its generation intact. Perceptual content under continuous input is held in place by that input and should be spared. The TMMC extends the same role to spontaneous endogenous content, but that extension cannot yet be tested in humans, because the duration of a spontaneous episode cannot be measured without the measurement interfering with it.

Existing evidence.

In humans, the delay dependence of MD involvement is supported by stimulation (Peräkylä et al., 2017) and by deficits after MD infarcts that appear with long delays and interference, including accelerated forgetting (Newsome et al., 2018; Pergola et al., 2018; Tu et al., 2014). What the TMMC adds is a combined pattern in the same patients: a steeper loss with delay, with generation and reproduction under continuous drive both spared. Two limits apply. Human MD lesions are rarely selective (Pergola et al., 2018), and rodent findings are not uniform: some lesion studies report delay-dependent deficits, others none, or deficits in acquisition rather than performance (reviewed in Parnaudeau et al., 2018).

Protocol. Patients with chronic paramedian thalamic infarcts involving the MD, tested more than three months after the infarct (Carrera & Bogousslavsky, 2006; Pergola et al., 2018), are compared with matched controls on three measures, each probed once at the end of a trial so that no report interrupts maintenance: the precision with which a cued visual content is reproduced after a delay; the latency to generate a cued image or memory; and the precision of reproduction while a single, unambiguous stimulus remains present. Delays are sampled from a few seconds to about a minute, and the primary outcome is the slope of precision against delay. A contribution of long-term memory at the longest delays would then appear as a change in slope rather than contaminating the whole measure. Vigilance is measured and entered as a covariate, so that residual reductions in L(t) are not misread as content deficits. Lesion-symptom mapping separates MD involvement from damage to the mammillothalamic tract, which independently produces thalamic amnesia (Danet et al., 2015). Where deep brain stimulation contacts reach the MD, as in Peräkylä et al. (2017), stimulation on versus off provides a reversible within-subject version of the same test. In animal models, temporally restricted MD inactivation during the delay provides the causal version, as Bolkan et al. (2017) did for spatial working memory; the further test is whether inactivation timed to generation leaves generation intact.

Discriminating contrast.

Accounts in which the thalamus enables cortical processing without contributing to content predict that MD damage, once its effect on arousal is controlled, does not selectively steepen the loss of maintained content. The TMMC prediction fails under three outcomes: 1) a slope of precision against delay unaffected by MD damage; 2) delayed generation, which would suggest that the MD generates or routes content rather than sustaining it; 3) impaired reproduction under continuous drive. Prefrontal damage is also expected to impair maintained content, because the content is held in prefrontal cortex, but by removing the content rather than its sustainment. Separating these two effects requires the temporally restricted design, not a comparison of lesion groups.

6.3 Prediction 3: Rivalry switches test the frequency and directionality of thalamic gating

Prediction: Perceptual switches during binocular rivalry will be preceded by thalamusto-cortex directed connectivity in a thalamically originating frequency band. Theta (4-8 Hz) and alpha (8-12 Hz) are tested separately; current directional evidence favors theta.

Derivation. Directional evidence favors theta over alpha as a thalamically originating rhythm (Section 3.8), but none of it comes from a content-selection paradigm or from TRN recordings. Rivalry requires arbitration between two competing contents under constant input. It therefore tests whether the thalamus-to-cortex theta signature extends from attention to content selection, and the signature should be strongest in the window before a switch, when the competition is being resolved. The TRN gating cycle is provisionally placed at 125-250 ms, the theta range (Section 3.8); an alpha-band outcome would require that range to be revised.

Protocol. Simultaneous sEEG recording in the pulvinar and in occipital and parietal cortices, in neurosurgical patients viewing binocular rivalry. Switches are identified without report, from the optokinetic nystagmus elicited by gratings drifting in opposite directions in the two eyes. Without report, switch-related activity persists in occipital and parietal cortex but not in frontal cortex, where activity associated with reported switches reflects introspection and report (Frässle et al., 2014). For each switch, directional connectivity between thalamic and cortical sources is assessed in the 500 ms window preceding it, with transfer entropy and Granger causality computed separately in the theta and alpha bands. Because the TRN cannot be resolved with human depth electrodes, this protocol tests thalamic rather than TRN-specific origin. A TRN-specific test requires rivalry-like competition paradigms in animals combined with TRN subpopulation recordings, of the kind Wimmer et al. (2015) applied to cross-modal attention.

Discriminating contrast.

Three outcomes are distinguishable. (1) Thalamus-to-cortex theta flow before switches extends the attentional finding of Huang et al. (2024) to content selection and supports the TMMC, with theta as the carrier. (2) Cortex-to-thalamus dominance in both bands extends the resting finding of Halgren et al. (2019) to active selection and counts against a thalamic origin of selection timing, favoring an account in which the TRN gates amplitude rather than pacing phase. (3) If flow is corticothalamic at rest but thalamocortical during rivalry, in either band, thalamic pacing is conditional on selection demand, and the TMMC's directionality claim must be qualified by state.

6.4 A test of the selection pressures

The three predictions above test the TMMC's architecture and timing, not the selection pressures themselves. The separation of r_pred from r_coh (Section 3.2) yields one direct test. Mice perform a detection task with cued expectations and with familiar and novel stimulus configurations. Two manipulations are compared: optogenetic silencing of layer 6 corticothalamic neurons, as used to probe their role in processing sensory deviants (Voigts et al., 2020), and layer 5 decoupling by local blockade of the metabotropic glutamate and cholinergic receptors that couple apical and somatic compartments (Suzuki & Larkum, 2020).

The TMMC predicts a double dissociation. Layer 6 silencing should abolish the effect of a valid expectation on detection, and the expectation-dependence of core TRN responses, while sparing the advantage of familiar over novel configurations. Layer 5 decoupling should do the reverse: it should reduce the familiarity advantage and shell TRN drive while sparing the expectation effect. Neither manipulation should abolish responses to intensity contrast, which the TMMC assigns to r_sal. The measure in each case is a difference between conditions, so global gain changes produced by either manipulation do not by themselves count for or against the prediction.

If both manipulations degrade a single common quantity, the separation of r_pred from r_coh is unnecessary, and a single-inference account is preferable (Section 7.3). If the dissociation appears in behavior but not in TRN responses, the pressures are separable, but the TRN is not shown to be where they are integrated. Detection in mice is a proxy for conscious access, not a measure of it. 7. Discussion

7.1 What the TMMC contributes and what it does not claim

The TMMC makes three specific contributions to the neuroscience of consciousness. First, it provides a mechanistic account of how thalamic architecture determines the content -not merely the level -of conscious experience. This is a stronger claim than the established necessity of thalamic arousal systems and a different claim from the established role of thalamic relay nuclei in sensory transmission: it concerns the selection of which content among many competing candidates achieves stable thalamocortical re-entry. Second, it specifies mechanistically the mnemonic thesis, a claim with precedent (Section 4): all conscious content has mnemonic structure, and perception, thought, imagination and dreaming differ in the source, pathway and constraint conditions of mnemonic pattern activation rather than in kind. Third, it derives three falsifiable predictions and a direct test of the selection pressures that span levels of analysis and experimental modalities, providing a concrete research program.

The TMMC does not claim cortical irrelevance. The cortex is the substrate of the mnemonic pattern repertoire that constitutes conscious content; without it, there is nothing for the thalamocortical selection system to select. The model's departure from corticocentrism is not anatomical -it does not replace cortex with thalamus -but mechanistic: it locates the selection operation that determines which cortical states become conscious in the thalamic system rather than in intra-cortical dynamics. On this account, a cortical state becomes conscious when its channel wins the competition at the TRN while L(t), in the cortical territories its loops engage, permits them to couple. The coupling meant here is the layer 5 dendritic coupling that Dendritic Integration Theory places at the center of conscious processing (Aru et al., 2020; Section 3.2); the TMMC addresses which loop achieves it. Winning is defined independently of report, as a rise in higher-order collateral drive to the shell TRN in the winning channel relative to the losing channels, in which it falls (Section 3.2). For the winning channel to suppress its competitors, TRN neurons it drives must inhibit relay neurons of other channels, through the open-loop arrangement described in Section 3.7. Behavior can be guided without consciousness by channels that lose the competition, whose transmission is attenuated rather than abolished, and by routes whose influence does not depend on its outcome. This distinguishes selection from attention as usually measured: attention can enhance the processing of stimuli that remain unseen (Kentridge et al., 1999 (Kentridge et al., , 2008)), which on this account reflects gain changes within channels that do not win. That winning the TRN competition under adequate L(t) is sufficient for access is a TMMC hypothesis. It fails if a channel that wins by this criterion, in the waking state, is not available for report.

The TMMC also does not address the hard problem. It accounts for which content becomes conscious and through which circuits, not for why this is accompanied by experience (Sections 1 and 7.4). I hold that identifying the mechanism of access is a precondition for productive work on that question, not a solution to it.

7.2 Relationship to prior thalamocentric accounts

The TMMC's most direct ancestor is the thalamocentric framework of Llinás and colleagues (Llinas et al., 1998; Llinás, 2002; Llinás & Paré, 1991). It inherits three of its elements: the thalamocentric framing; the principle that conscious content is generated intrinsically by thalamocortical activity and constrained, not created, by sensory input, which becomes the mnemonic thesis when specified mechanistically (Section 4); and the principle that waking and dreaming share one architecture under different sensory constraint, which becomes the three operating modes (Section 5). Llinás did not specify what determines which content occupies the thalamocortical loop at a given moment. Competition at the TRN under four selection pressures, the MD's sustaining role for content without external drive, and the hippocampal source of episodic endogenous content and its coordination by RE extend the framework into that territory.

One difference concerns frequency. Llinás placed binding at 40 Hz thalamocortical resonance; the TMMC provisionally associates its gating rhythm with theta, for the reasons given in Section 3.8 (Fiebelkorn et al., 2019; Halgren et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2024). The two are not exclusive: gamma may carry local content coding within each theta-paced selection cycle, a cross-frequency relationship that remains to be tested.

7.3 Relationship to predictive processing

The TMMC is closer to predictive processing than to HOT, GWT or IIT. PP describes the cortex as a learned hierarchical generative model that predicts its inputs and updates its representations in proportion to precision-weighted prediction errors (Friston, 2010; Rao & Ballard, 1999). The TMMC does not dispute this account; the mnemonic thesis is a version of the same claim about learned top-down structure (Section 4). The r_pred pressure is the thalamic point of contact with this computation: layer 6 feedback imposes a channel-specific expectation on thalamic relay neurons, and violations of that expectation reach the TRN as burst-amplified bids (Varela et al., 2024; Section 3.2).

PP's answer to which predicted states reach consciousness is precision: hypotheses whose prediction errors are weighted most strongly dominate inference, and attention itself is modeled as the optimization of precision (Feldman & Friston, 2010). The TMMC gives a different, anatomically specified answer: content is selected by competition at the TRN under four pressures, of which prediction is one. The two answers are not incompatible. TRN inhibition is gain control over thalamic channels, and gain control is the physiological form precision would take; the thalamus, specifically the pulvinar, has already been proposed as a site of precision control (Kanai et al., 2015). Higher-order thalamus has also been proposed to update cortical beliefs from contextual signals (Rikhye, Wimmer, et al., 2018). The TMMC addresses a different step: not how beliefs are updated, but which of the competing configurations becomes conscious. The TMMC can therefore be read partly as a proposal about where and how precision is set for thalamic channels.

Two substantive differences remain. First, in PP precision reflects the expected reliability of signals and is set within the cortical hierarchy. In the TMMC, factors other than reliability, namely affective and goal relevance (r_val), enter the arbitration directly through routes outside the cortical prediction hierarchy: the amygdala, and prefrontal projections that reach the TRN directly or through the basal ganglia. Second, the TMMC treats prediction error and repertoire fit as separable signals carried by different corticothalamic routes, whereas PP folds them into one inference. In one thalamic extension of PP, layer 5 input to higher-order thalamus is itself treated as a prediction signal (Rikhye, Wimmer, et al., 2018); the TMMC assigns prediction to layer 6 and reads layer 5 drive as repertoire fit. The second difference is directly testable (Section 6.4): if layer 6 silencing and layer 5 decoupling degrade a single common quantity rather than dissociating, PP's single-inference account is preferable.

7.4 Open problems

Five open problems are identified as priorities for the TMMC's development, beyond the volition operationalization and pinhole formalization already discussed in Sections 4.5 and 3.7.

The binding problem. The TMMC proposes that conscious content is a low-dimensional configuration of cortically distributed mnemonic patterns, selected by the TRN and sustained by the MD. It does not yet provide a full account of how these distributed patterns are bound into the unified, spatially and temporally coherent experience that characterizes normal waking consciousness. Llinás's 40 Hz binding proposal remains the most developed answer to this question within the thalamocentric tradition (Llinas et al., 1998), and its integration with the TMMC's selection mechanism is a theoretical priority. One possible account: selection at the TRN gating cycle defines what enters the conscious episode; gamma-band thalamocortical resonance within the selected content provides the binding that produces unity within that episode. This two-timescale account is consistent with both frameworks but requires explicit formalization.

Ontogenetic development.

The TMMC describes a mature thalamocortical system operating on a rich cortical mnemonic repertoire. It does not yet account for the development of that system -the process by which thalamocortical connectivity matures, the cortical pattern repertoire is built through experience, and the selection pressures achieve their adult operating characteristics. This is not merely a developmental question: it has implications for what kinds of conscious experience are possible at different stages of development, and for what the TMMC predicts about infant and early childhood consciousness, which existing theories treat very differently (Passos-Ferreira, 2024).

Phylogenetic scope.

The TMMC is grounded in mammalian thalamocortical anatomy. The question of whether the model's selection mechanism has a phylogenetic boundarywhether it applies only to mammals with a well-differentiated thalamic reticular nucleus, or whether functionally equivalent architectures in other taxa could implement the same computation -is both scientifically important and theoretically productive. Fish, birds, and cephalopods are candidates for non-mammalian consciousness that present anatomical architectures without a clear TRN homologue (D. B. Edelman & Seth, 2009). The TMMC's computational claims could in principle be instantiated by different anatomical substrates, but the model as currently specified does not address this possibility.

Consciousness without content.

Advanced meditation has been proposed as a way to study consciousness in its most minimal forms (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026). Two kinds of state bear on the TMMC. In minimal states such as the late formless absorptions, practitioners report awareness whose only content is the quality that defines each stage, and whether any fully contentless state exists remains uncertain (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026). These states test the mnemonic thesis at its limit: if experience persists while C(t) is reduced to a single minimal configuration, the thesis must say whether that configuration is itself learned. Cessation events are suspensions of all experience, brief or sustained, that advanced practitioners can induce volitionally, with no memory of the interval (Chowdhury et al., 2023; Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026). Minimal states and cessation can be distinguished by range of action (Section 5.1). That minimal states preserve the channel-nonspecific coupling that supports L(t) while channel-specific selection at the TRN is reduced, whereas cessation involves a collapse of that coupling, is a TMMC prediction. It would separate level from content within a single subject, without the pharmacological confounds of anesthesia. The evidence so far comes mostly from intensively sampled single practitioners, and to my knowledge thalamic activity has not been examined in these states.

The access/phenomenal distinction. As defined in Section 1, the TMMC is a theory of access consciousness -of what determines which content enters the thalamocortical re-entry state that makes it reportable, integrable, and available for voluntary control (Block, 1995). Whether the model has implications for phenomenal consciousness -the "what it is like" character of experience that constitutes the hard problem -depends on commitments that go beyond what the current framework can establish. Some theorists argue access and phenomenal consciousness are not distinct (Frankish, 2016), in which case a complete theory of access is also a complete theory of phenomenal consciousness. Others argue they are fundamentally different problems (Block, 1995). The TMMC is neutral on this dispute: its claims about the thalamocortical mechanism hold regardless of how the access/phenomenal distinction is ultimately resolved.

7.5 The research program the TMMC generates

Beyond the three specific predictions of Section 6, the TMMC generates a broader research program organized around three lines of work.

Computational implementation. The current paper presents the TMMC's conceptual architecture. A work in progress will implement the five-component system as a computational model in which the four selection pressures are formally defined as functions of neural activity variables, the pinhole is characterized as a constrained manifold in cortical state space, and the dynamics of L(t) and C(t) are derived from simulated thalamocortical networks. The goal of the computational implementation is twofold: to test whether the proposed architecture actually generates the low-dimensionality constraint it is claimed to produce, and to derive quantitative predictions from the model that can be compared directly with neural data.

Disorders of consciousness.

If the circuit that supports level also sets the threshold for content (Takahashi et al., 2020; Whyte et al., 2024), content cannot be selected below some level of L(t), and recovery of C(t) should be bounded by recovery of L(t). The TMMC adds an ordering among thalamic functions: externally driven content can be selected before MD function recovers, whereas content that must persist without external drive requires the MD, and RE coordination is required only for its episodic component. This ordering is a TMMC prediction.

It bears on thalamic deep brain stimulation, which has preliminary clinical support when targeted to the central thalamus (Arnts et al., 2022; Schiff et al., 2007). On this account, central thalamic stimulation acts on L(t) and should first restore responsiveness to external stimuli, whereas recovery of sustained endogenous content would additionally depend on the MDprefrontal loop.

The endogenous mode as a scientific object. The field has been organized mainly around the exogenous mode, the conscious response to controlled stimuli, largely because it is the most experimentally tractable. Spontaneous thought occurs in nearly half of experience samples taken during daily life (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010). The TMMC treats it, together with voluntary recall, imagination and dreaming, as a primary expression of the same selection system rather than as noise to be controlled away. The framework supplies specific targets for studying it: ripple-locked thalamic and prefrontal dynamics (Section 6.1), MD-dependent persistence (Section 6.2) and the coupling of the MD with default-mode cortex (Section 5.4).

It also gives a circuit-level reading of dissociations within the endogenous domain, such as preserved visual dreaming in people who cannot generate voluntary imagery (Zeman, 2024), which the TMMC relates to the reduced prefrontal control of content in the free mode (Section 5.2.2).

8. Conclusion

Research on the neural basis of consciousness has concentrated on conscious perception, the moment at which a controlled stimulus crosses a threshold into awareness. This focus has been productive, but it covers only part of conscious life, which also includes spontaneous thought, recall, imagination and dreaming. I have argued that the corticocentric consensus that grew out of this program reflects the technical accessibility of cortical tissue more than a demonstrated sufficiency of cortical circuits, and that the thalamus has been assigned an enabling role by default rather than by evidence.

The TMMC states a positive alternative. Conscious content is selected by competition at the TRN among thalamic channels, under four selection pressures with identifiable anatomical sources: salience, prediction, value and mnemonic coherence. The MD sustains prefrontal activity once content has arrived, the hippocampus generates episodic and scene-based endogenous content, RE coordinates its arrival in prefrontal cortex, and the amygdala biases the competition directly. What is selected is always a configuration of learned cortical patterns, a claim with precedent in the work of Llinás, Damasio and Edelman. The TMMC's contribution is the mechanism that selects among those configurations. The same mechanism, under different conditions of sensory constraint and motivational weighting, is proposed to underlie perception, thought, imagination and dreaming.

Three falsifiable predictions and a test of the selection pressures translate the architecture into experiments. They span temporal, lesion and oscillatory levels of analysis, and each specifies an outcome that would count against the model. The test of the selection pressures has the broadest consequence. If layer 6 silencing and layer 5 decoupling do not dissociate, the separation of prediction from mnemonic coherence should be abandoned, and with it one of the TMMC's two departures from predictive processing (Section 7.3).

Several problems remain open: the formal specification of the pinhole, the relation between theta-paced selection and gamma-band binding, the operationalization of volition, the development and phylogenetic scope of the selection system, and minimal states in which conscious content is reduced to its limit (Sections 4.5 and 7.4).

The central claim of the TMMC is that the thalamus determines not only the level of consciousness but also which content becomes conscious. If the predictions hold, the thalamus should be treated as part of the mechanism that selects what is conscious, not as a relay on the way to it.
