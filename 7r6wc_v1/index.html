Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Affective word meanings are often treated as stable lexical properties, although their organization may change across development. Using representational content analysis, we examined how semantic and form-based lexical information predicts valence and arousal judgments from childhood to adulthood. Across two experiments, Spanish affective ratings from children aged 7, 9, 11, and 13 years were modeled within and across age groups and compared with adult judgments. Semantic representations were the strongest predictors of both dimensions, but valence and arousal showed distinct developmental patterns. Valence remained comparatively stable across childhood and showed strong correspondence with adult judgments already at age 7.

Arousal, despite being systematically predictable within each child group, showed weaker crossage generalization, with semantic transfer declining as developmental distance increased.

Moreover, semantic information predicted word-specific age differences more accurately for arousal than for valence. Child-adult correspondence in arousal shifted from negative at age 7 to positive at ages 11 and 13, suggesting increasing similarity in lexical-affective mappings across development. These findings indicate that developmental variation in arousal does not reflect an absence of systematic organization in younger children but rather age-related differences in how lexical meaning relates to activation. Overall, affective lexical development combines a relatively stable evaluative structure with a more flexible organization of arousal.

[div-01]

Affective meaning is a fundamental component of language, allowing individuals to evaluate and communicate the emotional significance of their experiences. As children develop, changes in linguistic knowledge and emotional understanding may shape not only how they evaluate individual words but also how affective meaning is organized across the lexicon. Yet, little is known about whether this organization remains consistent across development or whether different dimensions of affective meaning follow distinct developmental patterns. The present study addresses these questions by examining how lexical information relates to valence and arousal judgments from childhood to adulthood.

Words convey not only conceptual information but also whether their referents are desirable or threatening, comforting or disturbing, and calming or activating. Such affective information influences word recognition, attention, and memory (Citron, 2012; Ferré et al., 2025; Hinojosa et al., 2020). Dimensional accounts characterize affective meaning primarily through valence, ranging from unpleasant to pleasant, and arousal, reflecting activation or intensity (Russell, 1980).

Although distinct, these dimensions are systematically related: both positive and negative stimuli can be highly arousing, whereas neutral stimuli tend to elicit lower activation, producing the nonlinear relationship commonly observed in affective norms (e.g., Ćoso et al., 2019; Moors et al., 2013; Stadthagen-González et al., 2017; Warriner et al., 2013).

Affective norms have traditionally treated valence and arousal as relatively stable properties of words, providing ratings used to select stimuli, match experimental conditions, and predict behavioral and neural responses. This research has established that affective content interacts with lexical, semantic, and contextual information during language processing (Citron, 2012; Hinojosa et al., 2020). Positive valence generally facilitates visual word recognition, whereas effects of negative content depend more strongly on emotional intensity and other lexical characteristics (Ferré et al., 2025; Haro et al., 2024; Kuperman et al., 2014; Rodríguez-Ferreiro & Davies, 2019).

Normative studies also document relationships among valence, arousal, discrete emotions, concreteness, and other psycholinguistic variables (e.g., Ćoso et al., 2023; Hinojosa et al., 2016; Montefinese et al., 2014; Speed & Brysbaert, 2024). However, affective ratings reflect judgments made at particular developmental stages, and treating them as age-invariant properties may obscure changes in how emotional meaning is organized.

Language and emotional knowledge develop together. Children learn to associate words with bodily states, actions, situations, goals, and social experiences, drawing on linguistic and paralinguistic cues, including intonation, acoustic properties, and facial expressions during interactions with caregivers (e.g., Dave et al., 2018; Golinkoff et al., 2015; Pons et al., 2004; Tamis-LeMonda et al., 2014). Because emotional experiences vary considerably in their perceptual and bodily features, linguistic labels help organize them into conceptual categories that support communication, memory, and prediction (Hoemann et al., 2019; Lindquist et al., 2015).

As linguistic knowledge expands, children may therefore change both the emotion concepts they represent and the dimensions used to organize affective information (Huang et al., 2025). Indeed, preschoolers integrate syntactic and situational information when learning novel emotion words (Shablack et al., 2020).

Developmental evidence suggests that valence provides an early organizing dimension of emotion knowledge. Children initially rely on broad positive-negative distinctions and gradually incorporate additional dimensions as their verbal knowledge develops (Nook et al., 2017; Woodard et al., 2022). Their use of emotion words becomes increasingly adult-like between ages 4 and 11, although valence remains the only clearly identifiable semantic dimension shared across all child groups (Grosse et al., 2021). This does not imply that younger children lack arousal information, but suggests that pleasantness may provide a more accessible distinction, whereas activation becomes increasingly differentiated with development.

Lexical-processing findings support the early importance of valence. Children's recognition of emotion words improves between ages 5 and 12, alongside developmental changes in the processing advantage for positive words (Bahn et al., 2017; Ponari et al., 2018). Valence influences auditory word recognition by ages 6-7 and may support abstract concept acquisition (Lund et al., 2019). It can also be predicted from children's developing semantic representations (Martínez-Huertas et al., 2021). Moreover, positive emotion-laden Spanish words are acquired earlier than negative and neutral words, even after controlling for relevant lexical characteristics (Sabater et al., 2023), with comparable advantages for positive emotion labels in English and Chinese (Li & Yu, 2015; Ponari et al., 2020).

Valence may therefore stabilize relatively early because it captures a broad evaluative distinction relevant to approach, avoidance, safety, and reward. Arousal, by contrast, may require more differentiated assessments of activation, intensity, and bodily engagement (Norman et al., 2011; Murty & Pessoa, 2025; Smith et al., 2025). Children must learn that positive and negative experiences can both be highly activating, that similarly valenced situations may differ in intensity, and that activation depends on context and experience. Changing social environments and expanding emotional knowledge during adolescence may further alter the arousal associated with particular concepts without necessarily changing their overall evaluation. Thus, the lexical organization of arousal may undergo greater developmental reorganization than that of valence.

Developmental affective norms support this possibility. The Spanish Affective Norms for Development in Children and Adolescents (SANDchild; Sabater et al., 2020) provide valence and arousal ratings for Spanish words from children aged 7, 9, 11, and 13 years. Although both dimensions differ across ages, agreement is consistently higher for valence, whereas seven-yearolds' arousal ratings are particularly distinct from those of older children. Younger children also rate some negative words as less arousing than older participants. These findings suggest that development may involve changes not only in average ratings but also in the relative affective positions of individual words (see Figure 1A). German norms similarly reveal the nonlinear valence-arousal relationship from ages 6 to 12, alongside age differences in rating extremity and response speed (Sylvester et al., 2016).

Adult comparisons provide a complementary perspective. Bahn et al. (2018) found high childadult agreement in valence and arousal ratings for 48 German emotion terms, although nine-yearolds reported somewhat lower absolute arousal. Together with evidence that arousal becomes increasingly important in emotion representations with age (Nook et al., 2017), these findings raise the question of whether child-adult correspondence develops similarly for valence and arousal across larger vocabularies and multiple child ages.

Importantly, agreement between age-group ratings does not establish whether the lexical information underlying those judgments is shared. Similar ratings may arise from different relationships between lexical features and affective judgments, whereas lower agreement may reflect either measurement variability or systematic developmental differences. Distinguishing continuity from reorganization therefore requires examining whether the relationships between lexical information and affective judgments generalize across ages.

Predictive modeling offers a way to address this question. Previous research has shown that distributional word vectors predict valence and arousal judgments (Hollis et al., 2017), while ridge regression applied to semantic representations predicts item-level (pseudo)word memorability (Aka et al., 2023; Gatti & Günther, 2026; see also Gatti, Raveling et al., 2024; Martínez-Tomás et al., 2026). This approach, termed representational content analysis (RCA; Hussain et al., 2024), examines which information can be recovered from high-dimensional representations. Evaluating predictions on unseen words establishes whether these relationships generalize beyond the training data (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017). Applied across development, strong cross-age prediction would indicate shared lexical-affective mappings, whereas declining prediction with increasing age distance would suggest developmental differences. Child-adult transfer can further establish whether these mappings become progressively more similar to those observed in adulthood.

Semantic information is a plausible basis for shared affective organization. Distributional semantic models represent words through their linguistic contexts, capturing graded relationships among concepts and emotional meanings (e.g., Günther et al., 2019; Kumar, 2021). Although these models do not directly represent children's internal semantic knowledge, they provide a common space for comparing lexical-affective relationships across ages and predicting human judgments (Mandera et al., 2017). Semantic vectors predict valence judgments (e.g., Gatti, Raveling et al., 2024), and models based on children's semantic representations can extend affective information to subsequently acquired words (Martínez-Huertas et al., 2021). If valence relies on semantic relationships established early in development, its mapping should generalize across ages. Arousal may also be predictable from semantic information related to action, intensity, danger, or excitement, but its mapping may change more substantially as children revise the activation associated with particular concepts.

Affective information may also be associated with word form. Languages contain statistical regularities linking phonological and orthographic patterns to meaning (form-meaning mappings; Dingemanse et al., 2015). Cross-linguistic studies indicate that phonemes carry weak but reliable information about valence, sometimes particularly in word-initial positions (Adelman et al., 2018).

Arousal may also contribute to sound-meaning associations, as sharper-sounding pseudowords and angular visual forms evoke higher activation (Aryani et al., 2020; see Hinojosa et al., 2026).

In Spanish, phonological and phonetic properties predict valence, emotionality, and arousal beyond several lexical and semantic variables (De Zubicaray & Hinojosa, 2024). Specific phonemes have also been associated with affective dimensions and discrete emotions, including associations of plosives with high arousal and some fricatives with highly arousing negative words (Calvillo-Torres et al., 2025).

Together, these findings indicate that affective information is distributed across semantic, orthographic, phonological, and articulatory levels. Semantic representations may provide the most direct information about affective meaning, whereas form-based features may capture weaker regularities related to phonological and acoustic properties, morphology, lexical history, or semantic categories. However, research on these relationships has focused primarily on adults, while developmental studies have largely documented differences in affective ratings without examining their lexical organization. It therefore remains unclear whether these mappings generalize across child ages, whether their feature weights remain similar, whether they predict which individual words change most strongly, and whether their correspondence with adult judgments increases with age. Addressing these questions can clarify which aspects of affective lexical knowledge show continuity or reorganization across development and whether these patterns differ between valence and arousal.

The Present Study

The present study examines the developmental organization of valence and arousal through two re-analyses of existing affective norms. Experiment 1 uses ratings from 7-, 9-, 11-, and 13-yearold children in SANDchild (Sabater et al., 2020). Using representational content analysis, we examined how semantic, orthographic, phonological, and articulatory features predict affective judgments for unseen words, both within and across age groups. We expected semantic representations to provide the strongest predictions of both dimensions, with weaker contributions from form-based features. Given the proposed earlier stabilization of valence, we also expected stronger cross-age generalization for valence than for arousal, with arousal prediction declining more sharply as developmental distance increased. Additional analyses examined whether transfer differed by developmental direction, whether feature weights remained consistent across ages, and whether lexical information predicted word-specific developmental differences.

Experiment 2 extends this framework to adulthood by comparing SANDchild ratings with adult Spanish affective norms (Stadthagen-González et al., 2017). Using the same representations and held-out prediction approach, we examined bidirectional transfer between children and adults and whether correspondence changed systematically with child age. We expected relatively high child-adult correspondence for valence throughout childhood, whereas arousal correspondence was expected to increase more strongly with age, producing a more pronounced developmental gradient. Finally, we examined whether these patterns were specific to semantic representations or extended to form-based lexical information.

Experiment 1 Method

Materials and affective ratings

The analyses used the published Spanish affective normative data for children and adolescents (SANDchild; Sabater et al., 2020). The original study recruited 1,276 native Spanish-speaking children and adolescents from schools in the Madrid region. The sample comprised 350 sevenyear-olds (173 girls and 177 boys; mean age = 7 years 7 months, SD = 4 months), 318 nine-yearolds (161 girls and 157 boys; mean age = 9 years 6 months, SD = 4 months), 297 eleven-year-olds (157 girls and 140 boys; mean age = 11 years 8 months, SD = 4 months), and 311 thirteen-yearolds (155 girls and 156 boys; mean age = 13 years 8 months, SD = 4 months). The schools represented areas with different socioeconomic characteristics.

SANDchild contains valence and arousal ratings for 1,406 Spanish words. The words were randomly allocated to 20 paper-and-pencil questionnaires: 14 questionnaires contained 70 words and six contained 71 words. Separate valence and arousal versions were created for each questionnaire. Each participant generally completed the valence and arousal versions of two questionnaire lists, thereby rating approximately 140-142 words on each affective dimension. A small number of seven-year-old participants completed only one questionnaire list. Ratings were collected using nine-point Self-Assessment Manikin scales. For valence, 1 indicated a very unpleasant response, 9 a very pleasant response, and 5 a neutral response. For arousal, 1 indicated feeling very relaxed and 9 feeling very activated. The published norms showed high mean interrater reliability for valence at ages 7, 9, 11, and 13, respectively (r = .89, .94, .95, and .94).

Reliability was lower, although still generally adequate, for arousal (r = .76, .80, .83, and .85). The present analyses used the age-specific mean valence and arousal ratings for the same complete set of 1,406 words.

Orthographic features

To construct spelling-based predictors, words were lowercased, transliterated to Latin ASCII, and restricted to the letters a-z. This normalization removed differences due to capitalization and diacritics, allowing the orthographic representations to encode differences in letter identity and ordering within a common alphabet.

Three levels of orthographic information were considered. Letter unigrams represented each word by the frequency of its individual letters, yielding 26 dimensions corresponding to the letters a-z.

This representation captures letter composition but not letter order: words containing the same letters in different arrangements can therefore receive identical unigram representations. Adjacent orthographic bigrams captured local letter-order information by counting consecutive letter pairs. Explicit start and end markers were added so that word-initial and word-final letter information was also represented. The resulting predictor space contained 728 possible dimensions: 26 startletter pairs, 676 letter-letter pairs, and 26 letter-end pairs. Thus, unlike letter unigrams, adjacent bigrams distinguish words according to local sequential structure as well as their constituent letters. Open orthographic bigrams provided a less strictly local representation of letter order. They counted every ordered pair of letters in the boundary-marked word, including pairs separated by one or more intervening letters. Consequently, open bigrams preserve information about relative letter order without requiring the two letters to be adjacent. Repeated occurrences of the same ordered pair contributed repeated counts, and the direct start-end pair was excluded. The resulting representation contained the same 728 potential dimensions as the adjacent-bigram representation.

Together, these three representations provide progressively richer descriptions of word form, from individual letter composition to local and nonlocal orthographic ordering.

Phonological and articulatory features

Spanish pronunciations were generated for all 1,406 words using Phonemizer 3.4.0 with the eSpeak NG 1.52.0 backend (Bernard & Titeux, 2021). Pronunciations were returned in International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) notation and segmented into individual phoneme tokens.

Stress marks were removed so that the resulting representations focused on segmental phonological structure rather than lexical stress. Across the complete stimulus set, the resulting pronunciation inventory contained 36 distinct phoneme tokens.

Two phonological representations were constructed. Phoneme unigrams counted the occurrence of each of the 36 phoneme tokens in a word, capturing its phonemic composition without retaining information about sequence. Adjacent phoneme bigrams additionally encoded local phoneme order by counting consecutive phoneme pairs. As for the orthographic bigrams, explicit start and end markers were included to represent word-initial and word-final phonemes. This resulted in 1,368 potential phoneme-bigram dimensions. Together, these representations allowed us to distinguish information about which speech sounds a word contains from information about their local sequential organization.

Two additional representations described pronunciations in terms of articulatory and phonological features rather than discrete phoneme identities. These were derived from a fixed PanPhon 0.22.2 feature lookup table (Mortensen et al., 2016), which characterizes IPA segments according to 24 distinctive features describing properties of speech production and phonological structure. Each phoneme therefore contributes a bundle of features rather than being represented as a single categorical unit. Positive and negative values for the 24 features were counted separately, yielding 48 possible feature counts. For the global articulatory representation, these token-level feature counts were summed across the entire pronunciation, producing 48 dimensions for each word. This representation captures the overall articulatory-feature composition of a word while discarding the precise order and location of its phonemes. The positional articulatory representation retained coarse information about where these features occurred by dividing each phoneme sequence into initial, middle, and final thirds and computing the same 48 feature counts separately within each region. This yielded 144 dimensions (48 features × 3 positions). Phonemes were assigned to thirds according to the midpoint of their normalized ordinal position in the sequence; the resulting bins therefore describe relative position within the phoneme sequence, rather than temporal or acoustic duration.

These four representations provide complementary levels of spoken-form information, ranging from phoneme identity and local phoneme order to more abstract articulatory-feature composition and its approximate position within the word.

Word embedding model

Semantic information was represented using the 300-dimensional vectors from a pre-trained Spanish fastText model (available at: https://fasttext.cc/docs/en/crawl-vectors.html; Bojanowski et al., 2017; Grave et al., 2018), trained on large Spanish-language corpora derived from Common Crawl and Wikipedia. FastText is an extension of the prominent word2vec model (Mikolov et al., 2013), a simple neural language model trained on a large corpus of natural language, with the training objective to predict the words immediately before and after a given word. To this end, word2vec uses a simple neural network model with one-hot vectors of the lexicon as input and output layers, and one hidden layer. After training, the weights between a given word in the input layer and the hidden layer is taken as the word embedding of that word. Since only the activation values of the hidden layer are directly used to predict the (context) words in the output layer, these activation values in the word embedding contain, in a condensed format, all the information required to make those predictions about (context) words. Thus, the word embedding encodes the distributional history of a word. Ultimately, these vectors carry semantic information and are thus generally employed as (distributed) semantic representations (Günther et al., 2019; Kumar, 2021).

A single 300-dimensional vector was extracted for each stimulus word prior to model fitting, providing a fixed numerical representation of that word that was subsequently used as the predictor space for both valence and arousal. Note that these 300 embedding dimensions should be interpreted jointly rather than as individually labeled linguistic features. Unlike predictors such as letters or phonemes, individual fastText coordinates do not correspond to predefined semantic categories such as concreteness, emotion, social meaning, or action. Semantic information is distributed across combinations of dimensions, and the orientation of individual dimensions is not inherently linguistically interpretable. Accordingly, predictive performance of the fastText models is interpreted as evidence that information encoded in the multidimensional distributional representation is systematically related to affective ratings, rather than as evidence for the contribution of any particular embedding coordinate.

Using a single pre-trained semantic space was particularly well suited to the present aims because the critical question concerned developmental changes in the mapping between lexical meaning and affective judgments, rather than developmental changes in the semantic representations themselves. Keeping the fastText vectors fixed across ages ensured that all age groups were evaluated within the same representational coordinate system. Consequently, differences in predictive performance or cross-age transfer can be attributed to differences in how a common set of distributional regularities relates to valence and arousal ratings, rather than to differences between independently trained embedding spaces. This also avoids potential confounds arising from the substantially smaller and less comparable language resources available for constructing age-specific semantic models, where differences in corpus size, genre, dialect, or lexical coverage could be mistaken for developmental change. Moreover, the large and heterogeneous corpus used to train the Spanish fastText model provides stable representations for the full stimulus vocabulary and reduces the influence of idiosyncratic sampling variation. The resulting shared semantic space therefore provides a controlled reference system for testing developmental reweighting of lexicalaffective information while holding the underlying distributional representation constant.

Analytical approach

Analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2025). For each age group, separate models used each lexical representation to predict valence or arousal and cross-age prediction tested whether a mapping learned from one age group generalized to another. For RCA, Gaussian ridge regressions with an intercept and an L2 penalty were fitted using glmnet (Friedman et al., 2010). Balanced five-fold partitions of word identities were repeated 50 times, with the same outer folds reused across ages, outcomes, and representations. Within each partition, the held-out words were excluded from training at every age. Within each outer fold, a model was trained on four-fifths of the words, and its penalty was selected by a separate five-fold cross-validation within the training words, minimizing mean squared error. Outcomes were centered and scaled using the source-age training words, and predictors (i.e. orthographic, phonological or semantic features) were standardized within training. Each fitted model predicted the held-out words at every target age without refitting. Pooling combined predictions from the five held-out sets, so each word contributed one prediction per repetition. Spearman correlations between pooled held-out predictions and observed ratings were calculated within each repetition and then averaged.

Developmental-distance slopes indexed changes in cross-age prediction per year of absolute separation between child groups. These slopes tested whether prediction accuracy declined as the training and target ages became farther apart. Word-specific developmental slopes were estimated by regressing each word's four mean ratings on ages 7, 9, 11, and 13; the same prediction framework was then used to predict these slopes. These word-level slopes, expressed in rating points per year, captured the direction and rate of differences across age-group means rather than change within individual children.

For slope-prediction correlations and paired-bootstrap tests, each word's held-out predictions were first averaged across the 50 repetitions; correlations and contrasts were then calculated from these ensemble predictions. Paired comparisons resampled word identities together across the outcomes and models being compared, using 499 bootstrap resamples. The same sampled words were used across ages, outcomes, and models to preserve the paired comparisons. Bootstrap inference was conditional on the fitted predictions. Models were not refitted during resampling; the intervals therefore quantify word-sampling uncertainty for the fitted prediction ensembles. Significance was assessed using Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate adjusted q values within the respective test families (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

Experiment 2

Experiment 1 showed that lexical-affective mappings generalized across childhood, but with markedly different developmental patterns for valence and arousal. Whereas valence showed strong cross-age stability, arousal became progressively less transferable as developmental distance increased, suggesting greater age-related change in its lexical organization. Experiment 2 extended these findings beyond childhood by examining whether child lexical-affective mappings increasingly converge with those observed in adults, and whether this developmental convergence differs between valence and arousal and across lexical representations.

Method

Materials

The child data were the age-specific mean ratings described in Experiment 1 for children aged 7, 9, 11, and 13 years. Adult ratings were obtained from the norms of Stadthagen-Gonzalez et al. (2017), which contain valence and arousal ratings for 14,031 Spanish words.

The adult norms were collected from 512 native Spanish speakers enrolled in undergraduate psychology courses at the University of Murcia. Valence ratings were provided by 350 participants (mean age = 22 years and 3 months), whereas arousal ratings were provided by 395 participants (mean age = 22 years and 5 months). As for child ratings, adult ratings were collected using ninepoint numerical scales, for valence, 1 corresponded to unhappy and 9 to happy; for arousal, 1 corresponded to quiet or calm and 9 to excited.

From the set of 14,031 Spanish words available, we included only those matching between the two databases using exact lowercase, whitespace-trimmed, Unicode-normalized Spanish forms. Of the 1,406 words in SANDchild, 1,373 had both valence and arousal ratings in the adult norms, corresponding to 97.65% of the child database, and the remaining 33 words were excluded. Every retained word therefore had complete ratings for all four child groups and adults, ensuring that all correlations and predictive comparisons were based on the same word identities.

Lexical representations

The analyses used the same eight predictor spaces described in Experiment 1: letter counts, adjacent orthographic bigrams, open orthographic bigrams, phoneme counts, phoneme bigrams, global articulatory features, positional articulatory features, and 300 Spanish fastText semantic dimensions.

Analytical approach

The RCA procedure described in Experiment 1 was applied to the 1,373 words shared by the child and adult norms. Models were evaluated within each rating group and in both child-to-adult and adult-to-child directions. Child age indexed the training group in child-to-adult analyses and the target group in adult-to-child analyses. Linear trends indexed change in held-out Spearman correlation per child year, with paired word-bootstrap inference and false-discovery-rate correction across the 16 representation-by-outcome tests within each direction. Adult developmental-distance analyses were not conducted because the adult sample did not represent a single age. See Supplementary Material for pairwise age comparisons, directional asymmetry, prediction error, and transfer relative to the within-target baseline.

Results

Observed child-adult correspondence

Valence ratings already showed strong correspondence with adults at age 7, ρ = .769, and agreement increased to .820, .859, and .870 at ages 9, 11, and 13, respectively (Figure 1B; Table S6). Arousal followed a different pattern: correspondence was negative at age 7, ρ = -.124, close to zero at age 9, ρ = .067, and positive at ages 11 and 13, ρs = .201 and .260.

Semantic valence transferred strongly in both directions (Figures 1C and 3). Discussion

The present study identified distinct age-related patterns in the lexical organization of valence and arousal. Valence showed comparatively strong continuity across childhood and already corresponded closely with adult judgments at age 7. Arousal, by contrast, was reliably predictable within each child age group but showed weaker semantic generalization across ages and substantial differences from adult mappings in younger children. Semantic information also predicted wordspecific age differences more successfully for arousal than for valence. Together, these findings suggest that affective lexical development involves both continuity and reorganization: valence provides a relatively stable evaluative structure, whereas the relationship between lexical meaning and activation varies more substantially across development. Importantly, greater age-related variation in arousal did not imply an absence of systematic organization in younger children.

Developmental Continuity and Reorganization of Affective Meaning

Dimensional theories identify valence and arousal as fundamental components of affect, reflecting whether an experience is pleasant or unpleasant and how activating it is (Russell, 1980). The present findings suggest that these dimensions follow different developmental patterns. Across ages 7-13, valence ratings showed substantial consistency, and lexical-affective mappings generalized well between child groups. Moreover, correspondence with adult judgments was already strong at age 7, suggesting that the relative evaluative organization of familiar words is established comparatively early. This finding is consistent with developmental research showing that valence provides a prominent basis for children's early emotion representations, while additional dimensions become increasingly differentiated with age (Nook et al., 2017; Russell & Ridgeway, 1983; Woodard et al., 2022). The present study extends this evidence beyond emotional expressions and categories to the organization of a broader vocabulary. Valence may provide a relatively stable framework because positive and negative concepts occur in systematically different linguistic and experiential contexts, allowing children to acquire broad evaluative distinctions early. This does not imply that evaluative meaning is fixed or independent of experience, but rather that the relative positions of words along this dimension change comparatively little across the ages examined.

Arousal showed a different pattern. Semantic prediction declined more strongly with increasing distance between child age groups, while child-adult correspondence shifted from negative at age 7 to positive at ages 11 and 13. Nevertheless, arousal remained predictable within every child group. Thus, younger children's arousal judgments were not simply unstructured or absent; instead, their relationships with lexical meaning differed from those observed at later ages.

Previous research has shown that emotion representations become increasingly multidimensional across development, with verbal knowledge contributing to the growing importance of arousal (Nook et al., 2017). The present findings extend this account by suggesting that systematic arousalrelated information is already present in younger children's lexical judgments, even when its organization differs substantially from that of adults. Development may therefore involve not only increasing differentiation of affective dimensions but also changes in which concepts are considered relatively activating.

Unlike valence, arousal may depend on integrating several sources of information, including anticipated bodily responses, intensity, novelty, urgency, perceived control, and situational context. As children acquire new experiences and enter changing social environments, the activation associated with particular concepts may change without corresponding shifts in their overall positive or negative evaluation. For example, words such as school, party, darkness, or dog may retain their evaluative direction while acquiring different levels of emotional intensity or physiological relevance. This interpretation is consistent with accounts emphasizing gradual changes in how children combine and weight information when representing emotions (Woodard et al., 2022). Evidence that emotion transitions become increasingly organized by valence and more adult-like as vocabulary expands also supports a close relationship between linguistic and emotional development (Nencheva et al., 2024). Language may contribute by helping children organize heterogeneous bodily and contextual experiences into meaningful conceptual categories (Hoemann et al., 2019; Lindquist et al., 2015), while increasing verbal knowledge supports more differentiated emotion representations (Nook et al., 2017). Developmental improvements in interoceptive awareness may further contribute to the differentiation of arousal as children become better able to identify and interpret internal physiological signals (Addabbo & Milani, 2025; Craik et al., 2026; Murphy et al., 2017).

Together, these processes may help explain why arousal shows greater age-related variation than valence. Rather than acquiring an entirely new affective dimension, children may progressively revise the activation associated with familiar concepts as linguistic, bodily, and social knowledge expands. This interpretation is compatible with constructionist approaches, which emphasize interactions among affect, conceptual knowledge, language, and situated experience in the development of emotional meaning (Hoemann et al., 2019; Lindquist et al., 2015).

Lexical Information as a Basis for Developmental Continuity and Change

Semantic representations provided the strongest predictions of both valence and arousal, suggesting that affective meaning is closely related to the broader organization of lexical knowledge. For arousal, semantic information predicted judgments within each age group, identified words showing larger age-related differences, and showed progressively stronger correspondence with adult judgments across child ages. These findings indicate that age-related differences in arousal were not entirely unsystematic: words with related meanings tended to show related patterns of variation.

This result can be understood in light of the distributed nature of affective information in language.

Hollis and Westbury (2016) identified distinct valence and dominance components in English semantic embeddings but found no single component uniquely corresponding to arousal. Instead, arousal-related information was distributed across multiple semantic dimensions. More broadly, emotional meaning has been characterized as high-dimensional and situated, reflecting patterns of attention, interpretation, and appraisal rather than isolated affective dimensions or explicit emotion labels alone (Hoemann et al., 2025). Whereas valence may correspond relatively closely to a broad positive-negative distinction, arousal may depend on combinations of semantic information associated with action, danger, novelty, intensity, and social experience.

Such distributed organization may contribute to the greater developmental flexibility of arousal.

Evaluating activation may require integrating multiple context-dependent cues whose relative importance changes with experience. The finding that semantic information predicted wordspecific age differences is consistent with this possibility: changes in arousal judgments were related to the conceptual organization of the lexicon rather than reflecting only random disagreement between age groups.

The child-adult analyses further clarify this interpretation. Because the same semantic embeddings were used across populations, differences in predictive transfer cannot be attributed to differences between embedding spaces. Instead, they reflect differences in the estimated relationships between a common semantic representation and affective ratings. The negative or near-zero transfer observed for younger children's arousal judgments suggests that semantic information associated with greater activation in these groups was not weighted in the same way as in adults. Increasing transfer with age indicates greater correspondence between these mappings, although it does not establish that children and adults internally represent words within identical semantic structures.

More generally, the findings distinguish the systematicity of affective organization within an age group from its consistency across development. High within-group predictability can coexist with weak cross-age transfer when different populations show different relationships between lexical information and affective judgments. A common representational space can therefore capture both developmental continuity and variation without requiring that the same lexical features contribute identically at every age.

Although semantic representations were the strongest predictors, orthographic, phonological, and articulatory features also contained some affective information. Their weaker performance is consistent with evidence that emotional content is closely integrated with lexical-semantic processing (Ferré et al., 2025), but does not imply that relationships between word form and affect are arbitrary. Previous studies have identified systematic associations between phonological properties and affective meaning, particularly for activation-related qualities (Adelman et al., 2018; Aryani et al., 2020; de Zubicaray et al., 2023; Schmidtke & Conrad, 2025). Such associations may provide probabilistic cues to intensity, movement, abruptness, size, or force. Because activation is more directly related than evaluative direction to properties such as tempo, sonority, articulatory effort, and abruptness, arousal may be particularly sensitive to some sublexical regularities (Bänziger & Scherer, 2005; Zheng et al., 2026). Nevertheless, their limited predictive contribution indicates that the developmental patterns observed here were primarily associated with semantic information.

These findings have broader implications for theories of affective and conceptual development.

Valence and arousal may define a common affective space while showing different degrees of continuity across development. In particular, possessing an organized arousal dimension does not necessarily imply possessing an adult-like organization of that dimension. This distinction is consistent with developmental accounts emphasizing the progressive differentiation and reorganization of emotional knowledge (Davis et al., 2023; Nook et al., 2018). Rather than requiring a complete reconstruction of the lexicon, development may involve changes in how affective information is associated with existing concepts. Language provides a shared system through which culturally recurring affective relationships are learned and communicated, while individual experiences contribute to the situated meaning of particular concepts (Winkielman et al., 2023). The present results suggest that this system supports both a comparatively stable evaluative organization and a more developmentally flexible organization of activation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations qualify these interpretations. First, the developmental analyses relied on crosssectional normative data. Age-related differences in ratings and word-specific slopes therefore cannot establish how individual children's affective judgments change over time, and cohort composition or sampling variation may contribute to the observed patterns. Moreover, the four child age groups provide only a coarse representation of development, and linear slopes cannot identify temporary transitions or nonlinear trajectories. Nevertheless, the findings converged across complementary analyses: cross-age prediction declined with developmental distance for arousal, while child-adult correspondence increased through age 11, with no supported additional increase between ages 11 and 13 (Table S10). Longitudinal studies with more densely sampled ages would help establish the timing and within-person course of these changes.

Second, analyses were conducted on age-group means rather than individual responses. This approach is appropriate for characterizing normative lexical organization and reduces idiosyncratic variation, but it does not capture individual differences in affective knowledge. Future studies could examine whether vocabulary, reading experience, pubertal development, interoceptive awareness, and social or emotional functioning explain variation in lexical-affective organization.

Third, the semantic embeddings were derived from a general-language corpus likely to reflect adult linguistic experience more strongly than children's language environments. Using a common representational space was analytically useful because it allowed differences between age groups to be attributed to their estimated lexical-affective mappings rather than to differences between separately trained embedding spaces. Moreover, successful prediction within child groups indicates that general linguistic regularities contain information relevant to children's affective judgments. However, these embeddings should not be equated with children's internal semantic representations, and differences in model weights do not directly demonstrate changes in mental representations. Developmentally trained semantic models could clarify how age-related differences in linguistic experience contribute to affective organization. Fourth, arousal ratings may be less reliable than valence ratings, particularly among younger children. SANDchild reported lower agreement for arousal and especially pronounced differences involving the youngest participants (Sabater et al., 2020). Reduced reliability may limit observable predictive performance and contribute to weaker cross-age correspondence. However, it is unlikely to provide a complete explanation: arousal was predictable within younger groups, its cross-age generalization varied with developmental distance, and semantic information predicted word-specific age differences. These converging patterns suggest that the observed variation contains systematic structure beyond measurement imprecision.

Fifth, the child-adult comparison combined ratings from separate normative studies with different samples and procedures. The adult sample consisted primarily of young university students rather than a continuously sampled adult population. Consequently, differences cannot be attributed exclusively to age, and adult ratings should be understood as a reference population rather than a developmental endpoint or ground truth. Nevertheless, convergence between observed rating patterns and bidirectional held-out prediction suggests that the main findings are not restricted to a single direction of predictive transfer.

Future research could extend these findings through longitudinal and cross-linguistic designs combining affective ratings with measures of linguistic, emotional, and bodily development. Joint modeling of valence and arousal could also establish whether age-related differences in activation depend on the evaluative position of individual words. Another important question concerns the relationship between lexical arousal and emotion differentiation. The present findings do not demonstrate that differences in word ratings correspond directly to differences in everyday emotional experiences. However, they reveal systematic age-related variation in lexical activation during a period characterized by substantial development in emotion knowledge and differentiation (Nook et al., 2018; Nook, 2021). Linking lexical judgments to children's everyday experiences could clarify how changes in affective word meaning relate to broader developments in emotional understanding and regulation.

Conclusion

The present findings suggest that affective lexical development combines continuity with selective age-related reorganization. Valence showed a comparatively stable evaluative organization across childhood and substantial correspondence with adult judgments from age 7. Arousal showed greater developmental variation: its semantic mapping generalized less successfully across child ages, became progressively more similar to adult mappings, and showed word-specific differences systematically related to lexical meaning. Importantly, limited early child-adult correspondence did not imply that younger children lacked an organized arousal dimension. Rather, their judgments were systematically related to lexical information in ways that differed from those observed in older groups. These findings suggest that affective development may involve the progressive reweighting of information within an existing lexical organization, rather than the simple acquisition of an initially absent dimension.

Distinguishing the relative stability of valence from the greater developmental flexibility of arousal connects dimensional theories of affect with constructionist and developmental accounts of conceptual reorganization, while highlighting the importance of treating affective word meanings as developmentally situated.
