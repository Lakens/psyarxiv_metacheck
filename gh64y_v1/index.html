Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Among perceptual-cognitive skills, decision-making is the most frequently targeted in virtual reality (VR)-based training. Although VR shows promise, heterogeneity in study designs and methodological limitations highlight the need for more rigorous research. The present study aims to (i) assess the effects of a custom-made VR180 immersive training program on decision-making accuracy, (ii) evaluate embodiment (VEQ), sense of presence (SUS), and technology acceptance (TAM), and (iii) explore near transfer effects on processing speed, cognitive flexibility, and response inhibition, assessed with the Vienna Test System. Twenty-seven Under-13 and Under-14 soccer players participated in a within-subject design. The study consisted of T0 (baseline assessment), followed by 4 weeks of conventional training only (no intervention); T1 (post-control assessment); and 4 weeks of VR180 cognitive intervention (three sessions per week, before conventional training), followed by T2 (post-intervention assessment). The effectiveness of the training is evidenced by the comparison between T2 and T1. Results showed improvements in decision-making accuracy from T1 to T2, and particularly, in the following scenarios: feinting and dribbling, marking, off-the-ball movement, passing, and shooting and heading. Improvements in cognitive tests were observed both between T0 and T1 and between T1 and T2, suggesting that these gains may be attributable to a learning effect rather than to the training itself. On a 1-7 scale, participants reported good levels of body ownership, sense of presence, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use of the technology. In conclusion, VR180 training was effective in enhancing decision-making accuracy in soccerspecific situations. However, further on-field testing is necessary to evaluate its transfer effects in real situations.

1. Introduction

Decision-making is one of the most extensively studied perceptual-cognitive skills in sports context (Kittel et al., 2024). It is defined as the ability to process information from the environment, evaluate possible courses of action, anticipate outcomes, and select the most appropriate response to a given situation (Bel-Eli et al., 2011). Team sports are inherently complex and dynamic environments. They involve multiple stimuli, such as opponents, teammates, the ball, and contextual information like the current match score, that must be continuously updated to guide action. Decisions often need to be made within a few seconds and under pressure, requiring the integration of several higher-order cognitive processes, including anticipation. Decision-making is generally refined with age and experience and, because it relies on core executive functions such as processing speed, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control, it is a particularly relevant skill to target during youth development.

Given the importance of decision-making in team sports, simulation training in virtual environments has increasingly been adopted to enhance both the speed and accuracy of athletes' decisions. Virtual environments can simulate game scenarios, allowing athletes to practice in situations that closely resemble real match conditions (Jia et al., 2024). In sports, virtual environments are usually provided through immersive technologies, such as head-mounted displays (HMDs) and CAVE systems (Gray, 2019). The degree of realism and immersion afforded by these systems is generally considered a key factor that may facilitate the transfer of learning to real-world sporting contexts thanks to their ecological validity. However, in the context of extended reality, computer-generated virtual reality (VR) and 360° videos, delivered through HMDs, may further enhance learning, not only by leveraging ecological validity but also by promoting embodiment through a first-person perspective (1PP). The Sense of Embodiment (SoE) refers to the psychological experience of perceiving, owning, and controlling a virtual body as if it were one's own biological body or an extension of it (Kilteni et al., 2012). It encompasses three components: self-location, body ownership, and agency. Self-location refers to the subjective experience of where the self is perceived to be situated, typically within the virtual body, and is strongly facilitated by a 1PP (Alsmith & Longo, 2014). Body ownership is the feeling that the virtual body belongs to oneself and is the source of experienced sensations (Kilteni et al., 2012). Agency refers to the sense of being in control of one's actions and movements, encompassing the subjective experience of intention, motor control, and voluntary action (Blanke & Metzinger, 2009). Together, these components contribute to the sense of embodiment and shape the virtual experience of users (Repetto & Scerrati, 2026).

Research on action observation provides partial support for the hypothesis that the sense of embodiment may contribute to learning processes. Studies on the mirror neuron system (MNS) have shown that sensorimotor regions involved in action execution are also activated during action observation when actions are viewed from a third-person perspective (3PP) (Fogassi et al., 2005; Gallese, 2009; Gallese, Keysers, & Rizzolatti, 2004). However, a recent review emphasizes that MNS activation is shaped by multiple interacting factors, including the characteristics of the observed action, the actor, the observer, the relationship between them, and the surrounding context, suggesting that no single explanatory model can fully account for its functioning (Kemmerer, 2021). Consistent with this perspective, emerging evidence from rehabilitation research indicates that the perspective from which actions are observed may influence cognitive outcomes. For example, in a VR-based intervention, participants with post-stroke naming deficits who observed gestures from a 1PP showed greater improvements in word retrieval than those who observed the same actions from a 3PP (Cancer et al., 2026). These findings suggest that immersive, embodied action observation may enhance learning by engaging mechanisms related to embodied simulation, potentially by increasing the sense of body ownership and self-location while reducing the need for visuospatial perspective transformations.

Quantitative controlled intervention studies in team sports have primarily focused on baseball and basketball, with only one study investigating soccer. Overall, these studies have generally reported positive effects of immersive training on decision-making skills, although Kincaid et al. (2021) found no significant effects and Panchuk et al. (2018) reported mixed findings. Specifically, Panchuk et al. examined the effectiveness of immersive video for training decision-making in elite youth basketball players, assessing performance through an immersive test and during small-sided games. Improvements were observed in both the VR training group and the control group, which participated only in their usual training. Similar patterns have been reported in other studies, including Gray (2017), Pagé et al. (2019), Fortes et al. (2021), and Tsai et al. (2021), where both experimental and control groups showed improvements. Control groups either received no intervention or engaged in alternative non-immersive training; however, experimental groups consistently demonstrated greater gains. Pagé et al. investigated varsity-level basketball players and compared video simulations presented on a computer screen with VR, alongside a third group that viewed game footage from a 3PP on a screen. After four training sessions, both the video simulation and VR groups outperformed the third group in "trained" plays, while the VR group also outperformed both groups in "untrained" plays. Similarly, Fortes et al. compared screen-based video simulation with VR for training passing decision-making in young soccer players. Both groups improved in decision-making performance with greater improvements observed in the VR group. These findings suggest that VR may lead to greater improvements than traditional video-based training, possibly because of the greater degree of embodied simulation it provides. Tsai et al., in a basketball context, focused on offensive players in defensive scenarios and developed an immersive decision-making training program incorporating motion capture, which provided feedback following incorrect decisions. They compared a control group undergoing tactics-board training with groups trained using prerecorded 360° videos and a computer-generated VR environment. Results indicated no differences between 360° video and computer-generated VR in knowledge acquisition, decision time, or perceived presence, while both approaches were superior to tactics-board training. Overall, these findings suggest that immersive training improves decision-making performance. However, improvements are often observed in control groups as well. Therefore, although VR appears to offer additional benefits, the extent and nature of these effects require further evidence, particularly in soccer.

To continue, even when VR-based interventions demonstrate effectiveness on sport-specific outcomes, it remains unclear whether these benefits transfer to broader cognitive functions or to onfield performance. Evidence regarding cognitive transfer is currently limited and inconclusive. On the one hand, Fendrian et al. (2024) found that immersive perceptual-cognitive training improved inhibitory control in karate athletes. They reported post-test differences in inhibitory control, assessed using the Stroop test, following the VR-based intervention compared to the control group. These differences were not present before the training intervention. Similarly, Fortes et al. (2021) showed improvements in inhibitory control (accuracy and response time) in both the VR and videostimulation groups, with no significant differences between groups. Moreover, evidence from nonspecific cognitive assessments remains inconsistent. Petri et al. (2019), for example, proposed a VR training program aimed at improving response behavior in karate kumite athletes. After 10 sessions in which participants responded to attacks from virtual opponents, the authors assessed both sportspecific response behavior (response time and accuracy) and non-specific response behavior using a standardized reaction test from the Vienna Test System (VTS). While improvements were observed in sport-specific performance, no improvements were found in information processing speed as measured by the VTS. In contrast, Casella et al. (2026) reported that an 8-week sport-specific VR intervention in soccer players enhanced performance on a visuomotor cognitive task and was associated with EEG changes indicative of improved sensorimotor integration and post-perceptual decision-making processes. To conclude, not only cognitive transfer but also on-field transfer remains uncertain. As reported in a recent review by Connolly et al. (2025), there is still no evidence that immersive VR training transfers to on-field performance. Therefore, the current literature highlights two main gaps. First, there is a limited number of controlled intervention studies in soccer. Second, there is a lack of clarity regarding the near transfer effects of immersive training on cognitive functions underlying decision making.

In this context, the present study aims to provide further evidence on the effectiveness of immersive perceptual-cognitive training in sports. Specifically, it addresses gaps in the literature, such as the limited number of controlled intervention studies and the underexplored nature of near-transfer cognitive effects in VR-based training, as highlighted by a recent review (Gala et al., 2026). This experimental study contributes to the growing body of research by developing a VR-based cognitive intervention designed to enhance decision-making in youth soccer players. The first aim of the study was to investigate the effectiveness of a 4-week VR180 immersive training program on the decisionmaking accuracy of youth soccer players. We hypothesized that decision-making accuracy would improve following the VR180 intervention compared to the control period. The second aim of the study was to explore the near-transfer effects of VR180 immersive training on the cognitive functions underlying decision-making, including processing speed, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. This objective was considered exploratory. Finally, the third aim of the study was to evaluate the user experience associated with custom-made immersive content delivered through an HMD, including enjoyment, sense of presence, body ownership, and technology acceptance. We hypothesized that participants would report high levels of user experience and technology acceptance.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Participants

Twenty-eight youth soccer players (n = 28) voluntarily took part in this project. They played for a professional club, competing in the Under-13 (n = 19) and Under-14 (n = 9) age categories. This age range was targeted because the difficulty level of the video scenarios was specifically tailored to the perceptual-cognitive abilities of players at this developmental stage. Participants (height: 163.25 ± 9.31 cm; weight: 50.08 ± 9.05 kg; BMI: 18.65 ± 2.00 kg/m²) had been playing soccer for an average of 7.96 ± 1.14 years and had been part of a professional club for an average of 4.68 ± 2.23 years. They were all enrolled in compulsory education with less than 9 years of schooling and had no previous experience with VR. Sample size was estimated using GPower v.3.1.9.7 with the power set at 0.80, α = 0.05, and an effect size of 0.25 based on similar outcome measures reported by Fortes et al. (2021). Only defenders (n = 8), forwards (n = 10), and midfielders (n = 10) were recruited, as the video scenarios were specifically designed to reflect game situations relevant to these playing positions. Exclusion criteria therefore included goalkeepers and individuals with a history of neurological or vestibular conditions. Nonetheless, all participants were healthy athletes with normal vision and hearing. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Psychology Department at the Catholic University of the Sacred Heart, Milan, Italy, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013), and written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

2.2. Experimental design

This is a repeated-measures, within-subjects design. All athletes took part in both the control and intervention phases. Following a baseline assessment (T0), participants completed a 4-week control phase consisting of conventional training only, after which they underwent a second assessment (T1). This was followed by an intervention phase consisting of 4 weeks of immersive video training (3x/week, administered before conventional training), concluding with a final assessment (T2). The intervention duration of four weeks, with three sessions per week, was determined based on previous intervention studies, which identified a training period of four to six weeks with two to three weekly sessions as a potentially effective protocol for perceptual-cognitive training using immersive technologies (Gray, 2017; Petri et al., 2019; Fortes et al., 2021). For feasibility reasons related to pretraining time constraints, participants were randomly assigned to two groups that started at different time points. 2.3. Equipment

Videos were custom-made for this research project and acquired using a GoPro Max camera (serial number C3353424612873, version 02.02) in HERO mode at a resolution of 1440p and a frame rate of 50 fps. Max HyperSmooth and Horizon Lock were selected to minimize the risk of motion sickness, and a 13-mm Max SuperView digital lens was used to provide an immersive, wide field of view. A head strap was used to record the videos from a first-person perspective, while three videos involving headers were filmed with a chest mount. Editing was performed in Microsoft Clipchamp. Videos were exported at 1080p and 30 fps and viewed in VR180 mode using a Meta Quest 3 or Quest 2 headset (Gala et al., 2026).

2.4. Materials

>Materials

2.4.1. Scenario filming

Ten amateur soccer players were recruited as actors and provided video recording consent form. The videos were recorded on half of an 11-a-side pitch during an eight-hour filming session. A total of 55 videos were recorded, with an average duration of 9.6 ± 2.3 seconds, consistent with previous work by Panchuk et al. (2018). The videos were designed to cover seven macro-areas, each comprising multiple variations: (i) ball control and defending (six variations); (ii) dribbling and feinting (eight variations); (iii) interception (eight variations); (iv) marking (six variations); (v) off-the-ball movement (eight variations); (vi) passing (nine variations); and (vii) shooting and heading (ten variations). Actors were instructed to play naturally and run at full speed. Before filming each scenario, they were informed of the intended outcome and given time to practice their predefined responses. All actors wore soccer uniforms, with the first-person player's team always in black and the opponent in orange. The scenarios were developed by the research group and discussed with a team of soccer-related professionals. The videos were recorded with an 180-degree field of view because the game sequences were short and dynamic. However, prior to the start of each scene, the player wearing the GoPro presented an overview of the playing area, including the players behind him.

2.4.2. Training videos

A total of 35 videos were used for training. A black screen displaying the macro-area was presented for 2.5 s, followed by 2.5 s of the first video frame for orientation. The videos played and stopped at a critical point, pausing on the last frame. Players were instructed to take their time to verbalize their responses and pay attention to the feedback. Response time was progressively reduced throughout the training weeks, from 3 s in the first week to 2.5 s in the second, 2 s in the third, and 1.5 s in the final week. This approach aimed to guide athletes toward providing accurate responses in the shortest possible time. A short beep lasting 0.29 s was played to indicate that the response time had expired. The videos then resumed, showing the optimal action from a first-person perspective. The feedback was provided to enable athletes to visualize themselves by performing the optimal choices.

2.4.3. Testing videos

Twenty videos were used exclusively for assessment at T0, T1, and T2. Unlike the training videos, no macro-area or feedback was presented. A black screen displaying the video number (e.g., "Video 1") was presented for 2.5 s, followed by 2.5 s of the first video frame for orientation. The videos then played until a critical point, displaying the last frame for 1.5 s to allow time for verbal responses. A short beep lasting 0.29 s was played to indicate that the response time had expired.

2.5. Variable measurements 2.5.1. Decision-making accuracy

To assess decision-making accuracy, the 20 unique testing videos were presented in VR180 with HMD. The test included at least two videos from each of the seven macro-areas, with the order randomized and presented differently for each participant at each time point. Participants were instructed to imagine themselves as the first-person player, observe each scenario, and verbally indicate the solution they would choose whenever the video was paused. They were given 1.5 s to provide the most accurate response possible, with an auditory beep signaling the end of the response time. If participants had already begun speaking before the 'beep', they were allowed to complete their response. Participants were informed that the scenarios encompassed seven macro-areas, which were explicitly mentioned, and that their team wore black while the opponents wore orange. Following the instructions, participants were presented with five practice videos taken from the training footage to familiarize themselves with the procedure. After the practice phase, they were invited to ask questions, and the instructions were repeated before the start of the testing session until doubts were resolved. The testing session lasted approximately 10 minutes. Evaluation was conducted using a predefined scoring system: 2 points were assigned to the optimal response, 1 point to each of the two acceptable responses, and 0 points to incorrect or null responses (responses provided after the auditory signal). Optimal and acceptable responses were defined a priori by the research group and soccer professionals. According to this grid, scoring was performed in a blinded manner to minimize the risk of bias. A similar procedure was used by Pagé et al. (2019). See Fig. 2 for an example of the testing apparatus and the players' view in the HMD.

2.5.2. Cognitive functions

In our study, we introduced cognitive testing to assess information processing speed, cognitive flexibility and response inhibition. These cognitive functions were not directly trained but were indirectly targeted by the demands of processing information rapidly in the VR environment, shifting between scenarios (macro-areas) and inhibiting automatic responses in favor of optimal ones. Computerized, valid, and reliable neurocognitive tests were administered using the Vienna Test System (SCHUHFRIED GmbH, Mödling, Austria), specifically TMT-L (parallel forms S1 to S3) and INHIB (versions S13-S14). TMT-L Part A was used to measure information processing speed. Numbers from 1 to 25 were randomly displayed on the screen, and participants were instructed to click the numbers in sequential order as quickly as possible using the mouse. TMT-L Part B was used to measure cognitive flexibility. Numbers from 1 to 13 and letters from A to L were randomly presented, and participants were instructed to click them alternately and in ascending order as quickly as possible. The test was supervised and took approximately 4 minutes to complete. The key variables were the raw scores for Working Time Part A and Working Time Part B, as well as the Ratio Score B/A. The INHIB test, using a go/no-go paradigm, required participants to decide whether a stimulus required a response or inhibition. A dominant response tendency was established through frequent identical responses. A total of 125 stimuli (circles and triangles) were presented one by one on the screen for 200 ms, with a 1-second interstimulus interval. Triangles occurred frequently and required a response by pressing the shift button on the keyboard, whereas circles occurred infrequently and required no response. The test was supervised and lasted approximately 4 minutes. The key variable was the raw number of Commission Errors. To control for learning effects, different versions were administered: S1, S2, and S3 for TMT-L, and S13 and S14 for INHIB. In addition, practice trials were administered before the test. Similar tests from the Vienna Test System have previously been used by Romeas et al. (2022) and Petri et al. (2019).

2.5.3. User experience

Questionnaires assessing VR constructs, including embodiment, sense of presence and technology acceptance, were administered to account for the importance of VR as the delivery medium for the training. Perceived enjoyment of the custom-made training was also measured using a 10-point visual analogue scale, ranging from 0 to 10. Embodiment was assessed with the Virtual Embodiment Questionnaire (VEQ), rated on a 7-point Likert scale (Roth and Latoschik, 2020). Only the first four items were used, since the Control/Agency and Change subscales were not relevant due to the noninteractive nature of the VR180 content. Sense of presence was measured with the Slater Usoh Steed (SUS) questionnaire (Usoh, 2000), which comprises six items rated on a 7-point Likert scale. Technology acceptance was evaluated with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) questionnaire (Davis, 1989), which includes 12 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale and assesses Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease of Use (PEU). Higher scores indicated greater enjoyment, stronger embodiment and sense of presence, greater perceived usefulness, and higher perceived ease of use. The SUS and TAM questionnaires have been previously used in VR-related sport research (Tsai et al., 2021), while the VEQ is widely adopted in VR research. All questionnaires were administered in Italian using Italian-adapted versions of the items previously used by Marini et al., 2022 (VEQ), Pallavicini et al., 2013 (SUS), and Repetto et al., 2023 (TAM). Questionnaires were administered during the last training session to maximize the fidelity of the reported VR experience and took approximately 10 minutes to complete.

2.6. Procedure

The intervention was developed based on previous methodological recommendations, and all training materials were made openly available to promote transparency and reproducibility (https://osf.io/9q4rs/overview?view_only=f112d57921d14e139096528a2798cd07). The study was conducted during the competitive season, from January to May 2026, and participants trained 3x/week for 2 hours, plus one or two matches per week. All the participants underwent first the control phase, which lasted 4 weeks and consisted of usual training without any manipulation. The intervention phase consisted of immersive video training conducted three times per week for 4 weeks, for a total of 12 sessions. Each session lasted 5 minutes, during which participants watched 15 videos in VR180 using an HMD and verbalized their choices within game scenarios. They were instructed to take their time when responding and to pay attention to the feedback. Training was supervised by members of the research group and took place before regular practice. The same training sequence was applied to all participants. The order of the videos was pseudo-randomized according to three criteria: (i) all 35 videos were presented over the course of a week, covering all seven macro-areas and variations; (ii) no video was repeated within the same session; and (iii) no video was repeated in consecutive sessions. As a result, 30 videos were repeated five times, and 5 videos were repeated six times. Due to issues with the Meta Quest 3, one participant completed 7 of the 12 sessions. See Fig. 2 for an example of the training apparatus and the players' view in the HMD. Assessments were conducted at baseline (T0) and repeated after 4 weeks of control (T1) and after 4 weeks of intervention (T2). The effectiveness of the training was determined by comparing T2 and T1. At T0, T1, and T2, decision-making accuracy was evaluated through a VR-based test, followed by the Vienna Test System tests, measuring information processing speed, cognitive flexibility, and response inhibition. At T0, a sociodemographic questionnaire was administered, and on the last day of training, questionnaires related to embodiment, sense of presence, and technology acceptance were administered. *** Fig. 2. about here ***

2.7. Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in Jamovi 2.7.18, with the significance level set at α = .05 and 95% confidence intervals. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean ± standard deviation. The primary outcome, decision-making accuracy, was analyzed using a Linear Mixed Model with Score as the dependent variable, MacroArea, Time, and their interaction as fixed effects, and random intercepts for Participant (N = 27) and Video (N = 20; N = 1620 observations). Secondary cognitive outcomes were analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with three levels (T0, T1, T2) and row scores from the tests as within-subject variables. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were Bonferroni-adjusted. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η²p). Questionnaire scores were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω.

4. Discussion

Although VR shows promise, there is currently a lack of methodological consistency across randomized controlled trials. The first aim of the present study was therefore to further explore the effectiveness of VR-based training on decision-making while taking into account some of the commonly reported methodological limitations in the literature. We aimed to investigate the effects of immersive VR180 video training on the decision-making accuracy of youth soccer players, and whether these effects transfer to underlying cognitive functions, including information processing speed, cognitive flexibility, and response inhibition. These cognitive functions were not directly trained but were indirectly targeted by the training protocol, as athletes were required to rapidly process information within the VR environment, shift attention between scenarios, and inhibit automatic responses in favor of optimal ones. Moreover, because the training was a cognitive intervention, these variables were included as outcome measures, given their relevance to decisionmaking processes. In line with Tsai et al. (2021), our findings suggest that the way VR is implemented may be more important than the specific VR technology used. In this study, we used custom-made videos recorded with a GoPro and viewed through a Meta Quest headset, suggesting that meaningful outcomes may be achievable even with limited resources, provided that the intervention is implemented rigorously.

In line with our first hypothesis, the immersive VR180 video training, performed three times per week for four weeks, was effective in enhancing decision-making accuracy in Under-13 and Under-14 soccer players. Players improved their ability to recognize macro-areas and to identify the optimal choice to perform in different game scenarios. Although decision-making speed was not included as an outcome measure because it could not be rigorously measured in our setup, it can still be argued that players showed an improved ability to select the optimal response within a short time window (1.5 s). Players improved in each macro-area, with the exception of defensive actions, ball control, and interception, suggesting that these situations may be more challenging to capture when designing the video content for the training. This study addresses the decision-making process, and although the videos were pre-recorded and a scoring grid was developed a priori to classify responses as optimal, acceptable, or null, this classification was not intended to reflect a normative approach, in which coaches predefine decisions for specific situations (Vaeyens et al., 2007). Given the contextual information available in each scenario, participants could choose among different possible courses of action, which were then classified through the a priori grid based on their functional effectiveness within the specific situational context. This aligns with Gigerenzer's (2006) ecological perspective on rationality, which posits that action is inherently linked to perception. For a given set of contextual cues, some responses are more functionally effective than others depending on how accurately the individual perceives and exploits the affordances available in the environment. The video stimuli were designed to preserve the ecological validity of the performance context by providing rich informational cues, such as spatial configuration, player movements, opponent positioning, and body orientation. Within this framework, decision-making emerges from the perception of affordances in the environment (Gibson, 1979). Therefore, despite the pre-recorded nature of the scenarios, participants were required to rely on these contextual cues to guide their choices toward functionally effective actions. Consistent with recent scoping reviews, our results provide further support for the potential of VR-based training to enhance decision-making in team sports (Jia et al., 2024). In our within-subjects design, improvements were observed only following the VR180 intervention (T1-T2), whereas no gains emerged during the control phase (T0-T1). This pattern differs from earlier quantitative controlled studies, which have often reported gradual improvements across both control and intervention periods, potentially reflecting ongoing sport-specific practice, learning effects, or developmental factors. In this regard, Connolly et al., (2025) highlighted the importance of employing control conditions to isolate the specific effects of VR interventions. The absence of improvement during the control phase in our study may provide compelling evidence that the observed gains were associated with the VR training itself. However, to better account for potential training effects, it should be acknowledged that our within-subjects design could not include a third active control group, as recommended by the methodological framework proposed by Gray (2019), nor did it assess onfield transfer measures.

Regarding our second hypothesis, participants showed significant improvements from T0 to T1 (control condition) in cognitive flexibility, as reflected by Working Time Part B and the Quotient B/A, as well as in inhibitory control. Additional improvements were observed from T1 to T2 (intervention phase), specifically in Working Time Part B. However, given that the Vienna Test System is a valid and reliable computerized assessment and that parallel test versions were used, these changes are more likely attributable to repeated testing effects, learning effects, rather than transfer effects resulting from the VR180 intervention. Because only a limited number of studies have examined prepost changes in cognitive outcomes following immersive perceptual-cognitive training, this research question was approached exploratorily rather than through a strong directional hypothesis. Overall, our findings align with the mixed evidence reported in the literature, where transfer effects on underlying cognitive functions remain inconclusive. For example, Fortes et al. (2021) reported significant improvements in inhibitory control, assessed via the Stroop test, in both a 360° VR training group and a 2D control group, with no significant between-group differences. Similarly, Petri et al. (2019), using the Reaction Test (RT) from the Vienna Test System, found no significant improvements in response speed following immersive training. In contrast, Fendrian et al. (2024), also using the Stroop test, reported a significant post-test between-group difference with a large effect size, suggesting a potential advantage of VR-based training over conventional approaches. Our results are also consistent with those of Heilmann et al. (2024), who examined the effects of an 8-week sportspecific cognitive training (though not in VR) on youth soccer players aged 13-15. They found no significant improvements in general executive functions in the intervention group compared to the control group, which received no intervention. Instead, both groups improved over time in cognitive flexibility, suggesting that the observed changes were due to learning or developmental effects rather than the training itself. These findings may be interpreted within a framework suggesting that standardized, abstract cognitive tests lack sensitivity to detect changes in sport-specific cognitive functions, where processing is integrated, context-dependent, and action-oriented rather than modular. This distinction reflects the difference between general executive functions and sportspecific executive functions, which are embedded in domain-relevant behaviors such as anticipation, decision-making under pressure, and multitasking under fatigue (Fransen, 2024). If a transfer occurs when virtual environments and real contexts are similar, then cognitive tests should meet the requirements of dynamic, complex sports contexts. For this reason, researchers have started investigating the possibility of assessing cognitive functions through virtual environments that could provide ecologically valid measurements (Heilmann et al., 2021; Knöbel and Lautenbach, 2023). In this context, increasing attention is also being paid to the assessment of both face and construct validity of the virtual environments (Feist et al., 2025).

Concerning user experience, we hypothesized high scores in enjoyment, embodiment, sense of presence, and acceptance of the VR system. Participants reported a mean enjoyment score of 7.8/10, suggesting that the task was appropriately tailored to their age and sufficiently challenging. Regarding body ownership, they reported a mean score of 4.9/7, indicating that the virtual body was experienced as partially belonging to them. Within the framework of embodied simulation, higher levels of embodiment are relevant as they strengthen perception-action coupling. Recognizing the virtual body as one's own leads to activating the action systems associated with the observed actions (Barsalou, 2008), thereby promoting learning. Higher levels of embodiment may be achieved in more interactive VR scenarios, where a stronger sense of agency is supported through visuomotor congruence between the observed stimuli and the user's own movements (Kilteni et al., 2012). Moreover, sense of presence within the VR environment was rated 4.7/7, indicating a good level of presence. However, when noise was present, participants became more aware of their physical location in the laboratory, creating a break in their sense of presence in the virtual environment. Using headphones in future sessions may mitigate this issue and further enhance presence. Another interesting finding was that participants perceived the technology as useful (5.1/7) and easy to use (4.9/7). They reported a positive attitude toward technology and indicated that regular use of VR as a complementary training tool could enhance their performance and productivity within their sport. Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the VR test was implemented because it is one of the most commonly used measures in literature to assess similar interventions (Panchuk et al., 2018; Petri et al., 2019; Harris et al., 2020; Romeas et al., 2022; Nasu et al., 2024). Responses were collected manually and verbal responses were numerically coded in a blinded manner. Although we controlled potential biases, future studies should aim to make the assessment more objective and minimize human intervention, for example by using automated or AI-based procedures. Furthermore, another limitation was the absence of an on-field transfer assessment. Future studies should consider using small-sided games (Fortes et al., 2021; Panchuk et al., 2018) or on-court tests (Pagé et al., 2019) to evaluate whether improvements gained in VR transfer to actual on-field performance (Gray, 2017).

Some future directions should be considered. The number of recorded videos in our study (n = 55) was not considered a limitation, as the aim was to enable athletes to recognize situations and learn the optimal actions to perform in similar scenarios. The 35 videos used in training were repeated 5 or 6 times each. However, future studies could include a larger number of videos and more stimuli, such as pressure-related cues (Shields et al., 2026). Computer-generated videos could also be considered in the future, allowing researchers to manipulate the response options more directly: if participants choose the correct action, they see the correct outcome; if they choose the wrong action, they see the wrong outcome. In this study, however, even when participants verbalized a wrong or acceptable response, they still saw themselves performing the optimal action. Building on this, a promising avenue for future research is developing adaptive VR training systems that integrate artificial intelligence. Within these systems, AI could model players' response patterns and progressively adjust task demands and feedback delivery. This adaptive, closed-loop framework could enhance individualized training and provide a more sensitive way to monitor perceptual-cognitive improvement over time. To conclude, future studies should also focus on women's soccer to address the gender data gap.

5. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that immersive VR180 video training can effectively enhance decisionmaking accuracy in youth soccer players, even when using accessible and affordable technology. These findings support the notion that the way VR is implemented may be more important than the specific technology used. No transfer effects were observed in general cognitive functions, likely due to repeated testing effects and the limited ecological validity of standardized cognitive assessments. This underscores the need for more sensitive, sport-specific evaluation tools.
