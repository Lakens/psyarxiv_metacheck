Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Successful social interactions depend on regulating approach-avoidance tendencies as well as accurately understanding those motivations in others. Direct gaze signals approach intentions, whereas averted gaze signals avoidance intentions. Older relative to younger adults display a stronger direct gaze bias (i.e., are more likely to perceive eye gaze as direct vs. averted). This bias in motivational cue perception likely impacts social trait attributions. This study, for the first time, leveraged a lifespan sample across a wide age range (18 -88 years; N = 307) to investigate adult lifespan differences in gaze perception using a novel set of gaze-modified face stimuli with varying expressions and tested whether perception of gaze aversion affected attributions of facial trustworthiness. Participants were recruited and the study conducted online via Prolific. Faces with more averted gaze were perceived as more direct-looking with age. This bias was relatively weaker for fearful faces compared to all other facial expressions. Further, greater perception of direct gaze was associated with greater facial trustworthiness attributions, and especially with age. Combined, these findings confirm a direct gaze bias in aging and support its impact on social trait attributions. These adult lifespan differences in approachavoidance motivational cue perception may reflect shifts in sensitivity, capacity, and/or motivation and are discussed as a possible mechanism in greater trust and susceptibility to deception with age. This work also adds substantially to the widely used FACES Lifespan Database corpus by making the newly gaze-modified faces, and their norm ratings, publicly available for research on motivational and cognitive aging.

Public Significance Statement: Direct gaze signals approach and averted gaze signals avoidance intentions, thus constituting crucial motivational cues. This study supports a direct gaze bias with age, which is associated with greater social trait attributions of trustworthiness to faces. These adult lifespan differences in cue perception related to approach vs. avoidance motivation could constitute one mechanism underlying greater susceptibility to deception among older adults.

Eye See Your Intentions: Age-Related Differences in Gaze Perception and

Trustworthiness Attributions

Approaching the positive and avoiding the negative represent key motivational processes that underpin sociobiological behavior (Baron-Cohen et al., 1995; Brothers & Ring, 1992; Elliot, 2006). Successful social interactions depend not only on regulating one's own approach or avoidance tendencies but also on the accurate interpretation of those intentions in others (Hietanen et al., 2018; Stins et al., 2011). Eye gaze constitutes a powerful social signal of approach-avoidance motivations (Adams & Kleck, 2005; Emery 2000; Hietanen et al., 2008).

The direction of eye gaze localizes a person's attention in the environment, i.e., as directed towards (direct gaze) or away from (averted gaze) the observer. Perception of gaze as direct (and thus approach-oriented) or averted (and thus avoidance-oriented) can modulate approachavoidance tendencies in the perceiver (Radke et al., 2014; Roelofs et al., 2010). In fact, viewing faces with direct vs. averted gaze has been shown to activate asymmetry in the frontal cortex associated with approach (left frontal activation) vs. avoidance (right frontal activation) behavior (Harmon-Jones et al., 2006; Hietanen et al., 2008). That is, gaze direction, as a motivational cue and signal of a gazer's intention, can trigger a differential physiological basis for approach vs. avoidance tendencies in an observer. However, while the accurate perception of both direct and averted gaze is important for inferring others' intentions and facilitating social interaction, evidence supports a heightened sensitivity to direct relative to averted gaze (Conty et al., 2016). This phenomenon is reflected, for example, in a bias to attribute (even subtle) gaze aversion as directed towards oneself (Boyarskaya et al., 2015; Mareschal et al., 2013). Such a "direct gaze bias" is likely an adaptive mechanism given the high value for survival, and the potential high social cost, of mislabeling someone's approach intention as avoidance (Emery, 2000). There also is a substantial processing advantage for faces with direct gaze, facilitating rapid and sustained attention capture (Breil et al., 2022; Palanica & Itier, 2012; Pittig et al., 2023), improved face recognition (Lopis et al., 2019; Vuilleumier et al., 2005), greater prosocial behavior (Wang et al., 2024), and positive social attribute judgments (Ewing et al., 2010; Kaisler & Leder, 2016; Kreysa et al., 2016). Averted gaze, in contrast, carries more ambiguity, as the object of the gazer's intention is less clear. This complexity likely underlies greater electrophysiological responses in temporooccipital brain areas to averted relative to direct gaze during early-stage face processing (Bagherzadeh-Azbari et al., 2023; Latinus et al., 2015; McCrackin & Itier, 2021; but see Tautvydaitė et al., 2022). Also, there is emerging evidence that faces with averted gaze are perceived as more dishonest (Slessor et al., 2012; Willis et al., 2011), with such negative social trait attributes linked to greater risk perception and avoidance behavior (Slepian et al., 2012).

Crucially, eye gaze can further qualify (e.g., intensify) other social signals in faces, such as emotion expression. Facial emotions constitute additional important motivational cues to infer approach (e.g., anger, happiness) vs. avoidance (e.g., fear, sadness) intentions (Adams et al., 2006; Carver & Harmon-Jones, 2009). According to the Shared Signal Hypothesis, congruency in signaling approach vs. avoidance motivation across eye gaze and facial expression facilitates face processing (Adams & Kleck, 2003 , 2005; Rigato et al., 2010). For instance, angry faces are processed more quickly (Milders et al., 2011; Pittig et al., 2023), and perceived as more angry (Ewbank et al., 2009; Sander et al., 2007) when presented with direct gaze. Similar patterns occur for other approach-oriented emotions like happiness (Bagherzadeh-Azbari et al., 2023). In turn, the processing of avoidance-oriented emotions like fear is enhanced when paired with averted gaze (Benton, 2010). In contrast, incongruent approach-avoidance cues across eye gaze and facial expression (e.g., an angry face with averted gaze) result in longer processing time, perhaps given their ambiguity (N'Diaye et al., 2009; Stussi et al., 2015). Thus, eye gaze and facial expression together communicate a social invitation, which can modulate an observer's approach or avoidance behavior (Radke et al., 2014; Roelofs et al., 2010). Willis et al. (2011) showed that the perception of approach vs. avoidance intentions via eye gaze and facial emotion shapes attributions of trustworthiness. Facial trustworthiness attributions occur rapidly (Todorov et al., 2009), often without conscious awareness (Freeman et al., 2014), yet can hold substantial impact for behavior (Slepian et al., 2012). This is likely due to their role in regulating social interactions by providing an approach-avoidance heuristic (Perez et al., 2026; Radke et al., 2018; Slepian et al., 2017; Todorov, 2008). Put simply, people instinctually approach those they perceive as trustworthy because of anticipated social gains and avoid those they perceive as untrustworthy because of potential threat. Previous work, however, has been largely limited to younger adults, with one exception. Slessor et al. (2012) showed that older adults to a lesser extent than younger adults associated direct gaze with honesty and averted gaze (both moderate and extreme gaze aversion angles) with deception.

Both age groups in their study, however, endorsed the stereotype that liars avert their gaze, suggesting a disconnect among older adults in picking up on eye gaze and their belief regarding eye gaze as a cue of deception.

In a currently largely separate literature, aging has been associated with challenges in perceiving information in the eyes. For example, older compared to younger adults showed deficits in detecting configural changes in the eyes (Slessor et al., 2013) and overall fixated less on eyes compared to lower portions of faces (e.g., the mouth; Chaby et al., 2017; Circelli et al., 2013; Firestone et al., 2007; Grainger & Henry, 2020; but see Ebner et al., 2011; Sullivan et al., 2007). Meta-analytic evidence further supports age-related reduction in gaze-cueing (i.e., the ability to reorient attention in line with the gaze of others; McKay et al., 2022). Thus, one possible explanation for age-related differences in the use of gaze as motivational cue could be that older adults distinguish less between direct vs. averted gaze, and perhaps especially so for more subtle degrees of gaze aversion. Indeed, Slessor and colleagues (2008) showed that older adults differentiated subtle gaze aversion even less from direct gaze than younger adults. Additional (or alternative) to an ability account, there is evidence of age-related selective desensitization to eye gaze when faces display negative (i.e., angry) compared to positive (i.e., happy/joyful) expressions (Slessor et al., 2010; Ziaei et al., 2016). That is, older, unlike younger, adults did not show differentiation in brain response or behavior between direct vs. averted gaze when paired with a negative emotion. Thus, older adults may be particularly sensitive to direct gaze when it is paired with positive, but not negative, emotions; this strong signal of approach intention may then particularly impact their social trait attributions to trust and approach the gazer. However, a direct link between the perception of gaze as direct vs. averted and social trait attributions of trustworthiness has not yet been established in a sample that covers across the adult lifespan, nor regarding an interplay of these processes with facial expression as an effect-qualifying (and maybe effect-intensifying) variable.

Purpose of the Study

This is the first study in a sample that covers a wide age range (18-88 years) to systematically examine adult lifespan differences in the perception of gaze and facial expression as motivational cues and its impact on facial trustworthiness attributions. For this work, we modified the gaze of face stimuli from the FACES Lifespan Database (Ebner et al., 2010), which also systematically vary in age and facial expressions, along three aversion angles, contributing a novel stimulus dimension to the existing and widely used corpus.

To first confirm the effectiveness of our eye gaze manipulation, we tested for a linear relationship between gaze aversion angle of the faces and participants' perception of gaze aversion (prediction of a main effect of gaze aversion angle on perceived gaze aversion across all participants). Next, based on initial findings of differences between younger and older adults in their perception of gaze aversion and evidence of a bias in older adults to mislabel subtle gaze aversions as direct (i.e., direct gaze bias; Hietanen et al., 2022; Slessor et al., 2008), we hypothesized that the association between gaze aversion angle and perception of averted gaze would be less pronounced with age (prediction of an interaction between gaze aversion angle and participant age on perceived gaze aversion).

As noted, facial expressions can interact with gaze direction on signaling approachavoidance intentions (Shared Signal Hypothesis; Adams & Kleck, 2003), with currently limited understanding of this interplay across adulthood and in aging (Slessor et al., 2010; Ziaei et al., 2016). Given the potential of facial emotion in qualifying the social invitation communicated by eye gaze, further characterization of adult lifespan differences in gaze perception across approach and avoidance-oriented emotions is warranted. Accordingly, this study determined the extent to which facial expressions moderated the interplay between gaze aversion angle and participant age on the perception of gaze aversion (prediction of an interaction between gaze aversion angle, participant age, and facial expression on perceived gaze aversion).

Finally, to capture adult lifespan differences in the impact of gaze perception on trustworthiness attributions, we tested whether perceiving less averted gaze (i.e., more direct gaze perception) would be associated with greater attributions of facial trustworthiness (prediction of a main effect of perceived gaze aversion on trustworthiness attributions) and whether this association would be stronger with age (prediction of an interaction between perceived gaze aversion and participant age on trustworthiness attributions). Again, we examined moderation by facial expressions on these effects, based on evidence that facial trustworthiness evaluations have been shown to vary across facial expressions in faces with direct gaze (Pehlivanoglu et al., 2023; prediction of an interaction between perceived gaze aversion, participant age, and facial expression on trustworthiness attributions).

This study recruited 324 participants spanning the adult lifespan from 18-88 years of age, with approximately equal numbers of women and men in each decade. Participants were eligible for the study if they (a) had a proportion of approved Prolific tasks of greater than 70% and were (b) aged 18-90 years, (c) a current U.S. resident living in the U.S., (d) able to understand written English, (e) able to see information on a computer screen, and (f) able and willing to complete the study on a laptop or desktop computer.

Study Procedure

All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Florida (Protocol #: ET00043371). The task was built in Qualtrics and launched remotely on the Prolific platform. Full study completion consisted of four 1-hour sessions per participant, each on a separate day over the course of two weeks (see Supplemental Materials for analysis pertaining to age differences in the number of days elapsed between sessions).

At the start of the study, participants signed an electronic consent form. For each session they then provided their unique Prolific ID and confirmed they were completing the task on a laptop or desktop computer. A brief demographic questionnaire was administered at the beginning of the first session. Participants then rated a set of faces on gaze aversion in the first two sessions, followed by facial trustworthiness ratings on the same set of faces in the last two sessions (for details see Face Rating Task below).

After the main task in each session, participants completed one of four brief and the General Trust Inventory (Yamagishi & Yamagishi, 1994), respectively. At the end of each session, participants also answered the attention-check question, reported their screen size/resolution, and could indicate any issues with the session. After completion of all four sessions or the two-week study period, whichever came first, participants were compensated $10 per completed session.

Face Rating Task

Stimuli. Face stimuli were taken from the FACES Lifespan Database, a collection of high-quality colored face images, consisting of 58 younger (29 women; 19-31 years), 56 middle-aged (27 women; 39-55 years), and 57 older (29 women; 69-80 years) individuals displaying each of six prototypical expressions (neutrality, happiness, sadness, fear, anger, and disgust), all with direct gaze. The FACES images had been created using a standardized procedure that included emotion induction to elicit naturalistic facial expressions and were subsequently validated to ensure widely recognized representations of each expression (Ebner et al., 2010). The database includes two copies of each face image, resulting in two parallel sets (i.e., Set A and Set B) with 1,026 images in each. We selected one copy of each face image from either Set A or Set B for gaze modification, based on which image had the more clearly visible, unobstructed eye region. Disgusted faces were not included for gaze modification because the eyes were not visible in most images. Additional face images of the other five facial expressions were excluded from the gaze modification if the iris or sclera were not visible, facial structures occluded the eyes (e.g., droopy eyelids), and/or the face was cross-eyed. Data Analysis

Cross-classified multilevel models were run to account for the nested data structure (i.e., trials were nested within participants and face identities, simultaneously). This approach permitted the modeling of both face-and participant-level variance (for a similar approach, see Pehlivanoglu et al., 2023; Voelkle et al., 2012).

A first model considered main effects and interactions of gaze aversion angle (continuous; 0 -3, higher values indicated greater gaze aversion angle), participant age (continuous; 18 -88 years), and facial expression (categorical; neutral, happy, sad, fearful, angry) on perceived gaze aversion (continuous; 0 -1). Participant age was centered at the grand mean (M = 51.33 years) to allow for interpretation of model intercepts and interaction terms. Perceived gaze aversion was computed for each face as |raw score -50| / 50. This transformation anchored scores at the midpoint of the response scale (from 0 to 1), independent of left vs. right gaze direction (as not relevant to testing our predictions regarding perceived gaze aversion and in line with prior work; Slessor et al., 2008). A perceived gaze aversion score of 0 indicated perception of a perfectly direct gaze, while greater perceived gaze aversion scores reflected greater deviation from the perception of a perfectly direct gaze. That is, greater gaze aversion was equivalent to less perceived direct gaze. A significant positive main effect of gaze aversion angle in this first model would support effectiveness of our gaze manipulation. A significant gaze aversion angle x participant age interaction would support our prediction that the association between gaze aversion angle and perceived gaze aversion would be reduced with older age. Further, a significant gaze aversion angle x participant age x facial expression interaction would support our expectation facial expression would modulate the interplay between gaze aversion angle and participant age on the perception of gaze aversion.

A second model considered main effects and interactions of perceived gaze aversion (continuous; 0 -1; see description above), participant age (continuous; 18 -88 years), and facial expression (categorical; neutral, happy, sad, fearful, angry) on trustworthiness attribution (continuous: 0 -100; with higher scores indicating greater trustworthiness attribution). Again, participant age was centered at the grand mean. Further, perceived gaze aversion in this model was centered within each participant to separate within-person effects from between-person differences in baseline response levels (Enders & Tofighi, 2007). A significant negative main effect of perceived gaze aversion would support our prediction that less perception of gaze aversion (i.e., more direct gaze perception) was associated with greater attribution of facial trustworthiness. A significant perceived gaze aversion x participant age interaction would qualify this effect, supporting our prediction that particularly with older age less perception of gaze aversion (i.e., more direct gaze perception) would be associated with greater attribution of facial trustworthiness. A significant perceived gaze aversion x participant age x facial expression interaction would support our expectation facial expression would modulate the interplay between perceived gaze aversion and participant age on trustworthiness attribution.

Both models reported here included sex of participant (0 = male,1 = female), face age (0 = young,1 = middle-aged,2 = older), face sex (0 = male,1 = female), face set (0 = A,1 = B), task version (1-5), and version order (0 = original,1 = reversed) as covariates. The second model also included gaze aversion angle as an additional covariate. Both models were run with and without race of participant (0 = White,1 = Black,2 = Asian,3 = mixed,4 = other), education attainment (0= high school,2 = undergraduate,3 = graduate), subjective physical health (continuous: 1-5), subjective vision (continuous: 1 -5), and general trust scores (continuous: 1-5) as covariates, since these variables differed by age in our sample (see Table 1). 2 Maximum likelihood was applied for estimations of model parameters. We conducted Wald tests to assess significance of model effects and conducted follow-up z tests for pairwise comparisons to interpret significant interactions, with Bonferroni correction applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Analyses were performed in Stata (v.18.0) and figures plotted in R/Rstudio (4.5.1) using the ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016), dplyr (Wickham et al., 2026), and patchwork (Pedersen, 2025) packages.

Discussion

Eye gaze and facial emotions provide important social signals of approach-avoidance intentions of others, and the correct interpretation of these motivational cues can impact our social interactions (Adams & Kleck, 2003 , 2005; Emery, 2000). Here, we leveraged data collected in an adult lifespan sample across a wide age range on a new set of gaze-modified faces, that systematically varied along three gaze aversion angles and five facial expressions.

Using these newly-developed stimuli, we comprehensively (i) characterized adult lifespan differences in gaze perception; (ii) delineated the impact of perceived gaze aversion on social trait attribution of trustworthiness; and (iii) validated and share this novel stimulus material for use in research.

The study generated several novel findings and is making important research contributions. First, we found support for a stronger direct gaze bias with age, reflected by our finding that with older age the association between greater gaze aversion angle and greater perception of gaze aversion was less pronounced. This bias, while observed for all facial expressions, was weaker for fearful faces. Also, for all participants, avoidance-oriented facial emotion expressions (i.e., fear and sadness) were perceived as more averted-looking, whereas approach-oriented facial emotion expressions (i.e., happiness and anger) were perceived as more direct-looking. Second, regarding the impact of gaze aversion perception on trustworthiness attributions, we observed that greater direct gaze perception was associated with greater attributions of facial trustworthiness. This effect again was particularly pronounced with age, and it was present across all facial expressions, but particularly strong for happy (and neutral) faces.

Together, this work highlights the importance of eye gaze, in its interplay with facial emotions, in signaling approach-avoidance intentions and communicating a social invitation.

Our results directly speak to adult lifespan differences in the perception of these motivational cues that can trigger approach-avoidance tendencies in the observer and facilitate social interaction. Next, we discuss in more detail the main findings from this study, also in light of factors contributing to greater trust and susceptibility to deception with age as well as with regard to age-related shifts in sensitivity to detecting, capacity in accurately reading, and/or motivation to process social signals in faces.

Age-Related Direct Gaze Bias

In support of an age-related direct gaze bias, we found that the association between greater gaze aversion angle and more perception of gaze aversion was less pronounced with age. Averted gaze conveys a heightened sense of ambiguity because the object of the gazer's intention is unclear, relative to direct gaze with clear relevance to the observer. Our results suggest that under this ambiguity, older adults in particular default to perceiving gaze aversion as directed more toward themselves. Theories like the Socioemotional Selectivity Theory (Carstensen, 1992; Carstensen et al., 1999) and empirical evidence supporting it (Kennedy & Mather, 2025; Reed et al., 2014) propose a motivational shift with age towards enhanced salience of positive, while reduced sensitivity to negative, information. Although greater perception of direct gaze as an approach-motivational cue is not equivalent to the positivity effect, as direct gaze can occur in a positive or negative context, the two phenomena may reflect a related tendency for older adults to resolve ambiguous social information in ways that are consistent with their motivational priorities. Indeed, the positivity effect in aging tends to be stronger when processing ambiguous cues (Barber et al., 2022; Dave et al., 2025; Kellough & Knight, 2012; Petro et al., 2021). Similarly, older adults may resolve ambiguous intentions such as reflected in averted gaze by perceiving them as less ambiguous but in fact more direct-gazing and self-relevant, aligning with their shifting goals to prioritize social goals and prosocial behaviors (Isaacowitz et al., 2021; Mayr & Freund, 2020).

An age-related direct gaze bias can also be interpreted within the Social Cognitive Resource (SCoRe) framework (Henry et al., 2023). This account proposes that both motivational influences and constraints on capacity give rise to age-related differences in social cognition (i.e., social perception, affective empathy, theory of mind, and social behavior), impacting older adults' social interactions and overall wellbeing. The age-related direct gaze bias may in fact be the result of a combination of adult lifespan shifts in what older adults want to attend to ( motivation) and what they can effectively detect or process (capacity). Regarding a motivational shift, prior research has demonstrated an age-related tendency to prioritize selfrelevant information (Cassidy & Gutchess, 2012; Leshikar et al., 2015), perhaps in an effort to conserve limited cognitive resources (Hess, 2014). At the same time, constraints on capacity, such as age-related deficits in contrast sensitivity and reduced configural face processing, could contribute to age-related differences in gaze perception (Murray et al., 2010; Owsley, 2016; Slessor et al., 2013). Supporting a role of decline in cognitive capacity, McKay and colleagues (2022) found that older adults particularly struggled with gaze-cued attention when gaze was predictive of target locations, necessitating deliberate rather than reflexive attentional orienting.

The current study cannot disentangle these two possible accounts of motivation vs. capacity, but future work will be able to do so, by e.g., manipulating self-relevance vs. cognitive effort of eye gaze perception. Also, eye-tracking or gaze-cued attention paradigms can be leveraged with our newly gaze-modified stimuli, to supplement self-report with objective behavioral data, in determining the extent to which age differences in gaze perception are driven by changes in attentional processes with age (Grainger & Henry, 2020; McKay et al., 2022).

Facial Emotion Modulates Gaze Perception and the Age-Related Direct Gaze Bias

As predicted, we found that facial expressions further modulated the effect of gaze aversion angle on perceived gaze. In particular, in line with the Shared Signal Hypothesis (Adams & Kleck, 2003; Ewbank et al., 2009; Lobmaier et al., 2008), participants perceived relatively greater gaze aversion in faces with avoidance-oriented expressions (i.e., fear) and relatively greater direct gaze in faces with approach-oriented expressions (i.e., happy). And, while the age-related direct gaze bias was present in all facial expressions, it was the least pronounced in fearful faces. One possibility is that gaze aversion can be more readily detected in fearful faces because averted gaze and fear together signal avoidance motivation. This congruency across gaze and facial expression facilitates the processing of these motivational cues in the faces (Adams & Kleck 2005; Benton et al., 2010; Rigato et al., 2010), and particularly so with older age as our results suggest. Alternatively, fearful faces are typically characterized by wide-open eyes, perhaps making it easier for older adults to differentiate eye gaze directions and different degrees of gaze aversions in fearful faces.

Greater Direct Gaze Perception is Associated with Greater Trustworthiness Attributions with Age

Greater direct gaze perception (i.e., less perception of gaze aversion) was associated with greater attribution of trustworthiness to the faces. This finding aligns with past work in younger adults showing that faces with direct relative to averted gaze are evaluated as more trustworthy (Kaisler & Leder, 2016; Kresya et al., 2016; Willis et al., 2011). This effect was particularly pronounced with older age, demonstrating it for the first time across the adult lifespan. That is, older adults appear to particularly rely on their perception of eye gaze as a motivational cue signaling approach-avoidance intention when attributing trustworthiness to others.

Given the present study design, it cannot be ruled out that previous exposure to the faces impacted trustworthiness attributions. While the fixed task order (i.e., all participants completed the gaze ratings prior to the trustworthiness ratings) was intentionally designed as a sequence control to minimize between-participant variability in this study, future studies will be able to determine the extent to which familiarity contributed to facial trustworthiness attributions.

It is possible that these age-related differences in eye gaze perception represent one factor contributing to enhanced trust among older adults (Bailey & Leon, 2019; Cassidy et al., 2019; Castle et al., 2012). The decision to trust or distrust others relies on the evaluation of choice-relevant social signals, which involve inferring the intention of others, and leads to value calculations for decision options (Frazier et al., 2019). Older adults experience challenges in trust-related decision making, which may contribute to their greater risk of susceptibility to deception (Calso et al., 2020; Ebner et al., 2023; Ruffman et al., 2012). Given the importance of direct and averted gaze in communicating motivational intent, age-related differences in eye gaze perception may represent a mechanism for deficits in detecting the intentions of others; with impact on social trait attributions, as shown here, and subsequent trust-related decision making (Henry et al., 2023; Itier & Batty, 2009; Stephenson et al., 2021).

Future research could directly test this proposed link to trust-related decision making in aging, such as in deceptive contexts where a social partner conceals their true intentions. Extending beyond static face images, a critical next step for this line of research will involve characterizing adult lifespan differences in gaze perception as a dynamic process in real-life face-to-face interactions (e.g., via novel dual eye tracking; Mayrand et al., 2023), as well as its direct impact on attributions of trustworthiness as a process that unfolds over the course of a social interaction. Such studies will inform adult lifespan differences in gaze perception and effects of gaze perception on social interactions in naturalistic contexts.

Facial Expression and Perceived Gaze Interplay in Trustworthiness Attributions

The effect that greater direct gaze perception was associated with greater trustworthiness attributions held for all facial expressions, suggesting a pervasive "direct gaze trustworthiness advantage" across expressions and participants. This finding contrasts with Willis and colleagues (2011) who, in their study with younger adults only, reported that angry faces with direct (compared to averted) gaze were rated as less trustworthy. Differences between the two studies could be due to stimulus characteristics (e.g., color images of naturalistic faces across a wide age with fine-grained gaze aversion angles in our study vs.

black-and-white face images of younger adults only with extreme gaze aversion in Willis et al.).

However, speaking to the interplay of facial expressions and perceived gaze in trustworthiness attributions, the positive association between direct gaze perception and trustworthiness attributions observed in our study was strongest for happy (and neutral) faces. In line with the Shared Signal Hypothesis, these two motivational cues combined communicate a highly selfrelevant social invitation for positive interaction (Breil et al., 2022).

Validation and Sharing of Newly Gaze-Modified Face Stimuli

A key methodological contribution of this work is the novel set of systematically gazemodified faces (displaying systematically varied subtle, moderate, and extreme gaze aversion angles). Our results confirm the effectiveness of these eye gaze manipulations, as greater gaze aversion angle was associated with greater perception of averted gaze. These novel stimuli have the potential to stimulate research on motivational and cognitive aging by overcoming some limitations of past work that used artificial face stimuli (i.e., grayscale, external features removed; Slessor et al., 2008), direct vs. extreme gaze aversions only (Willis et al., 2011), or lacked systematic variation of facial expressions beyond happiness and anger (Slessor et al., 2010). Both the gaze-modified stimuli and the picture-specific normative data (i.e., gaze and trustworthiness ratings) are publicly available to the scientific community and enrich the existing FACES Lifespan Database corpus (Ebner et al., 2010 (Ebner et al., , 2018;; Pehlivanoglu et al., 2023).

Conclusions

We observed a direct gaze bias with age and found that gaze perception, also in interplay with facial expressions, impacts social trait attributions of trustworthiness. These adult lifespan differences in approach-avoidance motivational cue perception may reflect shifts in sensitivity, capacity, and/or motivation and could constitute one mechanism underlying greater susceptibility to deception among older adults. In addition to advancing perceptual-motivational theory in aging, this work extends the FACES Lifespan Database corpus by validating and publicly sharing newly gaze-modified face stimuli for future research on motivation and cognitive aging.
