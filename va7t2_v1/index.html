Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. Abstract

Background: Exposure to natural environments has been associated with positive affective responses and perceived restorativeness. However, these experiences have primarily been investigated in hearing populations, and little is known about deaf adults' affective and restorative experiences in relation to natural environments.

Purpose: We examined whether deaf and hearing adults differed in post-walk positive and negative affect, perceived restorativeness, and nature relatedness. We also explored the sensory pathways associated with their affective and restorative experiences.

Methodology: We conducted a mixed-methods study with 15 deaf and 14 hearing adults who completed an outdoor nature walk in a national forest park in Cyprus. Participants completed the Positive and Negative Affect Scale, the Perceived Restorativeness Scale, and the Nature Relatedness Scale, alongside semi-structured interviews. Hierarchical multiple regressions examined group associations with the quantitative outcomes after adjusting for relevant covariates. Deductive content analysis then explored affective and restorative experiences and their associated sensory pathways.

Results: Deaf participants reported significantly higher post-walk positive affect than hearing participants (B = -3.82 for hearing relative to deaf group, 95% Confidence Interval [-6.89, -0.76], p = .017). No significant group associations were identified for negative affect, perceived restorativeness, or nature relatedness. Qualitative findings identified both high and low arousal positive affect experiences, alongside restorative experiences such as fascination and the feeling of being away. These experiences were linked to various sensory pathways in both groups, with visual pathways frequently described and auditory, tactile, and bodily/thermal experiences also contributing to participants' accounts.

Conclusion: Our study provides preliminary evidence of differences in post-walk positive affect between deaf and hearing adults while highlighting the diverse sensory experiences linked to affect and perceived restorativeness in natural environments. Larger studies are needed to clarify these findings and investigate the role of sensory experiences in nature restoration.

1. Introduction

Contact with natural environments have been shown to promote restoration and stress relief (Berto, 2014), at least in hearing populations. Two dominant theories in the field of nature restoration have been proposed to explain these effects. Attention Restoration Theory (ART) suggests that natural environments can support the recovery of directed attentional resources by engaging attention effortlessly (Kaplan, 1995). According to ART, restorative environments are characterised by four dimensions: fascination, being away, extent, and compatibility. Soft fascination allows opportunities for reflection without requiring sustained attentional effort. Being away refers to a sense of psychological distance from everyday demands. Extent refers to the perception of an environment as a coherent and rich setting that can engage the mind. It encompasses coherence (the sense that environmental elements form a connected and meaningful whole) and scope (the sense that an environment offers sufficient richness to engage the mind). Finally, compatibility refers to the extent to which the environment supports an individual's purposes and inclinations (Kaplan, 1995). In contrast, Stress Restoration Theory (SRT) proposes that exposure to natural environments evokes rapid automatic positive affect responses that facilitate recovery from stress (Ulrich, 1983; Ulrich et al., 1991).

The combination of both visual and auditory cues when experiencing nature scenes leads to enhanced immersion, better restorative effects (Annerstedt et al., 2013), and perceived restorativeness (Qiu et al., 2021) compared to only visual exposure to similar scenes. Qualitative evidence show that bird sounds can support perceived stress recovery through positive, low arousal affective responses and perceived attention restoration by providing effortless attention consistent with fascination and a sense of being away (Ratcliffe et al., 2013). While the role of nature soundscapes has been highlighted in the above work, the nature experiences of deaf people and people with hearing impairment have only recently started to be explored. In tourism research, visitors with hearing impairments have been shown to exhibit a different sensory profile compared to hearing peers when experiencing a natural environment (Chan et al., 2025). Another comparison showed that visitors with hearing impairment had stronger reported tactile experiences in a natural environment than hearing visitors, but no significant differences in other modalities (Chan et al., 2022a).

Furthermore, a growing work in human auditory ecology shows that acquired sensorineural hearing loss can change how individuals perceive and experience nature sounds. Miller-Viacava et al., 2024 showed that people with sensorineural hearing loss exhibit a lower ability to discriminate information within natural soundscapes. However, they did not measure affect, perceived restorativeness or wellbeing. In a secondary analysis from data collected in a previous trial (Lelic, Herrlin, et al., 2025), the authors found that when people with sensorineural hearing loss were prompted in an app-based journaling exercise to reflect on positive experiences with their recently fitted Hearing Aids (HAs), they mentioned that they enjoyed hearing nature with their HAs (Lelic, Picou, et al., 2025). Nature sounds accounted for 18% of the positive experiences they noted in their journal. Participants reported that rediscovering natural sounds was a positive and relaxing experience that contributed to their perceived quality of life. They also described noticing much richer natural soundscapes and nature sounds they had not realised were absent since their acquired hearing loss (Lelic, Picou, et al., 2025). The findings from Lelic, Picou, et al. (2025) were also attested in another study where hearing care professionals were asked about what their patients reported before and after receiving hearing aids (Apoux et al., 2025). They reported that the proportion of their patients hearing nature sounds increased from 7% before HA fitting to 45% after fitting. This was more pronounced for people living in rural areas. An increase in accuracy, pleasantness and importance of natural sounds was also noted, although reports of some unpleasant natural sounds increased, as well. In addition, the majority of respondents said that their patients found it important to listen to natural sounds and that they were satisfied with their HAs in listening to natural sounds. Other work suggests that although hearing loss can alter certain aspects of environmental sound perception, nature and animal sounds remain among the most pleasant sound categories for listeners both with and without hearing loss (Jorgensen et al., 2026). As a whole, these studies demonstrate that hearing loss changes auditory access to natural environments, that loss of information may not always be recognised, and that restored auditory access to nature sounds appears meaningful for hearing aid users. The studies, though, do not test whether hearing loss changes affect and perceived restorativeness of nature environments. They also specifically focus on people with acquired sensorineural hearing loss within an audiological rehabilitation framework. The experiences of people with profound deafness from early on in life is very different from people losing their hearing gradually later in life. To the best of our knowledge, this former group of deaf people has not been studied in previous work in terms of affective experiences and perceived restorativeness. Our work is the first that looks into how affective experiences (positive and negative) and perceived restorativeness compare between deaf and hearing people.

Our study aimed to examine whether deaf and hearing people had similar affective responses and perceived restorativeness following a nature walk in an outdoor natural environment. Drawing on evidence that people with hearing loss can have multisensory experiences in natural environments despite experiencing these environments differently from hearing peers (Chan et al., 2022a (Chan et al., , 2025)), we expected no systematic group differences in post-walk positive or negative affect or in perceived restorativeness between deaf and hearing participants. A secondary aim was to qualitatively explore through which sensory pathways, if any, participants experienced affect and perceived restorativeness. Finally, we examined whether deaf and hearing participants differed in terms of nature relatedness. Based on evidence that people with hearing loss may experience natural environments through a different sensory composition (Chan et al., 2022b (Chan et al., , 2022a (Chan et al., , 2025)), we expected no group differences, as connection with nature may be reached through different forms of engagement.

We tested these research questions in an outdoor natural environment, as previous research suggests that direct exposure to natural environments (e.g., walking, physical engagement) produces more consistent psychological benefits, in line with ART and SRT (Daube et al., 2026).

2. Methodology

>Methodology

Participants

We aimed to recruit participants 18 and 60 years old, with self-reported normal or corrected-tonormal eyesight, no colour blindness, no known history of stroke or brain injury, and no use of cochlear implants. We chose an upper age limit of 60 to limit potential heterogeneity associated with age-related differences in attentional functioning, which may be relevant to restorative experiences (Cassarino et al., 2019). Deaf participants were identified primarily through the Deaf Community in Cyprus, and they had to have self-reported severe or profound hearing loss. Participants in the hearing group needed to have 25 dB Hearing Level (HL) or below in a hearing test.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.2 for hierarchical multiple regression (fixed model, R² increase), with α = .05, power = .80, one tested predictor, and five total predictors. An effect size f² = .22 was assumed, informed by previous research examining positive affect following nature exposure (Browning et al., 2020). The analysis indicated a required sample of 38 participants (19 per group). Due to recruitment difficulties and time constraints, we recruited 31 participants using a combination of convenience and snowballing sampling. Two participants did not meet the inclusion criteria; a participant intended for the deaf group was a cochlear implant user and another participant intended for the hearing group had mild hearing loss (33 dB HL in the better ear). This resulted in a final sample size of 29 participants, 15 in the deaf group, and 14 in the hearing group.

We matched all participants for gender and age (+/-5 years) except three; two males in the deaf group (57 and 60 years old) and one female in the hearing group aged 32. The 15 deaf participants ages ranged from 27 to 60, M = 46.73, Standard Deviation (SD) = 11, whereas the 14 hearing participants ranged in age from 32 to 59, M = 43.79, SD = 10.12. In the deaf group, nine participants identified as females (60%) and six as males, whereas in the hearing group, ten identified as females (71.43%) and four as males.

Design and procedure

The study received ethical approval from the Cyprus National Bioethics Committee (ΕΕΒΚ ΕΠ 2025 45). The study employed a mixed design of quantitative and qualitative research methods. A cross-sectional study (between-within subjects factor) design was used for the quantitative part of the project, whereas the sections from the semi-structured interviews that discussed the specific nature walk were used for content analysis described later in detail. The test visit took place at Athalassa National Forest Park in Nicosia, Cyprus. We were flexible with scheduling the test visits to accommodate for unforeseen events and weather conditions. A recyclable bottle of mineral water was provided to participants, while the national park has serviced WC and a canteen. The data collection took place in a relatively quiet area of the park with wooden benches and tables.

Participants first signed consent forms. They then completed the hearing test and demographic questions. After this, participants completed the test session outlined in Figure 1 below. No prior fatiguing or stress induction was conducted, to make the condition as close as possible to an everyday life setting (Korpilo et al., 2024). After completion of the test session, participants received a gift card of €20 for a supermarket. Note: NBS: Natural Beauty Scale; NRS: Nature Relatedness Scale; PANAS: Positive and Negative Affect Schedule; PRS: Perceived Restorativeness Scale; SSQ: Speech, Spatial, and Qualities of Hearing Scale.

Screening tests and demographics

Given the outdoor nature of the test session, limited resources, and restricted access to an audiometer, hearing participants were screened outdoors using the Mimi Hearing Test, an appbased hearing screening tool. Validation research for the test demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for identifying hearing loss (Yesantharao et al., 2022), while more recent work has reported generally comparable pure-tone averages between the Mimi Hearing Test and conventional audiometry, particularly among individuals with normal to moderate hearing loss (Prendergast et al., 2026). However, accuracy varies across studies and individual frequencies, with poorer agreement reported at lower frequencies (Mui et al., 2025). We used the Mimi Hearing Test as a screening tool based on its convenience. We clarified to participants that this screening test does not substitute clinical audiometry.

Participants first completed the practice session provided in the app before proceeding to the test. We used the Mimi Hearing Test app version 6.9.0 by Mimi Hearing Technologies, on an iPad 11" A16, on iOS 26.2. The hearing test was delivered via a Sony WH-1000XM4 Wireless over-theear headphones, a headphones model calibrated in the Mimi repository, connected to the iPad via bluetooth. All participants used the same device, same app version, and same set of headphones to complete this hearing test. Screening took place in a quiet area of the park and when loud noise would come up the test was paused. As screening was conducted outdoors, the active noise cancellation function of the headphones was enabled to reduce ambient noise during testing. This decision was informed by previous research demonstrating that active noise cancellation can improve hearing screening performance in the presence of background noise (Cheng et al., 2023). This setting was maintained for all tested participants. We did not measure the sound level of the ambient noise during screening.

Based on the earlier World Health Organisation classifications of hearing, typical hearing was considered when the level was 25 dB HL or lower when calculated across the four frequencies of 500Hz, 1KHz, 2KHz, and 4KHz, in the better ear (Olusanya et al., 2019). Fifteen hearing participants were screened, of whom 14 met this criterion. One participant was excluded from the analyses because their average was 33 dB HL in the better ear. Among the 14 retained participants, their mean thresholds were 13.71 dB HL (SD = 4.59) for the left ear and 13.93 dB HL (SD = 6.36) for the right ear.

The five-item Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing Scale (SSQ5), derived from the original 49-item SSQ (Gatehouse & Noble, 2004), was additionally administered to obtain complementary self-report information on the abilities of participants in the hearing group (Demeester et al., 2012). A Greek translation of the English SSQ5 was used for this study. (Assef et al., 2022) identified a mean SSQ5 score of 7.3 as the optimal cutoff for screening hearing loss in a Brazilian Portuguesespeaking sample, with sensitivity and specificity of 81.8% and 78.3%, respectively. Although this cutoff has not been validated for the Greek translation used in our study, all 14 hearing participants scored above 7.3, with mean SSQ5 scores ranging from 7.4 to 10 (M = 8.56, SD = 0.75).

Chronbach's alpha in the hearing sample was .45.

Deaf participants were recruited primarily through the Deaf community in Cyprus. We did not administer the hearing test to deaf participants to minimise potential discomfort or distress associated with hearing assessment (Hulme et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2024). Instead, participants in both groups were asked to self-report their hearing status, selecting one of the following categories: no hearing loss, mild hearing loss, moderate hearing loss, severe hearing loss, profound hearing loss, or not sure. Deaf participants additionally reported their age at hearing loss diagnosis and their preferred language of communication between Cypriot Sign Language (CySL), Greek, and Other. Participants who used HAs were also asked about the duration and frequency of HA use.

In the deaf group, 14 participants self-reported having profound hearing loss, while one reported severe hearing loss. Nine deaf participants reported age of hearing loss onset under 12 months, five between 1 and 2 years old, and one participant around 5-6 years of age. Six preferred CySL as their primary language of communication, seven preferred both CySL and Greek, one preferred Greek, and one English. Ten participants did not use HAs, while five of them have reported using their HA daily, most of the hours. For all deaf participants who required CySL interpreting (n = 11), a certified CySL interpreter was assigned. Participants had the choice to choose an interpreter they wanted and ultimately, there were four CySL interpreters who facilitated the test procedure. One interpreter provided her services for six participants, two interpreters provided services for two participants each, and the fourth interpreter provided services for one participant. The rest of the four deaf participants did not require a CySL interpreter.

The demographic questions collected information on gender, age, ethnicity, mother tongue, level of Greek language, level of CySL, education level, self-reported visual acuity, and colourblindess. Other questions related to nature asked whether participants live close to nature (out in the country, forest, park) and the frequency of visiting a natural environment in the last year.

Outcome measures

We used the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; (Watson et al., 1988) that has been used in previous similar research (Browning et al., 2020). This questionnaire has been validated in Greek (Daskalou & Sygkollitou, 2012) and it assesses positive and negative affect as measured by a 20-item scale. Ten questions are used for assessing Positive Affect (PA) high arousal and 10 for assessing Negative Affect (NA). Item responses range from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). We summed the items from each subscale, with a higher PA score indicating improvement in mood and a higher NA score indicating greater levels of distress. In the present sample, Cronbach's alpha was .92 for PA at both baseline and post-walk and .62 and .79 for NA baseline and post-walk, respectively.

To assess perceived restorativeness, we used the 11-item short form of the Perceived Restorativeness Scale (PRS-11), developed from the original PRS (Hartig et al., 1997). Based on ART, the PRS assesses perceived environmental qualities associated with psychological restoration. The PRS-11 was developed to provide a shorter measure with improved psychometric properties (Pasini et al., 2014). Item responses are in a 7-point Likert scale ranging from Completely Disagree to Completely Agree. The PRS-11 has four subscales: Fascination, Being away, Coherence, and Scope. Responses to the 11 items were summed to calculate the PRS total score, with higher score indicating greater perceived restorativeness. Scores for the four individual subscales were also calculated and analysed in post-hoc exploratory analyses. We translated the questionnaire in Greek from the English PRS-11. Cronbach's alpha in our sample was .85.

To measure nature relatedness, we administered the Nature Relatedness Scale (NRS; (Nisbet et al., 2008). We used the NR-6 short form, which consists of six items and assesses the degree participants feel connected to nature (Nisbet & Zelenski, 2013). It was preferred over the use of other nature connectedness questionnaires, such as the Connectedness to Nature Scale (Mayer & Frantz, 2004) due to its short form. The scale of the NRS was adjusted from 5-point to 7-point to make it more sensitive, as suggested by previous research (Cox et al., 2017), with item responses ranging from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree. The mean score of all six items was calculated with higher mean NRS score indicating higher connection to nature. We translated the questionnaire in Greek from the English NRS. Chronbach's alpha was .65.

The Natural Beauty Subscale (NBS) of the Engagement with Beauty Scale (Diessner et al., 2008) was included as a covariate in our models, given previous findings that the NBS was a significant positive predictor of positive affect and perceived restorativeness following nature exposure (Browning et al., 2020). The NBS assesses engagement with natural beauty through participants' tendency to perceive and respond to beauty in nature through cognitive, physiological, emotional, and spiritual experiences. The subscale's four items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very unlike me) to 7 (very much like me). Responses were summed to create an NBS total score with higher scores indicating greater engagement with natural beauty. We translated the subscale in Greek from the original English NBS. Chronbach's alpha was .73 in our sample.

Nature walk

The test sessions took place between late January and mid-July 2026. The sessions and walk route were scheduled during daylight hours, from morning to afternoon, depending on season. The planned route was approximately 1.5 km long and followed a linear, out-and-back path, with participants returning to the starting point along the same route. The walk for deaf participants ranged from 10 to 32 minutes, M = 23.27, SD = 5.60, while for the hearing peers from 13 to 28 minutes, M = 19.50, SD = 3.57. As shown in the photographs of Figure 2, sections of the path passed through trees and led towards a wooden observatory. Participants were asked to follow the path but were allowed to deviate to observe anything of interest, provided they then return to the path. They were asked not to use their phones or talk to other people along the route unless it was an emergency. We also recorded actual temperature at the starting point of the walk, using the default weather app on an iPad 11" A16, iOS 26.2. The recorded temperatures during deaf participants' walk ranged from 14 to 36°C, M = 24, SD = 5.87, while for hearing participants it ranged from 10 to 32°C, M = 20.43, SD = 5.58.

Semi-structured interviews

After the nature walk, participants completed a semi-structured interview where they discussed the experience of the nature walk among other broader nature themes. Specifically for the walk, participants were asked to describe the walk, their observations, senses, emotions and feelings during the walk. Prompts were used to encourage participants to elaborate on these thoughts. The interviews were conducted and transcribed in Greek, with translation in English occurring for the purpose of this publication. For the 11 deaf participants who preferred communication in CySL, interpreters facilitated the interviews. For the qualitative analysis, described later, we only focused on the parts of the interview discussing the specific walk. The other interview themes were outside the scope of this study and will be explored separately in other publications.

Analyses

Statistical analyses

The quantitative measures were analysed using hierarchical regression with two models. To estimate results, we ran separate hierarchical regressions including in each the different outcome variables (PA post-walk, NA post-walk, PRS-11 Total score, NRS total score). Model 1 included the control factors age, gender, and NBS. PA baseline and NA baseline were used as an additional control in Model 1 when the outcome was PA post-walk and NA post-walk, respectively. Model 2 added the group variable with two levels (deaf and hearing) as predictor. We used R 4.1.3 and RStudio 2026.04.0 for all quantitative analyses.

For each regression model, linearity and homoscedasticity were evaluated using residuals vs. fitted plots and the Breusch-Pagan test. Residual normality was evaluated using Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and tolerance values, and potentially influential cases were examined using studentized residuals, Cook's distance, and leverage. Some influential cases were detected in some of the analyses presented later in Results. Therefore, sensitivity analyses removing these cases, where appropriate, were carried out to assess the robustness of the findings.

Bootstrapping was also conducted as a supplementary robustness analysis to assess the stability of the regression estimates given the small sample size and non-normality of the residuals in some analyses. We used 5,000 bootstrap samples (Banjanovic & Osborne, 2016) and calculated bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs), which adjust for estimated bias and skewness in the bootstrap distribution and have been recommended for small-sample inference, particularly for samples below 40 (Bentum & Asante, 2025). While bootstrapping does not overcome the limitations of a small or biased sample, it can provide information about the uncertainty of parameter estimates (Banjanovic & Osborne, 2016).

Content analysis

An exploratory deductive content analysis was conducted (Elo & Kyngäs, 2008) to excerpts of the interviews where participants discussed the specific walk they undertook. Coding was conducted in ATLAS.ti 8.4.26 and restricted to quotations corresponding to the quantitative outcome measures of PANAS and PRS-11 subscales. The rationale behind this analysis was to look into how the affective and restorative descriptions in the interviews correspond with the quantitative patterns found in PANAS and PRS-11 in the two groups. Additionally at a second level, we also recorded which sensory activations were mentioned (if any) when describing the affective and restorative aspects of their walk experience. Through this, we wanted to assess which senses may be linked to positive affect, negative affect and aspects of restorativeness during the nature walk in the two groups. Each author recorded half of the participant excerpts from each group, and whenever an excerpt was unclear, we discussed it and reached a consensus based on the codebook. The codebook framework is included in Table S3 in the Supplementary Material.

At the first coding level, excerpts were coded for positive or negative affective and restorative experiences. Positive Affect content was further differentiated by activation level. Positive Affect High Arousal (PA-HA) captured high arousal positive experience (e.g., excitement, enthusiasm, feeling energised), consistent with the item content of the PANAS PA scale, which indexes a high arousal PA (Watson et al., 1988). Positive Affect Low Arousal (PA-LA) captured low arousal positive experience (e.g., calmness, contentment, relaxation), which is not indexed by the PANAS, but is central to SRT's account on restoration that predicts a shift toward PA accompanied by reduced arousal (Ulrich, 1983; Ulrich et al., 1991). This distinction follows Ratcliffe et al., 2013) analysis, who coded valence and arousal jointly in analysing interview accounts of restorative natural auditory stimuli. Negative Affect was coded when capturing negative experiences consistent with the item content of the PANAS NA (e.g., annoyance, sadness, fear). Restorative experiences were coded using the subscales derived from the PRS-11 questionnaire (Pasini et al., 2014): Fascination (FAS), Being Away (BA), Coherence (COH), and Scope (SCO). Coherence and Scope can be understood theoretically as facets of a single Extent construct (Kaplan, 1995). Where excerpts negated an experience (e.g., absence of fear), we recorded this as a distinct code, denoted with 'minus'.

A second level of coding recorded the sensory pathway (visual, auditory, olfactory, tactile, bodily/thermal) associated with each coded excerpt, applied only where a sensory channel was mentioned in relation to an affective or restorative experience. More than one sensory modality could be coded for the same excerpt, if qualified. Excerpts without a sensory link were coded as having no identifiable pathway. An additional 'blended' code was applied in two cases where excerpts attributed an experience to a blending of senses (see examples later in results).

The unit of analysis was the participant and not the quotes. Each code was recorded as present or absent for each participant irrespective of the number of relevant excerpts a participant may have had on that code. This approach was adopted to prevent disproportionate influence from participants who produced more extensive interviews.

Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated narratively using a weaving approach, whereby qualitative material was considered alongside the corresponding quantitative outcome (Fetters et al., 2013). Integration focused on how the qualitative accounts complemented, contextualised, or extended the quantitative findings. This included providing information about the sensory context of participants' experiences and identifying descriptive patterns not apparent from the quantitative findings alone. Participant-level code frequencies were not treated as direct equivalent of the quantitative outcome scores, because they represented whether a qualifying experience was identified in an interview rather than the magnitude of that experience (which is what PANAS and PRS-11 captured). Accordingly, integration considered what additional insight the qualitative findings contributed to the quantitative results rather than whether qualitative frequencies simply reproduced the direction of the regression group comparisons.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

Positive and negative affect

Contrary to our expectation of similar post-walk PA between groups, deaf participants reported higher post-walk PA after controlling for age, gender, baseline PA, and NBS. This finding was maintained in the sensitivity and bootstrapping analyses. In contrast, no statistically significant association between hearing status and post-walk NA was identified. Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and the uncertainty reflected in the CIs.

Research has associated exposure to natural environments with increased PA and reduced NA, although pooled association was larger for PA (McMahan & Estes, 2015). Another meta-analysis also reported associations for both affective outcomes but identified substantial heterogeneity across studies and low to very low certainty of evidence (Yao et al., 2021). In an experimental study using PANAS, (Legrand et al., 2022) found that PA increased following a nature walk but not after an urban walk, whereas NA decreased following both walking conditions. These findings suggest that PA and NA may respond differently to walking and environmental exposure and that increase in PA may not always be combined with reductions in NA. However, unlike studies comparing natural and built environments, our study examined deaf and hearing participants undertaking the same nature walk, without a non-nature comparison condition.

The absence of a significant group association for NA should be considered in light of the substantial floor effects observed in both groups. A large proportion of participants obtained the minimum possible NA score at baseline, potentially restricting detectable reductions in NA and limiting the variability available to identify between-group differences. Participants' psychological state may also be relevant here. (Pasanen et al., 2018) found that participants reporting greater recent stress experienced larger improvements in mood valence and self-reported restoration following a forest walk. Similarly, (Daube et al., 2026) observed that reduction in negative emotions following nature exposure were particularly prominent in studies involving clinical populations. In addition, (Aldoh et al., 2025) found that state anxiety moderated the association between visual nature coverage and NA, with stronger negative associations among participants reporting higher anxiety. However, reductions in NA have also been documented in non-clinical samples (Legrand et al., 2022). As the clinical psychological status in our participants was not systematically assessed beyond some demographic questions, these factors cannot adequately explain the absence of a significant group association for NA.

The qualitative findings provide complementary information about participants' PA experiences.

Both groups described PA-HA and PA-LA, although these descriptions were more frequently identified among hearing participants. These frequencies are not directly comparable with PANAS scores, as they capture the presence rather than the intensity of reported experiences. Through our content analysis were able to capture PA-LA experiences, such as calmness and relaxation.

Given that such experiences are not captured by the PA subscale of the PANAS, the qualitative findings extend the quantitative results by providing information about positive experiences not covered by the questionnaire.

Furthermore, the sensory pathways associated with PA experiences varied within and between groups. Visual pathways were frequently linked to PA-HA and PA-LA in both groups, auditory activations were more frequent among hearing participants, while tactile and bodily/thermal activations linked to PA-HA were mentioned more frequently among deaf individuals. Other studies have similarly demonstrated the multisensory nature of affective experiences in natural environments. Kou et al. (2025) found that visual, auditory, olfactory, and haptic perceptions were positively associated with visitors' positive emotions in a nature-based destination. Aldoh et al. (2025) found that visual and auditory characteristics of natural environments interacted in their associations with PA and NA. Participants generally reported higher PA and lower NA when viewing scenes with greater visual nature coverage and lower anthropogenic noise. They also identified a moderating role of nature relatedness, suggesting that affective responses to sensory characteristics may vary between individuals (Aldoh et al., 2025).

Interestingly, our qualitative findings demonstrated that auditory activations related to PA-LA were linked to an absence of sound in both groups. Therefore, in participants with different hearing profiles, presence or absence of sounds was associated with feelings of relaxation and calmness during the walk. Experimental studies have demonstrated that natural sounds can contribute to affective responses to virtual nature environments in hearing populations (Annerstedt et al., 2013; Smalley et al., 2023). For example, (Smalley et al., 2023) found that adding natural sounds to a virtual nature scene resulted in higher ratings of calmness, excitement, and perceived restorative potential compared with viewing the same scene without sound. However, experimentally removing nature sounds from nature scenes viewed by hearing participants is not equivalent to how individuals with early-onset deafness experience natural environments.

In a study involving visitors with hearing impairment, (Chan et al., 2022b) found that nature sounds were frequently evaluated positively, whereas an inability to hear sounds was sometimes described negatively. This suggests that perceived environmental quietness and reduced auditory access should not be treated as equivalent experiences. In addition, (Chan et al., 2025) identified differences in sensory experiences between visitors with and without hearing impairment, with vision and smell being prominent among individuals with hearing impairment. Moreover, selective adaptations in visual attention and sensory processing associated with early deafness have been identified (Bavelier et al., 2006), while (Rosen, 2012) has proposed that deaf and hard-of-hearing individuals vary in their sensory orientations, encompassing visual, auditory, and tactile modalities. The present qualitative findings are consistent with this broader conceptualisation of sensory diversity, as PA experiences were associated with different sensory pathways in both groups, with the deaf group exhibiting more variable sensory activations in terms of PA experiences. Nevertheless, our study did not assess sensory weighting, attentional processing, or the relative contribution of individual sensory modalities to PA. Consequently, whether differences in sensory processing contributed to the observed association between group and PA remains uncertain.

Other aspects of the walking experience may have also contributed to the findings. A greater proportion of deaf participants were visiting the park for the first time, although unfamiliarity with the specific walking route was similar between groups (see Table 1 for details). Studies suggest that familiarity may be associated with affective responses to natural environments. For example, (Liu et al., 2026) found higher NA in less familiar virtual urban park environments, but no significant difference in PA. In contrast, (Jones et al., 2021) showed that mood-related benefits were maintained across three visits to the same natural environment. This suggests that familiarity does not necessarily influence all affective outcomes in the same way. Furthermore, deaf participants in our study also spent approximately four minutes longer on the walk than hearing peers, potentially allowing more opportunities for engagement with the environment. (Richardson et al., 2021) found that nature connectedness and engagement in simple nature-related activities were stronger predictors of psychological wellbeing than the reported amount of time spend in nature. Although these findings highlight the potential importance of engagement, the authors were concerned with general wellbeing rather than immediate affective responses following nature exposure. Thus, the possible contributions of walk duration, familiarity and engagement to our observed group difference remain uncertain.

Finally, interpretation of the group difference in PA is complicated by the ceiling effect observed in deaf participants, with 40% obtaining the maximum possible score following the walk. The relatively high baseline PA scores among deaf participants may have restricted the extent to which further increases could be detected. Moreover, measurement equivalence between spoken Greek and CySL users was not established. We therefore do not know whether linguistic or cultural differences influenced response patterns. These considerations, together with the small sample size are discussed further in the limitations, and indicate that the findings are preliminary and require replication in larger samples with further evaluation of the outcome measures' suitability for sign language users.

Perceived restorativeness

Group was not significantly associated with perceived restorativeness following the nature walk, after controlling for age, gender, and NBS. The exploratory analyses of the four PRS-11 subscales (fascination, being away, coherence, scope) similarly did not identify significant group associations. These results should not be interpreted as established equivalence between groups, given the small sample size and uncertainty of the estimates. The absence of significant group association with PRS is contrasted with the significant association we found for post-walk PA. This may reflect the different constructs assessed by these measures, as PRS evaluates the perceived restorative qualities of an environment rather than changes in actual psychological restoration. (Menardo et al., 2021) emphasised that perceived restorativeness should not be equated with objectively demonstrated cognitive recovery. In line with this distinction, (Johnson et al., 2022) showed that natural scenes were perceived as more restorative than urban scenes without analogous improvements in sustained attention or alertness. In addition, (Trammell et al., 2026) found that natural environments were perceived as more restorative than urban and control environments, but did not detect a significant effect of environmental exposure on PANAS PA.

Our qualitative findings provide complementary information on participants' restorative experiences. Fascination was frequently identified in both groups, predominantly associated with visual experiences, although other sensory pathways were also described. Similar with PA-LA, where deaf participants exhibited varied sensory activations in both groups, Fascination follows a similar pattern with higher frequencies in both groups compared to PA-LA. Auditory experiences associated with Fascination were more frequently identified among hearing participants, while two deaf participants described blended sensory experiences that could not be attributed to a single modality or into multiple separate modalities. We found particularly noteworthy the blended sensory description by one of the deaf participants, in which the observed movement of a leaf was associated with an otherwise inaudible sound and bodily sensations attributed to wind (see Results for quote). There is work that shows that deaf individuals can possess conceptual knowledge related to sounds despite limited auditory experience, with visual, motor, and linguistic information contributing to conceptual representations (Campbell & Bergelson, 2022; Trumpp & Kiefer, 2018). This account illustrates how perceived sensory information and knowledge about sounds may be integrated in the experience of Fascination, albeit previous work did not examine this for nature sounds.

Being Away was also identified in both groups, but its sensory associations varied descriptively.

Hearing participants more frequently linked Being Away to auditory experiences, whereas deaf participants often described this restorative quality without explicitly identifying a sensory pathway. There were a few visual and bodily/thermal activations in both groups related to the feeling of being away. These findings raise the possibility that similar perceptions of restorative qualities, such as fascination and being away, may be associated with different sensory experiences. Descriptions of Coherence and Scope were relatively infrequent and provided limited additional information.

The possibility of different sensory pathways contributing to restorative perceptions is consistent with research highlighting the multisensory character of nature-related restoration. (Kou et al., 2025) found that visual, olfactory, and haptic perceptions were associated with self-reported restorative qualities assessed through ART-based dimensions (i.e., extent, being away, fascination, compatibility), whereas soundscape perceptions were indirectly associated with these evaluations through positive emotions. Other work has demonstrated that soundscapes can themselves be evaluated in terms of perceived restorative qualities (Payne, 2013). It could thus be suggested that different sensory dimensions may be associated with restorative perceptions in different ways, but do not establish that any individual modality is necessary for perceiving a natural environment as restorative. Our qualitative findings are compatible with the possibility that similar restorative evaluations may arise through different sensory experiences. Nevertheless, the relative contributions of individual sensory modalities to PRS scores were not measured. Therefore, whether differences in sensory experiences contributed to the absence of a significant group association needs further exploration. Future research with larger samples and complementary measures of attentional restoration is needed to better understand these findings.

Nature relatedness

Deaf and hearing participants reported nearly identical nature relatedness scores, with no significant association between group and NRS after accounting for age, gender, and NBS. As nature relatedness was assessed before the walk, this finding reflects participants' broader connection with nature rather than their responses to the particular natural environment. The significant positive association between NBS and NRS suggests that participants who reported greater engagement with natural beauty also tended to report stronger connections with nature, irrespective of hearing status. This is expected given that NBS and NRS assess related constructs, while their association should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationship. (Lumber et al., 2017) identified contact, emotion, compassion, meaning, and appreciation of natural beauty as pathways to nature connectedness. (Chen & Yang, 2025) also found positive associations between nature connectedness and self-reported nature experiences across visual, auditory, olfactory, tactile and gustatory modalities. Furthermore, (Bell, 2019) illustrated how people with sight impairment experienced meaningful connections with nature, while emphasising the influence of physical and social environments on their opportunities for engagement. Together, these studies highlight the diverse experiences involved in developing a connection with nature across populations with different sensory profiles. Their relevance to how deaf people build their connection to nature needs to be examined. Future work could explore how sensory experiences, opportunities for nature engagement, and individual relationships with nature contribute to nature relatedness among deaf and hearing people.

Limitations

The relatively small sample size (N = 29), which fell below the a priori target of 38 participants, is a key limitation. This resulted in a reduced statistical power relative to the original plan, particularly for detecting effects smaller than anticipated. Consequently, nonsignificant findings should not be interpreted as evidence of absence of group differences. The small sample may have also reduced the precision of the regression estimates, warranting cautious interpretation of the observed effect and their CIs. Replication with larger samples is needed to establish the robustness and precision of these findings.

Due to resource limitations, hearing screening was conducted outdoors using an app-based hearing test rather than conventional audiometry in a soundproof environment. Although the Mimi Hearing Test has demonstrated screening validity, its accuracy can be affected by testing conditions and is lower at certain frequencies (Mui et al., 2025). We also conducted the test having the active noise cancellation function of the headphones enabled, which differs from the manufacturer's recommended testing condition. This was done to reduce the ambient noise, since the test was conducted outdoors. While previous research suggests that active noise cancellation can improve hearing screening performance in noisy environments (Cheng et al., 2023), its effects on the accuracy of hearing thresholds derived from the Mimi Test under our testing conditions remains unknown. Furthermore, ambient noise and headphone settings may affect the accuracy of app-based hearing assessments (Mui et al., 2025). Therefore, the possibility of inaccurate hearing threshold estimates and misclassification of hearing status, particularly for participants near the screening cutoff, cannot be excluded.

Sign languages carry affective and intensity marking in non-manual features (e.g., facial expressions, mouth morphemes, sign size and speed), and those were not coded in our interview transcripts. This may have underestimated, in our content analysis, the affective expressions in the deaf group relative to the hearing. Future work involving deaf signers, should either use video recordings alongside audio or note in real-time such features, to capture the breadth of affective features carried by sign language users. Ceiling effects were observed for PA among deaf participants, potentially limiting the instrument's sensitivity to higher levels of PA and restricting the measurement of further increases. Similarly, floor effects for NA in both groups may have limited the detection of reductions in NA and contributed to the uncertainty surround the non-significant group difference. In addition, deaf participants reported significantly higher PA at baseline. Some deaf participants required the CySL interperter to interpret the questionnaires or to explain certain items. Subtle shift in item meaning or intensity during interpretation could have altered how extreme certain respones options felt. Consequently, this may have potentially compressed the range towards the ceiling or floor effects. These considerations, together with the small sample size, warrant caution when interpreting the findings related to affect. Future studies could consider complementary measures of affective valence and arousal, such as the Self-Assessment Manikin (Bradley & Lang, 1994) or the Affective Slider (Betella & Verschure, 2016). These mostly pictorial measures may be useful in capturing low-arousal positive experiences, such as calmness and relaxation, which were identified in the qualitative findings but are not adequately represented in the PANAS PA subscale. Their reduced reliance on verbal emotional descriptors may also be advantageous when assessing participants whose primary language is sign language. However, their suitability and measurement equivalence for sign language users would require evaluation and validation.

5. Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare affective responses, perceived restorativeness, and nature relatedness between deaf and hearing adults in the context of an outdoor nature walk, while also exploring the sensory pathways associated with these affective and restorative experiences. The quantitative results indicated higher post-walk positive affect among deaf participants after accounting for baseline affect and other covariates, whereas no group associations were identified for negative affect, perceived restorativeness, or nature relatedness. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and measurement limitations. Our qualitative findings extended the quantitative results by identifying both high and low arousal positive experiences and illustrating how affective and restorative experiences were associated with diverse sensory pathways in both groups. Overall, our study provides preliminary evidence highlighting the importance of considering sensory diversity when investigating people's experiences of nature. Further research with larger samples, appropriately evaluated measures for sign language users, and complementary methods is needed to better understand how hearing status and sensory experiences relate to the affective and restorative outcomes following nature exposure.
