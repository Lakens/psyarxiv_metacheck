Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Heterogeneity in autistic development is usually attributed to variation in child-level characteristics. We argue that it is better understood as the output of a process. The child and family system continually calibrates the balance between environmental demands and available capacities, and those calibrations accumulate. When demand exceeds capacity, families lower demand, often through accommodation, or raise capacity through scaffolding and support. Each calibration changes what the system brings to the next encounter. Iterated across daily life and developmental transitions, this process pulls trajectories apart, producing what has been termed chronogeneity. The components of this account are established, but they have been invoked separately, and the field still largely models the child alone. This leaves basic questions unanswered, including why children who start in similar places diverge, why the same response helps in one family and constrains in another, and why divergence concentrates at developmental transitions. This paper is the first to specify a family-system mechanism that accounts for all three, and to establish that it cannot be described without three interlocking elements. Context defines the demands the system faces. Transactions are the operations through which calibration occurs. Development is how each calibration alters the terms of the next. The model identifies the fit between demands and capacities, rather than a focus on the child alone, as what assessment should measure, what intervention and policy should target, and what outcome measures should index, and it generates testable predictions about when and why trajectories diverge.

Beyond the Child: Reframing Autism Research Through a Contextual, Transactional, Developmental Model

Family and contextual factors have received growing attention in autism research, but largely as influences on individual child outcomes rather than as the organizing framework for understanding development (Mailick et al., 2025). This framing reflects a foundational tension. Autism is defined and measured at the level of the individual, and our measurement traditions have not kept pace with the complexity of relational and contextual assessment.

Research has split between studies of how family processes affect child outcomes (e.g., Szatmari et al., 2021) and studies of how the child's diagnosis affects the family (e.g., McStay et al., 2014; Pakenham et al., 2005). Both directions preserve a one-way model, in which one side acts and the other responds.

Centering the family is not a return to deficit-based models that blamed parents. It is an expansion of the unit of analysis to include the systems within which development actually happens.

Each component of the account we offer is already established in the literature.

Transactional models of development are decades old (Sameroff, 2009). The contextual nature of disability is a foundational claim of disability studies and the neurodiversity movement (den Houting, 2019; McDermott & Varenne, 1995). Family accommodation, first described in obsessive-compulsive disorder (Calvocoressi et al., 1995), is a well-described clinical phenomenon that extends to restricted and repetitive behaviors in autism (Feldman et al., 2019). Chronogeneity has been named and studied (Georgiades et al., 2017). Each of these literatures is mature.

The most direct precedent sits in the intellectual disability literature. Zigler's developmental approach argued, against a field that had reduced intellectual disability to cognitive deficit, that performance is shaped by motivational and experiential factors accumulated through a person's history (Zigler, 1969). This is very close to what we are arguing, and it was argued more than 50 years ago in an adjacent field. That it needs restating for autism research suggests how slowly ideas travel across disciplinary boundaries. Zigler established the principle, but he did not specify the mechanism, and the principle has rarely been carried into autism research.

What has not happened is the integration of these literatures. These ideas are typically invoked separately, cited in different sections of different papers, and applied to different questions. The result is that autism research can acknowledge all of them and still, in practice, model the child alone. This gap carries real costs. Assessment describes the child but misses the mismatch. Intervention mostly targets child skills, and even parent-mediated programs usually treat the parent as a route to the child's skills rather than as part of the demand side.

Longitudinal studies, in turn, sample too rarely, and at the wrong times, to capture the process that produces divergence. We argue that these existing ideas describe a single process, we specify what that process is, and we set out what follows for research, practice, and policy if it is taken seriously.

As Winnicott (1960) observed, an infant cannot be described apart from the care that surrounds it. Development happens inside regulatory systems made of caregivers, routines, expectations, and social structures. What looks like an individual phenotype is always embedded in relational context. Autism research has been slow to act on this, for reasons worth naming. Diagnostic frameworks center the individual. Relational constructs are hard to standardize. Perhaps most consequentially, the field still works in the shadow of the psychoanalytic tradition that dominated autism research into the 1970s and blamed parents for their children's autism (e.g., Silverman, 2012). That history left the field wary of foregrounding family processes, producing a pendulum that swung past the family rather than toward a more integrated view. This shift carried its own cost. Biomedical explanations of psychopathology reduce blame, but they also increase pessimism about prognosis and lower confidence in psychosocial treatment (M. S. Lebowitz & Appelbaum, 2019). An account that locates autism entirely in the child risks the same trade-off.

The Calibration Loop

Taken together, we argue, the existing literature describes a single process.

At any moment, the child and family system faces a demand load, meaning what the environment requires for participation, regulation, and engagement. Against this it holds capacity, distributed across the child, caregivers, siblings, and available supports. When demand exceeds capacity, the system calibrates. It can lower demand, most often through accommodation. It can raise capacity, through scaffolding, skill building, or bringing in outside support. Either way, the calibration leaves something behind. It changes the capacities the system brings to the next encounter, and it frequently changes the demand landscape itself. Iterate this across daily life and across the sharp demand shifts of developmental transitions, and trajectories that started close together move apart.

Chronogeneity, on this account, is less a fixed property of individual children than the compounding output of a loop that has been running for years. Context, transactions, and development are therefore not three separate lenses to be held at once, but the three elements without which this loop cannot be described. Context is the demand side, and without it there is nothing to calibrate against. Transactions are the calibrating operation, and without them there is no mechanism. Development is the fact that each iteration changes the terms of the next. Without it, calibrations do not accumulate and trajectories cannot diverge. The three-part structure is not a checklist. It is the minimum needed to specify one process.

In response to recent calls from Mailick et al. (2025) and Rosenbaum (2025), the sections that follow take each element in turn, as three views of the same process. That functioning is contextual is well established. McDermott and Varenne (1995) argued that disability is made in the relations between people and the settings they are asked to function in, not in individuals alone. The neurodiversity movement makes the same point, that disability reflects environments that fail to accommodate rather than intrinsic deficit (den Houting, 2019).

What is less often noticed is that the DSM agrees. Criterion C allows that symptoms may emerge fully only once social demands outstrip limited capacities (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Criterion D requires clinically significant impairment, which depends not only on the child but on whether supports are adequate for participation. The diagnostic threshold is, at least on paper, an expression of mismatch between person and context, not a property of the person. The criteria describe a demand-capacity imbalance. They simply stop there and never ask what the system does about it.

Demands come from multiple levels, from expectations for social reciprocity and sensory tolerance to classroom routines and cultural and socioeconomic constraints on participation. Capacities are similarly distributed. They include child communication and regulation, but also caregiver emotion regulation, family coordination, and whatever scaffolding the environment can provide. Mismatch occurs when demand exceeds the system's regulatory capacity at a given moment, and it is mismatch that sets the loop in motion.

Inside the family, environments are active regulatory contexts. Routines, expectations, and supports shape what is possible for communication, self-regulation, and participation.

Family functioning is associated with later outcomes alongside child characteristics such as language and cognitive ability (e.g., Szatmari et al., 2021), and home environments contain modifiable affordances that can support autonomy (Marcotte et al., 2023). Heterogeneity, from this perspective, is not fixed individual variation. It reflects differences in the demand landscapes families face and what they can bring to bear on them. The family is the most proximal regulatory system, but it sits inside school systems, service structures, and communities that shape the demands it must absorb.

Context sets the terms, but it does not tell us what happens when demand outstrips capacity.

Transactions: How the Loop Operates

While context defines the landscape, transactions are the proximal mechanisms through which the system detects mismatch and responds to it. Sameroff's (2009) transactional model established the basic architecture, in which children and environments continually reshape one another. Variation in joint engagement with caregivers predicts later language (Adamson et al., 2009), and interventions that improve interaction quality produce measurable gains (e.g., Ginn et al., 2017; Green et al., 2010; Kasari et al., 2010), in one trial still evident years later (Pickles et al., 2016). A child's behavior pulls a caregiver response, and that response changes the child's regulatory state.

Mother-child dyads with an autistic child may spend more time in mismatched emotional states than dyads with typically developing children (Guo et al., 2017). Iterated over time, these small adjustments shape both the child's regulatory capacity and the family's emotional climate.

Family accommodation is the clearest case. Parents modify routines, sensory environments, or expectations to reduce a child's distress. In the short term, accommodation restores equilibrium by lowering demand to match current capacity, which is precisely what calibration is meant to do. But when accommodation becomes the default, it can constrain skill acquisition by removing the opportunities where flexibility and distress tolerance get practiced. In childhood anxiety, a parent-based treatment built on reducing accommodation is as efficacious as cognitive-behavioral therapy (Lebowitz et al., 2020). In autistic children, family accommodation of restricted and repetitive behaviors is associated with more disruptive behavior and other negative child and family outcomes (i.e., Koller et al., 2022). This is where an integrated account becomes essential. Neither a purely contextual nor a purely transactional lens can explain what is transpiring here, because the same act, lowering demand to meet capacity, is adaptive or constraining depending entirely on what it leaves behind for next time. Flexible, time-limited accommodation lowers demand in a way that leaves room for capacity to grow. Rigid, escalating accommodation does not.

So, the useful question is not whether families accommodate. Most families do (Feldman et al., 2019), and perhaps appropriately so. We must ask how accommodation is patterned across time, contexts, and developmental stages, and whether that pattern is expanding or contracting the capacity the system carries forward.

One clarification is warranted. Accommodation is also the term the neurodiversity movement uses for what environments owe autistic people, and the two uses are not in tension. Some demands are unreasonable. Removing them, whether a family or a school does it, is the environment doing its job, not a calibration gone wrong. The real question is whose standards define the demand and what lowering it costs. Rigid, escalating accommodation does the most harm when it removes opportunities the child and family themselves value. A fit-based account also has to measure who sets the demand, whether the child, the family, or the setting.

The child is also a calibrator, not only the object of calibration. Avoidance, selfaccommodation, and later camouflaging all lower the apparent mismatch, sometimes at a significant cost the system and the individual only pay later.

Accommodation is one clear instance, but far from the only one. Families also scaffold, recruit outside support, change settings, redistribute who carries what, and reframe what counts as success. The loop is indifferent to which move is made. What matters is that a calibration occurred and that it left something behind.

These transactional processes extend beyond the parent-child dyad to the sibling relationship as well. Having siblings is associated with better social communication and adaptive functioning in autistic children (Ben-Itzchak et al., 2016 , 2019; Rosen, McCauley, & Lord, 2022), and a sibling's own diagnostic profile is associated with the child's trajectory of maladaptive behavior (Rosen, Schiltz, & Lord, 2022). Sibling interaction is itself a setting for social engagement, in which autistic children show prosocial behavior toward their typically developing siblings (Rum et al., 2021). Siblings change the demand landscape and shift the household's regulatory climate. Autistic children with older typically developing siblings show better social functioning, and those with older autistic siblings show a similar but weaker trend, possibly through modeling, companionship, or accumulated parenting experience (Rum et al., 2025). Siblings thus change both sides of the loop at once, adding demand and adding capacity.

Transactions explain how the system responds in the moment, but not why the same response leads different families to different places.

A developmental lens adds more than time to this model. It recognizes that the loop has memory. Each calibration changes the terms of the next. Like two snowballs released side by side at the top of a hill, both grow as they roll, but small differences in the ground they cross early send them down very different paths. Chronogeneity, the patterned divergence of trajectories over time (Georgiades et al., 2017), is better read as the accumulated history of how a family system has handled prior mismatches than as fixed individual difference. Functioning is not only a trait but also a timesensitive property of the child-family system. Ecological momentary assessment shows that autistic toddlers display more independence in daily life than one-time assessments capture (Hillel et al., 2025). A single measurement samples only one turn of a loop that has been running for years, and then we treat it as a characteristic of the child.

Importantly, divergence does not reflect how much a family calibrates, but how its calibrations are patterned over time. Two family systems may calibrate equally often and accumulate equally rich histories of adjustment, yet arrive at very different places. A family whose accommodation has become rigid and escalating has not done less work. It has often done far more, building an elaborate and effortful structure of routines and workarounds. The difference lies in whether that pattern expanded or contracted the capacity the system carries forward. This distinction matters, because the alternative reading, that divergent outcomes reflect families who did less, is precisely the deficit framing this model seeks to displace.

Transitions are often where the recursion becomes visible. Demand landscapes shift abruptly, and families have to reorganize routines, expectations, and supports. Those adjustments either buffer the mismatch and build new skills, or they entrench restrictive patterns. Trajectories turn at these points. Recent work shows that improvement in symptom severity may plateau at the transition to school for most children (Georgiades et al., 2022).

Two families facing the same transition, with children of the same initial capacity, can calibrate differently. The gap between them is likely to be wider afterward than it was before.

Transitions are not only periods of vulnerability. They are windows of plasticity, the moments when calibration matters most, which is why they are also the best opportunities for intervention. The double empathy problem (Milton, 2012) holds that communication difficulty between autistic and non-autistic people is mutual misalignment, not one-sided deficit. Each party struggles to read the other, and the struggle may ease when both share a neurotype.

Applied to the family, shared communication styles may lower some social demands while raising others. Rather than treating multiple autistic family members solely as genetic risk, this account recognizes that neurotype composition changes what the system has to calibrate against, and therefore changes what accumulates. adaptation. Ours is the child's developmental trajectory, and the demand shifts that drive it are developmental transitions. Whereas the FAAR model explains how a family regains balance, our model explains how the history of those rebalancings produces divergence between children.

What the Model Adds

The second is developmental theory. Thomas and Chess (1977) argued that outcomes depend on the goodness of fit between a child's temperament and the environment, and Masten and Cicchetti (2010) described how the consequences of interactions and transactions cascade across domains and over time. Our account inherits both. The demand-capacity balance extends goodness of fit from the child's temperament to the capacities of the whole family system, and the calibration loop is a cascade with a specified mechanism. What we add is the operation in between. We specify the moves, lowering demand or raising capacity, through which a family system responds to a mismatch, and the reason the same move can narrow the next mismatch in one family and widen it in another.

The third is ecocultural theory, which used the term family accommodation before clinical research did (Gallimore et al., 1989). In that tradition, accommodation means the way families construct a daily routine around a child with developmental delays, and the central question is whether that routine is sustainable. The clinical construct we build on comes from a different line, beginning in obsessive-compulsive disorder (Calvocoressi et al., 1995), where accommodation means the changes families make in response to a relative's symptoms. Our account needs both. Sustainable routines are the setting in which calibration happens. Symptom-focused accommodation is one of the calibrating moves.

The fourth is Bronfenbrenner's bioecological model, which locates development in proximal processes between a person and their immediate environment, shaped by person characteristics, context, and time (Bronfenbrenner & Morris, 2006). Context, transactions, and development map closely onto that structure, and our model builds directly on it. What the bioecological model does not specify is the operation itself, meaning which moves a family system makes when demand exceeds capacity and why the same move can expand capacity in one family and narrow it in another.

Of these, family stress models and cascade models have both reached autism research, but neither has been used to explain heterogeneity in the child's development. Cascade models have been applied mainly to cascades within the child, across motor, cognitive, sensory, and regulatory domains (Bradshaw et al., 2022). Family stress models have been applied to parent adjustment and family quality of life (McStay et al., 2014; Pakenham et al., 2005). Neither has been used to explain how a family system's calibrations shape divergence in the child's trajectory. That is the gap this model fills.

Testable Predictions

A model of this kind is useful only if it could be wrong. Ours makes five predictions that child-centered and purely transactional accounts do not make.

First, how a family system calibrates before a transition should predict how the child's trajectory changes across it, beyond the child's characteristics at entry. Child-centered accounts expect transitions to matter. This model specifies what determines which way a trajectory turns.

Second, the same family response should predict different outcomes depending on how it is patterned. At equal overall levels, accommodation that is flexible and time-limited, eased as the child copes, should predict growth in the child's capacity. Accommodation that is rigid and escalating, extended to more situations over time, should predict its narrowing.

Third, short-term calibration should predict long-term slope. Patterns of calibration measured densely within families, over days and weeks, should predict the direction of the child's trajectory over years.

Fourth, the fit between demands and capacities should predict later adaptive functioning and participation beyond baseline child characteristics. If it does not, the model is wrong about where the explanatory weight lies.

Fifth, changing the calibration should change the trajectory. If calibration is a mechanism, and not just a marker of a child already on a declining path, then reducing rigid accommodation before a transition should change the slope of the child's trajectory across it.

Parent-based approaches that reduce accommodation already exist and work (Lebowitz et al., 2020). This is the strongest test available, because it separates the model from the alternative in which children who are struggling simply draw out more accommodation.

What This Means for Research

If the calibration loop is the mechanism, research must be built to observe it running.

This has specific and substantive consequences.

Context must be measured, not controlled for. Demand structures and regulatory capacities need systematic measurement at multiple levels of the system. Treating context as background variance is not a neutral modeling choice, but a decision not to measure the process that we know drive development.

Designs must be dense enough in time to see calibration happen. Annual assessments capture the residue of calibration but cannot observe the loop itself. If we want to know how families calibrate, we need methods that can catch calibration in progress, such as ecological momentary assessment, intensive longitudinal designs, and repeated observation around transitions. This is not methodological sophistication, but rather what the model requires.

Models must be bidirectional. Studies need to explicitly model coupling across repeated time points, capturing how caregiver regulation changes child adaptation and how child behavior changes caregiver regulation. Examining child or family variables in isolation is a choice to focus on one variable within a bidirectional process.

Outcomes must index the system, not just the child. Participation in family and community life, regulatory autonomy, and adaptability across settings are as meaningful as symptom counts. If functioning is a property of the demand-capacity balance, then measuring only the child measures one side of a two-sided quantity.

Infrastructure must catch up. Longitudinal platforms like the Pediatric Autism Research Cohort (PARC) Study, a multisite prospective study tracking adaptive functioning and family context across developmental transitions (Kata et al., 2024), provide a foundation. But they need repeated assessment of family interaction, accommodation, and contextual demand alongside child-level measures. And sampling should cluster around transitions, where calibration matters most, rather than at arbitrary fixed intervals.

Implications for Practice and Policy

The central implication for practice is straightforward. The unit of assessment must incolde the fit between demands and capacities, not only the child.

In practice, this changes what a clinician asks. A standard assessment describes what the child can and cannot do. A fit-based assessment also asks what the child's environments demand, what capacities the family system holds, where the mismatch sits, and how the family has been calibrating against it. Accommodation is one of the things this assessment will often find, but it is not the target. The target is the mismatch. Sometimes that means building child capacity. Sometimes it means lowering environmental demand. Often it means helping a family see that a calibration that once served them well has started to cost them.

Existing tools offer a starting point. The ICF Core Sets for autism place environmental factors alongside individual functioning (Bölte et al., 2019), the Participation and Environment Measure for Children and Youth indexes environmental supports and barriers to participation (Coster et al., 2011), and family accommodation scales capture one class of calibration (i.e., Feldman et al., 2019). None yet measures demand and capacity together, and building that tool is part of the agenda this model sets.

For policy, the implication is that support should follow the fit and not only the diagnosis. In many service systems, eligibility rests on a child's diagnosis and severity, assessed at a single point in time. The Lancet Commission on autism has already called for stepped, personalized care that responds to changing needs across the life course (Lord et al., 2022). This model adds both a mechanism and a timetable to that approach. Need is a property of the system, and it changes, rising sharply when demands shift. That argues for reassessing need at predictable points of change and for resourcing support around transitions such as school entry, when demands shift most and where the model predicts intervention will have the most leverage. Fit-based assessment should add to diagnosis-based entitlement, not replace it.

For children and families, this means support that arrives where and when the mismatch is, rather than after trajectories have already diverged.

Conclusion

Autistic children develop within systems of relationships, routines, and shifting expectations. The account offered here identifies one mechanism running inside those systems. The child and family system continually calibrates the balance between demands and capacities, and those calibrations accumulate into divergent trajectories.

A school transition shows the loop turning. The new classroom brings unfamiliar sensory conditions, new social expectations, different institutional routines. That is a shifted demand landscape (context). The family reorganizes by adjusting mornings, negotiating supports, and accommodating distress where it shows up (transactions). Whether that reorganization builds the child's capacity to meet what comes next, or lowers demand in ways that leave capacity unpracticed, sets the terms for the transition after that (development). No single element, whether the classroom, the family's response, or time, explains the outcome.

The explanation lies in the loop.

The pieces are established, and some of them are very old. Zigler made a version of this argument for intellectual disability in 1969. What is new is the claim that these pieces belong together, that assembled they describe a single mechanism, and that the field's habit of invoking them separately is precisely what has allowed autism research to nod at context while continuing to model the child in isolation. Specifying the mechanism tells us what to measure, when to measure it, and where to intervene.

A note on generalizability.

The studies we draw on come mainly from high-income, largely Western countries, and their samples skew toward socioeconomically advantaged families. The model is meant as a scaffold applicable across cultural and socioeconomic contexts, but its specific claims about demand structures, accommodation, and family regulatory processes will need testing across diverse populations. Cultural variation in family organization, caregiver roles, and community support all shape the demand-capacity balance.
