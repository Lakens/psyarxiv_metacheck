Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Abstract

News feeds are digitally available all around us, yet the cognitive mechanisms by which we decide what to spend time on, and what to skip, are not well understood. We formulate digital news consumption as an information foraging problem. In this formulation, users move between news articles, formalized as information patches, exploited them like agents consuming food in a canonical patch foraging task. The switching time between news article can be seen as a navigational process that happens at a time akin to the travel time in the canonical patch foraging formalism. To unravel mechanisms determining news reading times, we fit an evidence-accumulation model separately to each of 2,820 readers of a Norwegian newspaper across 1.9 million reads. Reading times are described by accumulation from a variable starting point, in which readers arrive already partly informed. We also show that readers can be divided into two groups reflecting different behavioral patterns that we name: committed and conditional reflecting different distributions of the fitted evidence accumulation model parameters. Our study opens up the opportunity to study the detailed cognitive mechanisms underlying news consumption and our preliminary analysis show that our information foraging approach can be generalized to our types of digital media human interact with on a daily basis.

1 Introduction

Modern humans are constantly exposed to digital information in the form of news, posts, videos, etc. The ubiquity of the digital medium around us has potentially shaped how we cognitively process this barrage of information Meshi et al. [2015], Lara and Bokoch [2021]. One pertinent question is how a reader selects which articles to open or interact with while scrolling a feed, and how long to stay with each. Extensive surveys of news habits and sources describe what people consume Barthel et al. [2020], but not the potential cognitive mechanisms that generate these choices. News consumption can be regarded as analogous to resource consumption, with information as the resource, and the move from one article to the next as foraging between patches. Decisions of this type have been studied extensively in behavioural ecology within the framework of optimal foraging theory.

Optimal foraging theory describes how animals allocate time across resource patches by balancing the diminishing rate of return within a patch against the expected gain from travelling to a new one Charnov [1976], Stephens and Krebs [1986]. In order to tackle the potential cognitive mechanism underlying this behavior, recent work by Davidson and El Hady Davidson and El Hady [2019] formalised this trade-off as a foraging drift-diffusion model (FDDM), in which patch-leaving decisions arise from a noisy evidence-accumulation process. The FDDM provides a mechanistic account of patch-leaving decisions by linking Charnov's marginal-value theorem Charnov [1976] with sequential-sampling models from perceptual decision making Ratcliff and McKoon [2008]. In the idealised noiseless limit, Davidson and El Hady derive analytical conditions under which the drift rate and decision threshold reproduce MVT-optimal mean residence times. Under realistic noise conditions, numerical simulations show that the framework additionally generates full residence time distributions whose shape depends on the foraging strategy employed.

Information foraging theory has been proposed as a generalisation of optimal foraging theory, in which the resources consumed are informational rather than nutritional. It was first introduced by Pirolli and Card in a series of seminal papers that established the information scent that guides search, the patch structure of information environments, and the cost-benefit accounting that governs how long a forager stays in an information patch Pirolli and Card [1999], Markant et al. [2013], Pirolli and Card [1995, 1997], Pirolli [1998], Pirolli and Card [1998], Pirolli and Fu [2003], Pirolli [2007 Pirolli [ , 2003 Pirolli [ , 2006]], Card et al. [1996].

Subsequent work has tested the framework quantitatively in web search, image retrieval and exploratory mobile browsing Liu et al. [2010], Ong [2017], Jaiswal et al. [2023, 2020], Lambros [2005], where it accounts for observed patterns of interaction. The advantage of formulating a generative model is the ability to fit a model to each person performing a behavior, adding quantities that can be used to uncover latent cognitive variables. Similar models have been used to study how saccadic eye movements can be mapped onto a foraging problem Nahari and El Hady [2025] and how agents search for a mate Bernstein and Hady [2026]. The drift-diffusion model, used to formulate those foraging problem, has been used extensively in studying decision making processes Brunton et al. [2013], Piet et al. [2018], Gold and Shadlen [2007]. As mentioned above it has been extended to provide a mechanistic account of foraging behaviours for both individual Davidson and El Hady [2019], Kilpatrick et al. [2021] and social agents Blum Moyse and El Hady [a,b] . The information foraging framework has in turn been used to study perceptual decisions during active visual sampling Ludwig and Evens [2017], which makes a drift-diffusion account a natural choice here. In the majority of studies, evidenceaccumulation models have been developed and tested on animals in controlled environments and on humans in laboratory tasks, where the stimuli are designed a priori and the alternatives are known. Whether a leaving rule estimated this way describes decisions people make unprompted, among alternatives nobody arranged, is largely untested and remain an active area of inquiry.

Digital reading records offer a way to test this, as they provide the quantity a first-passage account requires (the time a reader spends in a patch) for enough information patches per person that a model can be fitted to each reader separately rather than to the population. That matters because an individual fit is what allows a parameter to be treated as a property of a person and tested against others in the population, where a fit to the aggregate yields a description of the sample and cannot reveal individual variability in behavioral parameters.

Here we use reading records from a regional Norwegian newspaper to ask whether the decision to leave an article has the structure of an accumulation to a threshold process. We show that reading times can be described by accumulation from a variable starting point, in which readers arrive at an article already partly informed, and this holds in a reader by reader case rather than only in aggregate. We also tested whether the same type of processes (first passage models) can account for the switching time between articles. We found that a constant-start first-passage model does not describe the interval between articles, which is barely distinguishable from a memoryless process, so, the model accounts for certain aspects of foraging behaviour, but does not provide a comprehensive explanation of the entire process. Moreover, the fitted parameters predict behaviour the model was never fitted to, each predicting a different behaviour: a reader's evidence threshold predicts both how readily they abandon an article and the route they take to the next, while the minimum evidence they gather predicts abandonment alone. The clustering analysis identifies two distinct groups of readers: committed readers, whose initial evidence states are drawn from a restricted range well below the decision threshold, and conditional readers, whose initial evidence states can span the full range from zero to the decision threshold. The dissociation is what justifies claiming the parameters are properties of readers rather than quantities that improve a fit. Our study establish a mechanistic framework to uncover cognitive mechanisms underlying digital news consumption, that can be generalized to other digital platforms humans interact with.

2 Results

2.1 News consumption is an information foraging process modeled as a bounded evidence accumulation

A reader arriving at a feed is confronted with more articles than they could possibly read and must make two decisions, namely which article to open and when to leave once they have done so (Fig. 1a). In practice, the observed behaviour reduces to a set of durations, but what we seek to explain are the underlying decision mechanisms that produce them. Information foraging theory provides the interpretive framework which connects the two. Framed as a foraging problem, the decision sequence comprises a repeating cycle, where the reader arrives at an information patch (article), exploits it for a given time, and pays a temporal cost in transitioning to the next one. More concretely, and in foraging terms, an article is a patch, the time spent in it is the patch residence time, and the interval between leaving one article and opening the next is the travel time τ (Fig. 1b). In this context, the decision to leave is the behaviour to be explained and the distribution of residence times is the behavioral observable. We model the decision to leave as an evidence-accumulation process (Fig. 1c). Within each visit, a decision variable X(t) accumulates noisy evidence about the article's value at rate α until it reaches a given threshold θ, at which point the reader decides to leave (Methods, Eq. ( 2)). Moreover, accumulation does not begin from a state of zero evidence. Indeed, before opening an article, readers have already seen the headline, the associated image, and potentially its source, meaning that some evidence is already available at the outset. Therefore, on each visit, we sample the starting point afresh, leaving a remaining distance to the threshold that varies from visit to visit between d min and θ (Fig. 1c). Each reader can then be characterised by three parameters, each with a direct behavioural interpretation. θ is the maximum patience, α is the rate at which an article produces evidence to that reader and d min is the least evidence the reader ever gathers before judging an article, however strongly they arrived disposed to leave it (the assessment floor).

The natural alternative is a constant-start drift-diffusion model, which preserves the accumulation mechanism and the leaving threshold θ, and differs only in doing away with the assumption that readers arrive at articles already partly informed. Although similar, the two models predict distinct shapes for the reading-time distribution (Fig. 1d). Under a constant-start, every visit faces the same evidence requirement, so short visits arise so short visits occur only when noise favours an early decision, resulting in a unimodal distribution in which density first increases toward a late mode and then decreases. On the other hand, under a variable-start, the larger z frac , the more visits begin close to threshold, so the mode moves earlier and the tail thins, while at z frac → 0 the model reduces to the constant-start case. The two curves in Fig. 1d share θ and α and differ exclusively in whether the effective distance d is fixed or variable, so the difference can be attributed to the starting point alone. These signatures are qualitatively distinct, in particular where they place the mode and how heavily they weight the tail, so reading-time distributions can discriminate between these two models.

2.2 Accumulation from a variable starting point predicts News reading times

Reading times in a digital context, and particularly on news websites, are strongly right-skewed, with most article visits ending within a minute and a minority continuing for several. A similar pattern can emerge across multiple families of probability distributions, and a sufficiently flexible distribution will reproduce it without describing any process at all. Therefore, distinguishing the two models of Fig. 1d) is not the same as confirming the existence of a potential evidence accumulation process, therefore we treat the two questions separately. First, we ask whether the starting point varies, which Fig. 2 addresses in three complementary views, and then how an evidence accumulation perspective performs in comparison with descriptions that lack generative content.

To investigate these questions, we started by analyzing all 1867477 reads as if they had come from a single reader. The variable-start model fit attains -5.1280 nats per read against -5.1630 for the constant-start model, and the shapes differ in the way each of the mechanisms predicts (Fig. 2a). One can observe that the direction of the constant-start model's failure is itself informative. The constantstart density overestimates the frequency of mid-range visits while underestimating the frequency of short visits and a subset of long visits. This pattern is consistent with a first-passage density whose mode occurs later than that of the observed distribution.

A similar contrast can be observed within single readers (Fig. 2b). The reader displayed in Fig. 2b) contributed 1056 reads and was selected at the median of fit among those in the top decile by read count (n = 284 readers), so selection was based here on sample size, rather than on goodness of fit. Across all readers the median threshold is θ = 17.41 and the median drift rate α = 0.1597, both in units of noise amplitude. Moreover, with 96.8% of readers favouring the variable-start over the constant-start model, the advantage is nearly universal rather than driven by a small subset of readers. Furthermore, since no pooling of individuals occurs, this advantage cannot be attributed to the aggregation of heterogeneous individual responses.

It is not, however, decisive and misfits in individual densities can offset one another in the average, so agreement at the population level is necessary but not sufficient for the individual fits to be correct. The three views agree that the starting point varies. Whether that confirms evidence accumulation is a separate question, since both candidates are drift-diffusion models, and we addressed it by comparing the same fits against a wider set, six other drift-diffusion models with time-varying drift and seven descriptive families with no generative content, at all three levels of aggregation. We revisit the same three views against this wider set.

For the pooled view, the variable-start model gives the closest match to the empirical distribution of any candidate on both the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Wasserstein-1 distances tests, at every analysis window (Supplementary Fig. S4). It does not, however, attain the highest pooled held-out likelihood and a seven-parameter drift-diffusion model with time-varying drift exceeds it on that measure at all three windows, without matching it on the Wasserstein-1 distance at any of them, consistent with additional drift parameters absorbing variation that arises between readers rather than within them. For the individual view, the variable-start model is preferred over every other drift-diffusion model (in particular, over the constant-start model for 97% of readers and over the previously mentioned seven-parameter time-varying accumulator for 67%) while three descriptive families are indistinguishable from it, a Gamma, a Weibull, and a power law with exponential cutoff, all within two points of chance (Supplementary Fig. S5).

For the mixture view, which has no free parameter at the population level, the variable-start model ranks first on all three measures. It exceeds every other drift-diffusion model, the seven-parameter time-varying one included (rank-biserial +0.33 on held-out likelihood, Wilcoxon signed-rank p < 0.001 after Holm correction), while the same three descriptive families remain indistinguishable from it (Supplementary Fig. S6).

On the other hand, the Gamma distribution, a two-parameter density with no generative content, predicts these reading times as well as the accumulation model does (as do the Weibull or the power law with exponential cutoff). But at the resolution these data provide, goodness of fit cannot separate an accumulation account from a sufficiently flexible description. In fact, what distinguishes them is not how well they describe the observed distribution but the explanatory content that survives the fit. The parameters obtained from the variable-start model are claims about a reader (e.g., how much evidence they require, and how fast an article supplies it), and both are recoverable properties of individual readers rather than artifacts of estimation (Supplementary Fig. S7). These reader-level parameters also have implications for other aspects of the same reader's behaviour and we test whether the individual differences captured by the variable-start model also explain differences in navigation behaviour in later sections. A fitted Gamma distribution, by contrast, provides no equivalent behavioural predictions (its parameters describe the shape of the reading-time distribution but do not characterise underlying reader-level processes).

Furthermore, one can observe that these conclusions do not depend on the analysis window and repeating the analysis at t max = 100, 300 and 900 s leaves the ordering unchanged (Supplementary Figs. S4, S5 and S6). The one exception is the mixture view at t max = 100 s, where the descriptive families overtake the variable-start model, but it should also be noted this is the least informative window, since truncation at 100 s places a substantial part of many readers' fitted densities outside the observed range, and was used as a limiting case.

2.3 Evidence accumulation model does not account for the switching time between articles

If the decision to leave an article can be interpreted as an accumulation to a threshold, the natural next question is whether the decision to go and open the next article can be accounted for using a similar process. The two are different problems within the foraging framework (one concerns the allocation of time within a patch, the patch problem, and the other the evaluation of cues before moving to the next patch, the scent problem), but the formalism is not a priori necessarily confined to the first, and whether it transfers to the second is an empirical question

The interval between leaving one article and opening the next is equivalent to the travel time, τ , within the foraging framework. The data format allows us to observe the transition rather than infer it. A reader leaving an article either opens another one directly or returns to a navigational page first. We count only returns to the front page as feed visits and exclude transitions passing through a section page, which only shows articles from that single section, so a return there cannot expose the reader to alternatives from elsewhere and would attenuate the topic contrast rather than inform it (these represent 9.1% of all transitions within the data we are using). For returns to the front page, the tracker records the time spent there (Fig. 3a). After this exclusion, 8,703,877 within-session transitions from 682522 readers remain, of which 59.0% pass through the feed and 41.0% are direct. The feed visit represents a search episode in which readers browse for their next article and we treat its duration as the travel time, τ.

The two routes differ in how often they lead to an article from the same section. Direct transitions lead to an article from the same section as the previous one for 65.4% of 3571698 cases, while feed transitions do so for 42.7% of 5132179, (Fig. 3b). The within-reader permutation baseline is 41.0% (obtained by shuffling each reader's destinations within their own reading history so that their section composition is preserved and only the ordering destroyed). An accumulation process describes the decision to leave an information patch, which is the patch problem in the foraging framework, but it does not describe the interval that follows, which belongs to the scent problem. Foraging models themselves treat that interval as a parameter rather than as a decision Charnov [1976], Davidson and El Hady [2019], and our result is consistent with that treatment. A plausible explanation is that feed dwell is dominated by the mechanics of scanning a list rather than by a decision making process with a termination rule (e.g. a reader scrolls until something catches their attention, and the time this takes depends on what the feed contains). One cannot test this hypothesis with the data we are using, since the set of headlines visible at each moment is not recoverable from server logs (the same limitation that prevents us from modeling the diet problem of selecting an article among available alternatives). What the comparison is able to establish is that the interval between patches is not a constant-start first passage to a threshold, and what describes it best improves on a memoryless process by less than one hundredth of a nat.

2.4 Patience and assessment floor predict different behaviours

The previous section established that accumulation to a threshold describes the time spent within an information patch, but not the interval between patches. It leaves open however whether the model parameters can tell us some about the behavioral heterogeneity of the readers.

The model assigns to each reader a patience and an assessment floor. Whether those numbers describe the reader or only the fit does it has to be settled before anything is analyzed on them. In order to do this , we simulated realistic readers to reproduce the dynamics of new reading observed in the data. On simulated readers the estimator recovers patience and drift rate with rank correlations of 0.972 and 0.899 (Supplementary Figs. S7a and b), respectively. On real readers, fitted independently to two disjoint halves of their own reads, the same parameters reproduce at 0.887 and 0.780 (Supplementary Figs. S8a and b). Patience and the drift rate can therefore be considered stable properties of a reader. The start fraction replicates less closely, and the consequences of that for the assessment floor are laid out in the Supplementary Information.

Across readers the fitted assessment floor (d min ) is bimodal, with one mode around d min = 3 and one close to d min = 0, and almost nothing between them (Fig. 4a). A Gaussian mixture on the fitted parameters (Methods) recovers that exact division without being given the assessment floor. The number of components was chosen by split-half reproducibility rather than by the Bayesian information criterion. Two of the three components have median assessment floors of 0.0016 and 0.0027 and are separated by patience rather than by the value of the floor, with the third having a median of 3.12. The two groups occupy almost the same range of patience, θ, with their marginal distributions overlapping across it (Fig. 4a), top histogram). The fitted assessment floor is bimodal across readers (Hartigan's dip test on log 10 d min , D = 0.151, p < 10 -3 ), while patience is unimodal (Hartigan's dip test on log 10 θ, D = 0.005, p = 0.98). The clusters are therefore separated by the assessment floor, not by a difference in patience.

3 Discussion

In this study, we applied the information foraging framework to news reading, treating an article as an information patch and the decision to leave it as the outcome of an evidence-accumulation process (a class of models conventionally used in neuroscience to study decision making). The framework distinguishes three problems Pirolli and Card [1999], the patch problem (time allocation within articles), the scent problem (headline evaluation on the front page) and the diet problem (which article is selected). We model the first directly, observe but do not decompose the second, and do not address the third, because the set of alternatives visible at any moment is not recoverable from server logs of the news data we are using in this study.

We have shown that reading times within an article are described by accumulation from a variable starting point, in which a reader arrives already partly informed. In systems and cognitive neuroscience, decision-making has been extensively studied using the concept of evidence accumulation Stone [1960], Laming [1968], Link [1975], Ratcliff and McKoon [2008], Falmagne [1965], Bogacz et al. [2006], Piet et al. [2018]. Evidence accumulation has been implicated for example in social decisions [Krajbich et al., 2012], sensory decisions [Brunton et al., 2013 , Hanks et al., 2015], economic decisions [Gluth et al., 2012], memory decisions [Ratcliff, 1978], visual search decisions [Purcell et al., 2010], and value decisions Milosavljevic et al. [2010]. What distinguishes the present application is not the model but the setting and the resolution. In our study, the decisions are unprompted, the alternatives were not selected by an experimenter and the record is long enough per person to estimate a leaving rule for each.

On the other hand, the evidence accumulation process was not able to predict the interval between articles. A constant-start drift-diffusion model is a poor description of the time a reader spends on the feed before opening the next article, and the best of the candidates we compared offers little improvement in holdout likelihood over an exponential distribution, which represents a memoryless process. In foraging terms, the patch problem and the scent problem are different problems, and our results suggest they are not described by the same formalism. Foraging models themselves treat the interval between patches as a parameter rather than as a decision Charnov [1976], Davidson and El Hady [2019], and our result is consistent with that treatment. One plausible explanation is that feed dwell time is governed by the mechanics of scanning a list rather than by a decision process with a termination rule, so that the time taken depends on what the feed contains rather than on what the reader requires. We cannot test this hypothesis with the present data, for the same reason we cannot address the diet problem.

Fitting each reader separately revealed that readers are not distributed smoothly in the parameter space. A Gaussian mixture model separates readers into two groups that differ in their assessment floors, even though this quantity was not used in the clustering process. For one group, the upper bound of the initial evidence distribution falls below the decision threshold, so the model requires a minimum amount of evidence that must be accumulated before a decision can be reached. For another group, it requires none the data can detect. Readers in this second group abandon articles within 10 seconds of arrival at more than twice the rate observed in the first group. Thus, the groups are distinguished not only by their fitted model parameters but also by differences in independently observed reading behaviour. This suggests that the clustering captures meaningful differences between readers, rather than simply partitioning the parameter space.

The assessment floor and patience capture distinct aspects of reader behaviour. Holding the assessment floor constant, patience predicts both how often a reader abandons an article and whether they return to the front page before opening the next. Holding patience constant, the assessment floor predicts abandonment strongly and does not predict navigation between articles. Note that a parameter that correlates with every measured behaviour tells us little about what it specifically captures, while one that predicts the behaviours it is expected to predict, but not those it is not, provides evidence that it captures a specific aspect of reader behaviour. This asymmetry also argues against the alternative that both parameters reflect the same underlying reader disposition. If they did, we would expect both parameters to predict both behaviours. Instead, their distinct predictive patterns suggest that they capture two different properties of readers rather than measuring the same property twice.

In the information foraging framework, patch exploitation and the traveling between patches can be conceptualized as separate problems, each with its own decision rule. Here one parameter reaches both. A reader who requires more evidence before abandoning an article also takes a more direct route to the next, and the relation survives holding the assessment floor constant. The assessment floor, by contrast, predicts only what happens within an article, which is what a quantity, setting the minimum evidence any decision requires, should do. We take this as evidence that patience is a disposition a reader brings to the session rather than a quantity defined only within an article.

Our study have some limitations. First, the data are from a single regional newspaper, in one language, over ten weeks, so we cannot say whether the behavioral categories we report reflects something general about readers or something about this outlet and its particular audience. Second, the readers analysed are by construction the most active on the site, since fitting three parameters per reader requires a long record, and they are therefore not representative of the totality of its visitors. Third, the assessment floor is identified in ordering rather than in value for readers near the parameter bound, so the conditional group is defined by the absence of a detectable floor rather than by an estimate of a small one. A second dataset with per-article engagement times and comparable per-reader depth would test whether the division and the dissociation replicate.

Several concrete next steps could build up on this study. A dataset with richer metadata would allow the scent and diet problems to be addressed rather than observed, giving a deeper understanding of digital news consumption. This approach can also be extended to platforms such as Reddit, X, Bluesky and TikTok Pirolli [2020], Drias and Pasi [2020], where the difficulty would be establishing the nature and amount of information a user is sampling and evaluating when interacting with a post on any of these platforms Roozenbeek et al. [2025]. The model could also be extended to include social information Pirolli [2009], Luca et al. [2009], Pirolli [2008], since time spent on an article may be shaped by knowing that others have read or shared it, a question better addressed by experiments in which readers are asked about such influences Metzler and Garcia [2024]. Comparing the models we developed in this study to a reinforcement learning algorithm might give us insights into potential learning processes involved in news consumption, which could equally be formalised as Bayesian inference Kilpatrick and El Hady [2026], with reading shaping a reader's posterior probabilities making them more likely to read some articles rather than others and so differentiating readers over time. The latent variables of a drift-diffusion model may also be used to study how misinformation spreads, for example by asking which of them communicate confidence in information.

More broadly, our study further highlights the idea that foraging is a general adaptive process operating over internal as well as external resources Hills et al. [2008, 2012], Hills and Butterfill [2015], Todd and Hills [2020], Safavi and Dayan [2022], and contributes to the increased interest in casting information as a key currency in defining the ecological, neural and behavioural constraints animals and humans face in their environments, an area of inquiry termed information ecology, which studies how individuals use information to enhance fitness Metzler and Garcia [2024].

4 Materials and methods

4.1 Data description

We analyzed user activity information from Adresseavisen, a regional newspaper published in Trondheim, Norway, released as the Adressa dataset which was originally prepared as part of the RecTech project on recommendation technology Gulla et al. [2017]. These activity logs provided fine-grained information about users' interactions and navigation behavior, enabling the reconstruction of digital movement alongside identification of information foraging events.

The data were collected for 10 weeks (from 1 January to 31 March 2017) and are made up of 5.83 × 10 7 page-visit records from approximately 3.1 × 10 6 readers. Each record contains a pseudonymous reader identifier, the visited URL, a Unix timestamp of the page request, boolean flags marking session boundaries, and activeTime (the seconds of active engagement on the page). Nevertheless, not every record corresponds to an article read. We therefore classified pages according to their URL patterns. Article pages (which correspond to the information patches in the foraging framework) were identified by the terminal pattern (for example, .ece or .html). The front page (adressa.no) is where headlines are assessed. Section pages (for example, /nyheter/ or /100sport/) are intermediate navigation surfaces. Across the dataset, 34.3% of records are articles, 56.6% are front-page visits, and 9.0% are sections or other pages. In the information foraging framework of Pirolli and Card Pirolli and Card [1999], this classification maps onto their three model types. In this paper, we model the patch problem (time allocation within articles) directly, we observe but do not decompose the scent problem (headline evaluation on the front page) and we do not model the diet problem (which article is selected), because the set of alternatives available at each moment is not recoverable from server logs.

4.2 Reconstruction of digital trajectories

To recover the reading sequences, we reconstructed per-reader trajectories from the raw event log by segmenting it into sessions, restricting it to article pages, and merging fragmented visits. In fact, the tracker reports events, not reading episodes per se, and these three reconstruction steps separate the two. In practice, for each reader, events were ordered by timestamp and a new session was opened whenever the interval between the end of one event and the start of the next exceeded 300 s, or whenever the tracker's own session flag was set. This 300 s threshold is a convention rather than a measurement and formalizes the assumption that a reading session remains engaging until a pause of five minutes or longer occurs, at which point a new session begins (we therefore verified that the results are insensitive to this threshold; see Fig. S4, S5 and S6). For every event, we also computed the time elapsed since the previous article was exited, the travel time (τ ).

Events were restricted to article pages (the patches of the information foraging framework) identified by URLs ending in .ece or .html. Front pages, section pages and other navigational pages were therefore excluded. Moreover, consecutive events sharing a URL and a session, and separated by 30 s or less, were merged into a single visit whose engagement time is the sum of the fragments and whose remaining fields are taken from the first. This step merged 329382 records and leaves 19688444 patch visits. Readers contributing more than 500 patch visits were retained, yielding 2820 readers and approximately 3.5 million visits. This threshold reflects an estimation requirement. Because each reader is fitted separately, a sufficiently large number of reads is needed to reliably estimate the model's three parameters. Note however that the retained readers are, by construction, the most active on the site, so the sample is not representative of all readers, and no inference about the wider population can be made from it.

4.4 Time between information patches

Each logged page view was assigned to one of three kinds. Articles were identified by URLs ending in .ece or .html, (the patterns the site uses for editorial content, 34.3% of records). The front page was the bare domain, https://www.adressa.no (56.6%). All remaining on-site pages were classified as navigational (e.g. section fronts, sub-section listings, tag pages and search results).

A transition is a pair of consecutive article views by the same reader within a session. The interval between them is the travel time, τ , computed as the timestamp of the second article minus the sum of the first article's timestamp and its engagement time. Pairs were retained if they fell within the same session, had0 ≤ τ ≤ 600 s, and had no navigational page between them. The last rule follows from what the two routes are meant to contrast. A reader returning to the front page is exposed to articles from every section and may switch topic (while section listings shows articles from one section only, so a return there cannot produce the comparison the analysis makes). Simultaneously, considering such a transition as direct one between articles would attribute to an in-article link a choice the reader made from a list.

Transitions passing through a navigational page are therefore excluded rather than assigned to either route. A transition was therefore classified as via feed if at least one front-page passage occurred between the two articles, and direct if nothing fell between them. A transition was considered samesection if the two articles shared a section. The observed percentage of this quantity is not interpretable on its own, because a reader who reads predominantly within one section will stay within it by construction, so the baseline was obtained by permutation. Each reader's sequence of destination sections was shuffled within their own reading history, preserving their section composition and destroying only the ordering. Averaging the recomputed rate over n = 25 permutations gives a chance level of 41.0%.

For each route type, direct transitions and feed transitions, we calculated the same-section rate. Similarly, for each reader, we then calculated the same-section rate separately for each transition type and computed the within-reader difference between the two rates. We tested these paired differences across the 43089 readers with at least ten transitions of each type using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation as the effect size.

Furthermore, for each reader with at least 20 transitions, we recorded the total number of transitions and the number that were direct (using counts rather than rates because the sampling variability of a rate depends on the underlying count). To obtain the expected distribution under reader homogeneity, we simulated the number of direct transitions for each reader using a binomial distribution. The number of trials was set to that reader's total number of transitions, and the success probability was set to the overall direct transition rate across all readers. We then compared this expected distribution with the empirical distribution of direct-transition rates.

The feed dwell is the engagement time recorded on a front-page interaction, restricted to the [1, 600] s interval and with longer visits being treated as interruptions rather than search. Only readers contributing at least 100 of such visits were retained.

Seven candidate distributions were compared: • Exponential distribution (the memoryless null) • Log-normal distribution • Weibull distribution • Gamma distribution • Two-component exponential mixture • Constant-start drift-diffusion model • Constant-start drift-diffusion model with offset.

The candidate set spans both mechanistic and descriptive accounts, so that a descriptive family winning would indicate that the data do not support a decision process rather than that the wrong mechanism was tried. Each candidate was fitted by a maximum likelihood routine from three starting points under box constraints, on a random half of each reader's visits, and scored on the other half.

4.5 Model Parameters, behavioural patterns and individual differences

Each reader contributes a set of fitted parameters from the variable-start model and two behavioural quantities obtained from the data. The first is the share of a reader's visits ending within 10 s, counted over the reads inside the [5, 300) s analysis window so that the quantity refers to the same observations the model was fitted to. The contrast between clusters is unchanged if the count is taken over all of a reader's reads instead (16.5% against 8.0%, compared with 9.4% against 4.0% within the window).

The second is browsing propensity, the share of a reader's article-to-article transitions via feed (i.e. transitions that pass through the front page), taken from the navigation analysis described above. ' Readers contributing fewer than 20 transitions are left out of the browsing analyses rather than assigned a rate (at that count the sampling noise on a rate is comparable to the between-reader spread the measure is intended to resolve).

Readers were clustered on the fitted parameter (θ, α 0 and z frac ). θ and α 0 were log-transformed before standardisation, so that equal ratios correspond to equal distances. z frac is a ratio bounded on [0, 1] and was left untransformed (a log would compress the values near one, which is where the two groups separate). Remember that, by definition, d min = θ(1 -z frac ).

Gaussian mixtures with full covariance matrices were fitted for k = 1 to 8. The number of components was selected by split-half reproducibility rather than by the Bayesian information criterion. BIC falls steeply over the first few values of k and then varies without a clear trend, reaching its lowest value at k = 7 (Supplementary Fig. S10). BIC measures how closely the mixture describes the parameter distributions rather than how reproducibly it assigns readers, and, since our aim is to identify groups of readers, the stability of cluster assignments is more important than goodness of fit alone Reproducibility was measured by fitting a mixture to each half of a random split and computing the adjusted Rand index between the two labelings, averaged over ten splits. Both half-sample models label the full sample, so the index compares two labelings of the same readers rather than of disjoint sets, which gives higher values than the usual construction. This procedure selects k = 3. A reader whose maximum posterior falls below 0.75 was left unassigned rather than forced to the nearer component (this applies to 0.7% of readers). The partition reported is between the two largest components, with the 94 readers of the third (3% of those with fitted parameters) being excluded alongside the unassigned.

Hartigan's dip test was applied to log 10 d min and log 10 θ across classified readers, and repeated on all readers with fitted parameters to confirm that any departure from unimodality is a property of the sample rather than of the partition.

The two example readers shown were selected by minimising a cost combining the following requirements: • each is located near its own cluster's median short-visit rate, so the pair is representative of the clusters; • each lies near the 85th percentile of read count within its cluster; • the second reader is matched to the first on drift rate and read count.

Matching is necessary because an unmatched pair could differ in drift rate or in sample size, and the panel could then display that difference rather than the one in the assessment floor.

Given that patience and assessment floor are correlated across readers, a marginal correlation cannot attribute a behaviour to either alone. Each reported partial is computed from the inverse of the rank correlation matrix of three variables (the two parameters and one behaviour), which is the Spearman analogue of a partial correlation and assumes nothing about functional form. We deliberately do not control for the second behaviour when analysing the first. Both behaviours are considered outcomes of the reader parameters, rather than predictors of each other. Controlling for one behaviour could therefore remove part of the relationship between the reader parameters and the behaviour we want to examine.

More than half of the fitted start fractions reach the upper bound on z frac , which potentially invites the objection that the bound was chosen so as to produce this outcome. Fit quality cannot answer it, since a wider bound can never fit worse than a narrower one.

Instead we report three quantities at each combination of bounds. In the first, each reader is fitted twice, on disjoint halves of their own reads, and the two estimates of θ are correlated across readers, to check whether the estimates replicate, Fig. S2 a). The second is the held-out log-likelihood, included here for completeness, Fig. S2 b). The third is the proportion of readers reaching the z frac bound, Fig. S2 c).

In practice, we refitted 200 readers on both halves of their reads at each of 27 combinations of the three bounds, varying the upper bound on θ over {100, 500, 2000}, the lower bound on α over {10 -4 , 10 -3 , 10 -2 } and the upper bound on z frac over {0.95, 0.99, 0.9999} (Fig. S2). Changing the upper bound on θ from 100 to 2000, or the lower bound on α from 10 -4 to 10 -2 , leaves all three quantities practically unchanged, which happens because no reader approaches either bound. However, lowering the upper bound on z frac from 0.9999 to 0.95 increases the proportion of readers at the bound from 59% to 62%, as the lower ceiling is more easily obtained, which suggests that the result is not an artifact of a non-restrictive upper bound, considering that it admits fewer readers to the bound than a tight one would. The effect on the other two measures is small in either direction, where across the full range of the upper bound on z frac the correlation varies by less than 0.002 and the held-out log-likelihood by less than 0.0005, with the tighter bound giving marginally the higher correlation and the lower likelihood (with neither difference being large enough to favour one value over another).

A uniform contaminant of weight π = 0.02 was included so that a single mistracked read could not dominate a reader's likelihood, and was fixed rather than estimated. Estimated freely, π could absorb misfit, and a reader the model is not able to properly describe would be reported as largely noise rather than as a failure of the model.

Refitting 300 readers at π ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05} (Fig. S3), the proportion at the bound increases monotonically from 50.7% at π = 0 to 52.3%, 53.3% and 56.3%, while the median θ rises from 16.69 to 17.99 and the median α from 0.1534 to 0.1728 (all changes under 13%). The most informative value is perhaps the endpoint. In fact, with no contaminant at all, 50.7% of readers still reach the bound. The pinning is therefore not produced by the contaminant, and the value adopted in the main analysis sits near the lower end of the range the sweep covers. The proportion is nonetheless not invariant to this choice (it spans 5.6 percentage points across π ∈ [0, 0.05], and 1 point between 0.01 and 0.02).

In conclusion, the three analyses demonstrate that the central estimates are insensitive to the procedural choices examined (the analysis window, the parameter bounds and the contaminant weight).

3 Model comparison

The variable-start model was compared against thirteen alternatives, chosen to span two kinds of account. Six are drift-diffusion models, describing a process that ends when accumulated evidence reaches a threshold, and their parameters refer to quantities a reader could, in principle, possess. Seven are descriptive families, so parametric densities with no generative content, which can reproduce a right-skewed distribution without committing to any mechanism that produces it. The drift-diffusion models share the first-passage structure of Eq. ( 2) and differ in the functional form of the drift. In the constant-start model the drift is constant and accumulation begins at zero. The variable-start model adds a starting point drawn afresh on each visit. The remaining five allow the drift to vary within a visit: • Linear : α(t) = α 0 + βt.

• Oscillatory: α(t) = α 0 + γ sin( 2π P t).

• Linear + oscillatory: α(t) = α 0 + βt + γ sin( 2π P t).

• Power + oscillatory: α(t) = α 0 (t/τ ) β + γ sin( 2π P t).

• Competing + oscillatory: α(t) = α 0 + βt -δ e -t/τ + γ sin( 2π P t). With constant drift, the expected evidence accumulated by time t is αt, and Eq. ( 3) gives the firstpassage density exactly. However, when the drift varies within a visit the expected evidence becomes A(t) = t 0 α(s) ds, and we obtain a density for each time-varying model by substituting A(t) for αt in Eq. ( 3). The substitution is not exact for the time-varying models, so each density was normalised numerically before scoring. The validity of this approach for inference has recently been questioned ?.

The descriptive families capture a broad range of temporal patterns through flexible continuous probability distributions. Unlike the drift-diffusion models, these families describe the distribution of observed times directly, each imposing a different functional form: • Gamma: f (t) ∝ t k-1 e -t/σ.

• Log-normal : f (t) ∝ t -1 exp[-(ln t -µ) 2 /2s 2 ].

• Weibull : f (t) ∝ t k-1 exp[-(t/λ) k ].

• Gompertz : f (t) ∝ e t/σ exp[ c (1 -e t/σ )].

• Power law : f (t) ∝ t -a.

• Power law + cutoff : f (t) ∝ t -a e -t/τ.

In practice, it is estimated as the mean of log f obs (t) over the reads withheld from estimation, with f obs the window-conditioned density of Eq. ( 5) evaluated at the parameters fitted to the other half.

• The Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance is the largest difference between the fitted and empirical cumulative distributions, max |F model -F n |. It focus on the single worst region and is insensitive to how well the remainder fits.

• The Wasserstein-1 distance integrates the same discrepancy, |F model -F n | dt, rather than taking its maximum like the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance. It can be read as the average distance probability mass would have to move to turn one distribution into the other. Unlike the other two it is expressed in seconds, and is therefore the only one interpretable in the units of the data.

4 Parameter identification

Despite the results presented in previous sections, a question remains unanswered. When the parameters are known, either by construction or by design, are we able to identify them correctly? Indeed, a fitted parameter is only interpretable if the model can recover it from data generated with that parameter. Furthermore, can the estimates obtained from part of the dataset be replicated using another part of the same dataset? The two questions address distinct problems related to parameter identification.

To address the first question, we generate synthetic readers using the fitted parameter values of individual real readers. Each synthetic reader is assigned the same number of reads as its real-data counterpart. These reads are drawn from the variable-start model using the corresponding fitted parameter values and are truncated to the analysis window. The resulting sample is then refitted using the same procedure employed throughout, with the analysis window being considered to ensure that generation and estimation use similar bounds. This allows us to assess whether the estimation procedure can recover the parameters used to generate the data. The recovered value is then compared with the value used to generate. Simulating at fitted values rather than over a parameter lattice was a deliberate choice. A lattice assess recovery at parameter combinations that no real reader occupies, while implicitly assigning them the same weight as combinations that are actually observed. By contrast, simulating at the fitted values of real readers keeps the assessment focused on parameter configurations represented in the data, making the recovery analysis directly relevant to the empirical population under study.

Patience and drift rate are recovered across their whole range (Fig. S7, with Spearman ρ = 0.972 and 0.899 respectively). The median recovered value differs from the true one by 1.4% for patience and 0.8% for the drift rate, and for half of readers the recovered value falls between -8% and +5% of the true one for patience, and between -7% and +6% for the drift rate. The assessment floor behaves differently, with its ordering properly recovered (ρ = 0.717) but not necessarily its value. Binning on read count separates the three parameters (Fig. S7d). Patience and drift rate are recovered almost equally well at every sample size, which indicates that their recovery is a property of the model rather than of how much data a reader provided. 5 Clustering selection and robustness

The main text classifies readers into two groups and claims that this division predicts behaviour. However, an important question is whether alternative, equally reasonable methodological choices might have produced a different partitioning of the readers' behavioural patterns. This question can be broken down into two smaller, more specific ones. First, how the number of components was selected, and second, how sensitive the resulting partition is to variations in the choices made during its construction. To obtain said partition, Gaussian mixtures with full covariance matrices were fitted for k = 1 to 8 on the log-transformed patience and drift rate together with the untransformed start fraction. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) falls steeply from k = 1 to k = 3 and then varies without a clear trend, reaching its lowest value at k = 7, Fig. S10 a). The preference for seven components reflects the parameter distributions not being Gaussian (further components capture their shape rather than identifying further groups of readers). So, although the selection by BIC is the conventional choice, it is not available here.

The number of components was therefore selected by split-half reproducibility, Fig. S10 b). A mixture was fitted to each half of a random split of readers, both half-sample models were used to label the full sample, and the adjusted Rand index (ARI) between the two labelings was computed. The process was repeated over ten splits, and the error bars are the standard deviation across them. The index peaks at 0.96 for k = 3, above the values at other numbers of components, and this number was therefore selected. Note that, because both half-sample models label the full sample, the ARI compares two labelings of the same readers rather than of disjoint sets, and only the comparison across k matters for the selection. Moreover, BIC and reproducibility answer different questions. A mixture with seven components describes the parameter distributions more closely than one with three, which is what BIC represents, while assigning readers to components in a way that does not survive refitting on half the sample. Where the objective is a division of readers rather than a description of a density, reproducibility is the property that matters. The mixture returns, for each reader, a probability of belonging to each component. Readers whose largest maximum posterior probability fell below 0.75 were left unassigned rather than forced to the nearer component, which applies to 0.7% of readers. The mixture was fitted to patience, the drift rate and the start fraction, but the division it returns separates readers by only one quantity. Hartigan's dip test, applied to the assessment floor and to the two fitted scale parameters across all readers with fitted estimates, locates the departure from unimodality, Fig. S11a), b) and c). The assessment floor (D = 0.151, P < 10 -3 ) rejects unimodality, while patience (D = 0.005, P = 0.99) and the drift rate (D = 0.008, P = 0.29) do not.

Each panel draws the distribution for all readers with the three groups' densities over it. In fact, the two clusters occupy distinct regions of the assessment floor and overlapping regions of patience and the drift rate, which encapsulates what the main text claims from the mixture, that the division is produced by the assessment floor. The excluded readers span both modes of the assessment floor, which is why the mixture could not place them on one side of the division. On patience they are indistinguishable from the two clusters. The behavioral patterns identified so far rest on a single population (Adressa dataset, Norway). To test whether they generalise to other kinds of digital content, we analysed an independent dataset, the Kulshrestha German web-tracking panel, composed of 2148 participants recorded over one month Kulshrestha et al. [2021], with 9.15 million page views across 49918 domains. The URLs in this dataset are anonymised, so pages cannot be classified by their address and navigational pages have to be identified from behaviour alone.

The Adressa analysis separates articles from front and section pages by their address, and no equivalent is available in this dataset. Here, 2148 participants are spread over 49918 domains, so most domains are visited by too few people for any rule that relies on many readers visiting the same page. We therefore adapted the maximal forward reference procedure of Chen, Park and Yu Chen et al. [1998], which segments a browsing session into forward paths and discards backward references on the grounds that these are made for ease of traveling rather than to consume content. The algorithm classifies no pages, so the extension here is to count, for each reader, how many of their own forward paths contain a given page. Counting within a reader rather than across readers is what makes the rule applicable here. A page's classification depends on how that specific reader uses it, and the count is available whether a domain has two readers or several thousand. Validated against Adressa, where the URL gives the answer, the maximal forward reference rule produces 96.9% balanced accuracy when pages appearing in fewer than three of a reader's paths are treated as content, recovering 97.1% of articles and 96.7% of front pages. Cuts between two and ten all give between 92% and 97%, so the threshold is not carrying the classification. A dwell-time cutoff in the manner of the reference length method Cooley et al. [1999], with the assumed share of navigational references set from the Adressa labels themselves, produces 63.8% on the same test in comparison.Page views were processed as in the main analysis. Consecutive views of the same page (with the same url) within 30 s were merged into a single visit (5.6% of views, against 1.7% on Adressa), sessions were separated at a pause of 300 s, and the remaining visits were restricted to the [5, 300) s window. This leaves 1642585 visits from 2141 participants, of which 1265 contribute at least 300 visits and 968 at least 500. Applied here, the maximal forward reference rule classifies 32% of views as content against 35% on Adressa. However, it does not separate page durations, with both the retained views and the discarded ones having a median of 8 s, whereas on Adressa the discarded views are substantially the shorter.

Pooled across the retained visits, the distribution of visit times is described by accumulation from a variable starting point (Supplementary Fig. S12a), and the model returns a median patience of θ = 4.20 across the 1265 participants with at least 300 visits, against 17.35 on Adressa. The fitted density follows the empirical one with one systematic departure, a shoulder at 180 s, where the observed density rises above the fitted curve and then falls below it within a few seconds, a feature the model has no mechanism to produce. Moreover, engagement times in this dataset fall on even values far more often than chance, 82% against 49% in Adressa. A second difference from Adressa is in what is being visited. The Adressa dataset is composed of a single newspaper, so every retained visit is to an article. Here the retained visits span the whole of a participant's browsing (news sites, video platforms, social networks, retailers, forums, web mail, etc.). A page on these is scrolled, watched or searched as readily as it is read, and the leaving decision the model describes need not govern how long a participant stays. The quantity fitted below is therefore the duration of a page visit rather than of an article read, and the parameters should be read with that in mind.

The start fraction is bimodal at the bounds, with 37% of participants reaching the upper bound, and a further 38% reaching the lower one, where the model reduces to a constant start and every visit requires the same evidence. On Adressa, 54% reach the upper bound and only 3% the lower. The participants reaching the lower bound are not badly fitted. Between each participant's visits and visits simulated at their own fitted values, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic has a median of 0.078 and no pants for whom the start fraction cannot be identified. A dataset suitable for a more comprehensive replication would need to combine per-participant observation depths comparable to those in Adressa with content consisting predominantly of news articles.

Second, the readers included in our analysis are, by construction, among the most active visitors to the site. Estimating three parameters for each reader requires sufficiently long behavioural records, which systematically excludes less engaged visitors. These readers may exhibit different foraging strategies, and their absence limits the extent to which our findings can be generalised to the newspaper's entire readership. In particular, the observed behavioural division may partly reflect the characteristics of highly engaged readers rather than the full range of reading behaviours.

Third, the information-foraging framework distinguishes three related problems Pirolli and Card [1999]. The patch problem, concerning time allocation within information sources. The scent problem, concerning the evaluation of information cues, such as headlines, before accessing an article. The diet problem, concerning the selection of articles from the available alternatives. Our analysis directly models the patch problem by examining how readers allocate their attention within articles. We indirectly observe aspects of the scent problem through readers' access patterns, but cannot fully decompose the processes underlying headline evaluation and article selection. We do not directly address the diet problem, since server logs do not provide a reliable reconstruction of the set of alternatives visible to each reader at any given moment. Consequently, our results characterise an important component of information foraging, rather than the complete decision-making process through which readers navigate an information environment.

Finally, the assessment floor is identified in terms of its ordering rather than its precise value for readers near the parameter bound. For these readers, the conditional group is therefore defined by the absence of a detectable assessment floor, rather than by an estimate indicating that the floor is small. This distinction matters for interpretation. The results establish a difference in the presence or detectability of an assessment floor, but do not necessarily imply that the underlying floor is negligible for every reader in this group.

2 Estimation

During the estimation process, three methodological choices were made (namely, the analysis window, the bounds placed on the parameters and the weight of the uniform contaminant). Each one of these could, in theory, be responsible for a result attributed to the data. This section reports the practical impact that each choice had in practice.

In theory, a fitted density assigns probability to reading times of any length, including those longer than the window. In practice, a large part of a reader's implied density falls below t max , so truncating there is not costly for most readers, but this holds to different degrees for different readers. Furthermore, twenty optimiser restarts return the same solution to four decimal places, and lowering the bound from 0.01 to 10 -4 moves where the estimates accumulate without dispersing them. For these participants the data provide no evidence of evidence gathered before the visit, so the variable-start parameters are not interpretable and they are excluded, as participants at the opposite bound are treated separately in the main analysis. This removes more than a third of the sample, and the behavioural result reported below does not hold without it.

Across the 787 remaining participants the assessment floor is again bimodal. A Gaussian mixture on the fitted parameters, which do not include the floor itself, separates them into 569 with a floor close to zero (median d min = 0.0007) and 148 with a substantial one (median 2.06). A third component of 67 participants, whose fits sit at the patience bound, is excluded as in the main analysis (Supplementary Fig. S12b). The group medians are similar to those obtained on Adressa, 0.0016 and 3.12. The two groups differ in the shape of their visit-time distributions in the way the assessment floor predicts, with the difference concentrated in the shortest visits (Supplementary Fig. S12c). They also differ in the share of visits ending within 10 s, 25.3% against 19.9%, a rank-biserial correlation of +0.37 (P = 2 × 10 -12 , Supplementary Fig. S12d). The direction is the one the main analysis reports, where the same contrast is 9.4% against 4.0% at a rank-biserial of +0.77. Computed over all 1265 participants, without the exclusion, the correlation is -0.10, a small effect in the opposite direction.

The navigational behaviour does not separate the groups. The analogue of returning to the front page is here inferred from the paths rather than read from the URL. That share slightly declines across the range of patience (partial rank correlation -0.06 controlling for the assessment floor, Supplementary Fig. S12e), but the two groups do not differ on it, 41.9% against 41.3% (rank-biserial -0.05, P = 0.35, Supplementary Fig. S12f). On Adressa the corresponding partial correlation is -0.29.

We attribute the difference to what is being measured rather than to the participants. The quantity fitted here is the duration of a page visit on any site, not of an article read. News accounts for 2.6% of this panel's browsing, and restricting the analysis to news domains leaves 8 participants above the threshold used in the main analysis, which is too few to fit. What survives the classification is dominated by social, retail and video sites, where a page is scrolled or watched rather than read, so the decision to leave is still being made, but over a different kind of content and plausibly on a different timescale. The median patience of 4.20 against 17.35, the failure of the classifier to separate page durations, and the absence of a navigational contrast are all consistent with this.

The division therefore recovers in a dataset collected in a different country, in a different year, by a different instrument, and over content that is largely not news, which argues that it is not an artefact of the Adressa dataset. Its behavioural signature is weaker and conditional on excluding participants for whom the start fraction is not identified. A dataset suitable for a full replication would require per-participant depth comparable to Adressa's together with content that is predominantly articles.

7 Limitations of the study

Several limitations qualify the interpretation and generalisability of our results.

First, the main dataset comes from a single regional newspaper, in one language, over a ten-week period. Consequently, we cannot establish whether the behavioural division we identify reflects a general feature of readers' information foraging behaviour or characteristics specific to this outlet and its audience. We examined the robustness of this finding using a second dataset collected in a different country, in a different year, using a different instrument, and covering content that was largely nonnews.
