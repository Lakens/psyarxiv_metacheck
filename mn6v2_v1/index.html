Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

First-person reports reveal a surprising convergence between symptoms of depersonalisation disorder (DPD) and certain forms of advanced meditation (AM). In both, individuals describe a fading, and even disappearance, of the ordinarily stable sense of being an embodied self. Yet the experiential tone and functional consequences strikingly differ. In DPD this alteration is endured as distressing alienation, while in AM it is deliberately cultivated towards psychological transformation and flourishing. We propose that this difference stems from opposite configurations of interoceptive permeability along the same computational axis. In DPD, defensive attenuation of ascending interoceptive precision limits the influence of bodily evidence on higher-level inference and stabilises a detached self-model. In AM, sustained practice increases the influence of bodily evidence on higher-level inference, allowing interoceptive signals to reshape and at times loosen the processes that sustain the sense of self. We account for meditation-related depersonalisation, a documented adverse effect of contemplative practice, by emphasising that increasing interoceptive precision without the regulatory conditions that render it tolerable reproduces the configuration that anchors the DPD pole. Finally, we outline the insula-prefrontal axis as a plausible substrate for this regulation. Clinical neuroscience has not characterised the regime in which bodily evidence constrains the self-model without recruiting defensive withdrawal, and advanced meditation provides a foundation from which to address this gap.

1. Introduction

The seamless integration of mind and body provides a quiet backdrop to conscious thought, supporting the ordinary sense of being an embodied subject. Yet, this integration can sometimes be disrupted. In recent years, neuroscience has turned to the farther reaches of subjective experience, examining states where this unity is disrupted or radically transformed. The profound distress associated with the clinical condition known as depersonalisation disorder (DPD) and the altered states achieved through advanced meditation (AM) provide informative cases for examining alterations to the baseline sense of self.

At first glance, DPD and AM could hardly seem more distinct. DPD is endured as a distressing condition, marked by alienation and an unwanted sense of being cut off from one's own body and actions (Medford et al., 2005). AM is typically pursued as a deliberate practice oriented towards insight and, in many accounts, psychological flourishing (Bodhi, 2000; Burbea, 2025; Dahl et al., 2020). Despite these differences, a closer examination of DPD's and AM's phenomenology reveals a striking resemblance in the structure of self-experience.

Ordinary experience carries a tacit sense of being the one to whom it is happening: the body is inhabited by a subject, who perceives itself through that body, initiates actions with it, and experiences these actions' consequences. Because it constitutes the theatre of experience, this sense is rarely itself noticed, and its absence is correspondingly difficult to report. One patient with DPD captures this with unusual clarity: "I feel like a robot, like I am listening to someone else talking, like I am looking at myself from the outside, but it is not another voice or body -it is mine, it is me, it just doesn't feel like it" (Baker et al., 2003, p. 432). The body is recognised, but the pre-reflective sense of mineness -the felt immediacy of being the subject of experience (Billon, 2013) -is strongly attenuated. A similar shift is sometimes described in accounts from advanced meditators: "the body is so spread that it's very difficult to know where it is and what it is", and "there's no personal point of view, it's the world point of view, it's like the world looking, not me looking" (Ataria et al., 2015, p. 141). Such reports evidence how the normally implicit sense of being a bodily subject, located at a particular point of view, recedes, leaving awareness unanchored to a clearly defined centre of perspective or ownership.

How can the loosening of the self lead to outcomes as different as a clinical disorder riddled with anxiety and states of reduced or absent suffering? DPD provides a model of how the sense of self can pathologically disintegrate, leaving experience marked by unreality and distress. Conversely, certain forms of AM present a case of how the self may be deliberately loosened or deconstructed, often accompanied by reports of clarity, equanimity, or well-being. Though DPD and AM are seemingly opposite in their affective tone and lived significance, their phenomenological overlap provides an opportunity to examine them within a shared computational framework.

Previous work within computational psychiatry and computational phenomenology frameworks has successfully applied predictive coding and active inference to DPD on the one hand (Ciaunica & Safron, 2024; Ciaunica et al., 2022; Gerrans, 2019; Saini et al., 2022; Seth, 2013), and to AM on the other (for a review and discussion see Tal et al., 2026). In both domains, a particular emphasis is placed on the role of precision weighting in shaping embodied self-experience, especially in relation to interoceptive signals. Nevertheless, the two literatures have rarely been brought into systematic dialogue despite the shared framework, a notable exception being Deane and colleagues (2020).

Here, we propose that DPD and AM can be understood as opposing configurations of a single computational architecture, differing in the degree of constraint that bodily information places on self-modelling, that is, in how strongly interoceptive prediction errors are permitted to propagate upward and revise the self-model. Framing these states as divergent configurations of embodied constraint accounts for their distinct experiential profiles and functional consequences, while also helping to explain why DPD and AM may be so differently valenced. Importantly, this framing also suggests a route by which meditation may lead to distressing experiences of no-self akin to those reported by patients with DPD: practice can increase the weight given to bodily signals faster than the capacity to integrate them develops. This mismatch in timing is, in principle, amenable to formal modelling. This paper is organised as follows: we begin by introducing self-modelling as a function of allostatic regulation, understood as the brain's anticipatory control of the body's physiological state. We then review computational accounts of DPD and of AM, integrating them into a single continuum of embodied constraint. We focus on depersonalisation as a phenomenon rather than on DPD as a diagnostic category because it facilitates isolating the mechanisms pertaining to the self (as opposed to, for example, derealisation, the sense of unreality that often accompanies DPD as a diagnostic category). This allows the mechanisms to be generalised to instances where depersonalisation may be experienced in the absence of the syndrome, such as in panic, in post-traumatic states, and following psychedelic use or intensive contemplative practice (Britton, 2019; Frewen et al., 2015; Hugemark et al., 2026; Lindahl et al., 2017; Wilkhoo et al., 2024). Similarly, while we acknowledge that different meditation practices may impact differently on the selfmodel, we focus on deconstructive practices, in which the structure of experience is itself taken as the object of inquiry. Constructive and devotional trajectories may engage distinct computational mechanisms and lie beyond the continuum proposed here (Dahl et al., 2015). Finally, we propose the insula-prefrontal axis as a plausible neural substrate, and close by considering what this account implies for the recalibration of interoceptive processing in psychopathology and for the study of interoceptive capacities beyond ordinary functioning.

2. Interoception, Allostasis, and the Phenomenology of the Self

Embodiment, understood as the sense of inhabiting a body and perceiving the world from a bodily perspective, is widely regarded as the most elementary layer among the multiple dimensions that constitute selfhood. This basic sense of a bodily self is actively constructed through the modelling of interoceptive signals (Blanke & Metzinger, 2009; Gallagher, 2000). Interoception refers to the sensory system that conveys signals about the body's internal physiological state to the brain, providing a continuous stream of information that is integrated into higher-level cortical representations (Craig, 2009). The insular cortex plays a particularly central role in this process, supporting representations of the body's physiological state that are sufficiently abstracted to bear directly on self-modelling and affective experience (Craig, 2009; Seth, 2013). Through this ongoing modelling of internal bodily signals, the organism maintains a pre-reflective sense of being an embodied subject.

Within predictive coding accounts, interoceptive processing can be framed as a hierarchical dialogue between top-down predictions about the body's expected internal state and bottom-up error signals conveying changes to physiological conditions. Within this generative architecture, lower levels of the hierarchy contain inferential mappings that link ascending visceral signals to representations of the body's immediate physiological state; at higher levels, these signals are progressively integrated with contextual and conceptual structures, supporting their categorisation into affective categories and cultural interpretations (Barrett, 2017). The mappings that support this categorisation are themselves acquired and shaped by the regulatory history in which they were developed (Barrett, 2017; Barrett & Simmons, 2015). Across the interoceptive hierarchy, the influence of prediction error on higher-level mappings depends on its precision, understood as the system's confidence in the reliability of that signal. Indeed, it is precision weighting of the different model parameters which determines the extent to which interoceptive evidence influences higher-level beliefs about the self. Normal functioning involves a dynamic balance between updating the internal model in response to prediction errors (i.e., perceptual inference) and attenuating those errors when they are deemed uninformative or unreliable (Seth, 2013; Seth et al., 2012; Seth & Tsakiris, 2018). In this sense, accurate prediction of interoceptive signals constitutes an immediate functional goal insofar as minimising prediction error supports a stable and coherent internal model of the body's condition.

At a more fundamental level, however, this local balance between updating and attenuation can be understood as serving a broader regulatory purpose beyond sensory accuracy. From an evolutionary perspective, the primary challenge faced by biological systems is the preservation of physiological integrity in the face of environmental uncertainty (Sterling & Laughlin, 2015). The principle driving this process is therefore not merely homeostatic (reactively correcting deviations from a fixed setpoint) but allostatic, namely the predictive regulation of the body to achieve 'stability through change' (Sterling, 2012). Within the active inference framework, allostasis is formalised as an inferential process through which the system selects actions (i.e., policies) expected to satisfy deeply entrenched prior expectations concerning physiological viability (McEwen & Wingfield, 2010; Pezzulo et al., 2015; Sterling, 2012). Crucially, this requires a generative model that can anticipate not just immediate sensory consequences, but also future physiological demands across multiple timescales, and initiate appropriate responses before they arise. Through this anticipatory modelling, the system proactively guides behaviour towards states that are conducive to survival and minimises uncertainty about its internal milieu (Friston et al., 2017; Pezzulo et al., 2015).

Beyond the preservation of physiological viability, this anticipatory regulatory process constitutes the physiological foundation upon which any sustained engagement with the external environment depends. By continuously monitoring and modulating the internal milieu, interoceptive inference also sustains a basic distinction between the organism and its environment. This distinction underpins the most elementary sense of being a bounded and embodied self (Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Engelen et al., 2023; Pezzulo et al., 2015). How that distinction is maintained becomes apparent at the higher levels of the interoceptive hierarchy, where interoceptive predictive inference shapes the allostatic strategies that anticipate and prepare for upcoming physiological demands. Ascending interoceptive signals registered at lower levels then provide ongoing feedback on whether those strategies are achieving their expected outcomes, allowing the highlevel model to refine its predictions over time through a regulatory loop coupling visceromotor commands with their afferent consequences (Barrett, 2017). The outcome of this loop is itself modelled, yielding an estimate of the system's own capacity to bring its physiological state under control (Stephan et al., 2016), and thereby of the extent to which the body falls within the domain over which it can act. In ordinary circumstances, this regulatory loop provides fast and efficient heuristics for guiding action, as changes in heart rate or visceral tone signal whether ongoing behaviour is consistent with expected physiological viability, allowing the organism to prioritise or revise actions without extensive deliberation (Barrett, 2017; Seth, 2013; Sterling, 2012).

Against this background, a prominent perspective in contemporary neuroscience holds that both the phenomenology of the self and core affective experience emerge from the interplay between interoceptive inference and allostatic regulation (Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Engelen et al., 2023; Fotopoulou & Tsakiris, 2017; Pezzulo et al., 2015; Seth, 2013; Seth & Friston, 2016; Seth et al., 2012; Seth & Tsakiris, 2018). On this view, the self-model is inherently affective because the predictions through which the brain regulates the internal milieu are experienced as affect, which reflects the momentby-moment status of bodily regulation and colours the sense of what it is like to be embodied at a given moment (Barrett, 2017; Barrett & Simmons, 2015). Emotions, in turn, are conceived as the outcome of an inferential process that categorises these interoceptive predictions according to context and prior learning, resulting in distinct feeling states (Barrett, 2017; Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Seth & Tsakiris, 2018).

Taken together, this perspective characterises selfhood as a dynamic and ongoing process rooted in the brain's imperative to regulate the body and preserve physiological integrity. When interoceptive signalling is disrupted, the phenomenology of embodied self-experience is correspondingly altered. Such alterations, and the allostatic conditions that produce them, are central to the phenomena examined in this manuscript.

3. The Disembodied Self: Computational Models of Depersonalisation

Depersonalisation is a psychopathological condition characterised by a persistent and distressing alteration in the subjective sense of self. While mild and transient depersonalisation episodes are common in the general population, particularly under conditions of stress or fatigue (Lee et al., 2012), more severe chronic forms of DPD are frequently associated with a previous history of anxiety and panic disorder (Mendoza et al., 2011; Michal et al., 2005), and commonly co-occur with post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and schizophrenia (Frewen et al., 2015; Wilkhoo et al., 2024). The unifying phenomenological thread is an estrangement from felt experience, where one's body is recognised yet no longer fully felt, and perception, though intact, loses its 'aliveness' or emotional colour. This estrangement is grounded in a selectively diminished autonomic reactivity to emotional stimuli (Giesbrecht et al., 2008; Sierra et al., 2002; Sierra et al., 2006), which weakens the interoceptive feedback that normally conveys affective salience while leaving interoceptive accuracy intact (Michal et al., 2014). Such blunting has been interpreted as an extreme form of defensive self-preservation wherein the organism downregulates autonomic arousal when faced with traumatic circumstances perceived as inescapable (Sierra & David, 2011). This blunting is accompanied by a consistent neural asymmetry, with heightened engagement of prefrontal control systems alongside reduced recruitment of limbic and interoceptive regions, particularly the anterior insula (Lemche et al., 2007; Medford et al., 2006; Phillips et al., 2001; Sierra & David, 2011). Functionally, this pattern is understood as a prefrontal dampening of the autonomic and affective responses that would otherwise reach awareness; however, how prefrontal suppression of interoceptive signalling produces these alterations in embodied self-experience remains unspecified.

Active inference accounts of DPD address this gap by locating the disruption in interoceptive processing, while differing in the hierarchical level at which they take this disruption to become decisive and, correspondingly, in which aspect of the disturbance they are positioned to explain. At the lowest level, the disruption concerns the integration process of bodily evidence. Seth and colleagues proposed that the sense of presence arises when informative interoceptive signals are successfully explained away by top-down predictions, and that in DPD pathologically imprecise interoceptive predictions produce an indiscriminate suppression of interoceptive prediction error, leaving the world perceptually intact but drained of felt reality (Seth, 2013; Seth et al., 2012). Gerrans (2019) relocates the key disruption to the level of self-attribution, where it concerns ownership: if the function of a self-model is to predict and explain fluctuations in affect, a world that fails to elicit the expected affective response will be accommodated by withdrawing ownership and construing the experience as alien. Accordingly, the sense of mineness (Billon, 2013) is the first casualty. The failure identified by Seth thus propagates upward and destabilises the self-model's explanatory function. Moving a further step up the hierarchy, Deane and colleagues (2020) locate the main disruption at the level of agentive self-regulation, where it concerns the system's estimate of its own capacity to control the outcomes on which it depends. In this account affective valence partly reflects whether prediction error is falling faster or slower than expected, and the system reads that signal as evidence of the degree of control it exerts over its own circumstances. When prediction error continues to rise regardless of the system's attempts to reduce it, confidence in its courses of action collapses, leaving no basis on which to select among them. The result of such disruption is experienced as a loss of affective grip on the world and of the sense of being the agent of one's own states.

The interoceptive silencing account (Saini et al., 2022) addresses two questions that have received less attention in the previous accounts: why the disruption can arise in apparently safe circumstances, and why it persists once established. On this account, DPD is a dissociative policy adopted in response to paradoxical arousal, a state in which interoceptive signals indicate high threat while exteroceptive signals register no corresponding external danger. When re-experiencing circumstances similar to those that originally caused the paradox, the system may be left without an available inference capable of reconciling the bodily alarm with the apparent environmental safety. Consequently, the system may engage in a systematic downregulation of the ascending interoceptive prediction errors through active modulation of precision weighting, thus preventing bodily signals from updating higher-level models. This in turn results in a loss of the felt quality of the body from the phenomenal self and produces the characteristic disembodiment of DPD. Crucially, this attenuation operates at the level of ascending interoceptive prediction error, leaving higher-order evaluative and narrative frameworks structurally intact but deprived of the bodily evidence that would normally ground them.

4. The Deconstructed Self: Computational Models of Advanced Meditation

Advanced meditation is an umbrella term encompassing a range of contemplative traditions, techniques, and developmental trajectories (Matko & Sedlmeier, 2019; Sparby & Sacchet, 2022). Interest in AM has increased markedly as meditation research has extended beyond basic practices, notably mindfulness, and their health-supporting mechanisms (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026a , 2026b; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026). While basic meditation practices often emphasise attentional stability or emotion regulation, AM refers to later-stage practices in which the primary aim shifts towards investigating the structure of experience itself (Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021; Sparby et al., 2026). AM thus extends beyond the familiar distinction between focused attention and open monitoring into the systematic inquiry into the processes that generate perception and the sense of self (Dahl et al., 2015; Josipovic, 2014; Lutz et al., 2008). Deconstructive practices in particular target the loosening of habitual interpretative frameworks through mechanisms such as sustained attention and meta-awareness, which allow experience to be apprehended in a more immediate manner (Farb et al., 2007; Pagnoni, 2019). As a result, deconstructive practices are frequently associated with profound alterations in self-experience, including attenuated identification with emotions and bodily sensations, as well as changes in agency and self-boundaries (Nave et al., 2021; Sparby et al., 2026). These alterations include gradual shifts sustained across practice and discrete culminations described as meditative endpoints (Laukkonen et al., 2023; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025).

From a computational perspective, deconstructive AM appears to alter self-experience through a deliberate modulation of inferential precision. Specifically, sustained attention directed onto immediate sensory signals raises precision at lower levels of the generative hierarchy while lowering the weight carried by high-level autobiographical priors (Farb et al., 2015; Jamieson, 2016). These priors typically sustain a temporally deep, conceptually structured narrative self which integrates autobiographical memory and abstract identity models (Gallagher, 2000; Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021). As precision is progressively withdrawn at the higher levels of the hierarchy, inference becomes increasingly dominated by lower levels encoding rapidly updating sensory evidence, particularly interoceptive and proprioceptive signals, shifting the locus of active modelling from the narrative towards the experiencing self.

Reducing the temporal depth of self-related prediction also alters how affect is constituted. Interoceptive perturbations are ordinarily categorised through temporally deep priors that draw on past experience to assign emotional meaning (Barrett, 2017; Barrett & Simmons, 2015). Withdrawing precision from higher-level priors implies that physiological arousal is encountered before conceptual evaluation. Bodily signals are then perceived as bare and minimally elaborated sensations, without the preferential stance that categorisation ordinarily supplies. Because these categorisations are learned (Barrett, 2017), sustained practice may in principle also revise the mappings through which interoceptive signals take on emotional meaning (Lutz et al., 2019; Pagnoni, 2019). A further step in this deconstructive process concerns the cultivation of meta-awareness, which enables increasingly fine-grained control over attentional precision. According to Sandved-Smith and colleagues (2021), training in meta-awareness involves learning to modulate the precision of attentional policies, essentially treating the mind's own modelling activity as trackable data. This account was later extended to include the global field of precision-deployment via hyper-modelling, permitting epistemic depth: namely, the capacity to know what one knows (Laukkonen et al., 2025). These mechanisms explain how a meditator may be able to observe subtle fluctuations in attention and the early stages of evaluative appraisal as phenomena generated by the brain rather than unmediated reports about the world or self. By perceiving modelling operations as constructed, the practitioner gains access to the mechanisms by which sensations are shaped into percepts and affective states, resulting in a deeper understanding of how the self-model is maintained and can be relaxed. When these capacities are sufficiently cultivated, precision may begin to be withdrawn from the higher-level predictions that stabilise the narrative self -a phenomenon described as defabrication of the self-model (Prest & Berryman, 2024). As precision is incrementally withdrawn from these highest hierarchical layers, the stability of the cross-temporally integrated self-models diminishes, allowing greater access to the underlying sensory and affective dynamics that support conscious experience.

Precision modulation is, therefore, the mechanism through which contemplative practice may alter the degree of embodied constraint on self-modelling, without compromising the allostatic regulation that self-modelling serves. Contemporary contemplative science treats such transformations as the outcome of learnable and structured training regimes, developed over extended practice and embedded within broader attentional and regulatory contexts (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025). The sections that follow address the conditions under which these regimes operate, and the consequences of pursuing them without those conditions in place.

5. Embodied Constraint on Self-Modelling: A Computational Continuum

Against this background, we propose that, despite their divergent origins and experiential profiles, depersonalisation and AM occupy opposing poles of a single computational continuum, defined by the degree of embodied constraint on selfmodelling, that is, how strongly moment-by-moment interoceptive prediction errors reach and revise the higher-level beliefs through which they are integrated into the self-model (Figure 1). Embodied constraint constitutes a continuous functional dimension, along which different allostatic strategies can be deployed depending on contextual demands and inferred controllability. It is modulated through precision, which conditions the permeability of hierarchical inference to embodied evidence and thereby the extent to which interoceptive evidence can revise the beliefs it reaches. Ordinary waking experience occupies a dynamically balanced position along this dimension, with interoceptive signals granted context-sensitive authority over the models that receive them.

5.1. The Two Poles

At one end of the continuum, depersonalisation is a defensive allostatic strategy recruited when high-level interoceptive inferences become persistently uninformative or counterproductive as guides for action. Specifically, depersonalisation minimises embodied constraint by attenuating the precision of ascending interoceptive evidence and preventing higherlevel likelihood updating, thereby precluding narrative self-model revision. This may occur when the higher-level likelihood mappings that translate bodily signals (ordinarily a fast and efficient guide for action; Barrett, 2017; Seth, 2013) into categorisations of physiological state come systematically to misrepresent the allostatic significance of bodily arousal, so that each interoceptive fluctuation renews a demand for control that cannot be straightforwardly satisfied. For example, during episodes of panic or stress, which are among the most frequently reported precipitants of DPD (Frewen et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2012; Wilkhoo et al., 2024), intense bodily arousal may be miscategorised as signalling urgency or threat despite the absence of any corresponding environmental evidence (Hunter et al., 2014).

Once such a configuration is established, one viable response is to reduce the precision afforded to interoceptive prediction errors, thereby limiting the extent to which these signals can update higher-level beliefs (Peters et al., 2017). This downweighting operates at the afferent stage of the interoceptive hierarchy, attenuating the channel itself rather than revising the higher-level likelihood mappings that produce the unhelpful inferences downstream. This represents a computationally tractable solution, given that correcting the miscalibrated mappings would be a slow process requiring learning. A marked attenuation of precision on the ascending interoceptive channel suppresses the influence of bodily evidence on higher-level inference and effectively disengages the system from its own physiological readout. Notably, reducing the precision of the priors receiving the evidence would equally neutralise those unhelpful inferences while leaving the channel intact; however, that route requires endogenous control over one's own high-level priors, whereas attenuating interoceptive gain draws on an operation the system routinely performs.

With repeated deployment, this strategy may become entrenched through habit formation, increasing the prior probability of selecting a dissociative policy (Saini et al., 2022). This allows initially episodic responses to become progressively chronic, with ever smaller deviations in bodily state triggering the same regulatory response. Crucially, the higher-level likelihood mappings producing maladaptive threat inferences are not corrected through this process; instead, they are systematically protected from revision, as downweighting the afferent channel deprives them of the evidence needed for perceptual learning, locking the system into a stable configuration in which the dissociative policy becomes increasingly dominant. This stability, however, is achieved at a cost, since suppressing interoceptive evidence erodes the sense of presence (Seth, 2013), disrupts emotional tone (Gerrans, 2019) and undermines the sense of agency (Deane et al., 2020), leaving a self-model that remains coherent yet emotionally blunted, experienced as detached or diminished in affective tone.

The same regulatory architecture can be recruited towards the opposite inferential end: at the AM pole, precision is modulated so that the self-model is updated based on bodily evidence. Even where mappings are well calibrated, the interoceptive heuristics of ordinary regulation may constrain the granularity with which bodily states are modelled and regulated, collapsing signals into coarse-grained categories aimed at rapid behavioural response. Under conditions of environmental safety and low allostatic demand, where immediate action is unnecessary and bodily fluctuations no longer demand correction, interoceptive signals can be engaged more directly (Pagnoni, 2019). This supports an alternative allostatic strategy oriented towards exploration (Friston et al., 2017), in which interoceptive prediction errors are afforded greater precision and shape inference across multiple hierarchical levels. Repeated exposure of these mappings to evidence ordinarily filtered out may progressively refine them, with effects that could generalise beyond the meditative session (Farb et al., 2015).

While the phenomenology of AM varies along the trajectory of practice, the defabricatory stages of deconstructive traditions exemplify particularly well the precision asymmetry that characterises this pole (Mago et al., 2024; Prest & Berryman, 2024; Tal et al., 2026). At these stages, the relaxation of top-down constraints and the precision afforded to interoceptive prediction errors are both at their most extreme, marking the maximally exploratory endpoint of the continuum, directly opposed to the defensive attenuation anchoring the DPD pole (Figure 2). The temporally extended, narratively organised self-model temporarily relaxes, allowing experience to unfold with reduced conceptual mediation as inference becomes increasingly driven by present-centred sensory updating (Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021; Millière, 2020).

5.2. The Asymmetry of the Continuum

Interoceptive permeability, as conceptualised here, is not merely a matter of signal propagation, since interoceptive prediction errors can reach the higher levels of the interoceptive hierarchy without necessarily updating the mappings that operate there. Because signal propagation and model revision are functionally separable, we use 'permeability' specifically to denote the extent to which higher-level mappings are updated by incoming interoceptive evidence (Figure 3). In anxiety and panic, for instance, interoceptive signals are afforded high precision and exert correspondingly strong influence on inference, where minor physiological variation acquires disproportionate salience (Paulus & Stein, 2006). The mappings themselves, however, remain intact as arousal arrives already categorised as threat, rapidly triggering a corrective response that confirms the threat-assessment before any competing evidence intervenes. This threat reading may in turn be sustained by higher-level priors that represent the situation itself as unsafe (Paulus et al., 2019). Thus, high interoceptive precision determines the weight data carries in inference, but precision is not on its own sufficient to revise the mappings that receive it. The continuum is therefore asymmetric: lowering interoceptive precision suffices to reach the DPD pole, whereas raising it does not by itself reach the AM pole.

What mapping revision may require in addition is an interval in which interoceptive evidence reaches higher-level mappings while the ordinarily triggered corrective response is withheld. This interval may expose the existing categorisation to disconfirming evidence. Crucially, while anxiety and panic close this interval once the interoceptive signal has reached the higher-level mappings, depersonalisation blocks it beforehand. Interestingly, contemplative practice appears to target this interval directly. The commitment to physical stillness, common across many meditative traditions, is thought to act as a strict prior on motor output. This constraint narrows the space of available behavioural policies, allowing bodily fluctuations to be sampled without immediate behavioural consequence (Laukkonen & Slagter, 2021; Lutz et al., 2019; Pagnoni & Guareschi, 2021). Within this constrained setting, deliberately suspending corrective responses widens the interval further: tolerance for interoceptive arousal develops as prior beliefs regarding safety and controllability prevent bodily fluctuations from being categorised as allostatic threats (Pagnoni, 2019; Tal et al., 2026).

When the corrective response is withheld, the operations categorising bodily signals are no longer immediately discharged into action; instead, they become accessible as objects of observation in their own right. The capacity to observe them represents the meta-awareness introduced above, modelled as an additional inferential level operating orthogonally to the predictive hierarchy (Sandved-Smith et al., 2021). As meta-awareness matures, precision constraints at higher conceptual levels relax, biasing inference towards more granular sensory sampling (Prest & Berryman, 2024). Over time, the system sustains this open interval through its own reflexive modelling of inferential activity, rather than relying on external constraints such as physical stillness. High interoceptive gain can thus be maintained without triggering defensive attenuation.

5.3. Opacity and the Divergence of Phenomenal Tone

Alongside its inferential function, meta-awareness involves a distinct phenomenological dimension: the extent to which a system registers its own representational processes beyond their content. In computational terms, a representational process is considered transparent when only its content is available to experience, and opaque when the process itself becomes accessible to awareness (see also Laukkonen et al., 2025; Limanowski, 2014; Metzinger, 2003). This difference may correspond to the precision afforded to the meta-observational channel through which the process is modelled (Sandved-Smith et al., 2021). DPD and AM instantiate distinct patterns of opacification, shaped both by their respective precision regimes and by what those regimes construct over time.

At the DPD pole, meta-awareness is preserved, but it is shallow, as interoceptive signals are stripped of epistemic authority, leaving affective inference without its sensory basis (Medford et al., 2005). Here, precision on the metaobservational channel is sufficiently high for the subject to register the ongoing alteration, generating the familiar 'as-if' quality of the depersonalisation experience and patient reports of feeling like a detached observer (Baker et al., 2003). Crucially, however, this precision remains insufficient to expose the generative operations behind the alteration. Although depersonalisation is characteristically accompanied by hyper-reflexivity and obsessional monitoring of internal states (Ciaunica et al., 2022; Deane et al., 2020; Sass et al., 2013), this activity is directed at a self-model deprived of interoceptive evidence and therefore has little on which to operate. The result is a partial opacification, in which the alteration itself becomes available to awareness while the mechanisms producing it remain inaccessible (see also Ciaunica et al., 2021). Consequently, these alterations appear within experience as a fact about how things now are (notwithstanding the preserved reality testing typical of depersonalisation experience), rather than as a configuration the system is producing.

At the AM pole, precision is withdrawn from higher-order conceptual and narrative priors, and meta-awareness is proactively cultivated simultaneously across attentional operations, evaluative and narrative priors, and the modelling activity that constructs the self-model. Here, the opacification extends beyond the contents of these processes to the generative processes themselves. As a result, the configurations sustaining the self-model appear within experience as configurations produced by the system (Lutz et al., 2019; Sandved-Smith et al., 2021). At its most refined, even the sense of being the subject of experience is itself disclosed as a constructed process within the field of awareness (Bonamino et al., 2026; Prest, 2026).

The divergence in phenomenal tone between DPD and AM follows from this difference in reflexive reach. Where the reflexive system registers an alteration without reaching the underlying processes producing it, this alteration is encountered as something happening to the person, an acquired quality. Indeed, with no access to the source of the alteration, the person undergoing the experience has no means of modulating it, and the phenomenon acquires the character of an imposition. This is the experiential ground of the unreality and distress that characterise DPD. By contrast, where the reflexive system reaches the processes themselves, the alteration appears instead as a configuration the system is enacting and could enact otherwise. The visibility of these processes is what allows the alteration to be sustained and deepened, supporting both dereification and the affective presence uncoupled from compulsive identification that characterise AM (see also Deane et al., 2020).

5.4. When the Conditions Are Not Met: Meditation-Related Depersonalisation

The asymmetry between the two poles of the continuum may have direct consequences for how bodily precision can be manipulated in practice. Because accessing the meditative configuration depends on an interval in which interoceptive evidence reaches higher-level mappings while the ordinary corrective response is withheld, amplifying interoceptive salience without maintaining that interval would fail to move the system towards exploratory openness and would instead reproduce the very conditions that anchor the DPD pole. This is most clearly illustrated by meditation-related depersonalisation, among the most frequently reported adverse effects of contemplative practice, with prevalence estimates ranging from nine to twenty-five per cent depending on population and assessment method (Britton, 2019; Cebolla et al., 2017; Lindahl et al., 2017; Schlosser et al., 2019; Wright et al., 2024). Such episodes may reflect the partial opacification described above, occurring because the underlying components reorganise on different timescales. While practice can heighten interoceptive precision relatively quickly, the reflexive capacity needed to recognise this shift as a process develops only through sustained training. Similarly, the higher-level mappings that integrate interoceptive signals with context update only gradually.

Early in practice, these three components can fall out of step: bodily signals arrive with heightened precision while the operations reweighting them remain outside reflexive reach, and the body may begin signalling a transformed state that existing conceptual mappings cannot assimilate. Detecting a change but being unable to attribute it to its own modelling activity, the system reaches the reflexive configuration of the DPD pole from the opposite direction. Consequently, the altered content is treated as a problem to be fixed, recruiting pragmatic rather than epistemic action where the system acts to change the content that feels wrong rather than sampling new data to learn how it came about. When pragmatic action fails to resolve the mismatch, dissociative suppression may become the next available policy.

The severity of this misalignment varies with practice conditions. Recent reviews suggest that practices aiming to deconstruct the sense of self are most likely to trigger depersonalisation experiences, particularly when undertaken at high volume and intensity, without balancing practice, and lacking the perspectival framing needed to interpret such experiences as benign rather than threatening (Ehmann, Sezer, et al., 2025; Hugemark et al., 2026). Attending closely to internal states in the absence of safety, tolerance, and sufficient meta-observational precision is likewise more liable to intensify arousal or recruit protective policies than to support flexible updating (Lathan et al., 2023; Levin et al., 2022). Crucially, all these adverse conditions widen the gap between the rapid rise of interoceptive precision and the slower maturation rate of the integrative capacities needed to accommodate such a rise.

The baseline state in which a practitioner begins further compounds this gap. Meditative practice presupposes internal mappings adequate to support ordinary regulatory demand. However, if those mappings already categorise intense bodily signals as threat, increasing interoceptive precision supplies further input magnifying threat-based inference. The resulting defensive response described at the DPD pole is thus the expected outcome, reached through practice rather than through typical clinical stressors (see also Deane et al., 2020).

From this perspective, depersonalisation following practice may be understood as a form of overreaching: a reorganisation attempted faster than the necessary integrative scaffolding can develop, much as injury follows training that outpaces bodily adaptation. In such cases, meditation-related depersonalisation would constitute an acute expression of the same mechanism that anchors the depersonalisation pole, differing mainly in its trigger rather than in the system's underlying response. Progressing towards the AM pole therefore requires the gradual cultivation of supportive conditions, extending beyond the individual practitioner to the broader ethical, attitudinal, and relational context in which practice is embedded, a dimension that remains to be modelled.

6. Towards a Neurobiological Model: The Insula-Prefrontal Axis as a Precision Regulator

At the neurobiological level, the proposed continuum can be situated within the interoceptive system described by predictive accounts of allostasis. Agranular visceromotor cortices, spanning the cingulate cortex, the posterior ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and the ventral anterior insula, issue the allostatic predictions through which bodily arousal acquires its anticipated significance. Meanwhile, the mid-posterior insula computes the resulting prediction error, propagating it back to the regions where those predictions originated (Barrett & Simmons, 2015). The higher-level mappings whose miscalibration anchors the depersonalisation pole are plausibly instantiated in this visceromotor circuitry, whose cytoarchitecture affords limited sensitivity to prediction error and correspondingly slow revision.

Within this system, the anterior insula is widely regarded as the principal cortical hub for interoceptive integration (Craig, 2009; Critchley et al., 2004; Seth, 2013). It is thought to encode the expected precision of ascending interoceptive prediction errors, thereby setting how far bodily evidence propagates through the hierarchy (Critchley et al., 2004; Seth, 2013; Zhang et al., 2024). Lateral prefrontal regions, occupying higher positions in that hierarchy, exert contextual modulation over this process, regulating the gain afforded to ascending signals through attentional selection and inhibitory control (Farb et al., 2015; Parr et al., 2020; Seth & Friston, 2016).

Within this network, DPD displays a characteristic imbalance: hyperactivation of the lateral prefrontal cortex, coupled with anterior insula hypoactivation (Lemche et al., 2007; Medford et al., 2006; Phillips et al., 2001). Interventional studies support the directional causal role of this axis: inhibitory stimulation of the lateral prefrontal cortex reduces depersonalisation symptoms, whereas excitatory stimulation induces depersonalisation-like phenomena (Geerts et al., 2015; Jay et al., 2016; Jay et al., 2014; Jiménez-Genchi, 2004). A comparable fronto-insular configuration is observed in healthy individuals during suppression of negative affect (Tabibnia et al., 2008), locating the emotional detachment of DPD within the spectrum of an otherwise adaptive regulatory mechanism. At the computational level, this configuration attenuates the precision of ascending interoceptive evidence, decoupling the self-model from the bodily signals that would ordinarily constrain it, yielding the affectively blunted, disembodied quality of experience characteristic of the disorder (Saini et al., 2022).

Meditation engages the same circuitry, driving it towards the opposite configuration. Early stages of practice are associated with increased anterior insula activation and reduced medial prefrontal engagement, consistent with greater weight afforded to ascending interoceptive evidence and reduced weight on high-level priors (Farb et al., 2010; Farb et al., 2007). At the network level, this shift manifests as reduced engagement of default mode regions implicated in highlevel self-referential processing alongside increased engagement of salience network regions anchored in the anterior insula (Brewer et al., 2011; Farb et al., 2013; Lutz et al., 2008; Prakash et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2010). Preliminary evidence supports the centrality of insular engagement, as transient inhibition of the anterior insula was associated with a reduction in the subjective intensity of experience during meditation (Cain et al., 2024).

Crucially, this pattern characterises the early stages of practice rather than later meditative development, when prefrontal recruitment appears to diminish as interoceptive regulation becomes more efficient (Ehmann, Sezer, et al., 2025). In the most advanced stages, including meditative cessation and self-dissolution, insular and prefrontal activity may both decrease or uncouple, plausibly reflecting reduced prediction error demand rather than active suppression (Chowdhury et al., 2023; Shinozuka et al., 2025; van Lutterveld et al., 2024; Yang, Chowdhury, et al., 2025; Yang, Kadambi, et al., 2025). This trajectory aligns with what the present account predicts. Reaching the meditative configuration requires establishing integrative conditions through training, initially held in place by deliberate attentional control. As these conditions come to be sustained by the practitioner's own modelling of inferential activity, the executive control that established them is no longer required, and prefrontal recruitment declines accordingly.

The two configurations therefore recruit overlapping circuitry engaged in opposite directions. In DPD, the balance between lateral prefrontal control and anterior insular engagement shifts towards top-down attenuation, leaving the selfmodel with little bodily evidence to draw from; in AM, the same regions are recruited towards interoceptive opening while preserving regulatory stability. Pathological interoceptive silencing and cultivated interoceptive opening thus likely draw on the same fronto-insular architecture, consistent with the continuity of these two configurations at the neurobiological level.

7. Discussion

The account we have developed treats depersonalisation and AM as opposing configurations of a single allostatic architecture. When bodily signals become uninformative or actively destabilising, suppressing ascending evidence offers a computationally viable workaround, of which depersonalisation represents an extreme instance (Saini et al., 2022; see also Deane et al., 2020). AM practices instead intentionally expose the self-model to ascending evidence, allowing top-down priors to be updated flexibly. We have argued that dissociative suppression develops when persistent bodily arousal cannot be attenuated through pragmatic action, turning into an entrenched regulatory habit through repeated recruitment (Saini et al., 2022). By contrast, the meditative configuration is intentionally cultivated, and depends on environmental safety and low allostatic demand, conditions that contemplative training systematically establishes and maintains under most, but not all, conditions (Pagnoni, 2019; Tal et al., 2026). Once established, the suppressive configuration forms a self-reinforcing loop as downweighting ascending interoceptive prediction errors prevents bodily evidence from reaching higher-level threat representations, insulating top-down expectations from corrective perceptual learning. This revision failure progressively increases the prior probability of deploying a dissociative policy under physiological arousal (Saini et al., 2022).

Recalibrating the higher-level mappings therefore requires restoring ascending prediction error. However, it is crucial to do so under conditions that prevent the defensive response, as, based on our account, readmitting interoceptive evidence while higher-level mappings remain miscalibrated would simply reinstate the initial threat response. Indeed, where the prior on dissociative suppression is already strong, the readmitted evidence overwhelms the system, which defaults back to the same protective policy (Lathan et al., 2023; Levin et al., 2022), instead of learning. Rather than more interoceptive evidence, what is required is an interval between signal and response, as it allows the system to register automatic threat categorisations as self-generated inference rather than objective bodily properties (Sandved-Smith et al., 2021). Clinical progress therefore depends on the sequence in which these conditions are established, and contemplative practices deployed without adequate scaffolding risk reinforcing the dissociative policy they aim to reduce.

Clinical neuroscience has historically studied mainly one side of this regulatory continuum. Research has characterised in detail the spectrum ranging from the suppression of interoceptive evidence in DPD to the hyper-reactive states seen in panic and trauma (Paulus & Stein, 2006). However, by focusing on populations defined by regulatory failure, this research effectively treats ordinary functioning, defined merely as the absence of symptoms, as the upper boundary of interoceptive capacity. The configuration in which high interoceptive precision constrains the self-model strongly without triggering defensive withdrawal remains largely unmodelled. Advanced meditation provides the empirical access point for this uncharacterised configuration, extending the theoretical models of self-regulation beyond the traditional boundaries of clinical pathology (Chowdhury et al., 2023; Laukkonen et al., 2023).

Standard empirical instruments carry a similar blind spot. Existing measures of interoceptive accuracy, sensibility, and awareness focus primarily on signal detection and subjective confidence (Garfinkel et al., 2015; Khalsa et al., 2018; Suksasilp & Garfinkel, 2022), indexing whether a signal reaches conscious awareness rather than whether it updates high-level priors. Patients with DPD perform comparably to healthy controls on heartbeat detection tasks, and symptom severity does not correlate with task performance (Michal et al., 2014). The disturbance therefore appears to lie beyond the detection of bodily signals, which is consistent with a failure to integrate interoceptive evidence into high-level selfmodels. Similarly, experienced meditators match non-meditators in objective interoceptive accuracy while differing mainly in the confidence they place in their performance (Khalsa et al., 2008). In both cases, detection tasks leave unexplained a difference that the present account locates in integration, a process these tasks were not designed to measure. Progress in this domain will require tasks able to measure how far interoceptive evidence revises higher-level beliefs about the body, independently of how accurately that evidence is detected, for instance by tracking how the predictions associated with a bodily signal change once the outcomes they anticipate repeatedly fail to occur.

A parallel limitation exists in computational psychiatry, where psychopathological conditions are routinely conceptualised as deviations in precision weighting across different levels of the predictive hierarchy (Paulus et al., 2019), with therapeutic success defined almost exclusively as a return to a baseline functioning. However, leaving parameter regimes beyond this baseline unmodelled leaves current frameworks unable to account for the upper bounds of human interoceptive capacity. Contemplative traditions provide a systematic methodology for navigating this parameter space, which conventional clinical interventions rarely reach. In this sense, the strength of meditative techniques lies in their ability to modulate interoceptive precision while concurrently regulating model permeability. The observation that long-term training produces enduring changes in self-experience and affect (Lutz et al., 2019; Prakash et al., 2025) suggests that the upper bounds of interoceptive capacity are considerably more plastic than current psychiatric models assume. Mapping the proposed continuum, therefore, may provide computational psychiatry with a formal perspective on interoceptive recalibration, showing how the limits of self-regulation can inform our understanding of its breakdown.
