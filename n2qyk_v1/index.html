Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Full text

Abstract

Dyslexia persists into adulthood. However, promising evidence on reading interventions for adults is limited. Their efficacy may depend on how the difficulty of the activities is modulated, which requires identifying the stimulus features that determine difficulty, manipulating them to build levels, and defining rules for progressing between levels. This narrative review examined these three aspects in reading interventions for adults with dyslexia, searching PubMed, ERIC and Google Scholar, and additional sources, up to October 2026. Six studies (Italian, Hebrew and Canadian English; 172 adults with dyslexia) were included. All modulated time constraints on reading, designed as stimulus exposure duration, speed of letter-by-letter erasure, or tempo of a rhythmic pacemaker. Only one study also varied linguistic features (syllabic structure, orthographic complexity and morphological structure), while lexical frequency was controlled. Progression rules relied on accuracy in comprehension questions or in item reading and were of three types: thresholds on a set of items (80% to 95%), rule-based on latest responses, and trialby-trial adjustment. The size of the step was reported in three studies, while no study compared different modulation procedures. These findings provide a framework for designing and testing difficulty modulation in future reading interventions for adults with dyslexia.

Introduction

Specific learning disorders (SLDs) are lifelong neurodevelopmental disorders, affecting learning abilities in specific domains and despite average or above-average intelligence. These disorders are not caused by intellectual, sensory, or neurological deficits, but by difficulties in the way the brain processes information related to specific tasks, such as reading, writing, or arithmetic (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Grigorenko et al., 2020). In addition to dysorthographia and dyscalculia, SLDs include dyslexia, commonly defined as a specific reading disorder characterised by challenges in decoding written text, reading fluency, and/or reading comprehension (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cancer et al., 2026; Vender & Delfitto, 2025). Worldwide, the prevalence of developmental dyslexia is estimated around 7% in childhood (Yang et al., 2022) while the prevalence in adulthood is under-investigated and broadly estimated around 4% (Soriano-Ferrer & Piedra Martínez, 2017).

SLD diagnoses during childhood are generally associated with persistent difficulties into young adulthood. In detail, a longitudinal study following a sample of American English-Speaking readers from 5 to 42 years of age reported that the performance gap between readers with and without dyslexia persists well in adolescence and adulthood (Ferrer et al., 2023).

A recent systematic review found encouraging evidence in favour of interventions delivered to improve reading skills in adults with dyslexia (Vender et al., 2022b). This secondary study underlined a promising efficacy of reading interventions in adults with dyslexia (Vender et al., 2022b), although available evidence is limited. For example, in a randomized controlled trial on 20 Italian-speaking university students with dyslexia receiving an intervention based on 15 hours of video game play over 12 days and high-frequency transcranial random noise stimulation (hf-tRNS) over posterior parietal brain regions or sham stimulation, authors found greater improvements sustained over time in word and nonword reading accuracy in the hf-tRNS group than in the sham group (Bertoni et al., 2024).

Intervention efficacy is related to task specificity (Breznitz et al., 2013; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender et al., 2022b), intensity (Vender et al., 2022b), intervention activities' difficulty (Breznitz et al., 2013), difficulty progression in intervention delivery (Cheema et al., 2022; Vender & Delfitto, 2025). Accordingly, users' motivation, relying on an appropriate level of difficulty by adjusting difficulty levels, is commonly considered as crucial to support learning and engagement in users exposed to gamified activities (Darzi et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2024; Mortazavi et al., 2024). In this scenario, difficulty modulation is currently often considered as an active ingredient of reading interventions. The following paragraphs will analyze difficulty modulation in terms of items' features considered to develop activities with different difficulty levels, and progression rules across difficulty levels.

First, identifying the items' features determining difficulty activities of reading interventions should be a starting point to design reading interventions (Breznitz et al., 2013; Darzi et al., 2021; Vender & Delfitto, 2025). For example, a feature commonly used to modulate difficulty in reading training is the time constraint, which is the setting of the available time to process each stimulus. Time constrained reading should promote faster reading than the reader's habitual pace (also referred to as reading acceleration). Tightening the time constraint (less available time) increases difficulty, whereas relaxing it decreases difficulty (Breznitz et al., 2013). Second, designing interventions' activities relying on the modulation of items' features supports the development of reading interventions with different levels of difficulty (Vender & Delfitto, 2025), allowing brain plasticity to be stimulated by creating a discrepancy between an individual's cognitive abilities and the cognitive demands of the task (Lee et al., 2024; Plass et al., 2019). Finally, modulating the level of difficulty of intervention activities seems to shape the difficulty of the intervention (Cheema et al., 2022; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025), promoting engagement and compliance of readers exposed to intervention programs (Breznitz et al., 2013; Vender & Delfitto, 2025).

Given the promising but limited evidence available on reading interventions' activities for adults with reading difficulties, this study conducted a literature review to investigate which item features are considered to determine activities' difficulty (Breznitz et al., 2013; Vender & Delfitto, 2025), the manipulation of these characteristics to develop reading activities at different difficulty levels (Cheema et al., 2022; Vender & Delfitto, 2025), and methodology of difficulty progression across levels with different difficulty (Cancer et al., 2026; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016).

Objectives

This work focuses on the study of the difficulty modulation of reading interventions in adults with dyslexia focusing on the items' features manipulated to determine difficulty, the development of reading activities with different levels of difficulty, and progression rules across activities with different levels of difficulty. Specifically, we targeted three distinct research questions (RQ): • RQ1: Which are the items' characteristics manipulated to determine the level of difficulty of reading intervention activities?

• RQ2: How reading activities with different levels of difficulty are developed in reading interventions? • RQ3: How is progression determined across different difficulty levels within reading interventions?

Methods

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

A systematic narrative review of the literature was conducted for this study (Kranz et al., 2024). Literature search relied on three different databases: PubMed, ERIC, and Google Scholar. Given the exploratory aim of the current study, the search string was restricted to the title field. Search strings were tailored to each search engine. For PubMed, the string was: ("dyslexia"[Title]) AND ("adult"[Title]) AND ("intervention"[Title] OR "training"[Title]); for ERIC: [(dyslexia OR dyslexic) AND (adult) AND (intervention OR training)]; and for the advanced Google Scholar search: allintitle: dyslexia adult intervention. Searches in the databases ended on 1 October 2026.

The inclusion criteria required studies to have been published within the previous 15 years (between January 2011 and September 2026); to be written in English; and to have been conducted in a clinical or experimental setting; to involve adult participants (aged at least 18 years). Studies whose samples also included older adolescents (16-17 years) were considered eligible when most participants were adults, because adult-specific evidence on reading interventions is limited. Grey literature (unpublished records) has been included as well. Studies also had to report data on improvements in reading accuracy and speed of lexical-or sentencelevel stimuli and provide information on the difficulty of the rehabilitation activities delivered. We therefore included only studies describing how the difficulty of the intervention activities was modulated. Studies were excluded if they did not provide information on stimulus' difficulty, the structure of different difficulty levels, and/or training procedures and progression.

Literature screening and data extraction

Based on the eligibility criteria, two authors (GF and AP) independently conducted the selection of studies. Conflicts were solved by one senior author (MC). Following screening, the same authors extracted data from the studies included in the review. For each included study, the same authors (GF or AP) extracted: (i) study characteristics (language of the intervention, sample size, age and study design); (ii) intervention characteristics (duration, total number of sessions, session length, type of stimuli and outcomes); (iii) the items' features determining difficulty, and whether they were manipulated across levels; (iv) the procedures used to develop difficulty levels, and (v) progression rules to move across activities with different difficulty level. These data are summarised in Tables 1 and 2.

Consistent with the aim of this review to describe how difficulty was modulated rather than to estimate intervention effects, no formal appraisal of methodological quality or risk of bias was carried out. The design and sample of each included study are reported in Table 1 to let readers interpret the strength of the evidence.

Use of artificial intelligence tools

Claude (Anthropic; Claude Sonnet 5.5, October 2026) was used to double-check the consistency between text, tables and figure, to verify the information extracted from the included studies compared to their full texts. All AI-assisted content was reviewed, verified against the original articles and edited by the authors, who take full responsibility for the manuscript. AI tools were not used to create or edit research data.

Results

The literature search identified 53 studies. Of these, six studies met our inclusion criteria and were thus included in the current literature review. No discordances have been reported on inclusion decisions across authors who screened the records. See Figure 1 for the PRISMA flow diagram (Page et al., 2021) and Table 1 for a summary of included studies.

Together, the six included studies enrolled 172 adults with dyslexia, with two studies also including typical readers (N = 91) exposed to the same intervention (Breznitz et al., 2013); n = 55; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; n = 36). Participants were young adults, with mean ages between 24.1 and 29.0 years (Cheema et al., 2022; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016), and age ranges between 16 and 35 years; 22-29 (Breznitz et al., 2013 ), 19-26 (Cancer et al., 2026 ), 18-35 (Di Dona et al., 2026), and 16-30 (Vender & Delfitto, 2025) in the remaining studies. (Vender & Delfitto, 2025) included participants aged 16-30 years (M = 20.59) and was retained according to eligibility criteria for samples with older adolescents described in the Methods. When reported, participants were university students (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cancer et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016) or mostly high-school or university students (Vender & Delfitto, 2025).

Included records employed different research designs: sham-controlled randomised trials with reading interventions combined with brain stimulation (Cancer et al., 2026; Di Dona et al., 2026), a randomized comparison between two interventions (Cheema et al., 2022), a controlled study with a self-paced reading condition (Breznitz et al., 2013), and two before-after studies (Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025). All records were published in international peer-reviewed journals except for a doctoral thesis (Cheema et al., 2022).

Included studies delivered reading interventions in Italian (Cancer et al., 2026; Di Dona et al., 2026; Vender & Delfitto, 2025), Hebrew (Breznitz et al., 2013; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016), and Canadian English (Cheema et al., 2022).

The interventions differed in duration, number and frequency of sessions, and session length. Total duration ranged from two weeks (Cancer et al., 2026) to about one month (Di Dona et al., 2026) and eight weeks (Breznitz et al., 2013; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025). The current literature review showed that evidence on the characteristics determining items' difficulty in reading exercises relies on four features: reading time constraints (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cancer et al., 2026; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025), syllabic structure (length and complexity), orthographic complexity, and morphological structure (Vender & Delfitto, 2025).

Time constraint was the most frequent feature modulated to develop reading intervention activities with different levels of difficulty (six of the six included studies). Time constrained reading was designed in three ways, i.e. as the time during which each whole item were displayed in the screen (Vender & Delfitto, 2025); as the speed of letter-by-letter text erasure or fading, set individually from baseline reading speed (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016); or as the tempo of an external rhythmic pacemaker accompanying reading (Cancer et al., 2026). In all cases, shorter available time corresponded to higher difficulty.

Then, only one included study described linguistic features of the stimuli (Vender & Delfitto, 2025). In its "rapid reading" activity, participants read and rewrote syllables, words or nonwords displayed for a limited time (1,200-100 ms). Each task comprised stimuli balanced for the number of syllables, syllabic structure and lexical frequency, and the following features were taken into account for difficulty modulation: (i) syllabic length, from two to five syllables, kept constant within each task; (ii) syllabic complexity, from simple syllables only to both simple and complex syllabic structures; (iii) orthographic complexity, defined by orthographic groups that are difficult for readers with dyslexia (e.g. "sce" in scena, "schie" in schiena) and by groups that are phonologically or orthographically similar to them (e.g. gli/li, chi/ghi/che/ghe); and (iv) morphological structure, with the presence of simple or derived words. Lexical frequency and syntactic category were controlled rather than varied. Indeed, words were drawn exclusively from the 7000 most frequent words of the New Basic Vocabulary of the Italian Language (De Mauro & Chiari, 2016), selected for their high frequency in non-specialist written texts, and most were nouns, followed by verbs and adjectives, in line with their prevalence in the Italian lexicon.

RQ2: How reading activities with different levels of difficulty are developed?

The studies included in this review adopted different approaches for the manipulation of items' features to design intervention activities with different difficulty levels.

The most frequently manipulated items' feature is the modulation of time constraint of the time available to read items' activities (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cancer et al., 2026; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025). Linguistic features were varied systematically in one study only (Vender & Delfitto, 2025). Two further studies varied the type (Cancer et al., 2026) or length (Cheema et al., 2022) of the stimuli.

Specifically, looking at the time constraint available to read stimuli, all six studies designed difficulty levels by tightening the reading time constraints. In five of them, time constraints' setting is individually tailored following participants' reading speed individually measured during a pre-intervention baseline assessment (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025), while a record modified the tempo of the rhythmic pacemaker according to performance and gradually increased the difficulty (Cancer et al., 2026).

Regarding the manipulation of linguistic features, Vender and Delfitto (2025) built levels by manipulating syllabic structure, orthographic complexity and morphological structure. In detail, syllabic length increased from two to five syllables across levels and kept constant within each level. Then, syllabic complexity moved from simple to complex structures, and orthographic complexity increased too by introducing difficult orthographic groups sequentially and then combining them with phonologically or orthographically similar groups. Also, morphologically complex words were added from the sixth session, with suffixes introduced according to their frequency. These features changed along a pre-defined sequence of levels and sessions, and activities were selected for each participant on the basis of baseline literacy skills and performance during training. RQ3: How is progression across different difficulty levels determined within reading training activities?

Included studies modulated the progression of intervention activities' difficulty through the manipulation of time constraint available to read target stimuli (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cancer et al., 2026; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025); in Vender and Delfitto (2025) it also involved linguistic features, and in Cancer et al. (2026) the stimuli type.

Reading time constraints varied following different progression criteria concerning performance index, decision rule, and quantitative size of the step progression.

In detail, in four studies, progression relied on performance considered as accuracy in the comprehension question that followed each sentence (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016), whereas in the other two it depended on the accuracy with which the activity's items were read (Cancer et al., 2026; Vender & Delfitto, 2025).

Then, three types of rules emerged among included studies. First, participants had to achieve an accuracy threshold to access to a more difficult level of activity ranging from 80% (within a range of 1200-100 ms; Vender & Delfitto, 2025) to 95% (Cancer et al., 2026). Second, three studies required a series of correct responses relying on the latest items completed. Di Dona et al. ( 2026) analyzed the previous 10 comprehension answers (tightening the constraint when a 100% accuracy scores was achieved, leaving it unchanged between 80% and 100% and relaxing it below 80%). Similarly, Horowitz-Kraus (2016) tightened the time constraint only after 10 consecutive correct answers, while Breznitz et al. (2013) used a staircase method based on the number of consecutive correct answers, without reporting that number. Third, in one case the progression relied on the last performance only, with a continuous modulation where time constrained tightened after each correct answer and relaxed after each incorrect one (Cheema et al., 2022). Notably, three studies explicitly allowed the time constraint to be relaxed after poor performance (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026).

Finally, the quantification of a step size for the progression across levels was reported in three studies. The size of the progression step was calculated in terms of absolute (milliseconds) scores (Breznitz et al., 2013) or relative (percentage) values (Cheema et al., 2022; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016). Breznitz et al. (2013) stated the progression was modulated by tightening or relaxing the reading time constraint of 2 ms per letter, according to individual performance. Similarly, Horowitz-Kraus (2016) developed a progression system by modulating (tightening or relaxing) the reading time constraints of 2% per sentence. Lastly, Cheema et al. (2022) designed a modulation model where the progression of time constraint was modulated of 6% per item according to participants' performance. No included study compared different progression rules or step sizes.

Discussion

This work investigated the latest scientific evidence on the definition of difficulty levels in reading intervention activities, focusing specifically on (a) the items' characteristics that determine the level of difficulty of reading interventions' activities; (b) the development of reading activities with different levels of difficulty; and (c) how progression is determined across activities with different difficulty levels. To sum up, six articles met eligibility criteria and were thus included in the current literature review. Reading time constraint is the most common considered feature of reading intervention's items, with syllabic structure, orthographic complexity and morphological structure rarely considered. Similarly, in all included studies, reading time constraints is modulated to create progressively more complex levels within intervention's activities, after being individually tailored (following a reading speed value measured during a pre-intervention baseline assessment) in five of six included studies. Finally, progression across difficulty levels relied on the modulation of reading time constraints in all included studies, determined by different performance index, decision rule, and quantitative size of the step progression. Despite the small number of publications, these preliminary findings should be interpreted as the starting point towards guiding future larger and more robust studies in the development of both intervention activities across different difficulty and the related progression across activities with different difficulty levels.

Regarding the items' characteristics manipulated to determine the level of difficulty of reading intervention activities, the included studies have mainly focused on modulating reading time constraints, with one study reporting information on considering items' linguistic features, i.e. syllabic length, syllabic complexity, orthographic complexity and morphological structure (Vender & Delfitto, 2025). The evidence suggests that items' characteristics can be broadly divided into those linked to a language's orthography (e.g., syllabic length and orthographic complexity) and those largely independent of (e.g. reading time constraint). Consequently, findings concerning orthography-related features are difficult to generalise to other languages or regard as representative of them. Language-specific studies are therefore needed to guide the development of reading interventions in a particular language. Within this framework, it would be useful to design and report detailed information on the manipulation of items' characteristics determining activity difficulty (e.g. reading time constraints, orthographic complexity syllabic complexity) in order to collect reliable high level of evidence associated with detailed information on the structure of items' features within intervention activities.

Concerning how these characteristics are manipulated to develop intervention activities with different difficulty levels, reading time constraints are modulated in all included studies (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cancer et al., 2026; Cheema et al., 2022; Di Dona et al., 2026; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025), with one study reporting the modulation of linguistic features, and two studies underlining a less systematic manipulation of stimuli with different stimuli type (Cancer et al., 2026) or sentence length (Cheema et al., 2022). One potential advantage of modulating reading time constraints in reading intervention's activities is that the evidence may be adapted across languages. Specifically, increasing or decreasing this parameter can readily be applied to individuals reading languages with different orthographies, with the aim of promoting reading automaticity by increasing reading speed. However, the current review included evidence collected on three languages (namely Italian, Hebrew, and Canadian English). Thus, comparative findings across languages with different orthographies (Vender et al., 2022a) in terms of transparency (e.g. mainly opaque or transparent orthography) should test potential differences of time constraints' modulations across languages. Additionally, another potential advantage is the relative feasibility of implementing time-constrained reading's feature when designing a reading intervention program, with different web-based open-access resources available nowadays. Hence, the manipulation of this feature seems to be the first step towards a tailored difficulty progression when designing reading interventions. Lastly, there is limited evidence on the manipulation of items' linguistic features to determine the difficulty of reading activities. Only one of the six included studies varied them systematically and simultaneously with the time constraint; as a consequence, their respective contributions could not be disentangled (Vender & Delfitto, 2025). In the others, the content of the levels was either not varied or changed less systematically, following a pre-defined sequence (Cancer et al., 2026; Cheema et al., 2022). A possible explanation is that linguistic features require a selection of the stimuli tailored for each language. Because these features depend on the orthography as well as language-specific evidence cannot be generalised to others, studies that vary them separately and in combination (in languages with different orthographic transparency) are needed. For example, the effect of the separate or combined manipulation of multiple items' characteristics (e.g. time constraint and items' linguistic features of activities') on intervention efficacy could generate reliable language-specific findings, together with evidence collected across languages with different levels of orthographic complexity, e.g. Italian and English.

Finally, the evidence on difficulty modulation within reading intervention programmes supports progression from one level to another relying on the manipulation of reading time constraints, following different procedures in terms of performance index (accuracy in the comprehension question related to sentence just read; reading accuracy with which the activity's items were read), decision rules (achieving an accuracy threshold, accuracy on a set of latest items, a continuous modulation item per item), quantitative size of the step progression (absolute or relative values, possibility of relaxing time constraints when poor performance occur). Although these preliminary findings involve different difficulty modulation designs, these findings can provide initial insights for more robust evidence on progression across activities with different difficulty levels to design effective interventions, tailored to participants' reading performance, with the aim to deliver challenging and stimulating but not too difficult intervention activities. Further studies testing different progression rules across reading interventions' activities could support participants' compliance and interventions' efficacy.

No causal interpretation can be drawn about difficulty modulation and intervention efficacy. Indeed, all included studies reported effects of these interventions in terms of reading accuracy or comprehension (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cheema et al., 2022; Horowitz-Kraus, 2016; Vender & Delfitto, 2025) and reading speed (Breznitz et al., 2013; Cancer et al., 2026; Di Dona et al., 2026). However, no studies tested the effects of the manipulation of different items' characteristics, difficulty levels' development or difficulty progression design on these outcomes. Therefore, any inference can not support a potential direction towards intervention efficacy when difficulty levels are manipulated. Hopefully, further studies will bridge this gap with experimental evidence to develop interventions' activities with different difficulty levels and test the related impact on reading interventions' efficacy. This issue is central because the difficulty of an activity must increase at a pace tailored to the reader. Indeed, if the increments are too small, the activity remains too easy and does not challenge the reader, while if they are too large, it becomes excessively demanding. Defining and analysing these variables should play a key role in the development of reading intervention programmes.

Moreover, the need for the effects of difficulty modulation on intervention efficacy aligns with the assumption that rehabilitation interventions should draw on not only therapists' knowledge and patients' values, but also on structured and evidence-based interventions (Sackett et al., 1996) to ensure consistency of care and provide increasingly detailed guidance for clinicians and patients. Given the limited and preliminary scientific evidence highlighted by the current literature review on difficulty modulation in rehabilitation interventions for adults with dyslexia, the scientific community could direct resources towards testing multiple approaches to progression between levels. For example, the single effects on intervention efficacy of the modulation of either items' features (e.g. time constraint, syllabic length, lexical frequency, and/or orthographic complexity), development of difficulty levels (e.g. time constraints, items' linguistic features) or different progression rules (e.g. testing the effect of relaxing time constraints when certain poor performance are achieved) remain open research questions. Similarly, the effects on intervention efficacy of the combination of the modulation of multiple items' features with the development of both difficulty levels (e.g. time constraints, items' linguistic features) and progression rules (i.e. performance index, decision rule, and size of the step progression) might shed light on how to design effective reading interventions.

Finally, multidisciplinary studies developing cut-offs and difficulty modulation through adaptive processes based on machine learning would also be valuable. In this context, machine learning systems implemented through transparent strategies, in which decision-making processes are clear and reproducible, could be used to compare adaptive difficulty modulation based on machine learning algorithms with modulation based on participants' empirical performance (e.g., Darzi et al., 2021). This could enable highly specific training tailored to the user. Both the method of difficulty modulation and its impact on the proposed training warrant investigation.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the search was restricted to title fields in three databases and to English-language records, so relevant studies may have been missed, and most included studies were identified through additional sources rather than through the database searches. Nonetheless, research with a focus on reading intervention activities in adulthood should have labelled their title with the selected keywords. Second, the review is narrative: studies were not selected systematically, and no formal appraisal of methodological quality or risk of bias was carried out. The current research design was selected given the limited evidence available in the literature, as well as to provide insights to design and develop research designs including difficulty modulation when delivering reading interventions in adulthood. Third, only six studies were included, with small samples, different languages, designs and outcomes, which limits comparability and generalisation. Fourth, in four of the six studies the intervention was combined with neuromodulation or embedded in multicomponent programmes, so the effects of difficulty modulation could not be isolated. Fifth, several parameters, such as the size of the progression step or the number of consecutive correct answers required, were not reported by all studies, and one record was a doctoral thesis chapter. Despite these limitations, the review provides a first description of how difficulty is modulated in reading interventions for adults with dyslexia.

Conclusions

This review identified a small body of evidence (six studies) on how difficulty is modulated in reading interventions for adults with dyslexia. Difficulty was modulated mainly by tightening a time constraint on reading, which was developed in three ways, whereas linguistic features such as syllabic length, syllabic complexity, orthographic complexity and morphological structure were systematically varied in a single study. Progression between levels was based on participants' performance, but rules differed in the performance index (comprehension or reading accuracy), in the decision rule (thresholds from 80% to 95% on a block of items, rules based on recent responses, or trial-by-trial adjustment) and in the size of the progression step, which was reported in only three studies.

No study compared different modulation procedures, and the training was often combined with neuromodulation or embedded in multicomponent programs, so the contribution of difficulty modulation to intervention efficacy cannot be established. Future studies should describe in detail how stimulus features, levels and progression rules are defined, and test them experimentally, including in languages with different orthographies. Until then, the present findings provide a descriptive framework rather than a basis for recommending specific modulation procedures.
