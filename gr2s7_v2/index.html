Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Contemporary psychology provides well-developed accounts of framing, memory accessibility, salience, cognitive control, fixation, flexibility, and stabilisation. However, their functional integration during the formation and persistence of a cognitive pathway remains less clearly specified. This paper proposes a systems-level architecture in which cognition is understood as the currently active configuration of functional relationships within a highly interactive system. The current frame of mind represents this wider configuration, within which deployment profiles regulate the relative weighting and organisation of cognitive processes and capacities, memory access remains selective and state-dependent, and available information acquires differential functional prominence through salience. Candidate pathways are evaluated against current constraints and may become progressively stabilised, while feedback, changing conditions, closure modulation, and hysteretic dynamics can reinforce, modify, or reopen the developing state. The architecture distinguishes what is represented within the wider memory landscape from what becomes functionally accessible and subsequently salient, while treating stabilisation, closure, and reopening as dynamically related rather than as discrete terminal stages. Its contribution lies in specifying how interactions among these functions can generate qualitatively different outcomes, including efficient commitment, fixation, cognitive reorganisation, insight, persistence, and reopening. It therefore provides an analytical representation of a recursive, history-dependent system and generates testable questions concerning how cognitive pathways become accessible, influential, evaluated, sustained, revised, or reopened.

1. Introduction

Contemporary psychology provides well-developed accounts of processes involved in interpreting situations, considering responses, reaching judgements, and solving problems. Research on insight has examined changes in problem representation and restructuring (Wiley & Danek, 2024), while work on cognitive control has examined the regulation of stability and flexibility under changing task demands (Egner, 2023). Related literature addresses memory accessibility, attentional priority, salience, fixation, evaluation, and persistence. What remains less clearly specified is how these functions interact within the continuously changing cognitive system through which information is received, interpreted, developed, and translated into thought or action. Hence, a systems-level account can locate established functions in relation to one another and examine how changes in their configuration generate different cognitive outcomes.

Human cognition does not encounter incoming information from a neutral starting point. At any moment, the cognitive system is configured by prior experience, accessible memory, expectations, affective state, current goals, attentional priorities, recent processing history, and the relative recruitment of available cognitive processes and capacities. Consequently, the same incoming information can acquire different significance under different configurations. Kaplan and Simon (1990) demonstrated that successful insight can depend on discovering an effective problem representation, allowing previously obscure features or relations to become functionally useful. Conversely, Bilalić et al. (2008) demonstrated that a familiar solution can continue to organise attention even while an individual searches for a better alternative. Related work on mental set similarly indicates that prior solution procedures can shape subsequent problem solving, facilitating or inhibiting insight depending on the relation between earlier and later problems (Öllinger et al., 2008). Accordingly, the central problem is how the cognitive system is configured when information is encountered.

The term frame is used throughout this paper in the broad, everyday sense of a frame of mind. It is treated as dynamic and system-wide rather than a fixed cognitive structure or predefined interpretive template. A fuller definition follows once the principal components of the architecture have been introduced. One important component is the memory landscape, meaning the wider terrain of stored knowledge, episodic experiences, associations, procedures, affective traces, and other relations potentially available to cognition. However, potential availability should be distinguished from functional accessibility. Memory contains substantially more information than can influence cognition at any particular moment, and accessibility varies with retrieval cues, context, prior activation, goals, and internal state. Hutchison's (2003) review of semantic priming demonstrated how associative and feature relations can alter the accessibility of represented information, while Summerfield and Egner (2009) highlighted the importance of expectation and attentional selection in determining processing priority. Hence, information can remain represented within the wider landscape while exerting little or no influence on the current cognitive episode.

The present architecture represents this selectivity through a memory interface. The term is used functionally rather than anatomically and refers collectively to the processes through which information is accessed and retrieved from the memory landscape, and through which ongoing experience can be encoded. Therefore, the interface is neither a fixed boundary nor a binary gate. Tulving and Thomson's (1973) principle of encoding specificity established that retrieval depends partly on the relationship between stored information and available cues. Accordingly, the interface can change with the current cognitive configuration, allowing different regions and relations within the same landscape to become more or less functionally available without requiring the landscape itself to change. The deployment profile is a second major component. Although individuals possess repertoires of cognitive competencies, these need not contribute equally in every episode. The deployment profile refers to their current relative weighting and organisation. A particular configuration could strongly recruit exploratory search, associative retrieval, perspective shifting, or discrimination, while giving greater or lesser weight to rapid convergence, inhibition, sustained attention, or other available competencies. The repertoire itself may remain substantially unchanged while its deployment changes rapidly.

Consequently, the same individual can process apparently similar information very differently under different conditions without corresponding changes in stable ability or knowledge.

Figure 1 provides the simplest useful representation of the proposed architecture. The taper represents the progressive narrowing of a broad field of cognitive possibilities into the more constrained form required for thought, judgement, or action. The memory landscape is shown in its entirety rather than as a restricted input stream, distinguishing what is potentially represented from what becomes functionally accessible under current conditions. The interface influences both what becomes encoded within the landscape and which relations become functionally accessible at a given moment, with accessibility varying continuously as the cognitive configuration changes. Similarly, the deployment profile is not a discrete processing stage. Its effects can extend across perception, retrieval, salience, evaluation, persistence, and output because changing the relative contribution of cognitive competencies alters several functions simultaneously. This allows the term frame to be defined more precisely. Within the proposed architecture, the frame represents an analytical snapshot of the current configuration of the cognitive system as a whole. Its form is determined by the processes and conditions currently in play rather than imposed by a pre-existing structure. The distinction from deployment profile is important because the frame refers to the wider cognitive configuration while the deployment profile refers specifically to the relative weighting and organisation of cognitive competencies within that configuration. Changes in deployment can therefore alter the frame, which also reflects memory accessibility, salience, affective and motivational conditions, expectations, current constraints, evaluation, stabilisation, and ongoing internal and external input. Moreover, because the frame reflects the current organisation of the system, even relatively small changes in access, weighting, salience, affect, or expectation can modify it, while larger changes can produce substantial reorganisation. Hence, a frame shift can be treated as a consequential reconfiguration of an already dynamic cognitive state rather than movement between fixed predefined structures. While the possible range of configurations remains large, this is ultimately constrained by the individual's cognitive repertoire, memory landscape, current conditions, and physiological limits.

Within this changing configuration, salience describes differences in the functional prominence acquired by available information. Accessibility does not guarantee salience because information may become available without receiving enough weight to alter the developing cognitive passage. Research on attentional priority similarly indicates that only a subset of available information is preferentially selected for further processing, with priority reflecting more than stimulus properties alone (Rust & Cohen, 2022). Conversely, changes in deployment, context, expectation, or active interpretation can cause an already represented relation to acquire greater prominence. Therefore, salience is relational rather than an inherent property of information. Once a weak relation becomes sufficiently prominent, it can be amplified or attenuated through subsequent processing by recruiting associated information, changing attentional priorities, or altering the developing interpretation.

Candidate pathways that acquire sufficient functional prominence can then be evaluated against the conditions governing the episode. Evaluation can involve logical coherence, compatibility with constraints, causal plausibility, feasibility, anticipated consequences, or other task-relevant requirements. Pathways that survive evaluation can become increasingly stabilised, which is necessary because effective cognition and action require some narrowing of the possibilities within active processing. However, commitment need not be final. Previous states alter subsequent processing, and entry into and departure from a stabilised state need not occur under equivalent conditions. Hysteresis is used here to characterise this history dependence, where once a pathway has stabilised, stronger or different disturbance may be required to reopen it than was required for it to be established. The resulting architecture is recursive rather than linear, where incoming external information is processed by a system that is already configured. Internally generated thoughts, retrieved memories, predictions, and previous processing outcomes can also enter the developing episode, while physical action changes the body or environment and produces further input, and current processing can modify the memory landscape through encoding. Consequently, the cognitive system changes while operating. Even an unsuccessful pathway need not simply disappear, because considering and rejecting it can become part of the recent cognitive history against which the next possibility is processed.

This systems perspective allows apparently different cognitive phenomena to be examined within a common architecture. Efficient commitment and fixation may differ partly in the calibration and persistence of the same stabilising processes. Judgements can change when information is accessed or weighted differently (Lord et al., 1979), while creative reorganisation can occur when prior experience has furnished the memory landscape with useful relations and the current deployment configuration allows a previously weak relation to become accessible and salient (Beaty et al., 2023). Therefore, the explanatory question shifts from asking which isolated mechanism produces each named phenomenon towards asking how the configuration of a common cognitive system changes across them. The architecture specifies relationships among memory landscape, selective memory interface, deployment profile, salience, evaluation, stabilisation, hysteresis, and closure modulation. These functions are analytically distinguishable but dynamically interdependent. Rather than reproducing the full complexity of cognition or treating these functions as discrete neural modules, the framework provides a systems-level representation through which cognitive pathways can be examined as they become selected, developed, stabilised, revised, or reopened.

2. Deployment Profile

The deployment profile describes the relative weighting and organisation of cognitive processes and capacities within the current frame of mind. Individuals possess broad repertoires of such capacities, including cognitive flexibility, inhibitory control, associative retrieval, sustained attention, evaluation, perspective shifting, discrimination, and rapid commitment, although these do not necessarily contribute equally to every episode. Research on cognitive control similarly indicates that the relative emphasis placed on stability and flexibility can change with task demands and recent processing history (Egner, 2023). Different situations can therefore recruit, suppress, prioritise, or sequence these capacities differently without corresponding changes in the underlying repertoire. For example, a familiar routine task could place greater weight on rapid retrieval, procedural fluency, sustained attention, and efficient closure, whereas a novel or ambiguous task may recruit broader search, associative retrieval, perspective shifting, inhibitory control, comparison between alternatives, or greater tolerance of uncertainty. Social situations may require different configurations again, including attributional reasoning, affective regulation, perspective taking, or empathic processing. Therefore, the important distinction is not which competencies an individual possesses, but how they are currently deployed. The same person can consequently display different cognitive behaviour across contexts while retaining substantially the same underlying capabilities.

The deployment profile should not be understood as a switch between discrete cognitive modes. All available competencies remain potentially recruitable, but their relative weighting can vary. A competency might dominate processing in one context, contribute weakly in another, or become functionally negligible where its weighting is sufficiently low. Related metacontrol research similarly indicates that cognitive-control biases can shift within individuals according to situational conditions rather than remaining fixed (Hommel & Colzato, 2017). Changes in deployment can also involve sequencing. For instance, exploratory search may initially dominate before giving way to discrimination, evaluation, or rapid commitment as the cognitive episode develops. Figure 2 illustrates this system-wide role. The deployment profile is positioned centrally because changes here can influence several functions simultaneously rather than acting at one point in a processing sequence. Different configurations can alter memory accessibility, salience, evaluation, stabilisation, and the regulation of closure or reopening. The deployment profile contributes to the current frame of mind but is not equivalent. The same underlying repertoire can support different cognitive outcomes. Strong weighting of exploratory search and associative retrieval can broaden the range of candidate relations entering consideration, whereas stronger weighting of rapid evaluation or commitment could narrow that range quicker. Neither configuration is inherently superior. Rapid closure can be adaptive where a familiar response is required, while prolonged exploration might be maladaptive where delay carries significant cost. Conversely, exploration may be advantageous where current interpretations are incomplete, misleading, or poorly matched to the situation. Cognitive effectiveness depends partly on whether the deployment profile is appropriately calibrated to current conditions, which is consistent with evidence that cognitive stability and flexibility can be regulated independently rather than as a simple trade-off (Geddert & Egner, 2022). This also avoids assigning particular competencies exclusively to categories such as creative, analytical, or routine cognition. Cognitive flexibility, associative retrieval, inhibitory control, sustained attention, evaluation, perspective shifting, discrimination, and rapid commitment can each be useful or disruptive depending on their relative weighting, timing, and interaction with the wider system. Accordingly, creativity emerges from configurations of capacities used elsewhere in cognition (Beaty et al., 2023).

Deployment profiles can change during an ongoing cognitive episode. Feedback, failed attempts, altered constraints, new information, affective change, or internally generated thoughts can modify the relative weighting of available competencies. Importantly, such changes need not be consciously initiated. Related metacontrol research indicates that cognitive-control biases can shift within individuals in response to situational factors (Hommel & Colzato, 2017). A cognitive episode can therefore become progressively more exploratory, evaluative, defensive, persistent, or convergent while remaining part of the same evolving frame of mind. Repeated deployment could also have developmental consequences, where frequently recruited competencies can become more fluent or readily available, whereas rarely recruited ones receive less practice. Hence, the relationship can be reciprocal, with the existing repertoire constraining possible deployment profiles while repeated deployment influences the future accessibility, fluency, and weighting of those competencies. The extent of this development remains an empirical question. The next section considers what these configurations make functionally available from the wider memory landscape by distinguishing stored potential from selective access.

3. Memory Landscape and Selective Access

The memory landscape refers to the wider structured terrain of knowledge, experience, associations, procedures, affective traces, and other stored relations potentially available to cognition. It is necessarily broader than the information that can influence cognition at any particular moment. Therefore, the focus here is on the distinction between what is represented within that landscape and what becomes functionally accessible under the current cognitive configuration. The landscape is not assumed to be uniform, as different regions can vary in density, depth, variety, contrast, organisation, accessibility, entrenchment, and representational structure. Some might contain densely interconnected relations supporting several routes of retrieval, whereas others may be more sparsely organised or dominated by comparatively coarse representations, consistent with accounts of structured search within semantic memory (Hills et al., 2012). Expertise can create highly differentiated and efficiently organised regions, while repeated use may strengthen familiar pathways and increase entrenchment around particular relations (Wiley, 1998).

The selective memory interface describes the processes through which the wider landscape becomes functionally engaged. It is not considered a single mechanism or anatomical boundary. Rather, it is a functional description of processes governing access, retrieval, suppression, and encoding under the current frame of mind. The interface is graded rather than binary. For instance, information can be strongly accessible, weakly accessible, peripheral, or effectively unavailable without being absent from memory. Retrieval depends partly on the relationship between stored information and currently available cues (Tulving & Thomson, 1973), allowing the same underlying landscape to support different cognitive episodes. Changes in deployment profile, attentional state, affect, goal structure, expectation, or context can alter which relations become accessible, while the memory landscape remains substantially unchanged (Summerfield & Egner, 2009). Moreover, previously peripheral information can become functionally available, while other relations recede in influence. Accordingly, the system can alter what information is available and how incoming information is interpreted, without requiring new information to be created or stored. Figure 3 illustrates how task constraints, personal factors, and selective processing interact within the current frame of mind. The selective effects of the current configuration are not limited to memory retrieval. Incoming sensory or contextual information is interpreted against the relations currently most accessible and influential within the system, so similar inputs can acquire different functional meanings under different cognitive states. For example, in an unfamiliar building at night, heightened vigilance may increase the accessibility and weighting of threat-related relations, causing an otherwise innocuous sound to acquire greater significance than under familiar or relaxed conditions, consistent with evidence that arousal can amplify the processing priority of already salient or goal-relevant information (Mather & Sutherland, 2011). Although the physical input has not changed, the configuration through which it is interpreted has altered. Selective access and salience are therefore closely coupled, where the relations made available by the current state influence which features of incoming information become prominent, while salient inputs can alter subsequent accessibility and weighting.

The interface is also bidirectional. Current cognition does not merely retrieve from memory. It can also alter the landscape through subsequent encoding. New experiences, internally generated thoughts, failed attempts, successful solutions, affectively significant events, and revised interpretations can all contribute to subsequent memory structure. For instance, a rejected pathway might cease to guide the present episode yet still alter future cognition by becoming part of the landscape against which subsequent processing occurs. This is one reason why the architecture is recursive rather than static.

The relationship between deployment and memory access is reciprocal rather than serial. While a deployment profile can influence what becomes accessible from the memory landscape, accessible information can alter salience, evaluation, affect, expectations, and subsequent deployment. The memory interface is therefore a changing zone of selectivity within an evolving cognitive system rather than a one-way filter between storage and processing. This distinction is especially important for thinking creatively. Rather than treating creativity as a unitary trait or property, the present account focuses on regulation that allows alternative relations and pathways to emerge and remain available long enough to be explored, evaluated, and developed. However, a rich memory landscape does not automatically produce such cognition because potentially useful relations can remain inaccessible, weakly weighted, or insufficiently salient. This is consistent with evidence that the organisation and accessibility of semantic memory can facilitate or constrain creative cognition (Beaty et al., 2023). Conversely, changes in deployment or selective access can make an existing relation newly influential without requiring new knowledge to be acquired. Hence, thinking creatively can depend both on what the memory landscape contains and on how effectively it can be accessed, reorganised, and brought into relation under changing conditions.

4. Salience

Accessibility alone is insufficient for a relation to influence the developing cognitive episode. Information can be functionally available while remaining peripheral, weakly weighted, or effectively unnoticed. In this paper, salience refers to the differential functional prominence acquired by accessible information. In other words, some relations become sufficiently influential to enter further consideration while others remain in the background. Salience is not treated as an inherent property of a feature, memory, or relation, nor as the output of a single dedicated mechanism. The same information can be highly salient under one configuration and comparatively insignificant under another, depending on the interaction between what is available and the current state of the wider cognitive system, consistent with evidence that processing priority reflects both stimulus-related and goal-dependent influences (Rust & Cohen, 2022). Potential contributors include relevance to current goals or constraints, structural or feature overlap, prior association, familiarity, recency, novelty, contrast, expectation violation, affective significance, learned value, frequency of prior use, and convergence of several individually weak cues. Affective and arousal states can themselves alter the competitive priority assigned to available information (Mather & Sutherland, 2011). These influences need not operate independently. For instance, a familiar relation can acquire prominence because it is strongly established and aligned with the current cognitive configuration, while a less familiar relation might become salient through converging weaker cues, strong contrast with an existing expectation, or increased relevance. Anderson (2016) indicated that learned value can shape attentional selection, while Mather and Sutherland (2011) proposed that arousal amplifies competition between representations, favouring information that is already perceptually salient or goal relevant. The present framework does not specify the neural or computational mechanisms through which such influences are integrated. Salience is treated at the system level as differences in functional prominence among already accessible relations. Accordingly, a new cognitive possibility need not require new information, as an existing relation can move from peripheral availability into functional significance because the configuration against which it is weighted has changed. Shifts in deployment profile, context, expectation, affective state, task demands, or active constraints can alter a relation's prominence without altering the underlying memory landscape. The same stored information can consequently support different interpretations, judgements, or actions under different system states.

Salience can also develop recursively. Initial detection of a weak relation may redirect attention or retrieval towards associated information, increasing access to supporting material and strengthening its prominence. Desimone and Duncan (1995) described attention in terms of biased competition, whereby representations gain or lose processing priority according to their relevance to current behaviour. Therefore, a marginal correspondence can become progressively more influential as related information is recruited, while the reverse can occur if subsequent information weakens its relevance, compatibility, or support. Hence, salience is better understood as dynamically amplified or attenuated than as simply present or absent. The support required for a relation to become sufficiently prominent is also unlikely to be fixed, varying with current goals, task demands, deployment profile, prior experience, affective state, and competing relations. Corbetta and Shulman (2002) similarly distinguished goal-directed attentional selection from reorienting towards behaviourally relevant or unexpected stimuli, illustrating how processing priority can shift with both current goals and incoming information. Familiar or strongly expected relations might only require relatively little additional support to dominate consideration, whereas weakly represented or unconventional relations may require stronger convergence. The same relation can therefore become salient rapidly under one configuration while remaining effectively unnoticed under another.

This account provides one route through which thinking creatively can occur. For example, a potentially useful relation may already exist within the memory landscape yet fail to influence cognition because it lacks sufficient salience. Beaty et al. (2023) demonstrated that the structure and accessibility of semantic memory can influence the relations available during creative cognition, supporting the distinction between possessing potentially useful information and that information becoming functionally influential. Changes in deployment, access, context, or expectation can increase a relation's prominence until it becomes competitive with a dominant pathway. Creative reorganisation can therefore emerge because an existing relation acquires different functional weight within the changing frame of mind. However, salience does not imply correctness. As Nickerson's (1998) review of confirmation bias illustrates, information compatible with an existing interpretation can receive disproportionate weight, even when competing evidence remains available. Hence, a relation might become prominent because it is familiar, emotionally charged, recently activated, or strongly compatible with the current interpretation, whereas a less salient alternative may ultimately provide the more coherent response. In this sense, salience determines which candidate relations become influential enough to enter evaluation, not whether they are viable.

5. Evaluation and Logical Coherence

Salience brings a candidate relation into consideration without establishing whether it is useful, viable, or appropriate. Evaluation concerns the processes through which a candidate pathway is tested against the conditions governing the current episode, including compatibility with explicit constraints, internal consistency, causal plausibility, feasibility, anticipated consequences, functional adequacy, or correspondence with known information. Kaplan and Simon (1990) showed how problem solving can depend on the constraints governing search and on whether an effective representation can be found within them. Logical coherence is therefore used here as an umbrella term rather than as a reference to formal logic alone. Although a candidate pathway may be sufficiently salient to attract attention, it could fail at evaluation due to it conflicting with a constraint, depending on an implausible causal relation, proving impractical, or otherwise failing to provide an adequate response. Equally, an unconventional pathway can remain viable where it satisfies the relevant conditions, and the evaluative standards need not change simply because the pathway differs from the currently dominant one. Moreover, evaluation should not be understood as a single final checkpoint. Different aspects of a candidate pathway can be assessed at different moments, while evaluation interacts recursively with salience, access, and deployment. In addition, a promising pathway could recruit further information and become more coherent, whereas new evidence can weaken an initially plausible interpretation. Evaluation is therefore dynamic and able to strengthen, modify, suspend, or reject a candidate rather than simply classify it as correct or incorrect. Coherence is not equivalent to correctness, because a familiar pathway may satisfy current constraints while alternatives remain unexplored or insufficiently salient (Bilalić et al., 2008). If the first coherent pathway is treated as definitive, further search can terminate before competing relations are adequately considered. Nickerson's (1998) review of confirmation bias similarly showed how an established interpretation can influence subsequent evaluation. Fixation can therefore arise despite competent reasoning because the limitation may lie not in evaluation itself, but in the range of pathways to which it has been applied.

Failure at this stage does not necessarily terminate the cognitive passage. A rejected candidate can redirect attention towards another salient relation, alter the weighting of current information, change expectations, or modify the wider frame. Where constraints have been misunderstood or changed, evaluation can also reveal that the reference conditions themselves require revision rather than the candidate pathway alone. Therefore, a rejected pathway is not cognitively neutral because considering and testing it becomes part of recent experience, altering what is subsequently expected, accessible, or salient. Hence, an unsuccessful attempt can influence subsequent processing even without becoming stabilised. In this way, evaluation contributes to the history dependence of the architecture by changing the conditions under which subsequent candidates are processed.

Evaluation should also be distinguished from certainty. In situations involving incomplete information, ambiguity, or open-ended problems, a pathway need only become sufficiently coherent to justify continued exploration. Cognition can therefore proceed on the basis of provisional adequacy rather than final proof. This is especially important for thinking creatively because a novel relation may initially appear less coherent than a familiar pathway simply because fewer supporting associations are active. If evaluation closes too rapidly around the first adequate interpretation, potentially useful alternatives may not receive enough time or support to develop (Bilalić et al., 2008). Conversely, postponing evaluation indefinitely would prevent convergence and action. Hommel and Colzato (2017) similarly emphasised regulating persistence and flexibility according to current demands rather than treating either as universally advantageous. Accordingly, productive cognition depends on regulating when and how strongly evaluation constrains the developing pathway.

6. Stabilisation, Closure Modulation and Hysteresis

A candidate pathway that survives evaluation can become sufficiently stabilised to support continued exploration, judgement, decision, or action. Stabilisation refers to the point at which a pathway acquires sufficient persistence to remain active rather than being displaced by competing possibilities. However, it should not be equated with final closure, because a stabilised pathway can remain provisional, continue to develop, or be revised. Some degree of stabilisation is necessary because cognition cannot remain indefinitely open to every alternative. Maintaining multiple unresolved possibilities imposes continuing cognitive demands (Musslick & Cohen, 2021), whereas stabilisation concentrates resources on a candidate pathway, supporting elaboration, testing, communication, and action while preserving the possibility of later modification.

The broader regulatory issue concerns closure modulation. Here, closure is not treated as a binary terminal event in which cognition switches from open to closed. Instead, the degree, timing, and reversibility of commitment can vary continuously. Closure modulation describes how readily alternative pathways are narrowed, how strongly a developing interpretation is maintained, and how easily search or reconsideration can resume. Closure can therefore be delayed, accelerated, partial, or reopened as conditions change, meaning that closure modulation operates across the developing cognitive episode rather than only at the end. For example, a configuration that places greater weight on exploratory search, comparison, or perspective shifting can preserve pathway plurality for longer, whereas stronger weighting of rapid evaluation, confidence, or commitment can promote earlier convergence. Geddert and Egner (2022)indicated that cognitive stability and flexibility can be regulated independently rather than as a simple trade-off. Neither tendency is inherently adaptive or maladaptive, as their value depends on whether openness or commitment remains appropriate to current demands.

This distinction is important because effective cognition requires both persistence and revisability. Weak stabilisation can produce continual reconsideration, indecision, or failure to act, while excessive persistence can produce fixation or resistance to relevant counterinformation. Evidence that cognitive stability and flexibility can be regulated independently supports the view that effective cognition depends on their calibration rather than maximising either tendency (Geddert & Egner, 2022). The architecture uses hysteresis to characterise the history dependence of this persistence. In a hysteretic system, the conditions required to maintain or leave a state are not necessarily identical to those that allowed entry. Kelso (2012) similarly described cognitive and neural dynamics in terms of multistable and metastable states whose persistence depends on the system's evolving history. Applied here, a candidate pathway may require a particular degree of salience, coherence, or support to become stabilised, yet once established can persist even when that support weakens. Stronger or qualitatively different countervailing information can therefore be required for displacement or reopening. Hence, hysteresis differs from simple threshold crossing because a threshold describes the point at which a candidate becomes sufficiently prominent or coherent to enter sustained consideration, whereas hysteresis concerns what happens after that state has been established. The same incoming information can consequently have different effects depending on whether the pathway is weakly represented, newly stabilised, or already strongly persistent. In other words, the history of the cognitive episode becomes part of its current functional state.

Strong stabilisation can be adaptive in routine, familiar, or highly constrained situations because it protects a viable pathway from irrelevant disturbance and enables efficient action. However, under ambiguous or changing conditions, the same persistence can become maladaptive if counter-signals fail to generate sufficient pressure for reopening. The key issue is whether the threshold for reconsideration remains appropriate to current conditions. Wang et al. (2023) showed that overcoming fixation depends partly on its source and on the mechanisms available to disrupt or restructure the dominant pathway. Reopening can occur through several routes. For example, new evidence might become sufficiently salient to challenge the established pathway, changed constraints may render the current interpretation inadequate, repeated failure could weaken its support, or changes in affective, motivational, or cognitive state can alter the balance between persistence and reconsideration (Geddert & Egner, 2022).

Importantly, reopening does not return the system to its original condition. Previous processing has already altered expectations, recent experience, salience relations, and potentially the memory landscape itself. Accordingly, a reopened episode proceeds from a changed system state rather than from a reset. This path dependence is important for understanding fixation and flexible thought within the same architecture. Persistence and flexibility need not be opposing capacities because the system requires sufficient stability to develop and act upon a viable pathway while remaining sensitive to information that justifies reopening (Geddert & Egner, 2022). The implications for thinking creatively are direct. Generating alternatives is insufficient if the system closes before weak but potentially useful relations can develop, while indefinite openness would prevent those alternatives from being evaluated and translated into action. Hommel and Colzato (2017) similarly framed adaptive cognition in terms of regulating persistence and flexibility according to current demands. Thinking creatively depends partly on regulating closure, which means maintaining enough openness for alternative pathways to emerge while allowing sufficient stabilisation for promising pathways to be pursued. Stabilisation is not an endpoint. It is a temporarily persistent state within an ongoing recursive system. Subsequent feedback, changing constraints, altered deployment, competing salience, or new internal and external inputs can reinforce or reopen that state under revised conditions.

7. Architecture in Operation

The preceding sections have treated the principal functions of the architecture separately for analytical clarity. However, they remain dynamically interdependent in operation. Changes in deployment can alter memory access, accessible relations can acquire changing salience, evaluation can strengthen or weaken candidate pathways, and stabilisation can itself influence the conditions under which subsequent information is processed. The following examples illustrate how different cognitive outcomes can emerge through changes in the configuration of the same underlying system rather than through separate domain-specific mechanisms.

7.1 Design example: curved beam

The following example is drawn from a primary school classroom episode observed by the author. Children were asked to construct a curved wooden beam for a model structure using available balsa sections (beams) and thin sheet. Initial attempts focused on softening, forcing, or otherwise deforming the straight beams. Within the present architecture, the current frame of mind had become organised around a particular interpretation of the task, where the broader requirement to produce a curved beam had effectively narrowed into the more specific problem of how a straight beam can be bent. Under that configuration, cognitive processes and accessible relations associated with bending, flexibility, softening, force, and deformation received greater functional weight, while other potentially relevant relations within the memory landscape remained comparatively inaccessible or weakly salient.

Repeated attempts progressively exposed the limitation of that pathway. The balsa could not be bent sufficiently to produce the required curvature and, even where some deformation was achieved, it would not reliably remain in that form. For one child, the problem then became more specific: if the required shape could not be produced by bending a straight section, some other means of producing the curved form was needed. This did not yet provide the solution, but it altered the conditions against which other relations could become relevant. The shift occurred when the child recalled a plywood product constructed from thin laminated layers.

That memory introduced a relation between the current problem and a previously encountered material structure, resulting in the realisation that a curved member did not necessarily have to be bent from a single piece, as it could be constructed from thinner components. At that point, the problem was effectively reconfigured from how this straight beam can be bent to how a curved beam can be made. The thin balsa sheet, which had previously carried little apparent relevance to the dominant bending pathway, now became functionally useful because curved profiles could be cut from it and laminated to produce the required thickness and form.

Within the proposed architecture, this provides a simple account of the apparent 'aha' moment. Nothing new had appeared in the environment. The thin sheet had been available throughout, and the principle of lamination already existed within the child's memory landscape through the remembered plywood product. What changed was the functional relationship between those elements. The plywood memory became sufficiently accessible and salient to connect the unresolved constraint with the properties of the available material. That relation altered the current cognitive configuration and opened a pathway that had previously carried little or no functional weight. This interpretation is consistent with work linking insight to sudden representational change rather than to the appearance of wholly new information (Danek et al., 2020). Once that pathway had emerged, the remainder of the solution could be evaluated through ordinary reasoning. Curved profiles could be cut from the thin sheet, several identical layers could be bonded together, and the resulting laminated component would possess both the required curvature and sufficient thickness. The pathway was therefore coherent with the task constraints and became sufficiently stabilised to support action. The episode can reasonably be described as an instance of thinking creatively because the pupil was not reproducing a supplied plan or demonstrated solution. The creative contribution lay in detecting and developing a relation between two previously separate elements of experience. In retrospect, the solution appears straightforward, but that is precisely the point. The 'aha' moment did not require a mysterious creative faculty, because it simply emerged when an existing relation became accessible, salient, and useful within a changing cognitive configuration. The apparent suddenness of an 'aha' experience could reflect the point at which a developing pathway becomes sufficiently accessible and salient to emerge into conscious thought, rather than the instant at which the pathway itself first began to form.

7.2 Social example: interpersonal judgement

The same architecture can operate in a very different domain. Consider an ordinary interaction in which one person encounters another and initially has little information on which to form a judgement. The current frame of mind may remain relatively open, allowing behaviour, context, previous experience, affective state, and incoming information to contribute without one interpretation strongly dominating. However, suppose that the other person makes a remark that is interpreted negatively. That event may become salient and begin to reorganise the wider configuration. Deployment could shift towards attributional or defensive processing, while information congruent with the emerging interpretation acquires greater functional weight and alternative explanations receive less. Such a pattern is consistent with evidence that prior interpretations can bias the subsequent weighting and evaluation of information (Lord et al., 1979; Nickerson, 1998). In other words, the frame of mind has changed. However, this is not because one discrete frame has been replaced by another. Instead, relative organisation of access, salience, affect, expectation, and cognitive deployment has changed. Subsequent information is then encountered by this altered system. Tone of voice, facial expression, previous remarks, or ambiguous behaviours that might previously have carried little significance can become increasingly salient because they are compatible with the developing judgement (Lord et al., 1979). At the same time, wider contextual information can remain available but receive insufficient weight to modify the emerging pathway.

Once a negative interpretation has become dominant, maintaining it is cognitively comparatively easy because subsequent information can be processed within the existing configuration. Congruent details require little reinterpretation and can be incorporated readily, while contradictory information demands more substantial cognitive work because it requires the current configuration to be questioned, alternative relations to become salient, and the established interpretation to be reopened (Lord et al., 1979). The asymmetry is therefore not simply between positive and negative information. Instead, it is between information that can be assimilated into the dominant configuration and information that requires that configuration to change. Anderson et al. (1980) demonstrated that explanatory frameworks can continue to influence judgement even after the information on which they were based has been discredited, illustrating the persistence of an established interpretation. In hysteretic terms, once an interpretation has become stabilised, congruent information can be incorporated with relatively little disruption, whereas contradictory information must exert sufficient pressure to overcome the higher threshold required for reopening. Hence, the cognitive history of the episode matters because the same piece of information may have a different effect depending on whether it is encountered before or after the dominant interpretation has become established.

Although the two presented examples differ substantially in content, they can be described using the same functional architecture. In one case, reconfiguration enabled a previously weak construction relation to become salient, producing a different outcome through creative thinking. In the other, reconfiguration progressively narrowed interpretation and contributed to the persistence of an interpersonal judgement. Therefore, the architecture does not identify creativity, fixation, intuition, or social judgement as separate cognitive systems. Instead, it asks how differences in configuration, access, salience, evaluation, stabilisation, and reopening can produce different outcomes from the same broad cognitive machinery operating under the same constraints. Closure modulation becomes especially important within this broader developmental picture. Some degree of stabilisation is necessary because cognition cannot remain indefinitely open, yet excessive persistence can prevent reconsideration when constraints change or competing information warrants reopening. The capacity to maintain a viable interpretation while remaining sensitive to signals that justify revision represents an important form of cognitive regulation, consistent with evidence that stability and flexibility can be regulated independently rather than as a simple trade-off (Geddert & Egner, 2022). Thinking creatively is one context in which this matters, but the same regulatory problem extends to judgement, learning, dialogue, decision-making, and other forms of cognition in which commitment and flexibility must coexist.

8. Discussion

The proposed architecture offers a systems-level account of how established cognitive functions interact to shape pathway formation, stabilisation, and reopening. Its central implication is that apparently contrasting forms of cognition need not require separate explanatory systems. For example, efficient commitment and cognitive rigidity can emerge from the same underlying dynamics, consistent with evidence that cognitive stability and flexibility can be regulated independently rather than forming a simple opposition (Geddert & Egner, 2022). Strong stabilisation can be adaptive where constraints are clear and persistent yet impede reconsideration under ambiguous or changing conditions. Similarly, selective access can support efficient processing while contributing to fixation where potentially relevant relations remain inaccessible or insufficiently salient (Bilalić et al., 2008). The important question is therefore whether the system configuration remains appropriate to the constraints of the episode. This could help explain apparently incongruous responses, such as a child laughing while being reprimanded. The observable behaviour may be interpreted as amusement or defiance, but it could emerge from a configuration shaped by discomfort, arousal, uncertainty, or competing regulatory demands. This perspective also changes how cognitive 'failure' can be analysed, because superficially similar outcomes can emerge from different functional conditions. Relevant knowledge may exist within the memory landscape but fail to become accessible, an accessible relation might remain insufficiently salient, a salient pathway may fail evaluation, or a coherent interpretation can become so strongly stabilised that reopening becomes difficult. Treating these possibilities separately provides greater explanatory resolution than only describing the resulting behaviour in terms of broad outcomes such as fixation, inflexibility, or insight (Bilalić et al., 2008; Danek et al., 2020). The design example illustrates this distinction, where the external constraints remained essentially unchanged, while a change in cognitive configuration allowed an existing relation to become accessible and salient, reorganising the problem around construction rather than bending. The resulting 'aha' moment can therefore be described without invoking a separate creative mechanism.

This has consequences for how creativity is conceptualised. Within the present account, creativity is not treated as a distinct faculty that 'switches on' during particular episodes, nor is it assigned globally to an outcome. This process-based conception was developed by Rockliffe and McKay (2023) and Green et al. (2024), both of whom shift attention from creativity as a property of a person or product towards the processes through which creative contributions emerge. The more precise object of analysis is thinking creatively, meaning the manner in which ordinary cognitive processes are configured such that previously weak, remote, or nondominant relations can become accessible, salient, developed, and evaluated. Creative contribution can therefore occur at different points within a wider cognitive passage, including problem reorganisation, detection of an unexpected relation, development of a candidate pathway, or translation of that pathway into action. This also helps explain why apparently simple solutions can emerge from genuinely creative thinking. Once the relevant relation becomes salient, the resulting pathway can appear obvious in retrospect. In the curved-beam episode, the creative contribution lay principally in the emergence and development of a relation that reorganised the problem. The reasoning that followed remained rational and constraint-sensitive. The difference was the pathway along which that reasoning proceeded. The architecture therefore places creative thinking within ordinary cognition without requiring any suspension of logic or rationality.

The same architecture also helps explain movement in the opposite direction. In the interpersonal example, a developing judgement can progressively narrow access and increase the salience of information congruent with an emerging interpretation. Once stabilised, that interpretation can be easier to maintain than to reopen because congruent information can be assimilated into the existing organisation, whereas contradictory information must exert sufficient pressure for destabilisation. Anderson et al. (1980) demonstrated that explanatory frameworks can continue to shape judgement even after their original evidential basis has been discredited. Hysteresis makes this asymmetry explicitly history-dependent because the same information can have different effects depending on whether it is encountered before or after the dominant interpretation has become established. The contrast with the design example is important. One episode involves reorganisation that allows a previously weak relation to become salient and supports creative thinking, while the other involves progressive narrowing and persistence around an increasingly dominant interpretation. Nevertheless, both can be described using the same broad architecture. Rather than treating creativity, fixation, flexibility, or rigidity as independent cognitive systems, the model asks how differences in configuration, access, salience, evaluation, stabilisation, and reopening can produce different outcomes from the same broad cognitive machinery operating under the same constraints.

The architecture also directs attention towards the role of prior experience. What becomes accessible in a later episode depends partly on what exists within the memory landscape and on the form in which earlier experiences have been represented. Beaty et al. (2023) showed that the organisation of semantic memory can influence the relations available during creative cognition, suggesting that the structure of stored knowledge matters as well as its quantity. A representation that preserves relational, structural, spatial, procedural, or transformational information provides more potential points of connection with subsequent problems than one retaining only a coarse description of an event or outcome. Experimental evidence that increasing episodic specificity can facilitate divergent thinking provides a related example of how access to episodic detail can affect later cognition (Madore et al., 2015). This suggests that cognitive flexibility may depend not simply on the quantity of experience accumulated, but on the richness and organisation of the relations preserved from that experience. The plywood example illustrates this directly, where retaining something of laminated construction provides a transferable relation, whereas merely remembering that the object had been encountered would offer comparatively little. This raises a developmental question. Repeated cognitive activity may affect both what becomes represented within the memory landscape and how readily particular processes and capacities are subsequently deployed. A previously proposed creative-competency framework identified pattern recognition, sequencing, spatial reasoning, transformational thinking, translational thinking, and metacognitive awareness as teachable cognitive behaviours rather than as mechanisms of memory (Rockliffe, 2025). Repeated deployment of such processes could influence the relational detail preserved from experience and therefore the possibilities available to later cognition, although the precise relationship remains an empirical question. Frequently recruited configurations may also become increasingly fluent or readily available, while less frequently recruited processes receive comparatively little practice. The relationship could therefore be reciprocal, where the processes and capacities available to an individual constrain the deployment profiles that can be formed, while repeated deployment may influence their later availability and weighting (Hommel & Colzato, 2017). This shifts attention from occasional performance towards longer-term system conditioning. If cognitive configurations are repeatedly practised, development concerns how readily different configurations can be formed when conditions demand them, rather than simply what an individual knows.

A further requirement is that the architecture should generate predictions that distinguish it from a purely post-hoc descriptive framework. If a potentially relevant relation is already represented within the memory landscape, experimentally increasing its accessibility should increase the probability that it becomes salient without requiring new information to be introduced. Similarly, altering the deployment profile should produce systematic changes in the range and persistence of candidate pathways rather than arbitrary changes in outcome. The hysteresis account makes the stronger prediction that information capable of influencing an interpretation before stabilisation may be insufficient to reopen the same interpretation once established. Failure to observe such history-dependent differences under otherwise comparable conditions would count against that component of the architecture. More generally, changes in cognitive outcome should be traceable to corresponding changes in access, weighting, salience, evaluation, or stabilisation rather than being accommodated solely after the event.

Selective accessibility could be examined through cueing, retrieval latency, recall probability, or the likelihood that a previously available relation enters subsequent reasoning. Changes in deployment profile could be inferred from systematic shifts in exploratory search, inhibitory control, associative retrieval, switching, or persistence under otherwise comparable conditions. Salience could be examined through changes in attentional priority or the probability that accessible information influences subsequent judgement, while closure modulation could be investigated by measuring how readily an established interpretation is revised following counterevidence. Such measures would test whether the functional relationships proposed by the architecture produce reliable and dissociable patterns of behaviour rather than identify independent modules. Several limitations follow from the present proposal. The architecture remains theoretical and its proposed relationships require empirical examination. Further, the boundaries between its functions are analytical rather than absolute because the same information or process may contribute to several parts of a cognitive episode. The architecture also necessarily simplifies temporal organisation. Actual cognition is likely to involve parallel processing, recursion, rapid feedback, and transitions that are not consciously accessible, which is consistent with broader dynamical accounts of cognition and neural coordination (Kelso, 2012). Broader influences, including affective state, motivation, cognitive load, expertise, physiological condition, and metacognitive regulation, can also modify several parts of the system simultaneously. These limitations generate empirical questions concerning how deployment profiles alter selective memory access, how relations acquire sufficient prominence to become salient, whether different forms of prior encoding alter subsequent relational transfer, and how stabilisation changes the conditions required for reopening. Future work will also examine whether repeated experience modifies the structure and accessibility of the memory landscape, whether closure modulation can itself be developed, and whether particular cognitive configurations become more readily deployable through repeated use. Therefore, the value of the framework lies in the phenomena it can organise and in the questions it makes possible. By treating cognition as a changing relationship between constraints, deployment, memory access, salience, evaluation, stabilisation, and reopening, the architecture provides a common basis for examining adaptive commitment and cognitive flexibility without requiring a separate explanatory system for every observed outcome.

9. Conclusion

The present paper has proposed a functional architecture for understanding how cognitive pathways emerge, stabilise, and reopen within a continuously changing frame of mind. By locating deployment, memory access, salience, evaluation, stabilisation, and closure within a common recursive system, it provides a way of examining how different outcomes can arise from the same broad cognitive machinery. Creative reorganisation, fixation, efficient commitment, and persistent judgement can therefore be analysed in terms of differences in configuration rather than treated as products of separate explanatory systems. While the architecture remains theoretical and requires empirical testing, its value lies in providing a common analytical structure for asking where cognitive pathways become accessible, salient, coherent, stabilised, resistant to change, or capable of reopening, and whether those dynamics can themselves be developed.
