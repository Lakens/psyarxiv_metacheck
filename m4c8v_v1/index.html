Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Transformer-based language models (LMs) allow researchers and practitioners to develop and deploy systems that perform impressive feats such as generating coherent texts and answering questions across various domains. Yet, there is currently an interpretability problem regarding the internal representation of multidimensional psychological constructs (e.g., personality traits) throughout multiple layers of deep neural networks. Most of the current methodologies used for understanding how LMs represent concepts and information follow a bottom-up approach (e.g., neurons, attention heads, and circuit dynamics towards human-level concepts). Still, there is limited evidence regarding how and where complex psychological constructs emerge during computation. Here, we propose Construct Emergence Tracing (CET), a novel methodology to address this problem. CET utilizes ideas from Generative Psychometrics to trace the emergence of concepts from high-level meaning to underlying mechanisms. CET consists of Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis combined with three complementary indices reflecting the quality of representation (semantic coherence via normalized mutual information; NMI, entropy-based organization structure via Total Entropy Fit Index; TEFI, and topological compressibility via a compression-based proxy for Kolmogorov Complexity; KC). CET also computes a weighted composite of the above three indices in order to derive a metric named Representational Maturity (RM). We applied CET to fourteen LM models ranging from nominal sizes of 117 million to 32 billion parameters. We utilized a fixed design of five items per attribute representing the five attributes of grandiose narcissism and yielded a total of 9,801,000 network estimates across recorded states, embedding dimensions, and repeated item samples. Results show that across architectures construct recovery generally increases from early to intermediate states. However, its initiation, amplitude, alignment with different metrics, and termination vary considerably across models. Across Qwen2.5-Instruct, overall mean NMI followed a monotonic increase based on nominal model size from 0.504 (0.5B) to 0.653 (32B). This trend did not replicate for selected peak values or output layer retention. Furthermore, the final state was divided into two distinct patterns of behaviors: (a) five models showed zero or close-to-zero mean NMI (GPT-2, Phi-4-mini, Qwen2.5-32B, GPT-OSS-20B, and Muse-Glimmer-30B); (b) nine models maintained final state mean NMI within 0.504-0.777 range. Lastly, we observed no consistent convergence in the optimal layers regarding peak semantic coherence, optimal entropy fit, and maximal representational maturity. Overall, we demonstrated that both the number of parameters and the extraction from the output layer alone do not suffice in capturing abstract representations inside LMs. Through CET, researchers and practitioners obtain macro-level estimations of construct organization trajectories across different layers.

1. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs), with the transformers architecture can range from models with 117 million parameters as like GPT-2 (Brown et al., 2020) to hundreds of billions of parameters for more recent frontier architectures (Shao et al., 2024), have dramatically changed Natural Language Processing. Their progress has been dramatically fast, from generating human-like text passages to handling broad contexts and solving very complex problems. However, an important scientific issue about LLMs remains unanswered: How do these models represent and organize abstract information internally in their layers? Given the use of these models in several consequential applications applications, such as medicine, legal proceedings, education, finance, governance, science, and more, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind their internal organization is central to promote transparency, accountability, alignment and safety concerns (Bommasani et al., 2021). The current paper aims to contribute to the field of interpretability by investigating the organizational trajectory of abstract psychological constructs across the layers of transformer models.

Research on LLM interpretability has generated a number of methodologies to analyze the internal representational processes of transformer-based models. For example, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) and Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan, Taly, & Yan, 2017) quantify the importance of input features. Attention visualization and neural activation examination inspect how certain components behave in response to diverse stimuli (Olah et al., 2020; Vig, 2019). Mechanistic interpretability approaches, especially circuit tracing and attributions graphs (Lindsey, Gurnee, Ameisen, et al., 2025; Olah et al., 2020), describe specialized localized pathways within a rich connectionist tradition of studying learned representations (Rumelhart, Hinton, & Williams, 1986). Additionally, causality and counterfactual investigations explore model robustness, while activation geometries are compared via representational similarity analysis using metrics such as Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) (Kornblith, Norouzi, Lee, & Hinton, 2019), Singular Vector Canonical Correlation Analysis (SVCCA) (Raghu, Gilmer, Yosinski, & Sohl-Dickstein, 2017) and Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) (Kriegeskorte, Mur, & Bandettini, 2008). Probing classifiers, on the other hand, examine whether linguistic traits can be linearly decoded (Alain & Bengio, 2017; Belinkov, 2022).

All of these methods have in common an underlying directional assumption. They move from mechanisms to meanings. That is, starting from units such as neurons, attention heads, prompt tokens, pairs of activations, or specific activation geometries and moving upwards towards meaningful constructs. Accordingly, these methods have proven invaluable in addressing micro-level questions such as how does the model compute feature X? or is there linear evidence for property Y in layer l?. Yet, none of them addresses the macro-level research question posed in the field of interpretability literature: Where, When and How Coherently is a Multidimensional Abstract Construct Represented across LLM Transformer Layers? (Belinkov, 2022; Lindsey et al., 2025).

For nearly a century, Measurement Sciences in Quantitative Psychology faced this challenge. Psychological attributes such as narcissism, empathy, and anxiety are not simple dichotomous features. They constitute complex multidimensional entities where observable attributes are expected to form a coherent representation of the construct itself (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; eskin2012?). Measuring whether a large language model represents an abstract construct across its entire processing hierarchy requires the development of a methodology that turns the directionality of interpretability inquiry upside down. Such a methodology should start by postulating a meaningful attribute, and proceed to evaluate how accurately, parsimoniously, and stably the model's internal representations reproduce the construct organization across all stages of its computation. Thus, allowing mechanistic interpretation of the resulting representational spaces and their underlying mechanisms.

Recent advances at the interface between quantitative psychology and artificial intelligence provide the foundations to make such a reverse engineering attempt possible. In the framework of Generative Psychometrics (Russell-Lasalandra, Christensen, & Golino, 2024), LLMs have been incorporated into a network psychometric assessment framework to generate pools of test items, filter semantic redundancies and examine their dimensional structure before collecting any human data. In practice, Generative Psychometrics creates psychometric scales whose structure fits well with those observed in nationally representative samples (Garrido, Russell-Lasalandra, Rodríguez-Montoya, & Golino, 2026; Russell-Lasalandra et al., 2024; Russell-Lasalandra & Golino, 2026). Previous research focused on Generative Psychometrics' potential to assist the traditional process of psychometric scales construction. The central argument of the current paper is that Generative Psychometrics can be used to investigate the potential of Psychological Measurement Science for understanding artificial intelligence.

One breakthrough in LLM embeddings opened the possibility to reverse-engineer their representational process. Golino (2026) showed that LLM embedding spaces represent abstract psychological constructs in a non-linear way. Using Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis (DynEGA) (Golino, Christensen, Moulder, Kim, & Boker, 2022) across embedding coordinates and considering the coordinate order as pseudo-time, Golino (2026) found that dimensional structure is better represented on particular embedding configurations rather than being uniformly distributed across all dimensions of the embedding landscape. Optimizing for the accuracy via Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) (Strehl & Ghosh, 2002) and structural organization via Total Entropy Fit Index (TEFI) (Golino et al., 2021) provided more accurate estimations of the dimensionality structure of psychological constructs than standard cross-sectional analyses of embeddings. If LLM output embeddings represent searchable landscapes, then transformer hidden states must also be analyzed as representational landscapes at every processing stage.

The current paper introduces Construct Emergence Tracing (CET), a new framework for construct-centered interpretability research. CET extends representational landscape search (Golino, 2026) throughout the depth of LLM transformer architectures. Given a theoretical multidimensional psychological construct definition, CET performs DynEGA analysis at every processing layer, identifying three different but complementary aspects of its representational organization: (a) semantic coherence, quantified using NMI between predicted labels; (b) organizational structure, assessed using TEFI calculated based on Von Neumann entropy (Golino, Jiménez, Garrido, & Christensen, 2024; Golino et al., 2021); and (c) representational efficiency, evaluated using Kolmogorov complexity (Golino, 2025; Golino, Nesselroade, & Christensen, 2025; Kolmogorov, 1965). All three aspects of representational organization receive a score, which can be combined in a single index named Representational Maturity Index (RMI), tracing the construct emergence trajectory across the transformer depth. Therefore, CET establishes the interpretability pillar of Generative Psychometrics: applying psychological measurement science to artificial intelligence. Model hidden states are treated as proxies for empirical observations, embedding dimensions define the searchable representational space, and transformer depth acts as the developmental axis.

This manuscript contributes to the interpretability literature in three key ways. Firstly, it formalizes the multidimensional construct representation as a developmental trajectory across the depth of a transformer architecture, establishing a theoretical foundation for manifold separation preservation during time-delay reconstruction. In addition, it integrates Network Psychometrics, the Total Entropy Fit Index (TEFI), and Algorithmic Complexity to establish a reproducible layerby-embedding interpretability pipeline. Additionally, it explores CET performance in 14 Large Language Models (LLMs) varying between 117M to 32B parameters evaluated with a five-items-perattribute test design. Specifically, the paper investigates if construct recovery follows universal depth profiles, how scaling affects mean/peak recovery accuracy and if coherent constructs representations persist from intermediate layers to outputs. The aim of this study is to introduce CET and demonstrate its utility for interpreting language representations and assessing their multidimensional organization.

2. Related work

>Related work

2.1 Interpretability methods for transformer models

In the last decade, the interest in investigating the internal functioning of transformers models has grown substantially. Currently, there are several types of methods designed to investigate transformer models' interpretability at various levels of abstraction. First, attribution methods (e.g., SHAP, Integrated Gradients, Attention Rollouts) focus on quantifying the contribution of each feature or token to predicting specific output values. These methods do not allow us to investigate if a set of items forms a multidimensional latent structure, such as constructs with multiple attributes/dimensions. Second, attention and neuron analyses focus on understanding particular computations performed within attention heads/neurons. It involves investigating the role that particular sets of attention heads/neurons play during a particular task. Finally, mechanistic interpretability seeks to explore the computational mechanisms responsible for high-level processes like multi-step reasoning, entity tracking, and factual retrieval. Methods include: Circuit Tracing and Attribution Graphs (Lindsey et al., 2025; Olah et al., 2020). The primary drawback of these methods is their micro-orientednessthey seek to understand how microscopic processes operate. On the other hand, CET describes the trajectory through which complex constructs evolve into representations across layers.

2.2 Representation geometry and probing classifiers

The decoding capacity of transformers models' hidden layers has been explored using probing classifiers, whose purpose is to estimate if particular linguistic elements such as parts-of-speech, syntactic relationships, or semantic roles, can be linearly decoded from layer activations. Results have demonstrated that syntactic information becomes maximal at mid layers whereas semantic information tends to cluster at late layers (Jawahar, Sagot, & Seddah, 2019; Rogers, Kovaleva, & Rumshisky, 2020; Tenney, Das, & Pavlick, 2019). Other approaches use similarity-based metrics (Centered Kernel Alignment, CKA; Singular Vector Canonical Correlation Analysis, SVCCA; Representational Similarity Analysis, RSA) to compare geometrical structures between layer-wise activations, or compare to the behavioral similarity matrix.

Although the described methodologies play a pivotal role in interpretability research, two limitations should be addressed. First, probing methods assess decoding potential and thus cannot reveal information about whether a construct formed a coherent multidimensional structure. Second, while metrics like CKA and SVCCA enable comparing two representation geometries, they do not evaluate recovery accuracy according to a theoretical construct structure. In contrast, CET aims precisely at estimating construct recovery accuracy and the quality of recovered structures across layers.

2.3 Network Psychometrics

Finally, CET borrows fundamental notions from network psychometrics (Epskamp, Maris, Waldorp, & Borsboom, 2018; Epskamp, Waldorp, Mõttus, & Borsboom, 2018; Golino, Christensen, & Garrido, 2022; Golino et al., 2025), an interdisciplinary branch of quantitative psychology devoted to modeling multivariate psychological phenomena as undirected weighted networks. Using the Exploratory Graph Analysis framework (EGA), the method estimates the latent dimensionality via either regularized partial correlation coefficients (Christensen & Golino, 2021; Golino & Epskamp, 2017; Golino et al., 2020) via graphical lasso and EBIC-based model selection (Epskamp, Maris, et al., 2018; Friedman, Hastie, & Tibshirani, 2008), or via triangulated maximally filtered graph (TMFG) (Massara, Di Matteo, & Aste, 2016). Sets of densily connected nodes in the network are proxies for latent dimensions Christensen & Golino (2021) that can be recovered using community detection methods.

In contrast with factor analytic techniques, EGA uses the information provided by unique variance between pairs of variables rather than total shared variance across all variables, making the estimation resilient to high interfactor correlation. Consequently, extensive Monte Carlo simulations demonstrated that EGA leads to greater accuracy compared to traditional techniques based on latent variable models (Christensen, Garrido, Guerra-Peña, & Golino, 2024; Golino & Epskamp, 2017; Golino et al., 2020). Furthermore, CET adopts Dynamic EGA (DynEGA) (Golino, 2026; Golino, Christensen, Moulder, et al., 2022; Tomašević, Golino, & Christensen, 2024), which combines EGA with phase-space reconstruction via time-delay embedding and derivative estimation using Generalized Local Linear Approximation (GLLA) (Boker, Deboeck, Edler, & Keel, 2010; Deboeck, Montpetit, Bergeman, & Boker, 2009). Psychometric evaluation metrics provide quality criteria of the network partitioning: the Total Entropy Fit Index (TEFI) (Golino et al., 2024 (Golino et al., , 2021) ) evaluates partition entropy reduction in the correlation matrix via Von Neumann entropy (Golino et al., 2021); network topology is evaluated through a Kolmogorov Complexity (KC) estimator (Golino, 2025; Golino et al., 2025), measuring the compressibility of serialized edge lists generated with thresholding and single-layer canonical prime weight coding (Golino, 2025).

3. Theoretical Framework

>Theoretical Framework

3.1 Representational Development in Transformers

In general, the process performed within a Transformer can be seen as a hierarchical abstraction pipeline, whereby each decoding layer performs attentional and feedforward operations that transform token representation in increasingly rich context. As pointed in earlier sessions, the evidence from probing classifiers supports a layered progression from early layers that can capture local lexical and syntactic regularities, to middle layers that integrate broader context, forming abstract semantic groupings, to upper layers that change representations to support next-token prediction (Jawahar et al., 2019; Rogers et al., 2020; Tenney et al., 2019). CET formalizes this progression by defining what it means for a multidimensional construct to be represented at layer ' and by tracking its evolution across depth.

Definition 1 (Construct Representation). Let C denote a psychological construct of interest whose items (or operationalizations) form a multidimensional taxonomy of r dimensions. Such psychological construct C is said to be represented in the model's state ' if the activations of each element associated with C constitute a community structure aligned with its r dimensions. More precisely, the agreement between the clustering in such state and the taxonomy of C should exceed chance levels according to a proper null model.

Semantic Coherence: the NMI criterion measures the degree to which the community detection procedure aligns with the labeling of items pertaining to C based on normalized mutual information (Strehl & Ghosh, 2002), ranging from 0 (no agreement) to 1 (perfect agreement).

Representational Efficiency: Kolmogorov complexity (KC) quantifies the complexity of a system's topology (Golino et al., 2025). The KC of an arbitrary object is equivalent to the minimum possible length of the computer code necessary to reconstruct the object (Kolmogorov, 1965). Topologies with regular and parsimonious patterns are described more concisely compared to noisy networks. While the exact value of KC is uncomputable, KC can be approximated for networks using lossless compression algorithms. As implemented in Golino (2025) and Golino et al. (2025), the network KC estimator performs unweighted topology encoding-thresholding the network adjacency matrix to retain edges and assigning coordinate vectors (ij) and a constant retained-edge value 21 = 2. The serialized edge list is then compressed using gzip (Morzy, Kajdanowicz, & Kazienko, 2017; Santoro & Nicosia, 2020). Because the compressed length can change depending on the sequence of edge coordinates, KC values are estimated as the average over 1000 random permutations of the edges.

4.1 CET Pipeline

Figure 1 shows as a schematic description of the complete CET-DynEGA workflow. For each language model under evaluation, the pipeline uses the following nine steps: 1. Item sampling: Let the item pool consist of approximately 50 items (textual statements) for each attribute (facet or dimension) of the target construct. From such a pool, five items per attribute are randomly sampled without replacement, resulting in a set of 25 items per iteration. 2. Hidden-state extraction: Each item is passed to the model as plain text. At each recorded state ', the token-level hidden states are averaged, such that every item is described by a single activation vector of dimension d, where d denotes the hidden size of the model. 3. Standardization: Each hidden unit is standardized across the stacked item-by-state observations, and only thereafter are the individual states separated for network estimation. 4. Time-delay embedding: For each state and each item, a time-delay embedding matrix with n ∈ {3, 4, . . . , 200} columns is constructed, wherein the hidden-unit index is treated as a pseudotemporal ordering.

5. Derivative estimation (GLLA): The first-order derivatives are estimated from the embedding matrix of each item using Generalized Local Linear Approximation. 6. Network estimation (EBICglasso): The first-derivative vectors are column-bound across the 25 items, and a sparse Gaussian Graphical Model is estimated from their covariance matrix using EBIC graphical LASSO, i.e., te regularization parameter ˘is selected over 100 values (by the one that minimizes EBIC), with an adaptive hyperparameter fl ∈ {0.50, 0.25, 0}, where fl is decreased whenever a node becomes disconnected, following Golino et al. (2020). 7. Community detection: The Louvain algorithm is applied to the estimated network in order to identify the dimensionality structure (communities in the network). 8. Metric computation: For every cell defined by a state and an embedding dimension, three indices are computed: NMI (with respect to the five stipulated attribute labels), TEFI, and KC. 9. Aggregation and maturity: Steps 1-8 are repeated across iterations (100 iterations per model in the matched comparison grid). 4.3 Models and Construct

Models. Fourteen open-weight language models are analyzed, spanning nominal parameter counts from 117M to 32B and depths from 12 to 64 decoder blocks (Table 1). Here, individual models rather than families constitute the experimental conditions. The six Qwen2.5-Instruct conditions cover a large range of scales from 0.5B up to 32B parameters, allowing a structured within-family comparison across scale, whereas the rest of the models introduce additional variation regarding depth, hidden dimensionality, attention mechanism, and training methodology: GPT-2, TinyLlama 1.1B, Qwen2 1.5B, Phi-2, Phi-4-mini, GPT-OSS 20B, Qwen3.8 27B, and Muse-Glimmer 30B. Each of the conditions of the former group relies on its respective Instruct checkpoint. The condition for TinyLlama uses its Chat-v1.0 version, and the condition for Phi-4-mini employs the instructiontuned checkpoint. Condition Qwen3.8 refers to the stored Qwen3.8-27B BF16 GGUF identifier, with metadata specifying a Qwen3.5 text architecture made of 48 linear-attention blocks and 16 full-attention blocks (64 total). The Muse-Glimmer 30B architecture consists of 52 text blocks, of which 39 are sliding-attention blocks and 13 are full-attention blocks, in alternation. All evaluations examine text representations.

Construct and Item Sampling.

Grandiose narcissism was conceptualized based on five characteristics derived from the construct definition suggested by Pincus and Lukowitsky (2010). These characteristics include: Distorting Disconfirming External Information; Exhibitionism; Intense Envy; Lack of Empathy; and Repression of Negative Self-information. During every iteration, five randomly selected items without replacement were taken from each characteristic domain, resulting in a dataset consisting of 25 items in total, distributed in five distinct domains. The advantage of this procedure resides on the fact that since the number of items per attribute remained the same across the fourteen models examined, the comparison across them is not affected by this variable.

4.4 Activation Extraction and Computation

Items were passed throught the models as plain text inputs without any conversational template structure. The average pooling of token-level hidden states produced an activation vector per item and recorded state. Prior to separating the states for network estimation, the columns (hidden units) of the stacked matrix of items x states underwent z-score standardization. The embedding layer output and the 12 decoder-layer outputs constitute the 13 recorded states of GPT-2. For all remaining architectures, the decoder-block outputs were recorded excluding the initial embedding layer. It must be noted that relative depth refers to the position of a state in the recorded sequence of each architecture, and therefore state 1 refers to a different operation in GPT-2 than in the remaining architectures. Finally, the last hidden state followed the native output-normalization conventions of each implementation. That means that, in some cases, the returned terminal state may differ from the pre-normalization output of the final decoder block.

Computations were carried out in float32 precision using the HuggingFace Transformers library. Specifically, activations were calculated on a CPU processor for GPT-2, TinyLlama, Qwen2, and Phi-4-mini. On the other hand, activations were computed on an NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPU for the architectures Phi-2, Qwen2.5, GPT-OSS, Qwen3.8, and Muse-Glimmer, utilizing offloading to CPU memory whenever required. In particular, the publicly available MXFP4 quantized weights of GPT-OSS were initially dequantized in BF16 precision before conversion into float32 (without recovering the pre-quantized weights). In addition, Qwen3.8 was loaded from a BF16/F32 GGUF checkpoint and Muse-Glimmer was converted from the native safetensors into float32 weights. To investigate whether either the computation precision or the implementation framework affected the terminal profile of GPT-OSS, an additional reference run of 100 iterations was performed in OpenAI's official PyTorch codebase (where the MXFP4 quantized expert weights were dequantized to BF16 and computations were executed in BF16 precision with native float32 normalization parameters).

4.5 Simulation

In order to evaluate each of the models, simulations were performed using 198 embedding dimensions between 3 and 200, across all recorded states and across repeated item samples for each model. The simulation scale, involving all 495 total states (with 13 to 64 states per model), all 198 embedding dimensions, and all 100 iterations per model yields about 9,801,000 network estimates (Table 2). Automated validation of all results confirmed: complete state-by-embedding grids, uniqueness of iteration/state/embedding keys, finiteness of the primary metrics, as well as the range of NMI within [0, 1].

4.6 Aggregation and cross-model comparison

To compare the results from all 14 models tested in our simulations, an aggregation process based on identical rules was adopted: (1) for each combination of state and embedding dimensions, we compute the arithmetic mean over iterations; (2) the overall mean Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) for a whole model must not treat correlated grid cells as independent replicates; hence, it must be computed as the mean of the iteration means, each taken across the whole state-by-embedding grid. 95% Student-t Monte Carlo (MC) intervals capture variation among different sets of items (iterations). Relative depths are computed for each state ' along a sequence of length L: 100(' -1)/(L -1). NMIs are averaged across embedding dimensions at each state to generate depth-curves, whereas RM trajectories are computed as the maximum over embedding dimensions of the mean RM-surface at each state, capturing maximum observed composite maturity per state. Heatmap representations keep track of all pairs state/emb, without binning the depth axis. A common RM normalization was employed, with empirical bounds pooled across all 14 models: TEFI ∈ [-35.26579, 33.01256] and [201.711, 676.193]. Since RM is an affine function of its components, the mean cell-level RM can be estimated directly from mean cell-levels of NMI, TEFI, and KC. Finally, two additional conventions are introduced to facilitate description of trajectory patterns, namely recovery onset and terminal behavior. The former corresponds to the first depth point wherein the mean NMI over embeddings reaches the critical value 0.5. This criterion should be regarded as a heuristical cutoff-value, instead of a statistical test. The terminal behavior of each trajectory is classified as near-zero if its end-state mean NMI is smaller than 0.05; otherwise, it is classified as retained.

Rather than following a single canonical trajectory, the fourteen models cluster into four recurring developmental signatures.

5.3.1 Early-onset, sustained-recovery models (Phi-2, Qwen2 1.5B, Qwen3.8 27B, and Qwen2.5 0.5B-14B).

Phi-2 exemplifies this profile well. 6.1 Item Generation, Embeddings, and Construct Recovery

Advances made in the field of Generative Psychometrics have revolutionized how psychologists develop scales for multidimensional psychological constructs. Now, researchers can go from defining a construct theoretically to having a curated item pool, assessing whether the semantics used in the scale statements are redundant and how well they capture the correct dimensionality of the construct before collecting human responses (Garrido et al., 2026; Russell-Lasalandra et al., 2024; Russell-Lasalandra & Golino, 2026). Conceptually speaking, there are two distinctions that must be highlighted. First, the semantic relationships between item statements within the LLM embeddings are different from covariance patterns among individual differences in human responses; thus, embedding similarities demonstrate only conceptual coherence and not the validity of an instrument in capturing the intended construct.

Second, the way researchers analyze embeddings significantly influences which latent structure gets reconstructed. In a previous study conducted by Garrido et al. (2026), researchers explored the impact of the use of EGA vs. PCA in dimensionality assessments on embeddings obtained from three different item-generating LLMs and two different embedding models, including after network-based item reduction. The findings showed how PCA methods with either Kaiser's rule or parallel analysis tended to overestimate the actual number of dimensions, while EGA was able to recover the predefined structure far more accurately and aligned better than PCA with the dimensionality obtained using human respondents' ratings in Multidimensional Schizotypy Scale. Reducing the variance dimensionality of an embedding space and recovering the latent dimensionality of a psychological construct are different tasks, and CET targets the latter.

6.2 A developmental view of construct emergence

Understanding why the organization of representations plays such an important role in representing constructs is fundamental for several reasons. A developmental view of construct emergence can be adopted from developmental psychology (Golino, 2025). According to Piaget's notion of reflective abstraction, concepts of ever-higher levels emerge not by merely building up separate elements but by organizing already-existing representations into relational structures. In Construct Emergence Tracing (CET), statements phrased in diverse ways gradually form attribute-consistent communities as representations pass through successive attention and feed-forward transformations, which makes this relational coordination measurable. This developmental process does not imply, however, equating transformer layers with specific childhood cognitive development stages since transformers represent machine learning architectures.

According to neo-Piagetian theories of hierarchical integrative processes of development (Pascual-Leone's theory of attention/mental capacity, Case, Fischer, and Demetriou's hierarchical integration approach; summarized in Golino (2025)), what is crucial in representing multidimensional constructs is how information becomes effectively organized. The monotonic increase in full-grid recovery along with increasing model size (Qwen2.5) is consistent with this view of efficient representation organization; however, parameter count is not an analogue of human attentional capacity. In addition, the decoupling of NMI and TEFI optima warns against the idea of a rigid, unilinear stage sequence, since coherence with external construct labels and minimizing internal entropy do not always advance together across transformer layers.

According to Vygotsky's socio-cultural theory, meaning is created in socially and linguistically mediated interactions (see Golino (2025)). Because LLMs are trained on human-written text data from specific linguistic backgrounds, the dimensional structure obtained through CET partially reflects shared meanings of personality traits within particular cultures. Although training language models via pretraining differs dramatically from early-childhood learning experiences, an epistemological caveat should always be considered: one cannot consider construct trajectories obtained from the analysis of a given English-language item pool as universal without conducting additional testing across languages, cultures, and independently authored items.

Finally, it is critical to recognize that developmental trajectories analyzed here occur along processing depth through trained checkpoints rather than development through time in the course of training. Terminal collapses suggest that maximal processing depth is not equivalent to optimal separation in abstract multidimensional constructs' representations. Investigating the development of CET-based representation organization through training checkpoints allows one to address this limitation in the future.

6.3 Coherence, organization, and maturity

Do transformer architectures first develop structural organization and then semantic coherence, or vice versa? Results clearly illustrate that no universal temporal precedence exists. Comparing NMI and TEFI based on embedding-averaged results, we find that the lowest entropy in terms of TEFI (i.e., state 8) occurred earlier than the maximum mean NMI (i.e., state 11) in GPT-2. On the contrary, Phi-4-mini showed the reverse sequence (maximum NMI at block 17; lowest TEFI at block 25); Qwen2.5 32B showed a similar trend (maximum NMI at block 35; minimum TEFI at block 51); Muse-Glimmer 30B (maximum NMI at block 20; minimum TEFI at block 38); TinyLlama reached both maximum NMI and minimum TEFI simultaneously (at block 18).

Such discrepancies demonstrate that the Representational Maturity Index should accompany its components and not be treated as a replacement for them; e.g., Qwen3.8 27B and Muse-Glimmer 30B showed the same overall mean NMI value (0.662), yet they had mean TEFI values that differed nearly twofold (-11.01 vs. -5.85) and opposite terminal outcomes. Similarly, KC is a valuable indicator reflecting the degree of topologic regularity in partial correlation graphs but since this measure equally treats edges' presence without considering their strengths, compressibility alone cannot reflect a mature concept. Thus, treating RM as a weighted descriptive summary and exploring raw NMI, TEFI, and KC scores allows one to avoid obscuring structural compromises.

6.4 Terminal recovery and representation selection

One of the most consequential findings concerns the terminal states. In GPT-OSS 20B, mean NMI dropped from 0.742 in the penultimate block to 0.000 in the final state; in Qwen2.5 32B, it fell from 0.784 to 0.025; and in Muse-Glimmer 30B, Phi-4-mini, and GPT-2, the final-state NMI vanished completely. Had these models been evaluated only via their final hidden states, as is standard in API-level embedding pipelines, the conclusions about their representation of the construct would have been misleading.

However, terminal collapse under CET does not indicate that semantic information was destroyed. Final layer normalization near the language modeling head, residual streams' reorientation, or projections toward the vocabulary-prediction space can alter correlation structures and make community recovery ineffective while leaving semantic information recoverable via alternative methods. Controlled comparative investigations of pre-normalization block outputs, returned hidden states, and alternative probes would help us determine whether the observed phenomenon reflects output normalization or deep transformation of representation geometry.

6.5 Implications for model comparison, interpretability, and AI safety

Methodologically, CET complements other representational analysis approaches by shifting the perspective of layer selection from locating a single best layer to analyzing the developmental landscape.

With CET, one can easily differentiate between isolated high-scoring cells and consistent plateaus of good quality; additionally, one can investigate the impact of evaluation criteria on optimal layer choices (i.e., NMIs for coherence, TEFIs for entropy reduction, and RMs for maturity). Combining CET with linear probing and Centered Kernel Alignment [CKA; Kornblith et al. (2019)] constitutes powerful triangulation since convergent results strengthen representational hypotheses and divergent results identify sensitivities to linear versus non-linear geometry, to network regularization, or to the difference between feature presence and multidimensional organization.

For AI safety applications, CET provides a promising way of tracking the evolution of safetyrelevant multidimensional concepts (such as deception, sycophancy, or harmful intent) through transformer depths prior to and subsequent to alignment interventions such as reinforcement learning from human feedback or steering approaches. Realizing this potential requires, however, substantial psychometric groundwork. First, independently validated item pools and corresponding negative controls need to be developed; and the relationships between representational changes and model behavior should be rigorously documented through empirical observation.

6.6 Limitations and future directions

This study includes several major limitations and opportunities for future research. First, fourteen studied language models exhibit differences regarding architecture design, tokenizer configuration, attention mechanism specifics, training corpus, and checkpoint precision; in addition, six out of fourteen checkpoints correspond to a single release family (i.e., Qwen2.5). Thus, this study characterizes specific models and cannot isolate the causal influence of certain factors (e.g., scale, model quantization, or hybrid attention) on construct representations' emergence unless directly compared under matching conditions.

Second, this work focused exclusively on grandiose narcissism represented by a single item pool. Surface-level lexical regularities might contribute to clustering, and recovering the stipulated labels does not guarantee genuine semantic comprehension. Therefore, future studies must validate CET's applicability through other psychological constructs, independently reworded item formulations, cross-linguistic translation, random permutations of external labels, and matched nonsensical control constructs.

Third, treating the hidden unit index as pseudo-time during time delay embeddings represents a practical strategy rather than a literal physical time series. Though Theorem 1 establishes sufficient conditions for separation preservation on compact manifolds, it does not guarantee that all hidden layers satisfy them. Additional investigations involving permutations of hidden unit coordinates, variations of embedding range, community detection algorithms, and methods of activation standardization are necessary.

Fourth, the RM calculation depends on chosen weighting coefficients and pooled normalization bounds, selected grid peaks are exploratory maxima, and chosen thresholds (0.50; 0.05) are heuristic conventions. Identifying the underlying causes of emergence and terminal loss necessitates further activation patching experiments, matched analyses of pre-and post-normalization outputs, and convergent probing assessments.

7. Conclusion

To conclude, this manuscript introduced CET, which analyzes construct representations' emergence along the depth dimension in LLMs. Analyzing fourteen language models ranging in parameter counts from 117M to 32B, we have demonstrated clear divergence across models in emergence onset points, average representational quality, and alignment between coherence-and entropy-based metrics. Moreover, CET highlighted the critical phenomenon of terminal collapse in five of the fourteen models despite relatively high intermediate NMI scores. Our key finding is that neither a single selected peak nor a final hidden state alone suffices to characterize how a model represents a construct.
