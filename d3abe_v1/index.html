Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This study identified session-level patterns of therapists' and clients' perceptions of the therapeutic alliance and treatment progress and examined whether these patterns were associated with client-reported outcomes. Data included 15 therapist trainees, 86 clients, and 1,035 session ratings. After each session, therapists and clients separately provided ratings, and clients rated their personal functioning and psychological symptoms. A multilevel latent class analysis identified five session patterns: "all-high" (53.3%), "bond-focused" (16.3%), "goals/tasksfocused" (3.3%), "client-low" (18.5%), and "all-low" (8.5%). Longitudinal multilevel models revealed lower current personal functioning after client-low sessions, than after all-high or bondfocused sessions, and higher current psychological symptoms after client-low sessions than after all-high sessions. Clients reported higher next-session symptoms after client-low sessions than after all-high or bond-focused sessions. No difference was detected in outcomes between all-low or goals/tasks-focused sessions and the rest. This study has significant implications for treatment planning, monitoring of the therapeutic alliance, and supervision of therapists-in-training.

One Dance, Two Partners: Session-to-Session Therapist-Client Rating Configurations and Outcomes

Therapeutic work involves a partnership in which therapists and clients pursue meaningful changes through communication, collaboration, and active participation (Bordin, 1979; Flückiger et al., 2018). Although participating in the same sessions, therapists and clients may perceive and experience the therapy differently (Igra et al., 2020). Their evaluations may also emphasize different aspects of treatment, including the quality of their collaboration and perceived progress. Considering these perspectives together may offer a fuller understanding of the therapeutic process and its associations with client functioning and symptoms (Tryon et al., 2007).

The present study examined session-level configurations of client-rated therapeutic alliance and therapist-rated alliance and progress. Client ratings captured bond, agreement on goals, and collaboration on tasks, whereas therapist ratings captured overall alliance, withinsession progress, and progress toward treatment goals. These related but distinct evaluations characterize how clients and therapists perceive aspects of a session. Building on prior research, we aimed to identify session-rating profiles and examined their associations with client-reported functioning and symptoms at the current and subsequent sessions.

Therapist-Client Perceptions of Therapy

Therapist and client perceptions of therapy overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The most researched area within this was their perceptions of therapeutic alliance. Bordin's (1979) transtheoretical model defines the alliance through three interrelated components: agreement on the goals of treatment, agreement on the tasks needed to achieve those goals, and the affective bond between the therapist and client. A large body of literature demonstrates that stronger therapeutic alliances are associated with better therapy outcomes, with an overall allianceoutcome association of approximately r = .23 to .31 across meta-analytic evidence (e.g., Flückiger et al., 2018; Flückiger, Del Re, et al., 2020).

In terms of therapist-client separate ratings on alliance measures, Tryon et al.'s (2007) meta-analysis of 53 studies found a modest client-therapist correlation of r = .36. Clients also gave significantly higher alliance ratings than therapists, with a standardized mean difference of d = .63. Discrepancies were particularly pronounced in some less severely symptomatic and substance-use samples. A 2025 meta-analysis of 40 eating-disorder and anxiety-disorder studies also found a moderate overall discrepancy, g = .41, 95% CI [.28, .53], with clients typically reporting stronger alliances than therapists. The discrepancy was not moderated by diagnostic group, treatment orientation, or assessment timing (Lev Ari et al., 2025). Importantly, a therapist's lower rating should not automatically be interpreted as a lack of skill or pessimism.

Across several studies, moderately lower therapist ratings were associated with equal or better outcomes, possibly because a cautious therapist remains attentive to unspoken clinical concerns (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Kivlighan & Marmarosh, 2018; Li et al., 2024).

Research on trainee therapists supports this general pattern. For example, Bar-Kalifa et al. (2016) showed that trainee therapists were able to track clients' session-to-session fluctuations in their functioning, without significant average over-or underestimation. Yet, trainee therapists' underestimation can sometimes reflect specific personal difficulties (Kivlighan & Marmarosh, 2018). In one study, graduate student counselors rated the alliance lower than clients, and counselor attachment anxiety was associated with a larger negative discrepancy.

Thus, modest therapist caution may be adaptive, but persistent underestimation accompanied by poor tracking of the client's actual experience may reflect trainee anxiety rather than enhanced clinical vigilance (Kivlighan & Marmarosh, 2018).

Therapist-Client Perceptions Predicting Therapeutic Process and Outcomes

Past literature emphasizes the clinical significance of examining perceptions from both therapists and clients. Some highlight the importance of examining the level at which their agreement occurs, the direction of disagreement, and sometimes the point in treatment at which ratings are obtained. For example, Zilcha-Mano et al. (2017) found that client-therapist alliance congruence predicted their symptom levels approximately one month later; congruence explained a significant additional variance beyond therapist-reported alliance.

However, evidence also suggests that jointly high ratings are more associated with improvement than agreement alone. For example, Marmarosh and Kivlighan (2012) found that, among therapist-client dyads, sessions were perceived as smoother when the client and the therapist agreed that the alliance was strong, compared to agreement at a low alliance level.

Clients perceived sessions as less smooth when they rated the alliance lower than their therapists did. Further, positive therapist-client agreement early in treatment predicted greater symptom improvement. Similarly, Dietel et al. (2026) found that client and therapist ratings were moderately correlated, and clients again rated the alliance more positively. Jointly high ratings predicted greater reductions in depression and general distress, but congruence by itself did not predict outcomes, and there were no significant directional effects of incongruence. These findings caution against treating therapist-client perceptual agreement as intrinsically therapeutic.

High therapist-high client agreement may be favorable primarily because both participants experience a strong alliance, not necessarily because their scores are identical (Dietel et al., 2026).

Session-to-Session Ratings and Concurrent Symptoms and Functioning

Studies recognize the importance of examining session-to-session perceptions, as therapist-client perception alignment is partly state-like. For example, Chen et al. (2018) showed that time in treatment was associated with greater temporal therapist-client congruence and lower directional discrepancy. Synchrony was stronger for the bond dimension of alliance than for agreement about tasks and goals, suggesting that therapists may find changes in relational warmth easier to detect than changes in treatment rationale or therapeutic activities (Chen et al., 2018). Research also shows that the implications of agreement can vary by treatment phase. Laws et al. (2017) revealed that over 12 sessions of psychotherapy for depression, clients' and therapists' alliance ratings became more convergent. Alliance convergence was associated with greater reductions in self-rated depression three months later. In an 11-session cognitive behavioral treatment, Moshe-Cohen et al. (2024) found that early in therapy, the combined strength of client and therapist alliance ratings predicted lower posttreatment symptoms and a lower probability of dropout, regardless of the exact level of agreement. Late in treatment, however, outcomes were worse when therapists rated the alliance more positively than clients did, which may signal that the therapist failed to recognize ongoing dissatisfaction or inadequate therapeutic progress.

Furthermore, studies have linked the agreement levels to clients' symptoms and functioning. Atzil-Slonim et al. (2015) found that therapist and client ratings showed substantial temporal correspondence, although therapists generally gave lower ratings. Sessions with greater client symptom severity were also associated with larger therapist-client discrepancies.

Pretreatment symptom severity and personality-disorder status did not explain dyad-level differences in temporal congruence (Atzil-Slonim et al., 2015). The contemporaneous association demonstrates that session-level alliance discrepancy contains important information about the state of the client's current mental health; even generally well-coordinated dyads can become less synchronized when the client is experiencing greater than usual distress (Atzil-Slonim et al., 2015).

Session-to-Session Ratings and Future Symptoms and Functioning

Prospective evidence on therapist-client session perceptions is limited but meaningful. Rubel et al. (2018) examined clients, primarily treated for depression and anxiety, with cognitive behavioral therapy. At the between-dyad level, greater temporal congruence between therapist and client ratings on alliance bond was associated with better pre-to post-treatment outcomes.

The lowest posttreatment symptom levels were observed when therapists tended to rate the bond moderately, but not substantially, lower than clients (i.e., a quadratic association). This pattern again suggests that therapist caution, coexisting with attunement, can be clinically meaningful.

At the within-dyad level, Rubel et al. (2018) found that when therapist and client ratings agreed, higher bond ratings predicted fewer client symptoms in the next session. When a discrepancy was observed, sessions in which the therapist underestimated the bond (i.e., the therapist rated the bond lower than the client did) were followed by greater symptom distress than sessions in which the therapist overestimated the bond. The authors suggested that low therapist ratings may sometimes influence therapist behavior in the subsequent session or reflect difficulties not captured by the client's immediate rating (Rubel et al., 2018). However, this finding should be interpreted alongside other studies in which therapist overestimation was the more concerning pattern (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Kivlighan & Marmarosh, 2018; Li et al., 2024). The inconsistency indicates that discrepancy effects may depend on timing, clinical setting, treatment orientation, and whether the study assesses bond alone or the broader working alliance.

Evidence involving therapist estimation of the client's alliance is especially informative because it evaluates clinical accuracy rather than similarity between two personal viewpoints. Li et al. (2024) found that beginning Chinese therapist trainees generally underestimated clients' alliance ratings. At the between-client level, a trainee's average tendency to overestimate or underestimate the alliance was unrelated to clients' initial symptoms or overall rates of improvement. At the within-client (session-to-session) level, however, sessions in which trainees accurately recognized a strong client-rated alliance were followed by greater symptom relief before the next session than sessions in which they accurately recognized a weak alliance. When trainees were inaccurate, underestimating the client's alliance was followed by more favorable symptom change than overestimating it (Li et al., 2024).

Prospective evidence outside alliance ratings offers convergent support. For example, Chen et al. (2022) had therapists and clients rate session helpfulness and found their ratings changed together across a 12-session integrative treatment. Temporal synchrony itself did not predict outcomes, but therapists' tendency to rate sessions less positively than clients did was associated with greater improvement in psychological functioning and more favorable global treatment evaluations (Chen et al., 2022).

Therapists in Training

The clinical trainee literature is comparably smaller and includes substantial variation in trainee experience, supervision, client severity, and cultural setting. Fitzpatrick et al. (2005) examined 48 client-trainee dyads. Client and trainee alliance ratings increased from early to later treatment, but their divergence remained relatively stable. Disagreement concerning tasks, goals, or the bond did not predict clients' evaluations of session depth or impact. Client-rated agreement on tasks and goals, however, was associated with more positive session evaluations (Fitzpatrick et al., 2005). Daehler et al. (2025) observed 174 client-therapist dyads, with trainees or early-career clinicians. Congruence in positive client progress perceptions at treatment completion was associated with lower somatic symptom severity, but not disability. Client depression predicted lower congruence levels. Lampropoulos (2010) examined trainee-client agreement about treatment improvement at termination, and client and trainee ratings of improvement were positively correlated among treatment completers (r = .53) and unilateral dropouts (r = .30), but the difference between these two correlations was not significant. Bar-Kalifa et al. (2016) showed that greater temporal congruence of client functioning between trainee therapists and clients was associated with better posttreatment depression and general outcome scores, but directional bias was not significantly associated with outcomes.

The large, longitudinal study by Li et al. (2024) offers stronger evidence that, although beginning trainees can systematically underestimate their clients' alliances, session-specific accuracy predicted subsequent symptom relief, and underestimation was associated with greater symptom reduction than overestimation. Li et al. (2025) extended this work by suggesting that more sessions reduced the extent to which therapists' own perspective "clouded" estimates of the clients' perspective, but time alone did not increase their responsiveness to the clients' actual experience. Therapists who were more present with clients during sessions developed a more accurate perception of the clients' experiences (Li et al., 2025).

Gaps in Research

Overall, session-level studies and literature on trainee therapists alike suggest that therapist recognition of the client's experience is associated with clients' current distress, nextsession symptom change, and longer-term treatment outcomes. However, the literature is not yet large or uniform enough to conclude that shared perspectives between therapists and clients always predict improvement. The available research, therefore, supports routine dyadic monitoring and session-level analysis while also indicating the need for additional longitudinal studies that distinguish alliance strength, directional discrepancy, current versus future symptoms, and dimensions of alliance.

In addition, current variable-centered approaches that separately track accuracy or directional bias may overlook the interdependent nature of dyadic interactions, in which multiple perceptual processes operate simultaneously (i.e., the "dyadic dance" between therapists and clients). To resolve this, Li (2022) identified two latent groups of therapist-client dyads: dyadic independence, in which therapists and clients demonstrate self-consistency but non-significant influence on one another's scores, and mutual influence, in which their perceptions influence each other. However, no known study has examined latent configurations of sessions, which may help classify sessions based on distinct combinations of therapist-client synchrony, revealing clinically intuitive and commonly occurring patterns that remain obscured when relational parameters are analyzed separately (Vermunt, 2003). Linking these patterns to client outcomes, both concurrently and prospectively, will generate clinically informed decisions for treatment planning, ongoing monitoring, and revisions to the alliance process, as well as feedback for use in supervision with a therapist-in-training.

The Current Study

The present study extends prior research by identifying distinct session-level patterns of therapists' and clients' perceptions of the therapeutic alliance and treatment progress and by examining whether these patterns are associated with client-reported treatment outcomes, among a sample of therapists in training. We focused on two client outcomes: personal functioning and psychological symptoms. Personal functioning reflects clients' ability to manage their daily roles and relationships effectively, whereas psychological symptoms indicate their current level of emotional distress. Both domains are commonly assessed in studies of treatment effectiveness, particularly in naturalistic clinical settings (e.g., Chen et al., 2022).

Using longitudinal data from both clients and therapists, we adopted a latent class analytic approach to classify sessions according to therapist and client ratings. We then used multilevel models to examine whether these session-level patterns were associated with clients' current and subsequent functioning and psychological symptoms, controlling for clients' scores at the preceding session. Accounting for prior-session outcomes reduces the influence of temporal stability and provides a more stringent test of whether therapist-client session configurations were associated with changes in client well-being over time. This approach extends prior research, as not all studies of alliance agreement have accounted for clients' outcomes at the preceding session.

We addressed two research questions. First, would there be distinct session-level patterns of therapist and client ratings? Second, which patterns predict better concurrent and subsequent client outcomes, as indicated by fewer psychological symptoms and higher personal functioning?

Based on prior evidence, we expected to identify multiple patterns of therapist and client ratings.

We further hypothesized that sessions characterized by shared favorable perceptions would be associated with fewer psychological symptoms and better personal functioning both concurrently and at the subsequent session.

Method Participants

Data for the present study were collected at one on-campus couple and family therapy training center in the southeastern United States. Participants followed the study protocol of a larger Practice Research Network (PRN) consortium that uses shared assessment and datacollection procedures to improve the quality of practice-based research in marriage and family therapy (MFT; Johnson et al., 2017). Eligible clients were individuals who sought treatment at the center and consented to participate in the PRN project.

The analytic sample included 86 adult clients who received individual therapy across a total of 1,035 sessions. Clients attended an average of 12.03 sessions (SD = 12.73, range = 1-56). Of the clients, 28 identified as male and 58 identified as female. Their mean age was 24.95 years (SD = 9.29, range = 18-85). Most clients identified as White (64.9%), followed by Black or African American (17.5%), Hispanic (10.5%), Asian (5.3%), or another racial or ethnic group (1.8%). Regarding relationship status, 7.0% of clients were married, 17.4% were in a relationship and cohabiting, 25.6% were in a committed relationship but were not cohabiting, and 50.0% were single, separated, or divorced. Most clients reported an annual household income below $20,000 (72.1%); 23.3% reported an income between $20,000 and $50,000, and 4.6% reported an income above $50,000. Clients' highest levels of education included a GED or high school diploma (29.1%), vocational or technical school education (3.5%), an associate degree or approximately 2 years of college education (31.4%), a bachelor's degree (24.4%), or a graduate or professional degree (11.6%).

Fifteen therapists in the study were enrolled in a doctoral program accredited by the Commission on Accreditation for Marriage and Family Therapy Education (COAMFTE). Each therapist had completed a master's degree in MFT or a related mental health discipline and had accumulated at least 300 hours of direct client contact. Therapists treated an average of six clients (SD = 3.53, range = 1-14). Five therapists identified as male and 10 identified as female.

Eight therapists identified as White, four as Black, one as Hispanic, and two as Asian.

Procedures

Study procedures were approved by the institutional review boards of the participating universities. Trained research assistants approached treatment-seeking clients to assess their interest in and eligibility for study participation. Clients who agreed to participate provided informed consent and completed a demographic questionnaire at intake. After each therapy session, therapists brought an iPad to the clients to complete the questionnaires, and clients rated their psychological symptoms, personal functioning, and the therapeutic alliance after the therapists left the room. Therapists also provided informed consent and completed post-session ratings of their perceptions of the therapeutic alliance and the client's progress separately.

Therapists received weekly supervision from an American Association for Marriage and

Family Therapy (AAMFT)-approved supervisor. Supervision included direct observation of therapy sessions and discussion of clinical cases. In addition, the supervisor conducted formal therapist evaluations every 2 months and provided feedback regarding therapists' assessment, treatment planning, ethical decision-making, and intervention skills.

Measures

Therapist Session Ratings

Therapists completed a therapist questionnaire at the end of each session (Johnson et al., 2017). The questionnaire assessed therapists' perceptions of the therapeutic alliance, withinsession progress, and overall progress toward treatment goals. Alliance was assessed with the item "Therapy alliance within this session," rated from 0 (very poor) to 6 (excellent). Withinsession progress was assessed with the item "Client(s) progress within this session," rated from 0 (major digress) to 4 (major progress). Overall goal progress was assessed with the item "Client(s) overall progress toward treatment goals," rated from 0 (very poor) to 6 (excellent). The three-item measure demonstrated good internal consistency in the current study (Cronbach's α = .86).

Client-Rated Therapeutic Alliance

Clients' perceptions of the therapeutic alliance were assessed at the end of each session using the Individual Therapy Alliance Scale-Revised, Short Form (ITAS-R-SF; Pinsof et al., 2008). The measure assesses three dimensions of the alliance: bond, agreement on therapeutic goals, and agreement on therapeutic tasks. Its 13 items are rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (completely disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Sample items included "I do not feel accepted by the therapist" (bond), "The therapist and I are not in agreement about the goals for this therapy" (goals), and "The therapist does not understand me" (tasks). Negatively worded items were reverse-coded so that higher scores reflected a stronger therapeutic alliance. In the current study, the Cronbach's alpha was .79 for bond, .86 for goals, and .60 for tasks.

Personal Functioning

Clients rated their personal functioning after each session using the functioning subscale of the Intersession Report questionnaire (Johnson et al., 2010). The subscale contains five items rated on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (very poor) to 7 (excellent). Sample items assessed "my ability to function at work, school, or home" and "the positive sentiment, support, and collaboration in my life." Item scores were combined to create a total functioning score, with higher scores indicating better personal functioning. The measure demonstrated good internal consistency in the current study (Cronbach's α = .81).

Psychological Symptoms

Clients rated their psychological symptoms after each session using the symptom subscale of the Intersession Report questionnaire (Johnson et al., 2010). The subscale consists of two items: "I feel nervous, anxious, or unsettled" and "I feel hopeless, depressed, or down." Items were rated on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 7 (almost all the time).

Scores were combined to form a total symptom score, with higher scores indicating greater psychological distress. The measure demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the current study (Cronbach's α = .77).

Analytic Plan

We conducted multilevel latent class analysis (MLCA; Vermunt, 2003) to identify distinct session-level patterns of therapist and client ratings. MLCA was chosen because the data had a hierarchical structure, with repeated session assessments nested within clients. Preliminary analysis tested an unconditional three-level model to examine therapist-level effects where sessions were nested within clients who were then nested within therapists. The intra-class correlation (ICC) on the therapist-level was .075-.084 for client-rated therapeutic alliance variables, .046-.063 for therapist-rated session variables, and .023-.041 for client-rated outcome variables. As the differences among therapists were small in magnitude (ICCs < .10) and we had a relatively small sample, we decided to estimate only two-level models (sessions nested within clients). A series of two-level multilevel models specifying two through six within-client latent classes was estimated using six indicators: therapist-rated alliance, within-session progress, and overall progress toward treatment goals, as well as client-rated bond, goals, and tasks. The optimal number of classes was selected using several criteria: (a) the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better relative fit; (b) the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT), which compared a model containing k classes with a model containing k -1 classes; (c) adequate class size, defined as at least 1% of the observed sessions; and (d) the conceptual interpretability and distinctiveness of the resulting classes (Jung & Wickrama, 2008).

After the class solution was selected, sessions were assigned to their most likely latent classes, and class membership was represented using dummy-coded variables in the subsequent multilevel analyses.

Two longitudinal multilevel models were then estimated, with psychological symptoms and personal functioning examined separately as session-level outcomes. At the within-client level, predictors included session number, dummy-coded latent-class membership, and the client's corresponding outcome score from the preceding session, which were group-mean centered to isolate within-client deviations from each client's typical level of functioning or symptoms. At the between-client level, client sex was included as a covariate and coded as1 = male and2 = female. For each outcome, we examined whether session-level patterns of therapist and client ratings were associated with clients' functioning or symptoms reported in the same session, as well as at the subsequent session after controlling for the client's outcome score at the preceding session.

Better therapist ratings were related to higher client rated alliance variables, higher current and future client functioning, and lower current and future client symptoms. Higher client rated alliance variables were related to higher current and future client functioning, and lower current and future client symptoms. Female clients had higher selfrated alliance, and therapist-rated session progress and alliance, and higher functioning scores.

The session number was related to better therapist and client ratings of alliances and better client functioning scores. All correlations were in the expected direction.

The MLCA model fit indices are presented in Table 2. As the number of classes increased, BIC and adjusted BIC decreased, and BLRT results indicated differences relative to fewer-class solutions. However, the 6-class solution yielded a small class size (n = 5, fewer than 1% of the current sample) and was therefore deemed too small to produce interpretable results.

The 5-class solution was adopted based on the lowest BIC values and the BLRT results.

Furthermore, the 5-class solution produced meaningful classifications for the current sample. As presented in Figure 1, the 5-class solution indicated 5 types of sessions, which were named based on the reporter, the alliance aspects, and the levels of indicators (Figure 1): "all high" (53.3%, with highest mean ratings across the six indicators relative to other profiles), "bond focused" (16.3%, relatively favorable client ratings with bond more strongly endorsed than goals or tasks, alongside comparatively favorable therapist evaluations of alliance over progress or goals), "goals/tasks focused" (3.3%, stronger client task and goal ratings than bond ratings, alongside comparatively favorable therapist evaluations of progress or goals over alliance), "client low" (18.5%, lower client alliance ratings than aforementioned profiles, while therapist evaluations remain comparatively favorable), and "all low" (8.5%, with lowest mean ratings across all six indicators relative to other profiles), respectively.

Discussion

The present study identified distinct session-level patterns of therapists' and clients' perceptions of the therapeutic alliance and progress, and examined whether these patterns are associated with client-reported treatment outcomes. Findings of this study have significant implications for treatment planning, ongoing monitoring, adaptation to the treatment process, and supervision of therapists-in-training.

Distinct Session Patterns

The identification of five session types-all high, bond focused, goals/tasks focused, client low, and all low-demonstrates that therapist-client perceptions cannot be adequately represented by a single total score or a simple therapist-client difference score. Instead, sessionlevel therapist-client perceptions appear to form qualitatively distinct configurations and components. This person-centered finding extends prior therapist-client dyad research, which has generally examined linear agreement, directional discrepancy, or temporal congruence (Igra et al., 2020; Lev Ari et al., 2025). It also complements Li's (2022) multilevel mixture analysis, which identified patterns of therapist-client dyads rather than individual sessions according to contemporaneous alliance configurations.

The All-High Pattern

The all-high group was the most prevalent, accounting for slightly more than half of the sessions. This predominance is consistent with previous research showing that alliance ratings tend to be concentrated toward the positive end of their response scales (Tryon et al., 2007 (Tryon et al., , 2008)). Thus, the frequency of the all-high pattern may partly reflect the generally positive alliances developed in the present clinical setting, as lower-score clients tended to prematurely leave therapy (Tryon et al., 2007 (Tryon et al., , 2008)). Nevertheless, nearly half of the sessions were classified into one of the four alternative patterns, indicating meaningful session-to-session heterogeneity.

The Client-Low Pattern

The most consistent outcome differences involved the client-low sessions, accounting for nearly 20% of the sessions. Following these sessions, clients reported poorer current personal functioning and more current psychological symptoms than after all-high or bond-focused sessions. Yet, the concurrent findings may not establish causal associations, as alliance and symptoms are known to demonstrate reciprocal within-person associations (Atzil-Slonim et al., 2015; Flückiger, Rubel, et al., 2020). The next-session findings provide stronger evidence of temporal ordering. Client-low sessions predicted higher symptoms at the following session than did all-high or bond-focused sessions, controlling for previous symptoms. This result is consistent with prospective session-level evidence, such as Rubel et al. (2018), who found that higher therapist-client bond ratings predicted fewer next-session symptoms when the ratings were congruent, and Li et al. (2024), who found that session-specific therapist accuracy and discrepancy predicted symptom change before the next session. These results are also consistent with other between-dyad evidence that disagreement may be most concerning when therapists view the alliance more positively than clients (Marmarosh & Kivlighan, 2012; Moshe-Cohen et al., 2024). Together with the present findings, this suggests that a client-low session may reflect an underrecognized clinical difficulty. When therapists continue to perceive the treatment process positively despite low client scores, they may be less likely to invite corrective feedback, reconsider the treatment focus, or address an emerging rupture. The discrepancy may therefore persist beyond the immediate session and interfere with the client's symptom reduction (Rubel et al., 2018).

Notably, we did not identify a therapist-low pattern, despite evidence suggesting therapist underestimation of alliance in both general (e.g., Chen et al., 2022; Flückiger et al., 2018; Rubel et al., 2018) and trainee samples (e.g., Kivlighan & Marmarosh, 2018; Li et al., 2024). This may be due to our different assessments used with therapists and clients and the limited variability of the therapist-reported alliance score. This may also be caused by our MLCA approach, whereas the latent classes recognize session-level patterns, the multiple-level analyses also highlighted within-individual variations beyond between-individual differences. In comparison, past literature may have only compared therapist-client pairs or did not distinguish between versus within-individual differences. This finding, thus, will need to be replicated in larger longitudinal samples with analyses on session-level patterns.

The Bond-Focused Pattern

The bond-focused sessions accounted for 16.3% of all sessions and were associated with outcomes comparable to those of all-high sessions, and better current personal functioning and lower next-session symptoms than client-low sessions. This finding may suggest that a strong affective connection can provide an important relational foundation, even when agreement on treatment goals or tasks is comparatively less pronounced. However, the finding alone should not be interpreted as evidence that goal and task alliance are unimportant. Bordin's model (1979) conceptualizes bond, goals, and tasks as interdependent components of the working alliance, and meta-analytic evidence indicates that goal consensus and task collaboration contribute to treatment outcome, and sometimes more strongly than the bond component (Tryon et al., 2018; Webb et al., 2011). The present results may thus suggest that a strong bond can coexist with favorable short-term outcomes, but they may not demonstrate that the bond can compensate for unresolved disagreements over goals and tasks in therapeutic work.

The Goals/Tasks-Focused and All-Low Patterns

We did not detect a difference in concurrent or subsequent outcomes between all-low or goals/tasks-focused sessions and other sessions. These null findings require cautious interpretation. The goals/tasks-focused class represented only 3.3% of sessions, and the all-low class accounted for 8.5%. Estimates based on these comparatively rare classes are likely to suggest smaller statistical power and should not be interpreted as evidence that these patterns are equivalent to the more prevalent profiles.

The goals/tasks-focused pattern may represent a more instrumental form of therapeutic collaboration. The therapist and the client may agree about the problems to address, the procedures to use, and the progress made in resolving the problems, even though the affective bond can be less developed. Such a configuration could be sufficient for productive work in structured, problem-focused, or skills-based sessions. The rarity of the profile may nevertheless indicate that a strong goal-and-task collaboration usually develops alongside at least a moderately positive bond. Some evidence suggests that when training therapists recognized the goals and tasks of clients, clients rated the sessions more favorably (Fitzpatrick et al., 2005).

Still, further research is needed to determine the implications of goals/tasks-focused sessions.

The null finding for all-low sessions may initially appear inconsistent with studies showing that high therapist-high client agreement is more favorable than low therapist-low client agreement (e.g., Dietel et al., 2026; Marmarosh & Kivlighan, 2012). One possibility is that an all-low session represents a recognized clinical difficulty acknowledged by both participants.

Their shared recognition may increase the likelihood that the problem will be discussed or that the therapist will adjust treatment. By contrast, in a client-low session, the therapist may be unaware that the client is disengaged or dissatisfied. Alliance-rupture research indicates that ruptures are not uniformly detrimental; rupture-repair episodes can contribute to favorable outcomes when therapists and clients use them to renegotiate their relationship and therapeutic work (Eubanks et al., 2018). It is also possible that some all-low sessions involved difficult but therapeutically productive work. Challenging established interpersonal patterns, discussing painful material, or negotiating disagreement may temporarily lower both participants' session evaluations without producing immediate deterioration. Notably, the latent classes were based on relative rather than absolute levels, and the "low" ratings may not necessarily represent clinically poor alliances (as the multilevel analyses accounted for mean scores at the individual level).

Taken together, the results suggest that neither agreement alone nor alliance level alone fully explains the clinical significance of therapist-client perception patterns. All-high sessions were generally favorable, but bond-focused sessions produced similarly favorable outcomes despite relatively weaker goal or task ratings. Conversely, all-low agreement was not significantly associated with worse outcomes, whereas the discrepant client-low pattern was consistently associated with poorer functioning and higher symptoms.

Strengths and Limitations

This multi-informant, longitudinal study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that session-level therapist-client ratings form distinct and clinically meaningful configurations.

The prospective association between client-low sessions and next-session symptoms provides particularly important evidence that an underrecognized clinical difficulty may have consequences extending beyond the immediate session. However, this study is not without limitations.

First, several factors limit the generalizability of the findings, including the geographic location, the trainee context, and the relatively lower-resourced client sample, and potential social desirability in rating sessions. Clients may hesitate to provide critical feedback when completing ratings in the treatment setting, particularly if they expect their therapist to see their responses. Regular supervision may also have increased attention to how trainees evaluate their work and to opportunities to respond to client concerns, which may differ from a naturalistic therapy setting. We also acknowledge that Hispanic ethnicity and racial identities are not mutually exclusive; however, the present dataset included Hispanic as a response option in the race variable, which did not allow Hispanic participants to report their racial identity separately.

Second, due to a lack of data, the therapist and client ratings were not measured using the same assessment tool. As such, their scores (especially client-low) should be interpreted as relative to other sessions rather than relative to each informant. Third, we only predicted client functioning and symptom levels at the next session, although some evidence suggests that the effects may emerge longer-term, such as several sessions later (Zilcha-Mano et al., 2015; 2017), which should be addressed in future studies. Additionally, although a strength of the study was its identification of session-level configurations beyond the vast evidence on therapist-client pairs, the analyses accounted for sessions nested within clients but not for clients nested within therapists. Shared therapist influences, including systematic differences in rating tendencies and treatment delivery, may have affected both the identified session profiles and their associations with client outcomes. Moreover, missing data also limit the interpretation of the findings, particularly because therapist ratings were unavailable for a proportion of sessions. Missingness was associated with some observed client and session characteristics, and potential dependence on unobserved factors cannot be ruled out. Thus, model-based handling of missing data may not fully eliminate bias. Moreover, subsequent outcome models treated most-likely class assignments as observed and did not incorporate classification uncertainty. These limitations warrant caution when interpreting the profiles and their associations with client outcomes.

Finally, the measure of psychological symptoms only captured internalizing symptoms; however, clients also may experience externalizing symptoms or other concerns that were not captured by the current measures.

Future research is recommended to replicate the configurations across clinics and levels of therapist experience, use parallel measures when the aim is to study therapist-client agreement, and examine whether therapist progress ratings add information beyond client alliance and prior symptoms. Studies should also address therapist clustering, missing reporter data, social desirability, and classification uncertainty to provide more robust evidence.

Clinical and Training Implications

Clinically, the findings support the importance of routinely collecting client ratings rather than relying solely on therapist judgment. Nearly one in five sessions was classified as client-low in our trainee sample, indicating that potentially meaningful therapist-client discrepancies were not rare. For therapists, reviewing session-specific ratings could help identify occasions when clients feel less connected, less confident in treatment goals, or less engaged with therapeutic tasks than therapists realize. The ratings can open a conversation with clients as an opportunity to reassess and improve treatment, and clinicians can ask separately about feeling understood, the relevance of treatment goals, and the usefulness of session activities.

In supervision, trainees can examine client feedback alongside their own evaluations and distinguish feeling that a session went well from evidence that the client experienced it that way. Supervisors can help trainees consider specific moments in the session, practice inviting critical feedback, and identify an adjustment to discuss collaboratively with the client. Discrepancies in perceptions should be treated as information rather than evidence that either participant is "wrong." By facilitating more coordinated "dance" between therapists and clients, better treatment outcomes can be achieved.
