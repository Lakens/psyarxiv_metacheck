Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Abstract

Legal access to psilocybin almost always requires a medical diagnosis first, usually treatmentresistant depression (TRD) or severe PTSD, as though informed consent alone were not sufficient. This article makes two connected arguments. Extending the autonomy argument already accepted for terminally ill patients, competent adults who understand the potential benefits and risks of a psilocybin experience should be able to choose it for reasons of psychological wellness or existential anxiety relief, not only because a clinician has certified a qualifying illness, a right the legal literature calls "cognitive liberty". Just as important, the cost of access is not fixed; it reflects the regulatory framework under which psilocybin is provided. No jurisdiction has yet completed the medicines authorisation pathway; the furthest-advanced clinical programme, Compass Pathways' COMP360, met its Phase 3 primary endpoints and reported six-month durability data for TRD by July 2026, its US new drug application was completed in November 2026, and its developer has signalled an annual cost close to that of esketamine nasal spray, USD 50,000-65,000, or roughly USD 17,000-22,000 per dose. Meanwhile, Australia's narrower authorised-prescriber legal route for TRD, using GMP-manufactured but unregistered psilocybin, already costs AUD $20,000 per course. Three distinct non-medical routes cost far less. Truffles in the Netherlands, sold under food law with no diagnosis and no supervision required, and Colorado and Oregon's state-licensed psilocybin services, which require no diagnosis but do involve a supervising facilitator, cost up to $3,500 per session. Removing the diagnosis requirement while leaving this cost structure untouched risks replacing the regulatory barrier with the wealth-based one, unless expanded access is paired with public subsidy. The article also briefly reviews the safety evidence, the political origins of psilocybin's scheduling, and the double standard in how its risks are treated. A Canadian case illustrates the institutional and regulatory framework dysfunction, a dying man denied psilocybin for existential relief while approved for medical assistance in dying within weeks, before the article closes with policy recommendations addressing both access and funding.

2. Reviewing the Safety and Effect Evidence

What follows is a summary of what the current evidence shows about psilocybin's effects and safety, offered to inform policy rather than to argue clinical effectiveness or make a case for medical authorisation; nowhere discussed in this article is psilocybin an approved medicine. Figure 1 illustrates the range of individual outcomes this evidence documents, from a profoundly meaningful experience to no notable effect or a mild, transient adverse effect. The safety data available, though still developing, have not identified risks disproportionate to those already accepted elsewhere in regulated and unregulated activity. A pooled analysis covering 536 psilocybin sessions across 368 people in 14 studies found only modest, short-lived increases in blood pressure that returned to near-normal within five hours, and the researchers recommended loosening the cardiovascular cut-offs currently in use (Nayak et al., 2026). A separate pooled analysis of randomised crossover studies found tachycardia above 100 beats per minute in about 7 percent of administrations and no kidney or liver effects at study end (Straumann et al., 2024). Oregon's own no-diagnosis programme, discussed in Section 7, now supplies a large-scale real-world safety check on this picture directly: across more than 22,000 clients served since 2023, the severe adverse event rate requiring emergency services was 0.15 percent, below the roughly 0.9 percent rate observed across psilocybin clinical trials, which typically enrol higher-risk populations (Center for Psychedelic Policy, 2026). A peer-reviewed study of the same programme, following 346 people who used psilocybin at Oregon's licensed centres between late 2024 and mid-2026, corroborates this picture from an academic source: most participants described their session as beneficial, and more than half ranked it among the most meaningful experiences of their life, echoing the Johns Hopkins finding below in a real-world, no-diagnosis population studied at far larger scale (Korthuis et al., 2026). One clinical data scientist not involved in the study noted that the overall adverse event rate was in line with what would be expected from other psychiatric interventions, despite far less clinical supervision than a trial setting provides. A larger, two-state preprint sample reaches a similar picture and adds a further nuance: following 2,363 people who used regulated psilocybin services in Oregon and Colorado, researchers found depression, anxiety, and wellbeing scores had improved substantially at two weeks, though they were explicit that these were observational findings, not proof of causation. Notably, higher doses were associated with stronger mystical-type experience scores but not with better depression or anxiety outcomes, which did not differ between doses above and below 30 milligrams, complicating the assumption that safety or efficacy simply tracks dose intensity (Thompson et al., 2026).

In the founding Johns Hopkins study of psilocybin in healthy volunteers, none of whom had any qualifying diagnosis, 67 percent of participants rated their session as the single most meaningful experience of their life or among the top five, on a par with the birth of a child, with ratings holding at 14-month follow-up (Griffiths et al., 2006; 2008). This is significant precisely because the most widely cited result in the entire psilocybin literature comes from a study of psychologically healthy adults with no illness to treat, the very population most current access models exclude by design.

Diagnosed cancer patients have also shown durable benefit in randomised trials (Griffiths et al., 2016; Ross et al., 2016), but the largest prospective survey of naturalistic psilocybin use to date, 2,833 respondents who mostly reported using it for "self-exploration" rather than to treat a diagnosed condition, found persisting reductions in anxiety and depression alongside increased cognitive flexibility and spiritual wellbeing at two to three months, in a population no clinical trial would have enrolled (Nayak, Jackson, Sepeda et al., 2023). Diagnosis is plainly not what determines whether someone comes away with a meaningful, lasting shift in outlook; these findings were never intended to demonstrate a general medical indication for anyone. None of this is a small, marginal phenomenon: an estimated 30 million or more adults in the United States alone have used a classic psychedelic at some point in their lives, a base rate of real-world exposure that already dwarfs the total population enrolled across every psilocybin clinical trial combined (Krebs & Johansen, 2013). More recent, nationally representative data put past-year use alone at roughly 11 million US adults (4%) in 2025, making psilocybin the most widely used psychedelic in the country (RAND, 2026).

3. The Indication Gap: Why Wellness Has No Regulatory Home

Drug development proceeds by indication. A sponsor seeking regulatory approval must specify a diagnosis, most consequentially one recognised by the DSM-5-TR, the classification system that anchors regulatory submissions, trial endpoints, and insurance reimbursement in the United States, the largest and most commercially decisive market. Quality of life, personal meaning, and general mental wellness, the reasons a non-diagnosed adult would most plausibly want a supervised psilocybin experience, do not map onto that system at all. There is no diagnostic code for wanting to live better, and no clinical trial endpoint designed to capture it as a primary outcome. This is not a temporary gap likely to close as the evidence base matures; it is a structural feature of how medicines get approved, and it means the conventional pathway is unlikely ever to serve this population, however strong the underlying evidence for benefit becomes.

Even within the medical framing, the gap is visible. Demoralization, the clinical construct closest to general existential and quality-of-life distress, has had its own code in the International Classification of Diseases since ICD-11, MB22.2, although as a symptom or sign rather than a mental disorder diagnosis, yet it is still not recognised in DSM-5-TR (Fava & Guidi, 2023). That recognition carries less practical weight than it might appear: as of the World Health Organization's most recent reporting, only around 14 countries were actively using ICD-11 for clinical coding, and the United States, whose DSM-5-TR governs the regulatory pathway this article is chiefly concerned with, is not expected to implement it for several years, relying on ICD-10-CM in the meantime (World Health Organization, 2024). A sponsor seeking approval for a demoralization-focused indication in the United States has no established DSM category to submit against. Wellness and quality of life, being further still from a diagnosable condition, fare worse: they are not diagnoses in either system, only constructs measured by research scales, with no established route into a medicinal product or drug label under current frameworks at all. The predictable consequence is that commercial and clinical trial activity concentrates on conditions with an established diagnostic home and a validated endpoint, treatment-resistant depression, PTSD, and more recently anorexia nervosa, precisely because a sponsor can submit an application against them.

This gap has a mirror on the clinician's side of the table. A 2024 survey of hundreds of US healthcare professionals found physicians reported markedly lower openness to psilocybin's clinical use than other professional groups such as nurses, alongside generally low objective knowledge of its pharmacology and risks, a pattern the researchers attributed to absent formal training rather than considered clinical judgment (Wang, Mathai, Gukasyan, Nayak, & Garcia-Romeu, 2024). That absence of training is structural, not incidental: because psilocybin holds no accepted medical use under US law, it is not, as a rule, taught in medical school curricula at all, a connection a separate 2023 survey of medical students traced directly to its Schedule I status (Wang, Sun, Nava, Sampiere, & Jacobs, 2023). Diagnosis-gated access does not only leave patients without a route in; it leaves the clinicians meant to guide them without the training or professional knowledge to do so.

The instability runs deeper than any single diagnostic manual. Unmet medical need, the broader concept that governs priority review, accelerated approval, and reimbursement decisions across the EU as well as the US, turns out to have no shared definition even among the regulators, health technology assessment bodies, and industry stakeholders who apply it daily: a 2026 mapping study of EU health-policy stakeholders identified eight distinct purposes, five framing dimensions, and five separate criteria in active use, with interpretation depending heavily on why the concept is being invoked in a given case (Claessens et al., 2026). This is not a psychedelic-specific finding, which is precisely what makes it useful here: if the regulatory establishment cannot agree on what counts as medical need even for indications everyone accepts as legitimate, treating the presence or absence of a diagnosis as a bright, self-evident line for psilocybin access asks a contested, purpose-dependent concept to do work that, by the field's own admission, it is not equipped to do.

None of this is an argument against safeguards. It is an argument that diagnosis is doing a job it was never designed to do. A diagnosis describes a category of illness; it does not measure an individual's capacity to weigh risk and consent, and it does not measure the actual risk a specific supervised session poses to a specific adult. Screening for those two things, capacity and risk, is a different exercise from checking a diagnostic code, and nothing in the evidence or the regulatory literature reviewed above suggests the two should be treated as interchangeable.

American law already contains a partial precedent for prioritising informed consent over full proof of efficacy, though a revealing one. The federal Right to Try Act of 2018 permits terminally ill patients to access investigational drugs that have completed only Phase 1 safety testing, bypassing FDA and institutional review board sign-off, on the basis of physician certification and the patient's own written informed consent (Pub. L. 115-176, 2018). The law demonstrates that American regulators are willing, in the right circumstances, to let a documented, individual act of consent substitute for the full evidentiary case ordinarily required before access is granted. But its eligibility criteria, a life-threatening diagnosis, exhausted treatment options, inability to join a trial, show that even this consent-forward model is still diagnosis-gated. It extends autonomy to a narrow category of the already diagnosed dying, not to the adult population generally. The argument developed here asks why that same willingness to trust individual consent should stop being available the moment a person is not sick.

4. Cognitive Liberty, Autonomy, and Informed Consent

The legal and philosophical literature already has a name for the right at stake: cognitive liberty, an individual's sovereignty over their own mental processes, developed most directly in drug policy scholarship by Charlotte Walsh, who has argued that the prohibition of psychedelics itself constitutes an infringement of the right to autonomous self-determination over one's own mind (Walsh, 2016). The concept has since moved from drug-policy scholarship into mainstream legal theory. Nita Farahany describes cognitive liberty as an emerging human right built on three pillars, mental privacy, self-determination, and freedom of thought, and argues this sovereignty over one's own mind is a baseline entitlement, not something a person has to be ill to deserve (Farahany, 2023). Cognitive liberty is not synonymous with a right to be free of illness; it is a right to determine the contents and character of one's own consciousness, whether or not that determination serves a therapeutic purpose (Bublitz, 2013). A regulatory system that only recognises this right once a person is diagnosed with something has, in effect, redefined a fundamental liberty as a treatment.

Nothing here turns on whether psilocybin can be shown to help any particular person. Someone who has been told the risks, uncertainties, and limits of a supervised session should be free to accept or decline them, regardless of whether the experience carries a certified medical benefit or treats a diagnosed condition. Informed consent doctrine already asks the right question: does this person understand the risks, and are they capable of deciding? A diagnostic requirement replaces that individual judgment with a blunter rule, competence plus illness. Even if every wellness finding surveyed in Section 2 were later disproven, this piece of the argument would hold, since it never rested on demonstrated benefit, only on whether risk has been disclosed and capacity honestly assessed.

The evidence reviewed above makes the case stronger without being required to make it at all. If a psychologically healthy volunteer with no diagnosis can report one of the most meaningful experiences of their life, and if naturalistic, self-directed use in a non-clinical population shows durable improvements in wellbeing, then the population currently excluded by diagnosis-gating is not a marginal edge case. It may be closer to the population the existing clinical evidence already describes best.

Three objections merit brief consideration. The concern that non-diagnosed use trades a therapeutic framework for a purely recreational one collapses a distinction that does not track risk: a supervised, screened, and prepared experience undertaken for reasons of wellness is not pharmacologically or procedurally different from the same experience undertaken to treat a diagnosis, and the precautionary case for screening applies equally to both without requiring diagnosis as the gate. The concern that removing the diagnosis requirement would flood an under-resourced system ignores that this article does not call for unlimited access, but for individual capacity and risk assessment to replace diagnosis as the relevant test, a change in criteria, not a removal of screening. And the concern that wellness use is inherently less serious than treatment use is itself the position this article disputes: cognitive liberty, as set out above, does not distinguish between using one's mind well and using it to feel better.

5. The Double Standard, Regulatory Origins, and the Case for a Non-Medical Pathway

How does psilocybin's risk profile compare with things society already allows any adult to do freely, diagnosis or no diagnosis, without a screening form beyond a basic health questionnaire?

The most rigorous empirical answer to that question does not favour the current legal hierarchy: when an independent panel of scientists scored twenty drugs across sixteen harm criteria, both to users and to others, alcohol ranked most harmful overall and psilocybin ranked among the least harmful of all twenty substances assessed (Nutt et al., 2010). Alcohol is the clearest comparison: heavier drinking carries well-documented, dose-dependent cardiovascular and other risks, yet no diagnosis is required to drink, only age. Tobacco is starker still: legal for any adult, no screening at the point of sale, and responsible for an estimated 8 million deaths a year worldwide (World Health Organization, 2023). Neither exemption is an accident of oversight: the Controlled Substances Act explicitly excludes "distilled spirits, wine, malt beverages, or tobacco" from its own definition of a controlled substance, and the U.S. Supreme Court acknowledged that tobacco products fit the literal statutory definitions of a drug and device under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, yet held that Congress had excluded them from FDA jurisdiction (FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 529 U.S. 120, 2000). The double standard, in other words, is written into the law by name, not a gap regulators overlooked. Activities marketed as adventurous, oncein-a-lifetime experiences show the same pattern from a different angle: heart problems have been a leading cause of scuba diving deaths across five decades of Australian fatality data, yet no diving organisation requires a diagnosis, only a medical questionnaire and, where a condition is disclosed, a doctor's referral (Lippmann, 2025). The comparison has a well-documented precedent in drug-policy scholarship itself: David Nutt, then chair of the UK government's official drug advisory council, calculated that horse riding produced serious adverse events at a substantially higher rate than MDMA use, and was dismissed from his advisory post later that year, after further public remarks on drug harms (Nutt, 2009). The reaction illustrates the point as clearly as the comparison itself: what determines whether an activity's risk is treated as tolerable is a policy judgment, not the underlying numbers and evidence. What actually governs access in each case is an individual medical declaration and consent process, not a diagnostic gate, precisely the model this article argues should apply to psilocybin regardless of the reason a person seeks it.

Bioethicists have named this pattern "psychedelic exceptionalism": the worry that these substances face tighter regulatory scrutiny than comparably risky activities without anyone having to state why (Cheung et al., 2025). Either way, the diagnosis-gating this article examines is not defended by a documented difference in risk between diagnosed and non-diagnosed users, and no feature of psilocybin marks it as distinctive enough to warrant a rule applied to no other substance with effects on the heart or mind.

How strict that original scheduling decision needed to be is itself questionable. Nutt, King, and Nichols (2013), writing in Nature Reviews Neuroscience, trace the psychedelic restrictions of the late 1960s and early 1970s less to new evidence of outsized danger than to the substances' association with a youth movement resisting the draft, and they rank the resulting decades-long freeze on research among the more severe instances of politically motivated scientific suppression in modern history, comparable in their account to the Church's treatment of Copernican astronomy. Schedule I status for psilocybin followed in the Controlled Substances Act of 1970, which placed psilocybin in the most restrictive schedule before the evidence had been reviewed.

This history matters for the regulatory pathway question directly. Turning a documented physiological or psychological effect into a registered medicine means GMP-standard production, a formal clinical programme, and marketing authorisation, a slow, costly route built for the diagnosis-anchored model this article critiques. Even the furthest-advanced psilocybin programme illustrates how slow that route actually is: Compass Pathways' synthetic formulation, COMP360, met its primary endpoint in the first of two pivotal Phase 3 trials for treatmentresistant depression in mid-2025 and in the second in early 2026, with its US new drug application completed only on 9 November 2026, and no jurisdiction anywhere having yet completed the process (Compass Pathways, 2026a, 2026b). Financial analysts have begun drawing their own comparison. Stifel's initiation of coverage frames COMP360 as "Spravato 2.0", a reference to Janssen's esketamine nasal spray, one of only two FDA-approved treatments for treatmentresistant depression, and predicts a similar intermittent, clinic-based treatment model will prove commercially scalable (Stifel, as cited in Benzinga, 2025). No official list price had been published at the time of writing, but at an investor conference in September 2026 Compass Pathways' Chief Commercial Officer indicated that COMP360 would be priced per dose, at an annual cost in the approximate range of the USD 50,000 to 65,000 the company attributes to Spravato (Investing.com, 2026; MarketBeat, 2026). Because the company expects patients to receive about three doses a year, within a range of two to four, that target implies roughly USD 17,000 to 22,000 per administration. That figure covers the drug alone and excludes the six hours of in-clinic monitoring the company expects each dose to require; FDA's final guidance on psychedelic clinical investigations (July 2026) recommends two trained monitors for the full duration of each dosing session, so staffing adds materially to that figure (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2026). The US cost-effectiveness model discussed in Section 7 put the cost of an entire course of psilocybinassisted therapy, clinician time included, at USD 5,000, rising to USD 20,000 in sensitivity analyses (Avanceña et al., 2025), so a single dose of the drug at the signalled price would already sit close to or above the top of that range. The commercial logic is simple enough. Spravato is typically given 25 to 50 times a year, so a product given three times a year can only match its annual revenue by charging many times more per session. This remains an informal signal from a company executive rather than a confirmed price, but it is the clearest indication yet of what the medicines pathway is likely to cost per session, roughly six to seven times the upper end of what an Oregon client pays for a full facilitated session. A second application may test that price. The nonprofit Usona Institute completed its own new drug application for psilocybin in major depressive disorder on 2 November 2026; both it and Compass hold Commissioner's National Priority Vouchers targeting a one-to-two-month review, and the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER) will assess COMP360's value at a public meeting in May 2027 (Psychedelic Alpha, 2026b; Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, 2026). Usona, which has not patented its synthetic psilocybin, has announced no price but has long said it will deliver an approved therapy at the lowest possible cost (Lucid News, 2020); whether a second, unpatented product disciplines prices will be an early test of this article's argument that cost follows the regulatory and commercial framework rather than the molecule. Nothing in that route follows automatically from the underlying evidence: file the identical effect under a different regulatory category and it can reach consumers directly, at a fraction of the price, so long as no medical claim accompanies it, exactly what happened when the European Food Safety Authority approved a bowel-function claim for dried plums as a food rather than a medicine (Attaluri et al., 2011; European Food Safety Authority, 2012). Tellingly, chronic constipation, the condition dried plums are approved to address, is also treated by registered laxative medicines built to GMP pharmaceutical standards, the very trial establishing the dried-plums claim compared it directly against one such product, psyllium; the two routes rest on comparable evidence, yet the regulatory category alone accounts for most of the price gap (Attaluri et al., 2011). Psilocybin-containing truffles are sold in the Netherlands under exactly this logic: a naturally occurring, unpatentable product, regulated as a food-adjacent item, available to any adult for reasons of quality of life and wellness with no diagnosis, no prescription, and no clinical infrastructure required. This is not a loophole; it is the same regulatory logic that lets a supermarket sell prunes without a marketing authorisation. A regulatory system that recognises only the medicines pathway for psilocybin, however wellintentioned, forecloses the non-medical pathway that already exists in principle and is already operating in practice in one EU member state. Legal scholars have begun to make this case directly: on independent, multi-criteria harm assessments psilocybin ranks below alcohol and tobacco, yet only alcohol and tobacco are regulated as consumer commodities rather than controlled substances, a distinction that reflects political history, not a consistent judgment about actual harm (Etkin & Joralemon, 2026).

This asymmetry is compounded by patent economics. New medicines typically progress to wellfunded trials because a patent lets a company recover the cost of approval; whole psilocybin mushrooms and truffles are a naturally occurring product that cannot be patented in the way a synthetic compound can. Capital has found patentable, synthetic psilocybin analogues at a scale it has never found the unpatentable natural form: single 2026 financing rounds for patentable synthetic formulations, LSD among them, have run to hundreds of millions of dollars. Calling for large-scale definitive trials before revisiting the regulatory category sounds neutral, but for a natural, unpatentable substance it may in practice be a demand for evidence the underlying economics of drug development make unlikely ever to fully arrive, regardless of how the diagnosis question is resolved.

The regulatory logic this article calls for is not unprecedented outside psychedelics. Functional foods and wellness products already occupy the same non-medical space psilocybin truffles occupy in the Netherlands: melatonin is sold over the counter as a dietary supplement in the United States and Canada, although it remains prescription-only in much of the EU, the UK and Australia; adaptogenic herbal supplements marketed for stress and mood are regulated as food or supplement products in most Western markets rather than as medicines; and CBD wellness products, in many jurisdictions, are sold explicitly on quality-of-life grounds without any requirement that the buyer hold a diagnosis. None of these products is claimed to cure a named illness on the label, and none requires one. Psilocybin's exclusion from this same regulatory category is not a function of its risk profile relative to these products; melatonin and CBD are not risk-free, and adaptogens have a thinner evidence base than psilocybin does. It is a function of psilocybin's scheduling history, addressed above, not a considered judgment that it uniquely requires medical gatekeeping that these comparable wellness products do not.

South Korea illustrates the far end of this spectrum. Its criminal law reaches beyond domestic prohibition and applies the nationality principle to its own citizens, so someone who used psilocybin entirely lawfully in the Netherlands, Oregon, or Jamaica can face prosecution at home simply for having done so, with the legality of the act where it happened counting for nothing (Republic of Korea, Criminal Act, Art. 3; Cho, 2019). Every other jurisdiction surveyed in this article limits itself to controlling what happens inside its own borders; South Korea claims something further, the authority to punish a citizen for a choice that broke no law anywhere it was made. Japan and China sit close behind. Japan closed a 1990s loophole in 2002 by banning the mushrooms themselves under its Narcotics and Psychotropics Control Law, with possession now penalised on a par with cocaine, and China's drug law carries some of the most severe penalty structures in the world with no medical or research exception in practice. All three jurisdictions combine this with the same double standard already documented above: alcohol and tobacco remain normalised, everyday responses to stress, while psilocybin, whose risk profile compares favourably to both, is treated as categorically different. Prohibition has not removed demand; it has displaced it. A November 2026 report from Tokyo documents psilocybin "analogues" such as 4-Pro-MET sold as "not for human consumption", gummies whose active ingredient is an undisclosed proprietary blend, and products that municipal test purchases found did not match their labels; one Japanese family gave such a product to a mother with ALS after reading about psilocybin-assisted therapy abroad (Inoue, 2026). Where a competent adult's choice has no legal route, it moves to products no regulator has inspected, the inverse of the NVWA-supervised Dutch model described in Section 7. What makes the region worth naming, rather than filing away as more of the same prohibition, is that scientific and clinical work is proceeding from inside it anyway, at real professional and personal cost: researchers at the University of Hong Kong have published peer-reviewed findings on psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression despite the surrounding legal climate (Fang et al., 2024), and Professor Hiroyuki Uchida at Keio University is running Asia's first twenty-first-century psilocybin clinical trial, now with backing from a domestic pharmaceutical partner (Yonezawa et al., 2026). These remain modest efforts operating inside systems built to make them difficult, but their existence undercuts any reading of this as fixed cultural preference rather than policy choice; the mismatch between documented need and permitted choice traced throughout this article simply reappears here at wider scale. The question it raises is the same one, only starker: whether competent adults are entitled to make this choice for themselves, or whether the state gets to make it for them indefinitely, wherever in the world they happen to be standing when they do.

6. When Institutional Risk Aversion Outlasts Patient Autonomy

A case out of Canada, covered in media, brings the problem this article addresses into sharp focus (The Journal, Sarnia-Lambton, 2026; TheraPsil, 2026a). A 75-year-old Ontario man dying of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis sought legal psilocybin access through Health Canada's Special Access Program, with his own physicians' support. Eleven months later, the request was refused: officials judged he was not yet close enough to death and that other treatments remained an option. Within weeks of that refusal, the same man was cleared for medical assistance in dying. A system unwilling to authorise a reversible, comparatively low-risk intervention proved willing, almost immediately afterward, to authorise an irreversible one. This is a diagnosis-gated system failing on its own terms: the patient had a diagnosis, a terminal one, and institutional caution still produced this outcome. A regulatory framework built around diagnosis as the gate to access does not necessarily become more responsive by tightening the diagnostic criteria further; the failure here was not that the diagnosis was wrong, but that diagnosis-gating itself defers to institutional risk aversion over individual consent even in the hardest cases.

A second Canadian case reinforces the pattern. In 2020, Thomas Hartle became the first Canadian to obtain legal psilocybin access, seeking relief from the anxiety of a stage IV colon cancer diagnosis; the Saskatoon father then struggled for years to keep that access renewed, at one point travelling to Nanaimo, British Columbia, for treatment, before dying on 13 August 2024 after a decade of illness (CBC News, 2026). His case gave its name to Bill C-286, popularly known as "Thomas's Bill," which was brought before the Canadian Parliament on 16 June 2026 and would let physicians prescribe psilocybin directly while granting the drug priority regulatory review. Even where the Special Access Program succeeds, the cost is substantial: patients typically pay CAD $2,500 to $6,500 for the full course of prescriber oversight, preparation, dosing, and integration, none of it publicly reimbursed (TheraPsil, 2026b). Both cases involved a diagnosis; both still ran into the limits of a system that treats even documented illness as insufficient grounds for consent to be honoured quickly. That should give pause to any argument that simply tightening or expanding the list of qualifying diagnoses will resolve the underlying problem.

7. Alternative Regulatory Models and the Question of Cost

The Netherlands remains the clearest example of a jurisdiction operating outside the diagnosisgated model entirely: psilocybin-containing truffles (sclerotia) are legally available to adults, sold in licensed smartshops with no diagnosis, no prescription, and no individualised clinical screening requirement, provided no medical claim is made. Truffles remain legal under the Commodities Act (Warenwet), the Dutch equivalent of food safety law, and fall outside the scope of the Opium Act, which lists the mushroom's fruiting body but not its underground sclerotia (Netherlands Enterprise Agency, n.d.). They are sold in "smartshops" subject to standard food safety regulations, including expiration dates and age certification for adults 18 and over, with active oversight from the Netherlands Food and Consumer Product Safety Authority (NVWA), the same body responsible for inspecting ordinary groceries (NVWA, n.d.). This is an adult-use consumer product model: the state ensures the product is unadulterated and restricts sales to adults, but leaves the decision to consume it to the citizen. This demonstrates that the medicines pathway is not the only conceivable regulatory architecture for psilocybin access.

Nepal reaches the same result by a different route: its Narcotic Drugs (Control) Act, 1976 does not list psilocybin mushrooms, leaving them in a legal grey zone rather than affirmatively legalised (Government of Nepal, 1976), and they are traded informally at retreat centres and markets with no diagnosis, no age verification, and no clinical screening of any kind. Oregon's regulated psilocybin services depart from diagnosis-gating in the same way: Measure 109 explicitly requires no medical referral or diagnosis, open to any adult 21 or older who passes an individual health screen carried out by state-licensed facilitators (Oregon Health Authority, n.d. ; Center for Psychedelic Policy, 2026). The underlying statute reaches this result through a similarly non-medical legal architecture to the Dutch one: Oregon law defines psilocybin-producing fungi as a "crop" and regulates psilocybin products as a food or other commodity subject to the State Department of Agriculture, not as a controlled pharmaceutical, and separately bars the Oregon Health Authority from requiring a medical diagnosis for access (Or. Rev. Stat. § § 475A.570, 475A.574; Etkin & Joralemon, 2026). What Oregon has not escaped is a wealth-based gate in diagnosis's place: because sessions cost up to $3,000 out of pocket with no insurance reimbursement, roughly 43 percent of clients earn $95,376 or more annually, while only about one in ten falls below a Medicaid-eligibility proxy income line, and two-thirds travel from outside Oregon (Center for Psychedelic Policy, 2026). Colorado's Natural Medicine Health Act, approved by referendum in 2022, goes a step further than Oregon's model. Alongside licensed healing centres, which began operating in 2025 and charge a broadly comparable $1,000 to $3,500 per session (Psychedelic Beacon, 2026), the Act separately decriminalises personal possession, cultivation, and sharing, not selling, of psilocybin and several other natural psychedelics for adults 21 and older, entirely outside the facilitatedsession system. No diagnosis, facilitator, or licensed venue is required for this second tier at all; an adult may legally grow and use psilocybin at home unsupervised. This makes Colorado's personal use tier the most permissive model in the jurisdictions surveyed here, more so even than Oregon, since it dispenses with supervision as well as diagnosis. This is a genuine improvement on blanket prohibition and on diagnosis-gated models alike, but it illustrates precisely the risk this article flags below: remove one barrier and, absent public funding, a different one fills the gap. Portugal decriminalised personal possession and use of all drugs, including psilocybin, in 2001, and Spain does not criminally prosecute personal possession or cultivation on private property, though neither has established a legal market. Germany runs an administrative compassionateuse pathway operating under Article 83 of EU Regulation (EC) No 726/2004, Switzerland has run its own case-by-case named-patient exception system since 2014, and the Czech Republic became the first EU member state with a dedicated national medical psilocybin law, effective 1 January 2026, all three still diagnosis-anchored. Germany's programme has since been extended to June 2027 (Psychedelic Alpha, 2026c). Belgium may follow: a compassionate use programme for treatment-resistant depression, led by the non-profit psychiatric hospital operator Epsylon ASBL with the support of Psychedelic Society Belgium and supplied with GMP-manufactured botanical psilocybin (PEX010), is being submitted to the Federal Agency for Medicines and Health Products (FAMHP), with a decision not expected before spring 2027 (Rhelion Life Sciences, 2026b; Psychedelic Alpha, 2026d). Slovenia moved psilocybin from Group I to Group II of its drug classification in October 2026, a rescheduling that does not by itself create an access programme. In the United States, New Mexico's Medical Psilocybin Act (2025) runs in the opposite direction to Oregon and Colorado: a clinician-administered programme restricted to qualifying conditions, namely treatment-resistant depression, PTSD, substance use disorders and end-of-life care, with first patients targeted by December 2026 against a statutory deadline of 31 December 2027 (New Mexico Department of Health, 2026). Two EU-level advocacy and policy alliances, the Psychedelic Access and Research European Alliance and PsychedeliCare, have separately begun pressing regulators toward exactly the kind of broadened, non-exclusively-medical access pathway this article argues for, though neither has yet achieved binding regulatory change (Psychedelic Access and Research European Alliance, n.d. ; PsychedeliCare, 2026). EU institutions are not only being lobbied on this question; they are funding research into it directly. PsyPal, the first multi-site randomised controlled trial of psilocybin therapy funded by the European Union under Horizon Europe, the EU's key funding programme for research and innovation, is running across four clinical sites, in Groningen, Prague, Copenhagen, and Lisbon, comparing two escalating psilocybin doses (15 mg, then 25 mg) with two low doses (1 mg) for depressive symptoms and existential distress in patients with progressive, life-limiting conditions including COPD, ALS, multiple sclerosis, and atypical Parkinsonian disorders (PsyPal Consortium, 2026). Taken together, these jurisdictions show departure from outright prohibition happening by several different routes at once, but only the Dutch, Nepali, Oregon, and Colorado models depart from the diagnosis-gating this article critiques as well.

Nothing in this article's case depends on a particular business model; it rests on autonomy alone. Even so, honesty requires naming a real risk. The most likely first step in any jurisdiction that expands access is private, fee-based clinics with no public funding attached, and if diagnosis-gating disappears while paid access remains the only option, a diagnosis-based barrier has simply been swapped for a wealth-based one. Oregon's own data, discussed above, bears this out directly, and the pattern holds elsewhere too. A full course of MDMA-assisted therapy in Australia, where authorised psychiatrists may prescribe it for PTSD, costs about AUD $30,000, and psilocybin treatment for the same treatment-resistant-depression indication runs to roughly AUD $20,000 (Miller & Zheng, 2026). One insurer has begun testing exactly the kind of remedy this article calls for. In October 2025, Medibank, Australia's largest private health insurer, expanded an AUD $10 million psychotherapy programme to reimburse psilocybin-assisted therapy for treatmentresistant depression, having already funded MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD, though the scheme remains a small pilot rather than standard coverage (Medibank, 2025). Nationally, as of mid-2026, only about 40 psychiatrists held prescribing approval for both medicines, and just 238 patients in total had been treated outside clinical trials in the roughly three years since the law changed (Miller & Zheng, 2026). The Dutch food-based model is instructive here too: because it carries none of the GMP, clinical-trial, and marketing-authorisation costs of the medicines authorisation pathway, truffle sessions in the Netherlands are available at a small fraction of these prices, direct evidence that the cost difference tracks regulatory category, not underlying risk or evidentiary strength. The corporate map now mirrors the regulatory one. Red Light Holland, founded to market food-law magic truffles in the Netherlands, acquired Filament Health in 2026 and renamed itself Rhelion Life Sciences; one group now spans a no-diagnosis consumer product sold under the Warenwet and a GMP pharmaceutical candidate supplied to diagnosis-gated compassionate use programmes, including the proposed Belgian one (Rhelion Life Sciences, 2026a, 2026b). The active compound is the same; the regulatory pathway is what sets the price and the gate. A non-medical pathway is therefore not only a matter of principle; it is also the more affordable route, and jurisdictions serious about equitable access should weigh that directly rather than treating the medicines pathway as the only legitimate destination.

The cost problem is not incidental to the medicines regulatory framework; it is structurally built into it. Because psilocybin-and MDMA-assisted therapy are delivered as bundled, staff-intensive services rather than a dispensed pill, clinician time is consistently the largest cost driver in every published economic model. The pivotal MDMA-assisted therapy cost-effectiveness analysis found clinician time accounted for more than 80 percent of direct intervention costs across roughly 42 hours of contact per course (Marseille et al., 2020). A US model-based analysis of psilocybinassisted therapy for treatment-resistant depression put total cost per course at $5,000, rising to $20,000 in sensitivity analyses, with cost-effectiveness holding only toward the lower end of that range (Avanceña et al., 2025). A UK decision-analytic model reached a comparable conclusion, finding psilocybin-assisted therapy cost-effective for severe depression only when therapist support costs were cut by half (McCrone et al., 2023). Workforce supply compounds the price problem. One industry analysis estimated that treating just 1,000 patients a year in Australia would require more than 100 authorised prescribing psychiatrists and over 1,000 trained therapists, against fewer than 30 psychiatrists authorised as of late 2025 (Joralemon, 2026). Practitioners inside the legal state programmes raise a related point about the credentialing model itself, arguing that requiring physician-or therapist-level staffing for every hour of a 6-8 hour session is a cost driver largely independent of the substance's own risk profile, and that state psilocybin-service data suggest the same services can be delivered safely by a broader, less expensively credentialed workforce (Charalambides, 2026). Oregon's own real-world figures make the comparison concrete. Using the state's actual delivery costs and remission rates, psilocybin therapy costs approximately $5,660 per remission achieved, against $22,408 per remission for transcranial magnetic stimulation, one of the treatments Oregon's Medicaid programme already covers for treatment-resistant depression (Center for Psychedelic Policy, 2026). None of this is a reason to abandon the medicines pathway for the diagnosed populations it serves; it is a reason not to treat it as the only route non-diagnosed adults should be expected to wait for.

8. Policy Implications and Recommendations

This article's findings carry four direct policy implications. The evidence reviewed in Section 2 identifies no risk specific to non-diagnosed use that would justify making diagnosis a condition of access; regulators have a better tool available in individual health and capacity screening. The indication gap documented in Section 3 means the conventional medicines pathway is structurally unlikely ever to serve mental wellness and quality-of-life use, whatever the evidence eventually shows, because that use has no diagnostic home to be developed against. The affordability pattern documented in Section 7 means jurisdictions relying solely on a medicalised access model should expect cost to become its own exclusionary barrier, one a non-medical, food-adjacent pathway can substantially reduce. And the Canadian cases in Section 6 show that the diagnosisgating framework does not reliably serve even the diagnosed population it is designed for, undercutting the case for extending rather than replacing it.

 Recognise non-medical, food-adjacent classification as a legitimate parallel pathway to a medicines framework for naturally occurring psilocybin, available to any adult who passes an individual health and capacity screen, not contingent on diagnosis.  Where psilocybin access is already legally permitted, swap diagnosis-based eligibility for a screen focused on the physical, cardiovascular, and capacity factors actually relevant to individual risk.  Require research funders and licensing bodies to fund naturalistic and non-clinical research on psilocybin use among non-diagnosed adults, so that wellness-relevant evidence can accumulate rather than being structurally excluded from the research agenda.

 Pair any expansion of access, medical or non-medical, with public funding or subsidy mechanisms from the outset, so that removing a diagnosis-based regulatory framework barrier does not simply substitute a wealth-based one.  Fund public information efforts aimed at the general adult population, not only diagnosed patients, and close the psilocybin curriculum gap in medical education so that physicians can advise non-diagnosed patients accurately rather than only diagnosed ones.

9. Conclusion

The evidence available today does not show that diagnosis is required for safe or meaningful access to psilocybin, and the argument against diagnosis-gating does not need to wait for stronger evidence, because it never depended on psilocybin's benefits being proven for any particular medical condition. It depends on cognitive liberty, and on informed consent, being applied consistently to a population that has, until now, been treated as ineligible to choose unless first certified as ill. The evidence reviewed here, spanning healthy-volunteer studies, naturalistic wellbeing surveys, and clinical trials in diagnosed populations, is still early and does not settle the clinical question in either direction. That is precisely why this article does not argue for a medical indication for mental wellness use. Its claim is narrower: wherever the law already permits access, a competent adult's consent to an experience should not have to wait on a diagnosis first.

Taken together, the institutional and medicinal regulatory framework patterns traced in this article make diagnosis-gating harder to defend as neutral caution and easier to recognise for what it actually is, a policy choice, and policy choices can be revised once the reasons behind them stop holding up. Huxley's "principal appetite of the soul" (Huxley, 1954), after all, was never described as belonging only to the diagnosed. A non-medical, food-adjacent regulatory pathway is not a workaround to be tolerated at the margins; it is the more honest fit for a substance whose most consequential effects, personal meaning, mental wellness and psychological flexibility, were never medical claims to begin with, and it should be built out, funded, and regulated as such. Diagnosis should not automatically function as the legal price of cognitive liberty.
