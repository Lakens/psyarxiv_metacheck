How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Digital media use during early childhood is widespread yet unevenly documented globally, with most evidence drawn from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD; i.e., Minority World) societies. This registered report examines cross-country and contextspecific variation in digital media exposure among children 6-36-months-old. Guided by the Dynamic, Relational, Ecological Approach to Media Effects Research (DREAMER) framework, the ManyBabies 8 consortium investigates: (1) age of first direct media use; (2) amount of direct media use and indirect media exposure; (3) social context of media use (joint media engagement, caregiver media disruptions); and (4) caregiver motivations and rule-setting practices. Caregivers from 58 countries will complete standardized surveys in 2027. Countries will be grouped into empirically derived country-clusters based on country-level indicators. Preregistered linear and logistic mixed-effects regression models will examine differences across country-clusters, accounting for participants nested within countries and family structural characteristics included as covariates. It is hypothesized most children will experience direct use and media exposure before 2-years, and that direct media use, indirect exposure, caregiver practices, and motivations will vary across countries and family contexts. By harmonizing measures across countries, this study will provide the first comprehensive characterization of early childhood direct digital media use and indirect exposure.

Digital media has become a ubiquitous part of daily life for people around the world. For families with young children, the modern media environment presents both challenges and opportunities for raising children in the digital age. However, our understanding of digital media use during early childhood is limited by most existing studies focusing on Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD; i.e., Minority World) societies, which do not reflect global variation in patterns of digital media use. We plan to conduct one of the first systematic, comprehensive global studies of early indirect media exposure and direct digital media use, including the social context and caregiver motivations for direct use, among families with children 6-36-months-old from 58 countries.

Cross-country variation in early childhood family digital media ecology: A ManyBabies 8 registered report

Digital media use (also referred to as screen time) is becoming increasingly prevalent during early childhood, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic (Bergmann et al., 2022). A recent meta-analysis estimates that the majority of children under the age of 5 exceed screen time recommendations by the World Health Organization (WHO; McArthur et al., 2022). Although direct media use in early childhood varies worldwide (UNICEF, 2025), much of the existing research on early childhood digital media use focuses on samples drawn from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD; also referred to as Minority World) 1 (Henrich et al., 2010; Stengelin, 2026) populations, limiting our knowledge of early media use trends and their impacts across the world (Mannell et al., 2024). Furthermore, studies have primarily focused on screen time while overlooking the context of media use (e.g., joint media engagement, content of media, motivations for media use), which may moderate its effects on children's developmental outcomes (Barr et al., 2020; Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, & Radesky, 2024). Because the evidence base on early digital media use remains concentrated in the Minority World, documenting cross-country variation and contextual factors is crucial to inform policy and regulation globally.

Over the last 20 years, research has documented that greater, earlier, and more solitary direct digital media use in young children is associated with poorer social-emotional, cognitive, and physical development (Ahmer et al., 2025; Munzer et al., 2026; Sticca et al., 2025). From this perspective, family media practices are shaped not only by proximal caregiver-child interactions, but also by the broader resources, constraints, opportunities, and structural conditions surrounding families, which may differ systematically across countries. As a ManyBabies initiative, this research uses a "Big Team Science" approach, in which researchers across multiple sites implement the same protocols and measurement tools (Frank et al., 2017; The ManyBabies Consortium, 2020; Vaidis et al., 2026).

Aim 1: Cross-Country Variation in Age of First Direct Digital Media Use

The first aim of this registered report is to examine cross-country patterns in the age of first direct digital media use. Existing studies from several countries indicate that children's direct digital media use often begins during the first year of life and becomes increasingly common across infancy and toddlerhood.

In Minority World contexts, evidence from the United States shows that early direct media use is prevalent; in a low-income sample in the United States, 46% of infants were already watching television daily at 2 months of age, and 68% of infants had begun watching television by 6 months (Hish et al., 2021). Studies across European countries show similar early engagement among general populations, though the age of first direct media use varies by type of media and device. In France, approximately 28% of infants aged 5-12 months had used touchscreen devices, increasing to 87-91% by 24 months (Cristia & Seidl, 2015). In Germany and Switzerland, 50% or more of children were reported to be viewing screens by 12 months (Durham et al., 2021; Schmid et al., 2025), with children's first use of television tending to occur earlier than smartphone use. In Italy, the proportion of children reported to engage in digital media use increases from almost 14% at 2-3 months to almost 55% at 11-12 months, and almost 62% at 13-15 months of age (Pizzi et al., 2025). In Portugal, 80% of infants were reported to use digital media daily by 12 months (Ferreira et al., 2020). High rates of early media use are also observed in socioeconomically disadvantaged settings within Europe; by 15-18 months, approximately 92% of children in low-income contexts in Sweden were reported to use screens (Mekhail et al., 2024).

Direct media use also appears to begin early in development across Majority World contexts. In Latin America, just over 49% of mothers in Brazil reported that their children were first exposed to screens within the first 6 months of life (Bergonsi et al., 2022). In Saudi Arabia, the median age of onset was 12 months for television viewing and 18 months for mobile media use (Alroqi et al., 2022). In Türkiye, almost 11% of 3-month-olds are reported to use mobile media (Yiğit et al., 2025), but the median age of first use is closer to 12 months (Kılıç et al., 2019). In India the median age of first digital media use is 10 months, though some begin as early as 2 months of age (Meena et al., 2020). Finally, in South Korea, children's first use of television and smartphone both appeared to be around 8 months, but television use tended to be much more prevalent than smartphone use as the children aged (Chang et al., 2018).

Together, across the countries in these studies, most children first directly use digital media before 2 years of age, but the reported age of first direct use varies. These differences likely reflect variation in the types of devices examined (e.g., television, smartphones, tablets), the operational definition of direct use across studies, and rapidly changing patterns of technology adoption. Measurement also varies across studies and, although there are exceptions as listed above (e.g. Chang et al., 2018; Gago-Galvagno et al., 2025; Meena et al., 2020), much of this work has been limited to Minority World contexts. While these findings from previous research offer valuable insights, comparing them directly is challenging as the studies span across different years and often use different methods. This lack of standardization and the wide timeframe over which the research was conducted make it difficult to draw conclusions from the existing work. This highlights the need for the proposed systematic cross-country research using consistent definitions and measurement approaches.

Aim 2: Cross-Country Variation in Amount of Direct Media Use and Indirect Digital

Media Exposure

The second aim of this registered report is to examine cross-country patterns in the amounts of direct media use and indirect digital media exposure children experience. This aim is approached in two ways: the total amount of direct use and indirect exposure, and whether direct digital media use exceeds recommended global guidelines. Direct use of media by young children is on the rise worldwide, yet rates are highly variable. In Minority World contexts, upwards of 2 hours per day were reported on average for 17-30-month-olds in the U.S. (Kucker et al., 2024; Mann et al., 2025), with low-income U.S. homes reporting almost 4 hours/day (Dynia et al., 2021). In the Netherlands, caregivers reported a mean of 1 hour of direct media use per day for children aged 2-4 years with substantial variation between children (SD ≈ 1 hour), which temporarily increased to approximately 80 minutes per day during the COVID-19 pandemic (van der Klis & Junge, 2026). In a large-scale New Zealand study, mothers of 24month-old children reported a mean of 1.35 hours/day (Gath et al., 2026). In Majority World contexts estimates are similar. In Türkiye, daily screen time was approximately 1 hour among children younger than 2 years, increasing to nearly 2.5 hours among children aged 2-4 years (Uzundağ et al., 2022). Thus, children's use of media varies across countries, yet which countrywide factors may be associated with these differences is less clear.

In contrast to direct media use, the amount of time children are exposed to indirect or background digital media is less studied, but seemingly prevalent and variable. In Minority World contexts, Israeli parents of 3-12-month-olds reported using screens regularly in the background while their infants were present (Bar Lev & Elias, 2020). This was also the case in Switzerland (Gillioz et al., 2024), whereas in New Zealand, background exposure was estimated to be higher, at around 2.5 hours/day (Gath et al., 2026). In the U.S., 2-year-old children were exposed to approximately 4.5 hours/day of indirect background media exposure (Kucker & Schneider, 2026; Lapierre et al., 2012). These patterns shift over age as well; studies showed a tendency for children's direct screen use to increase with age while indirect background television exposure dropped slightly with increasing child age, a pattern documented from the U.S., UK and Denmark (Krogh et al., 2021; Ribner & McHarg, 2021), and meta-analyses (Duch et al., 2013). In Majority World contexts, including across 19 Latin American countries, background television was one of the most common forms of media exposure among children aged 12-48 months, averaging approximately 1.5 hours/day (Gago-Galvagno et al., 2025). In Saudi Arabia, 59% of mothers of children aged 1-3 years reported that television was left on in the background in their homes, with 32% reporting that this occurred often and 27% reporting that it occurred always (Alroqi et al., 2023); in Türkiye, longitudinal home observations of infants at 8, 10, and 18 months found that more than one third of the infants in the sample were exposed to background television (Keşşafoğlu, Yıldız et al., 2025). Together, these findings indicate indirect digital media exposure is a frequent part of young children's everyday environments, yet the precise levels may vary across countries.

Another way to quantify children's direct media use is by considering whether their direct use exceeds global or country-specific recommendations. Global recommendations include the WHO's 2019 guidelines of no or minimal screen time for children under 2 years, with a maximum of one hour per day for those aged 2-4 years (WHO, 2019). As a whole, families do not follow these recommendations. A meta-analysis of 89,163 children across five continents found that only 25% of children under 2 years and 35% of children aged 2 to 5 years met the WHO guidelines (McArthur et al., 2022).

Aim 3: Cross-Country Variation in Social Context of Digital Media Use

The third aim examines how social contexts of children's direct digital media use vary across countries, in line with the DREAMER framework's account that day-to-day dynamics in family media use, particularly the motivations and social context of use will cumulatively predict child development. Here, the social context of family digital media use refers to the people and interpersonal processes surrounding media use (Alroqi et al., 2023; Barr et al., 2020). Within this broader context, the present study focuses on (1) caregiver-perceived disruption due to caregivers' own media use (McDaniel, 2021) and (2) joint media engagement (JME) (Takeuchi & Stevens, 2011). Although cross-country evidence on these specific dimensions remains limited, existing research suggests that overall patterns of family media ecology differ across national contexts. The available evidence, however, is drawn entirely from Minority World contexts. For example, Chia et al. (2022) compared families of preschool children in Finland, Japan, South Korea, and Singapore using cluster analysis that included both child and caregivers' own screen-media use and caregiver-child engagement during screen-based activities. Their analysis identified distinct family media use profiles that differed in the extent to which parents and children used screens and engaged in screen activities together, and the distribution of these profiles varied substantially across the four countries. Evidence from Estonia further suggests that parents' media use is associated with children's direct media use (Tulviste & Tulviste, 2024).

These findings provide preliminary evidence that patterns of family media ecology use may differ across countries, while also highlighting the need for research examining specific socialcontextual constructs -that is, caregiver distraction during their own media use and JME -that may reduce or enhance interactional quality, and for extending this work beyond Minority World samples.

One aspect of social context is caregivers' own technology use in the presence of the child. Caregiver technology use can be quite common. In Minority World contexts, research in the U.S. suggests that caregivers may use their smartphones, on average, during 27% of the time they spend around their infant (McDaniel et al., 2023). Phone use by caregivers may disrupt caregiver-child interactions (McDaniel & Radesky, 2018), due to caregiver difficulty regulating or disengaging from device use during time spent with the child. Concerns have been expressed over these digital disruptions, as they may negatively impact the quantity and quality of caregiver-child interaction (McDaniel et al., 2025; Toledo-Vargas et al., 2025). A recent systematic review and meta-analysis confirms that virtually all existing work on parental technology use comes from Minority World contexts (Toledo-Vargas et al., 2025); no comparable evidence on caregiver-perceived disruption currently exists from Majority World contexts, underscoring the need for a more global approach.

A second aspect of social context is JME, which occurs when two or more people (e.g., caregivers and children, siblings or peers) engage with the same media together, rather than simply co-viewing side-by-side (Dore & Zimmermann, 2020; Ewin et al., 2021). For example, when reading a traditional book with a toddler about animals, a caregiver might prompt the child to remember a previous day when they saw that animal at the zoo. This collaborative approach offers benefits for children's attention, learning, and memory (Rasmussen et al., 2017) because caregivers can scaffold the material and adapt it to fit what the child already knows, which can support children's learning compared to solitary media use (Taylor et al., 2024). Similar to book reading, JME may also enhance learning by promoting shared attention, caregiver-child interaction, and meaningful conversations (Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, & Radesky, 2024; Dore & Zimmermann, 2020). As with caregiver-perceived disruption, existing JME research comes almost entirely from Minority World contexts; many regions of the world -especially Majority World countries -remain under-and unexplored (see the scoping review of Luo et al., 2025). Furthermore, the literature lacks a systematic and diverse range of cross-country comparisons, which limits our ability to understand how social context shapes children's digital media experience.

Aim 4: Cross-Country Variation in Caregiver Motivations for Direct Digital Media Use and Rule-Setting Practices

The final aim examines how caregivers differ in both their motivations and rule-setting practices for regulating children's direct media use (Nikken & Schols, 2015; Wang et al., 2023).

Motivations (i.e., why caregivers introduce digital media to children) and rule-setting practices (how caregivers regulate and support children's direct media use) focus on distinct aspects of caregiver decision-making and each likely vary across countries (Wang et al., 2023). However, many of the current models of motivation and rule-setting practices were developed and validated in Minority World contexts, and scant research has examined potential cross-country variability. The current study aims to fill this gap. When it comes to motivations for young children's digital media use, caregivers may allow early digital media use for multiple, often overlapping reasons (Cingel & Krcmar, 2013; Nabi & Krcmar, 2016; Nikken, 2019; Nikken & Schols, 2015; Suh et al., 2024). These reasons can include using media to regulate or manage a child's emotions or behavior; occupy or keep a child entertained while the caregiver attends to other demands, connect with others or foster family connections, and/or educate when the goal is to promote enrichment through educational content (Suh et al., 2026). These core motivations appear to recur across contexts, yet are often operationalized in different ways. In Minority World contexts, Israeli parents reported using screen media to facilitate mealtimes and bedtimes via occupying children and/or rewarding behavior (Elias & Sulkin, 2019). Caregivers in Norway, Japan, and Portugal commonly perceive touchscreen use as entertaining and educational, but also report using it to occupy children (Dardanou et al., 2020). In Majority World contexts, a study of caregivers in Nigeria (Njoku & Nwaoboli, 2024) and a study with 10-to 17-month-olds in Brazil (Becker & Donelli, 2024) identified several motivations -including calming, occupying, and education -as key motivations for direct digital media use. In Türkiye, caregivers of children aged 0-3 years most commonly reported using screen media to occupy their child while doing housework, followed by calming the child, aiding sleep onset, and assisting with feeding (Kurap Öcebe & Güney, 2025). Caregivers across 19 Latin American countries tended to co-view and jointly engage around entertainment-based media with their young children (Gago-Galvagno et al., 2025). In rural India, caregivers primarily used screens for entertainment for their 2-to 4year-olds, followed by calming, occupying them, and facilitating feeding (Singh et al., 2026).

The use of digital media as an early education tool is reported across many countries. In Minority World contexts, this pattern has been documented in the U.S. (Ferjan Ramírez et al., 2021), Switzerland (Schmid et al., 2025), South Korea (Dong, 2018), and Singapore (Chen et al., 2019). In Majority World contexts, similar patterns are reported in Brazil (Becker & Donelli, 2024) and the United Arab Emirates (Raffaele et al., 2026). In some cases, caregivers use media as a tool for language enrichment, which subsequently may support language learning and/or maintenance (Cycyk & De Anda, 2021). Indeed, caregiver motivations are associated with different patterns of direct digital media use and developmental outcomes (Kucker et al., 2024; Suh et al., 2026). For instance, using media to regulate has been associated with poorer self-regulation (Konok et al., 2024; Radesky et al., 2023), greater temperamental negative affectivity (McDaniel et al., 2026), greater child problematic media use over time (Coyne et al., 2021), and externalizing behaviors (Coyne et al., 2026), and has been shown to peak between 2-3 years of age before declining gradually across childhood (Christensen-Duerden et al., 2026).

In addition to motivations, caregivers' mediation of children's media use such as through rule-setting practices also shapes the timing and content of children's digital media use.

Mediation refers to the strategies and practices caregivers use to restrict, or to guide and support, children's digital media use to maximize developmental opportunities and minimize associated risks (Livingstone et al., 2017; Valkenburg et al., 1999). The framework proposed by Valkenburg et al. (1999) identified active mediation, which conceptually overlaps with JME described above, and restrictive mediation, which encompasses rule-setting practices. Restrictive mediation refers to when caregivers establish rules regarding the amount of screen time and/or the type of content children can access. Restrictive mediation is generally associated with lower direct digital media use (Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, & Radesky, 2024) across both Eastern and Western cultures (Chen & Shi, 2019), whereas active mediation is associated with greater engagement with age-appropriate, educational content and richer caregiver-child interactions during direct digital media use (Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, & Radesky, 2024).

Mediation and rule-setting practices vary across families and countries (Chen & Shi, 2019), but systematic cross-country evidence remains limited. In Minority World contexts, a prior review across 18 European countries found that caregivers' preferences for restrictive versus active strategies varied according to countries' childrearing values (Kirwil, 2009).

Australian caregivers reported using active and restrictive mediation more frequently than Singaporean caregivers (Shin & Lwin, 2022); in both countries, greater concern about media-related risks, stronger parenting self-efficacy, and higher levels of digital literacy were associated with more active mediation use among caregivers. In Majority World contexts, caregivers of preschool-aged children in India (John et al., 2021) and in Türkiye (Ata-Aktürk & Akman, 2024) more frequently reported using restrictive mediation, whereas active mediation was considerably less common. Similarly, greater digital literacy among caregivers was associated with more active mediation in China (Cao et al., 2022). Together, these findings suggest that restrictive and active mediation strategies recur across contexts but may vary in magnitude or rank order. In the present study, we separate joint media engagement (Aim 3) from rule-setting practices (Aim 4) to better differentiate how they each vary across countries.

Nonetheless, existing studies on motivation and rule-setting practices vary by child age, media type, and measurement. Most evidence is derived from Minority World contexts (Brito et al., 2017; Dardanou et al., 2020; Nevski & Siibak, 2016) with few systematic cross-country comparisons or work from the Majority World. Thus, it remains unclear whether these crosscountry differences reflect genuine country variation or methodological inconsistencies. The present study addresses this gap.

Structural Factors Shaping Direct Media Use and Indirect Media Exposure

Existing evidence suggests that the age of young children's media use (Aim 1), amount of direct media use and indirect exposure (Aim 2), social context (Aim 3), and caregiver motivations and rule-setting practices (Aim 4) likely vary across countries in important ways.

However, this variability is also likely modulated by within-country variation in family structural factors, including socioeconomic status (SES; encompassing caregiver education and household income) and family size.

Socioeconomic Status and Digital Media Use/Exposure

Evidence from both caregiver-report and day-long audio recordings suggests that lower SES is associated with greater direct digital media use across multiple countries. In Minority World contexts, this pattern has been observed in the U.S. (Ferjan Ramírez et al., 2021; Linder et al., 2021), Canada (Carson et al., 2014), the U.K. (Ribner & McHarg, 2021), Germany (Willard et al., 2026), Italy (Pizzi et al., 2025), and New Zealand (Stewart et al., 2019). In Majority World contexts, the same pattern has been observed in China (Wong et al., 2020), Indonesia (Sholilah et al., 2026), and Türkiye (Keşşafoğlu, Altundal et al., 2025; Uzundağ et al., 2022). Indirect background media is prevalent in both Minority World contexts like the US (Masur et al., 2015) and Majority World countries like Türkiye where young children from lower-SES households were exposed to more background television in the home both at 10 and 18 months (Uzundağ et al., 2024).

SES is, however, a complex construct that is both modulated by a variety of cultural and familial factors and measured in myriad ways. Often, rather than using a holistic SES metric, studies rely on caregiver education or income. Both are associated with the age of children's first direct digital media use and the amount of indirect exposure and direct media use. Lower parental educational attainment is consistently associated with greater direct and indirect infant screen exposure. In Minority World contexts, this has been shown across Italy (Bellagamba et al., 2021), Germany (Willard et al., 2026), Scotland (Kühhirt & Klein, 2020), and the U.S. (Radesky et al., 2014), with U.S. evidence showing that this link is observable as early as six months of age (Wiltshire et al., 2021). In Majority World contexts, the same pattern has been reported in Taiwan (Chiu et al., 2017). Lower parental education is also correlated with greater age-related increases in direct digital media use in Minority World contexts, including Scotland (Kühhirt & Klein, 2020), Finland (Matarma et al., 2016), and the U.S. (Trinh et al., 2020); comparable evidence from Majority World contexts is not yet available.

Lower parental education is likewise correlated with longer duration of indirect exposure to background television or adult-oriented programming. In Minority World contexts, this has been documented in the U.S. (Dore & Dynia, 2021; Wiltshire et al., 2021), Lithuania (Jusienė et al., 2019), and Spain (Pons et al., 2020); in Majority World contexts, a comparable pattern has been reported in Thailand (Chonchaiya et al., 2015). In the U.S., a lower income-to-needs ratio was associated with higher stationary television use by children under 2 years, but no differences in overall rates of background media (Kucker & Schneider, 2026). As a whole, this work suggests that SES, and specifically parental education and income, are associated with the age of children's direct digital media use (Aim 1) and amount of indirect exposure and direct media use (Aim 2).

Structural family factors also shape caregivers' digital media use around children, including caregiver phone use and JME (Aim 3) and motivations and rule-setting practices (Aim 4). Some work suggests caregiver education level may be associated with caregiver phone use around their child. In Minority World contexts, one U.S. study utilizing passive sensing of caregiver phone use during child time found that greater caregiver phone use around their infant was associated with lower caregiver educational attainment, although no association was found with family income (McDaniel et al., 2024); in a sample of 64 caregivers of 20-month-olds from New Zealand, lower education was associated with greater reported minutes on their phone but was unrelated to perceptions of caregiver disruptions due to phone use around their child (Fusha et al., 2025). In Majority World contexts, in a large sample of Turkish caregivers of a young child, lower education was associated with greater caregiver phone use (Uzundağ et al., 2022).

While a relation between maternal education and direct measures of JME was found in some

Minority World studies such as in Australia (Hesketh et al., 2007), Finland (Paavonen et al., 2009), and the U.S. (Tandon et al., 2012), this relation was not found in other Minority World studies such as in Norway (Gebremariam et al., 2020) and Spain (Pons et al., 2020). This inconsistency may partly reflect that co-viewing is not a uniform construct and may or may not include JME. This variability is highlighted within the experimental literature on co-use, with couse manipulations including spontaneous, instructed, or scripted caregiver co-use (Taylor et al., 2024). In Majority World contexts, such as a large sample of Turkish preschoolers whose caregivers had lower levels of education, nearly half of children were reported to use digital media devices alone -but most of this use took place in communal areas of the home, such as the living room or kitchen, where family members were present and could intermittently monitor or engage (Konca, 2021). Because existing studies rarely systematically measure JME, findings cannot yet be applied globally.

Likewise, structural factors are associated with caregiver motivations and rule-setting practices (Aim 4). Under the DREAMER model, caregivers' decisions about children's media may reflect the resources, constraints, and opportunities available within the family environment (Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, & Radesky, 2024; Slobodin et al., 2024). In Minority World contexts, parents with high educational attainment tend to report more active mediation, including JME, and less restrictive practices across Europe and the U.S. (Livingstone et al., 2017; Nikken & Schols, 2015; Suh et al., 2024). In Spain, higher maternal education is associated with reduced presence of a television in the child's bedroom and, because there are fewer digital devices, there are fewer household rules around television viewing (Pons et al., 2020). Education may also strengthen caregivers' digital literacy and confidence in evaluating content quality, influencing not only children's level of direct media use but also the quality of their media experiences (Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, & Radesky, 2024) -a finding confirmed in both Finland (Määttä et al., 2017) and the U.S. (Ochoa & Reich, 2020; Thompson et al., 2023). Comparable evidence linking caregiver education to motivations and rule-setting from Majority World contexts is not yet available.

Family Composition and Media Use

In addition to SES, family composition may be a related factor that is associated with children's direct digital media use and indirect exposure. A systematic review of mostly U.S. samples found inconsistent evidence regarding the relation between parental education and early digital media use (Duch et al., 2013). In Minority World contexts, a recent study in Denmark corroborated this variability in reports (Krogh et al., 2021); in the U.S., direct use of childdirected content was higher for younger siblings (Barr et al., 2010). In Majority World contexts, recent studies in India similarly corroborated this variability (Agarwal et al., 2026; Kaur et al., 2022). In Saudi Arabia, for caregivers of 1-to 3-year-olds, there were no significant associations between parental education, household income, and either the age of first direct digital media use or overall digital media use (Alroqi et al., 2022). Children under the age of six in Türkiye had greater direct digital media use and indirect background television exposure if they had a sibling and lived in a larger household (Keşşafoğlu, Altundal, et al., 2025; Uzundağ et al., 2022). Some of these varied findings suggest that family size or composition is a key additional indicator for direct media use; a systematic review showed that having older siblings is associated with greater direct digital media use (Paudel et al., 2017).

Structural factors and family composition may interact, though this evidence again comes largely from Minority World contexts. In a U.S. cohort, lower household income, along with having fewer children in the household and receiving care outside the home, predicted earlier television introduction (Hish et al., 2021). A German study found that greater family television viewing, having a sibling, and more limited living space, but not parental education, were associated with a higher likelihood of infant direct digital media use (Durham et al., 2021), whereas another German study with 0-to 4-year-olds found the opposite pattern: having siblings was associated with less direct media use (Paulus et al., 2024). In one U.S. study, household size was not related to the amount of direct digital media use (television and other screen devices) at 6 months (Wiltshire et al., 2021). Systematic reviews including mostly Minority World samples (mostly U.S. samples) have shown that firstborn status, two-parent households, number of children in the home, and non-parental childcare were not associated with duration of direct media use (Duch et al., 2013; Veldman et al., 2023). Comparable evidence on how structural factors and family composition interact from Majority World contexts is not yet available.

In families where extended family members play an active role in childcare, additional child-rearing support may reduce caregivers' use of digital media by providing alternative supervision and engagement (Gürmen & Kılıç, 2022; Kağıtçıbaşı, 2007). However, the involvement of multiple caregivers in the home may also lead to less consistent digital media practices due to intergenerational differences in rules regarding direct media use (Nimrod et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings indicate that associations between structural, socioeconomic, and family composition factors and children's digital media use are complex and context-dependent, in part because "number of people in the home" cannot distinguish a nuclear family from a household with multigenerational, shared caregiving, an arrangement that is common in many, if not most regions. Both family and country-level contexts remain important for understanding cross-country variation in children's direct digital media use, indirect media exposure, social context of use, and caregiver motivations.

Current Study/Hypothesis

The current study will address four primary aims using data from 57 countries. Due to the large number of countries participating and the potential for Type I error, we will first cluster countries into up to 6 groups on the basis of demographic, economic, health, educational, digitalinfrastructure, and family-policy factors in order to test patterns across groups of countries.

Consistent with DREAMER's bioecological foundations, these country-level structural characteristics can also be understood as features of the broader developmental ecology in which children and families are embedded. These broader structures shape families' access to resources, opportunities, and constraints and, through the proximal environments in which children participate, may influence how digital media become integrated into children's everyday developmental experiences. Grouping countries according to these characteristics therefore allows us to examine variation in family digital media ecology across broader structural contexts, while the multilevel models account for the non-independence of participants and variation among families within countries. This approach will allow us to characterize cross-country patterns in children's first direct use of digital media in any form (Aim 1). We hypothesize that most children will use media before 2 years of age, although the timing will vary across countryclusters. Aim 2 will document cross-country patterns in the amount of children's indirect and direct digital media use, both in terms of average total time (Aim 2a) and proportion of children/families exceeding WHO age-based screen time guideline thresholds (Aim 2b). It is hypothesized that children's amount of direct digital media use and indirect media exposure will vary systematically across countries under both metrics, though there are no specific directional hypotheses given the dearth of global literature in these metrics. Alongside the timing and amount of children's direct media use and indirect exposure, we will describe the social context of children's direct media use across country-clusters. To achieve this goal, Aim 3 will examine caregivers' perceived technology-related disruptions to caregiver-child interactions (Aim 3a), as well as JME during caregiver/child digital media use (Aim 3b). Aim 4 will examine caregivers' reported motivations and rule-setting practices for direct media use. We broadly hypothesize that perceived disruptions due to caregivers' media use, background media exposure, JME, caregivers' motivations, and rules for direct media use will vary across countries, but we do not hypothesize specific direction of effects because our examination of country-cluster differences is exploratory in nature.

Across all aims, we further hypothesize that beyond country-level differences, structural family factors, including household income, parental education, and household size will explain additional variation in each outcome of interest. We hypothesize that higher household income and caregiver education and smaller household size will be associated with lower indirect background exposure and direct media use but we have no directional hypotheses for social context, motivations, and rule-setting practices due to the exploratory approach and limited prior cross-country work. By going beyond "screen time" to examine social and contextual factors embedded in children's digital direct media use and indirect exposure (Barr et al., 2020) and using consistent measures across samples, this research will provide a more comprehensive understanding of how family media ecology during early childhood differs across countries.

Sample Power

Given the multilevel structure of the data and the planned country-clustering approach, statistical power was evaluated using simulation-based power analyses tailored to each of the primary preregistered mixed-effects regression models. Simulations assumed a minimum anticipated sample of 40 countries with approximately 50 participants per country (N ≈ 2,000).

Because the final number of country-clusters will be determined empirically, we evaluated solutions ranging from 2 to 6 country-clusters. To account for uncertainty in between-country variability, simulations were conducted assuming intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) of .01, .05, and .10, respectively. Standardized country-cluster fixed-effect sizes ranging from .05 to .30, in increments of .05, were evaluated.

For each combination of effect size, ICC, and number of country-clusters, 1,000 datasets were simulated and analyzed using the corresponding preregistered mixed-effects model.

Empirical power was estimated as the proportion of simulated datasets in which the target country-cluster effect was detected at α = .05, with 80% power used as the criterion for evaluating design sensitivity. Across the primary mixed-effects models, the smallest simulated effect achieving at least 80% power ranged from .10 to .25, depending on the assumed ICC and number of country-clusters.

With ICC = .01, an effect of .10 was sufficient under most country-cluster solutions, although some models required an effect of .15 with 5 or 6 country-clusters. With ICC = .05, effects of .15 achieved at least 80% power with 2-4 country-clusters, whereas an effect of .20 was required with 5-6 clusters. Under the most conservative between-country variance assumption (ICC = .10), effects of .20 were generally sufficient with 2-3 country-clusters, while effects of .20-.25 were required with 4-5 clusters and .25 with 6 clusters.

Thus, across all simulated conditions, the anticipated design was sensitive to standardized country-cluster effects between .10 and .25, with .25 representing the smallest effect evaluated that consistently achieved at least 80% power under the most conservative conditions. Full simulation assumptions, parameter values, model specifications, aim-specific power estimates, and analysis code can be found in the repository (URL TBD).

Participants

Each site will use direct participant contact to recruit known caregiver participants, thereby ensuring that all participants are eligible and confirmed to be the caregivers of a child between the ages of 6 and 36 months. This age range reflects the fact that direct media use during early childhood remains comparatively understudied despite the importance of this period for development. This age range also corresponds to that used in cross-country validation studies of the Media Assessment Questionnaire (MAQ: Barr et al., 2020; Bellagamba et al., 2021; Koch et al., 2025; Piper et al., 2026; Suh et al., 2026; Sundqvist et al., 2021 Sundqvist et al., , 2024)). At the time of submission of Stage 1 of this Registered Report, 156 sites in 58 countries committed to collecting data (median = 50-75 participants per site), totalling an estimated 11,075 participants.

Inclusion Criteria

To be eligible to participate, caregivers must be 18 years of age or older, be able to provide informed consent, and be the primary caregiver, defined as a parent, grandparent, or legal guardian, of a child aged 6;00-36;31 (months;days). Prior to receiving the survey, caregivers must pass the study screening process, which includes confirming that the child falls within the eligible age range and does not have a diagnosed genetic condition or developmental delay. Only caregivers who meet these eligibility criteria will proceed to the survey.

Exclusion Criteria

Eligibility criteria related to the child's age and diagnosed genetic or developmental delays will be reassessed within the survey to identify participants who may have incorrectly passed the initial screening. Participants will therefore be excluded from the final sample if the primary caregiver (a) reports within the survey that the child falls outside the eligible age range of 6;00-36;31 (months;days) or has a diagnosed genetic condition or developmental delay that impacts language (including uncorrected vision or hearing difficulty), (b) fails to complete the demographic and child digital media use sections of the survey, or (c) fails the data quality checks in Supplementary Material B.

Procedure

Participating sites will receive a unique link to a centralized Qualtrics survey adapted to their respective language and managed by the Principal Investigator (PI) for each site. Sites are required to distribute the survey link exclusively to identified individuals confirmed to be eligible. All responses will be collected and securely centralized on either a U.S.-based server at [anonymized for review] or an EU-based server at [anonymized for review] to ensure consistent data management and adherence to General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) statutes. Demographics

Media Assessment Questionnaire (MAQ)

The MAQ, developed by the Comprehensive Assessment of Family Media Exposure Consortium (CAFE) (Barr et al., 2020), incorporates three scales as well as general digital media information, and has been validated over several years (Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, Radesky, et al., 2024). The questionnaire, originally developed in English, has since been adapted to multiple languages (e.g., Spanish, Swedish, Mandarin). In the latest version (v2.4), caregivers are asked about their child's media ecology, including the amount of direct media use (minutes/day) of video, video chat, and videogames on any device, caregiver screen use including minutes per day, indirect household background media exposure, content, rule-setting practices, caregiver motivations for screen use (MEMO) (Suh et al., 2026), caregivers' perceived caregiving disruption due to their technology use around their child (DISRUPT) (McDaniel, 2021), and JME (JMES) (Barr, Kirkorian, Coyne, Radesky, et al., 2024; Koch et al., 2025). For this study, the MAQ has been adapted from v2.4. See Supplementary Material D for additional details about MAQ subscales.

Analytical Plan

A range of models will be developed and run to test each hypothesis. As described below, different models will be used depending on the nature of the research questions; all analyses will be conducted in R (Version 4.6.1, R Core Team, 2026).

Handling Missing Data

Responses of 'Don't know,' 'Prefer not to answer,' and unanswered items will be treated as missing data unless otherwise specified. Missing family media ecology outcome variables will not be imputed. Participants with missing data on a given outcome will not contribute to analyses of that outcome but may remain eligible for analyses of other outcomes for which they have valid data.

Missing data on prespecified participant-level covariates will be handled using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). To account for differences across national contexts, imputation will be performed separately within each country. Twenty-five imputed versions of the data will be generated for each country. Imputations with the same number will then be combined across countries (e.g., the first imputed version from every country will be combined), resulting in 25 analysis datasets in total. Each primary model will be fitted separately to these 25 datasets, and the resulting parameter estimates and standard errors will be pooled using Rubin's rules to obtain the final statistical results. The extent and pattern of missingness will be examined and reported for all primary outcomes and covariates, both overall and by country. Descriptive information on missingness will be provided in Supplementary Material H.

Handling Outliers

Potential outliers and influential observations will be identified for each fitted model using the check_outliers() function from the R package performance (Lüdecke et al., 2021).

Primary models will first be estimated using all available observations. As a sensitivity analysis, models will then be re-estimated after excluding observations identified as potential outliers or influential cases. Results from models with and without these observations will be compared to evaluate the robustness and consistency of the findings. If substantive conclusions differ following their exclusion, both sets of results will be reported and the discrepancy will be discussed. See Supplementary Material F.

Country-Clustering

Participating countries will be grouped into country-clusters based on similarities across prespecified country-level demographic, socioeconomic, health, digital infrastructure, and family-policy indicators. Indicators will include child dependency ratio, universal health coverage for reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health interventions, Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita, government expenditure on education, electricity access, internet access, mobile broadband access, and indicators of paid maternal and paternal leave. Indicators will be obtained from UNICEF, the World Bank, and the WORLD Policy Analysis Center.

Definitions and data sources for each indicator are provided in the Supplementary Material G.

Prior to clustering, continuous indicators will be standardized as z-scores (M = 0, SD = 1) so that differences in their original measurement scales do not disproportionately influence cluster estimation. Country-clusters will then be identified using Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) with the Mclust function from the R package mclust (Scrucca et al., 2023). GMM provides a model-based approach to clustering in which the observed distribution is represented as a mixture of multivariate Gaussian components, and allows alternative assumptions regarding the volume, shape, and orientation of the clusters to be evaluated statistically.

Models containing 1 through 6 clusters will be estimated across the covariance parameterizations available in mclust. The number of country-clusters will therefore not be fixed in advance. The final solution will be selected based on model fit, classification certainty and separation, minimum cluster size and interpretability using the preregistered criteria (see Supplementary Material G). When multiple solutions satisfy these criteria and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Integrated Classification Likelihood (ICL) do not identify the same solution, selection will prioritize BIC, followed by ICL, classification certainty, and cluster stability. This hierarchy will be preregistered to minimize researcher discretion in selecting the final clustering solution.

Main Models

Country-cluster membership will serve as the primary country-level predictor in the preregistered analyses. Because participants remain nested within individual countries, mixedeffects models will include country-cluster as a fixed effect and country as a random intercept, where appropriate. This allows the fixed effect of country-cluster to test systematic differences among groups of countries with similar structural characteristics while accounting for residual dependence among participants recruited within the same country.

Throughout, i indexes the child, j indexes the country, and k indexes the country-cluster.

3. Back-Translation

Back-translating with a second, independent AI tool is a fast and scalable way to check whether the native speaker's reviewed translation still conveys the original English meaning, across the large number of languages MB8 is translating into. Using a different AI tool than the one used for forward translation -rather than the same tool checking its own output -reduces the risk that a systematic blind spot in AI tool 1 goes undetected.

This step does not replace the linguistic and cultural judgment applied by a human reviewer in Step 2; MB8 may periodically ask a bilingual human reviewer to spot-check AI-generated back-translations. AI tool 2 should be noted in the Qualtrics codebook. Back-translation should be entered in the designated column of the MB8 Qualtrics Codebook. The following single, standardized prompt template is used for all languages: "Translate the following [instrument name] item(s) from [target language] to English for parents/caregivers of children aged [age range], based only on the wording provided, without attempting to identify, reconstruct, correct, or integrate it with any original English version you may recognise or know. 4. Comparative Review

The MB8 translations working group team will compare the original English version with the back-translated version, including the instructions, items, and response options. They will identify and record in the designated column of the MB8 Qualtrics Codebook any discrepancies that may affect: (1) the meaning of the instructions or item content; (2) the construct assessed by an item; (3) omissions, additions, or substitutions; (4) the item or response format.

The back-translation should be sufficiently similar to the default language version to provide evidence of semantic and conceptual equivalence, while differences that arise from natural variation in translation are acceptable.

Aim 2b: Exceeding WHO Screen-Time Guidelines

Aim 2b will examine whether the likelihood of exceeding age-specific World Health Organization sedentary screen-time recommendations differs across country-clusters. Guideline exceedance will be represented as a binary outcome. For children younger than 24 months, any nonvideo-chat direct screen use will be coded as exceeding the recommendation; for children aged 24 months and older, direct screen use exceeding 60 minutes per day will be coded as exceeding the recommendation. Country-cluster differences will be evaluated using the mixed-effects logistic regression model specified in the main-text Analysis Plan, controlling for child and caregiver age. Adjusted models will additionally include country-relative household income, caregiver education, and household size.

For transparency, the proportion and number of children exceeding the WHO recommendation will also be summarized descriptively overall and by country. Model-based country-cluster comparisons will be reported alongside these descriptive estimates. Where omnibus country-cluster differences are statistically significant, pairwise comparisons will use false discovery rate correction, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals will be reported.

Aim 3: Social Context of Digital Media Use

Aim 3 will examine country-cluster differences in complementary dimensions of the social context of digital media use: caregivers' perceived technology-related disruption during interactions with their child (DISRUPT) and active joint media engagement during video viewing and digital game play (JMES). These outcomes will be analyzed separately because they represent conceptually distinct aspects of the family media ecology.

For Aim 3a, internal consistency of the DISRUPT scale will be evaluated using Cronbach's α prior to computing the composite score. The DISRUPT composite will be calculated as the average of its constituent items, with higher scores indicating greater perceived caregiver technology-related disruption.

For Aim 3b, separate JMES composite scores will be calculated for video viewing and digital game play by averaging the constituent items within each scale, with higher scores indicating more frequent active joint media engagement. DISRUPT, JMES-Video, and JMES-Digital Games will therefore be treated as three separate outcomes.

Each outcome will be evaluated using the mixed-effects modeling strategy specified in the maintext Analysis Plan, with country-cluster as the primary fixed-effect predictor, country as a random intercept, and child and caregiver age included as covariates. Adjusted models will additionally include country-relative household income, caregiver education, and household size to examine whether participant-level structural factors explain additional variability beyond country-cluster differences.

Where an omnibus country-cluster effect is statistically significant, pairwise country-cluster comparisons will use Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Estimated mean differences and 95% confidence intervals will be reported for pairwise comparisons.

Supplementary Material B: Exclusion Criteria, and Data Validity Checks

Each response will first be verified against participant records provided by the individual participating labs. Each lab will provide a list of its enrolled participants that includes the participant ID assigned by the lab and responses to relevant screening questions. Caregivers will be required to enter this participant ID before completing the survey in Qualtrics. These records will be used to confirm that survey responses were submitted by participants who were recruited and screened through a participating lab, rather than by individuals who accessed a survey link that had been shared or posted elsewhere.

Participant IDs and screening responses submitted through Qualtrics will be cross-checked against the records provided by the corresponding lab, and responses that cannot be matched to an enrolled participant will be excluded from analysis.

Following participant verification, each response will be reviewed for data quality using a series of validity checks as laid out in prior work (Chmielewski & Kucker, 2020). This will include screening for 1) inconsistent responses, 2) illogical responses, and 3) oddities in answers. Inconsistent responses will be identified by comparing the caregiver's report of the child's birthdate and age across multiple pages and formats; if the dates/ages do not match, the response will be flagged. Illogical responses will be measured by examining reports of the number of children in the home and amount of media use.

Caregivers reporting an atypical number of children (e.g., more than 20) and those reporting media use that totals more than 24 hours/day will be flagged. Oddity in responses will be measured using shortanswer items and standardized media questionnaires. Caregivers who enter short-answer responses that are not related to the prompt (e.g., putting an address in a gender box, or when asked for a TV show the children watch, entering their email) or do not differentiate their responses (e.g., entering choice A for all items) will be flagged. Participants with more than 1 flag will be dropped from analysis. If the age of the child cannot be determined from the responses, such as when a caregiver did not provide the year of birth, that response will also be dropped.

General Principles

The MB8 SOP for translations and adaptations of the instruments in the MB8 study uses an AIassisted translation procedure, while keeping human & native-speaker judgment at the critical decision points.

We mean by "translation" the process of rendering the wording of an MB8 instrument -an item, instruction, or response option -from a target language into English, preserving its meaning, intent, and measurement properties rather than reproducing its literal wording.

We refer to "cultural adaptation" when we talk about a deliberate, documented change to an item, example, or response option that is necessary for it to be meaningful in a specific cultural context (e.g., replacing a locally unfamiliar media platform, adjusting a measurement unit) without altering the underlying construct being measured. Cultural adaptations must always be flagged, documented, and approved -never applied silently.

We use the term "validation" for the process, following translation and cultural adaptation, of confirming -through cognitive pre-testing and/or psychometric analysis -that the translated instrument measures the same construct, in a comparable way, as the original English version. This document does not provide guidance for the scientific validation of a translated measure.

The translations produced through this MB8 procedure are working translations for use in data collection -they cannot be taken as validated translations of the MAQ, IBQ-R-VSF, BITSEA, or Web-CDI instructions.

Following translation and cultural adaptation, after data collection is complete MB8 will examine test reliability (Cronbach's alphas) and factor structure for each translated tool, in an effort to determine whether measures developed in and for WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) contexts actually hold in the diverse cultures and settings represented in MB8.

MB8-affiliated research teams who wish to work on the psychometric validation of these translations (e.g., confirmatory factor analysis, measurement invariance testing) are invited to form a spinoff project and pursue this independently; it is outside the scope of the present SOP.

1. Forward Translation

The WG coordinators will use artificial intelligence to produce an initial forward translation from the original language [English] into each target language, using a single, standardized prompt template so that forward translations are produced under comparable instructions across countries.

The following single, standardized prompt template is used for all languages: "Translate the following [instrument name] item(s) from English into [target language] for parents/caregivers of children aged [age range]. This instrument measures [construct -e.g., infant temperament / social-emotional behavior and competence / media use]. Preserve exactly: (1) the response options and their order (e.g.,1 = Never …7 = Always, NA = Does Not Apply); (2) the reference timeframe (e.g., 'during the LAST WEEK'); (3) frequency/intensity qualifiers (e.g., 'often', 'a little', 'immediately'); (4) everyday parent register, not clinical or academic language. Do not add, omit, or reorder items. Where a term has no direct equivalent in [target language], provide the closest natural equivalent and mark it [NEEDS REVIEW] rather than inventing a new term."

The AI model used for all forward translation is the default/free plan of OpenAI's GPT; the exact model used is noted in the Qualtrics codebook.

The AI-generated forward translation of the MB8 questionnaire(s) is saved in the Qualtrics codebook.

2. Review by a native speaker

AI-generated translations might produce outputs that contain different types of risks -linguistic, conceptual, cultural, structural -which may compromise the initial meaning of the items, response options or instructions of the questionnaires.

Supplementary Material D: MAQ Subscale Details Screen exposure (MAQ2.4 adaptations)

The devices in the home, the age of first screen exposure to different screen-based activities, and the amount of indirect and direct exposure to screen and non-screen-based exposure and caregiver screen time are adapted from the MAQ.

Media Motivations Scales (MEMO)

Caregivers' motivations for their children's media use will be assessed using the Media

Motivations for Child (MEMO-C) scale as part of the MAQ v2.4 (Suh et al., 2026). The MEMO-C, originally developed and validated in English, includes 28 items with three subscales: Regulate, Occupy, and Connect. For each item, caregivers will rate how often screen media is used for a given purpose (e.g., "to prevent your child from getting overwhelmed or upset in a new or difficult situation") on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often). Scores will be computed by averaging the items within the subscale, with higher scores indicating more frequent use of screen media for that motivation type. The scales have demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach's alpha = .94) as well as convergent and incremental validity.

Distraction in Social Relations and Use of Parent Technology (DISRUPT)

The DISRUPT is a 4-item caregiver-report measure designed to assess caregivers' problematic tendencies related to phone or mobile device use during time spent with their child, including cognitive salience of the device and difficulty disengaging from it. Caregivers report on a 6-point Likert scale (0 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree). Items will be averaged to create a total score. Higher scores indicate greater caregiver distraction with mobile devices during caregiver-child time. Example items include "There are times that I could play with or interact with my child, but I am on my phone or mobile device instead."

The DISRUPT has shown good initial psychometric properties, including a single-factor structure, high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = .74 to .91; Corkin et al., 2021; McDaniel, 2021; McDaniel et al., 2023), and evidence of convergent, divergent, and predictive validity in samples of parents with children across a range of ages.

Joint Media Engagement Scale (JMES, Koch et al., 2025)

JMES is an 18-item scale in which caregivers report on their engagement with their child between 1 and 5 years during shared video and digital game use. The scale comprises two subscales: JMES for Video and JMES for Digital Games. Caregivers will indicate how frequently they engage in each behavior when using video content or digital games with their child, on a 5-point scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always). Example items include "I ask the child to tell me what they see on the screen, for example, 'What is that over there?' or 'What color is this?'" and "I relate the game we are playing to what the child has experienced, for example, 'Remember, we also saw ducks at the park!' or 'We also went swimming!'" Two scores are calculated: a video JME score and a digital game JME score. Higher scores indicate more frequent JME. The scale demonstrated both convergent and divergent validity (Koch et al., 2025). The JMES was originally developed and validated in Swedish and was subsequently adapted to English for MAQ v2.4.

Supplementary Material F: Handling Outliers and Influential Observations

Potential outliers and influential observations will be evaluated separately for each fitted model using the check_outliers() function from the R package performance (Lüdecke et al., 2021), as specified in the main-text Analysis Plan. Because the relevance of an observation as a potential outlier or influential case may depend on the specific outcome and model being estimated, these diagnostics will be evaluated at the model level.

Primary analyses will retain all available observations. Observations identified as potential outliers or influential cases will not be excluded from the primary analyses solely on the basis of these diagnostics. Instead, the corresponding models will be re-estimated without the identified observations as a sensitivity analysis, allowing us to assess the robustness and consistency of the findings. For transparency, the number of observations identified as potential outliers or influential cases and the number excluded in each sensitivity analysis will be documented separately for each model. Results from the primary and sensitivity analyses will be compared, and where exclusion of these observations results in substantive changes to the conclusions, results from both analyses will be reported and the discrepancy will be discussed.

Country-Level Variables Used for Clustering

Countries will be grouped into country-clusters using country-level indicators selected to capture demographic, economic, health, educational, digital infrastructure, and family-policy contexts. Indicators will be obtained from UNICEF, the World Bank, and the WORLD Policy Analysis Center.

Child Dependency Ratio

The child dependency ratio represents the number of children aged 0-14 relative to the workingage population aged 15-64, expressed per 100 working-age adults (UNICEF). This indicator captures differences in the demographic contexts in which families are raising children.

Universal Health Coverage for Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health

This index measures the coverage of essential reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health services on a scale from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater coverage (UNICEF). It provides an indicator of national maternal and child health infrastructure.

GDP per Capita

GDP per capita represents gross domestic product divided by the midyear population, expressed in current U.S. dollars (UNICEF). This indicator captures differences in countries' overall economic resources.

Government Expenditure on Education

Government expenditure on education represents total government spending on education as a percentage of GDP (UNICEF) and provides an indicator of national investment in education.

Access to Electricity

Access to electricity is defined as the percentage of the population with access to electricity (World Bank, 2026). This indicator captures basic infrastructure relevant to households' ability to access and use screen-based technologies.

Internet Users

Internet users will be represented by the percentage of the population using the Internet. The World Bank defines Internet users as individuals who have used the Internet from any location within the previous three months, including through computers, mobile phones, and other Internet-enabled devices (World Bank, 2026). This indicator captures national digital connectivity and families' broader opportunities to access digital media and resources.

Broadband Access

Broadband access will be represented by fixed broadband subscriptions per 100 people. The World Bank defines fixed broadband as subscriptions to high-speed public Internet through technologies including cable, DSL, fiber, satellite, and fixed wireless broadband (World Bank, 2026). This indicator provides an additional measure of the availability of digital infrastructure.

Paid Maternal Leave

Paid leave available to mothers will be based on the WORLD Policy Analysis Center and includes both paid maternity leave reserved for mothers and paid shared parental leave available to mothers (WORLD Policy Analysis Center, 2026). Leave will be coded as: (1) no paid leave, (2) <14 weeks, (3) 14-25.9 weeks, (4) 26-51.9 weeks, or (5) ≥52 weeks.

Paid Paternal Leave

Paid leave available to fathers will similarly include paid leave reserved for fathers and paid shared parental leave available to fathers (WORLD Policy Analysis Center, 2026). Leave will be coded as: (1) no paid leave, (2) <3 weeks, (3) 3-13.9 weeks, (4) 14-25.9 weeks, or (5) ≥26 weeks. Together, these indicators capture multiple dimensions of the broader structural contexts in which families are raising young children and accessing screen media.

Country-Clustering Procedure

Country-level indicators will be prepared and analyzed following the clustering strategy described in the main-text Analysis Plan. Continuous indicators will be standardized as z-scores (M = 0, SD = 1) prior to clustering to ensure that differences in measurement scale do not disproportionately influence estimation.

Country-clusters will be identified using Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) implemented with the Mclust() function from the R package mclust (Scrucca et al., 2023). Candidate solutions containing one through six clusters will be estimated across the covariance parameterizations available in mclust. The number of country-clusters will not be fixed in advance. Candidate solutions will be evaluated using the preregistered criteria described below. Each participating country will be assigned to the cluster for which it has the highest posterior probability of membership. Following selection of the final solution, descriptive summaries will be reported for each country-cluster, including the countries comprising each cluster, mean values of the clustering indicators, number of countries and participants within each cluster, and posterior classification probabilities.

1. Model fit. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) will serve as the primary statistical criterion for model selection. Under the mclust convention, the model with the largest BIC will be preferred. The Integrated Completed Likelihood (ICL) will be examined as a secondary criterion because it additionally considers classification uncertainty and tends to favor solutions with better-separated clusters. When BIC and ICL favor the same solution, that solution will be preferred, provided the remaining criteria below are satisfied.

2. Classification certainty and separation. Posterior membership probabilities and classification uncertainty produced by mclust() will be examined. Each country will be assigned to the cluster for which it has the highest posterior probability. Mean posterior probabilities closer to 1.00 and lower classification uncertainty will be interpreted as evidence of clearer separation between clusters. These measures will be reported rather than used as a stand-alone criterion for determining the number of clusters, because entropy/classification indices alone are not reliable criteria for selecting the correct number of mixture components.

3. Minimum cluster size. Solutions containing a country-cluster representing fewer than 5% of the countries will not be retained because very small clusters are more likely to represent unstable or sample-specific solutions. A 5% minimum has been used as a practical safeguard against very small classes in mixture-model applications, although cluster size will be considered alongside the other criteria rather than interpreted as evidence of model fit by itself.

4. Interpretability. Among solutions meeting the preceding statistical and sample-size criteria, the retained solution must produce distinguishable patterns across the prespecified country-level indicators that can be meaningfully described. Importantly, conceptual interpretability will not be used to override a clearly superior statistical solution solely because another solution produces more intuitively appealing country groupings. When multiple candidate solutions satisfy the preregistered criteria and BIC and ICL do not identify the same solution, model selection will follow the hierarchy specified in the main-text Analysis Plan: BIC will be prioritized, followed by ICL, classification certainty, and cluster stability. Conceptual interpretability will not be used to override a clearly superior statistical solution.

General Modelling Strategy

Across the preregistered analyses, country-cluster membership will be included as the primary country-level fixed-effect predictor, while country will be retained as a random intercept to account for the nesting of participants within countries. Primary models will first evaluate country-cluster differences while controlling for the prespecified age covariates relevant to each aim. Adjusted models will subsequently include the prespecified participant-level structural factors-country-relative household income, caregiver education, and household size-to evaluate whether these characteristics explain additional variability beyond country-cluster differences.
