Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Adolescents with chronic academic stress may benefit from accessible, non-pharmacological approaches to stress management. In this mixed-methods pilot study, six Grade 11 students completed a randomized, counterbalanced within-subject crossover design to compare the immediate effects of binaural-beat and singing-bowl listening. Each condition was preceded by a 3-min baseline and followed by physiological, device-derived, subjective, and qualitative assessments. Outcomes were heart rate, heart-rate variability (RMSSD), NeuroSky-derived Attention and Meditation indices, self-reported positive affect, negative affect, calmness, and open-ended reflections. Physiological responses varied widely across participants and no clear condition-level advantage was observed for heart rate, RMSSD or affective ratings. Five of six participants showed an increased NeuroSky-derived Meditation-index change in response to singing-bowl listening. All six participants selected the singing-bowl condition as more effective in promoting relaxation. In the qualitative reflections, people more often described the listening to singing bowls as calming, immersive and compatible with breathing. The results are exploratory and do not demonstrate therapeutic superiority or neural entrainment. They propose that perceptual comfort, attentional engagement and individual listening experience may be important components of sound-based stress regulation in adolescents.

1. Introduction

Adolescence is a critical period for development when mental health problems can impact on well-being, learning and everyday functioning. Mental disorders affect an estimated one in seven adolescents aged between 10 and 19, according to the World Health Organization [1]. A large national psychiatric epidemiological survey conducted in China and led by researchers including from the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences reported that the weighted prevalence of any psychiatric disorder among children and adolescents aged 6-16 years was 17.5% [2]. More recent evidence from schools in Guangdong Province found that 22.4% of 8,013 secondary-school students screened positive for abnormal mental health on the MMHI-60 [3]. Academic stress is also prominent: a study of 15,055 high-school students in Henan Province reported a 41.8% positive rate for mental-health problems, and academic stress was the most frequent problem at 58.9% [4]. Other studies have also found that academic stress is associated with depressive symptoms and worse psychological outcomes among Chinese adolescents [5, 6]. Taken together, these results point to the need for accessible, low-risk approaches that may be beneficial for adolescents with regulation of short-term stress.

Non-pharmacologic approaches to stress reduction have included music and structured listening. A large systematic review and meta-analysis reported beneficial effects of music interventions on physiological and psychological stress-related outcomes [7]. Experimental work suggests that auditory context can influence components of the psychobiological stress response [8]. In this study, we examine two specific acoustic paradigms-singing-bowl listening and binaural-beat listening-which have been popularized in the relaxation literature but studied with different methodologies and populations.

The evidence for singing bowl interventions is still limited and focused on adult samples. An observational study of 62 adults reported decreases in tension, anger, fatigue, and depressed mood following Tibetan singing-bowl sound meditation [9]. A randomized crossover study of 51 adults found greater reductions in heart rate and systolic blood pressure following exposure to Himalayan singing bowls than to silence before directed relaxation [10]. More recent studies in adult samples in self-reported anxiety, heart-rate variability, respiration, and EEG measures during or after singing-bowl interventions [11, 12]. Collectively, these studies suggest possible relaxation-related effects, while also suggesting that the evidence base remains small and methodologically heterogeneous.

Binaural beats are an auditory illusion perceived when two tones of slightly different frequencies are presented separately to each ear, creating the perception of a beat at the frequency difference [13]. Possible effects on anxiety, cognition and pain have been reported in reviews and meta-analytic work, but findings vary with stimulation parameters and study design [14, 15]. For example, one randomized controlled trial of day-case surgery adults found binaural-beat audio to produce a greater reduction of pre-operative anxiety compared to comparison conditions [16]. Simultaneously, a recent systematic review concluded that the evidence for reliable brainwave entrainment is inconsistent and that the methodology needs to become more standardized [17].

Existing research on singing bowls and binaural beats has therefore yielded mixed but potentially relevant findings, with much of the published evidence coming from adult or clinical settings rather than from adolescents [9] [10] [11] [12] [14] [15] [16] [17]. There seems to be especially little direct head-tohead evidence in high-school students comparing these two ways of listening. The aim of this pilot study was to explore and compare short-term physiological, device-derived, subjective and experiential responses to binaural beats versus live singing-bowl sound in adolescents under examination period academic pressure.

1.1 Research Questions

This study therefore asks whether different sound-based listening modalities are associated with distinct patterns of relaxation. Specifically, we examine whether relaxation is better understood as a frequency-specific neural response or as a broader psychophysiological experience emerging through interactions among sound, body, attention, and subjective perception.

Although binaural beats and singing bowls differ substantially in their acoustic and perceptual characteristics [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17], the limited and heterogeneous existing evidence does not provide a sufficient basis for strong mechanism-specific predictions. Accordingly, this pilot study adopted an exploratory approach. We examined whether the two listening interventions were associated with different patterns of short-term change across physiological, device-derived, subjective, and experiential measures. EEG-related observations were treated as exploratory and were not considered a definitive test of a brainwave-entrainment mechanism.

Hence the study examines whether the two listening conditions are associated with different patterns of short-term relaxation response rather than assuming a priori that they operated through different established mechanisms.

>Materials and Methods

2.1 Participants

Because five of the six participants were minors, the study protocol was submitted to and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Beijing Haidian Future Academy (BHFA), the research institution of the first author. The study protocol was approved by the BHFA Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. XXXX; approved Month Day, Year). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents or legal guardians prior to participation. No personally identifiable information (PII) was included in the dataset used for analysis or manuscript preparation. All participant-facing research activities and data collection were conducted by the first author at BHFA.

2.3 Experimental Design

This study employed a randomized, counterbalanced within-subject crossover design to examine the immediate effects of two auditory interventions-singing bowl meditation and binaural beat meditation-on participants' physiological and subjective states. Each participant completed both interventions, with intervention order determined using a random-number application. Three participants completed the singing bowl intervention first, followed by the binaural beat intervention, whereas the remaining three completed the interventions in the reverse order.

Prior to the formal experiment, participants attended a pre-experimental instructional workshop designed to introduce the concepts and practices relevant to the study. The workshop covered basic concepts related to brainwave activity, binaural beats, singing bowls, diaphragmatic breathing, and meditation posture. Participants experienced and practiced singing bowl meditation during the workshop and were invited to verbally reflect on their experiences.

Because of time limitations during the workshop, binaural beat meditation was not conducted on site. Instead, participants were provided with the binaural beat audio and instructed to complete at least one self-guided practice session at home before participating in the formal experiment. Thus, all participants had prior exposure to both auditory interventions before formal data collection: singing bowl meditation through the in-person workshop and binaural beat meditation through subsequent at-home practice. This familiarization procedure was intended to reduce stimulus novelty and ensure that participants understood the meditation procedures used in the experiment.

The formal experiment was conducted in the same enclosed classroom at Beijing Haidian Future Academy (BHFA) to minimize external auditory interference and environmental variability. Room conditions, participant posture, breathing instructions, listening duration, and playback procedures were standardized across experimental sessions. Participants and researchers remained in consistent locations within the room throughout data collection.

Each auditory intervention was preceded by a three-minute eyes-closed silent meditation baseline. Following the first baseline, participants completed the first five-minute auditory intervention according to their randomized intervention order. They subsequently completed a second three-minute eyes-closed silent meditation baseline before receiving the alternative fiveminute auditory intervention. The second baseline provided a condition-specific resting measurement prior to the second intervention and allowed potential order and carryover effects from the preceding intervention to be considered in subsequent analysis and interpretation; it was not assumed to eliminate such effects.

The resulting experimental sequence was therefore: with the order of the singing bowl and binaural beat interventions counterbalanced across participants.

2.4 Auditory Stimuli

Two auditory conditions were used in the experiment: singing bowl sound and binaural beats. Both conditions were designed as five-minute sound-based meditation experiences but differed substantially in their acoustic characteristics and mode of presentation.

For the singing bowl condition, a single singing bowl with a primary perceived pitch centered approximately on C4 was performed live by the experimenter in the same quiet classroom in which the formal experiment was conducted. No additional music, drone, or recorded accompaniment was used. Participants therefore listened to the natural attack, resonance, overtone structure, and decay produced by the bowl during live performance. The same singing bowl and experimenter were used throughout the study to maintain consistency across participants.

For the binaural beat condition, a prerecorded stimulus was created using two closely spaced carrier frequencies selected in relation to the primary pitch of the singing bowl. A 261-Hz carrier tone was presented to the left ear and a 267-Hz carrier tone to the right ear, producing a 6-Hz interaural frequency difference within the conventional theta-frequency range (4-8 Hz). The stimulus was presented through in-ear headphones to enable independent delivery of the two carrier frequencies to the left and right ears, as required for binaural beat perception. The use of 261 Hz as the reference carrier also provided an approximate pitch relationship between the binaural beat stimulus and the C-centered singing bowl condition, while preserving the distinct acoustic characteristics of the two interventions.

2.5 Procedure

Upon arrival, participants were first asked to fill out a questionnaire with demographic and experiential information, such as previous music education and previous meditation experience. Participants then completed a three-minutes eyes-closed silent meditation. Physiological measures collected during this time served as the baseline for the first auditory condition.

Following the baseline period, participants were exposed to the first five-minute auditory intervention, either singing bowl sound or binaural beats according to the randomized condition order. Physiological measures were obtained immediately following the intervention. Participants then completed the same subjective ratings of positive affect (PA), negative affect (nA), and calmness and provided brief written reflections describing their listening experience.

Participants then participated in a second three-minute eye-closed silent meditation. Physiological data collected during this period were used as the baseline for the second auditory condition and allowed potential carryover and order effects from the preceding condition to be considered in subsequent interpretation.

Participants then heard the alternative auditory intervention for five minutes. The same physiological measurement and self-report procedures were repeated immediately following the intervention. Participants again rated their positive affect (PA), negative affect (NA), and calmness and provided brief written reflections describing their listening experience.

At the end of the experiment, participants directly compared the two auditory interventions. They indicated which intervention they perceived as more effective in promoting relaxation and provided a brief written explanation of their choice and the perceived differences between the two listening experiences.

2.6 Measures

2.6.1 Physiological and Device-Derived Measures

Brain activity was monitored using a NeuroSky single-channel consumer EEG device. The NeuroSky MindWave systems record from a frontal dry electrode and provides EEG-related frequency information together with proprietary device-derived indices [18]. In the present study, the visual display information related to alpha and theta together with the device derived Attention and Meditation indices were used. We interpreted these outputs conservatively, since this consumer-grade single-channel EEG system is more susceptible to noise, artifacts, and measurement variability than research-grade multichannel systems [18, 19]. In addition， NeuroSky's Attention and Meditation eSense scores are proprietary indices ranging from 0 to 100 indices and should not be interpreted as direct clinical, psychometric, or conventional EEG measures [20].

Cardiovascular activity was monitored using an Apple Watch. Heart rate (HR) and heart-rate variability (HRV) data were collected at the end of each measurement period, including the baseline and auditory-intervention periods. HRV was summarized by the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), a common time domain measure of HRV that reflects shortterm variation in heart rate and is frequently used as a surrogate measure of vagally mediated cardiac regulation [21, 22]. Unlike the continuously displayed EEG-related measures, HR and RMSSD values were recorded at discrete measurement points rather than continuously analyzed across each experimental period. Because of the short measurement periods and the sensitivity of HRV to recording context, body position, respiration, and other methodological factors [21, 22], these values were considered exploratory physiological indicators rather than clinical measures.

2.6.2 Subjective Measures

Subjective psychological states were measured using study specific 0-10 self-report ratings. Three dimensions were measured: calmness, positive affect (PA), and negative affect (NA). Participants provided these ratings immediately following each auditory intervention. These brief ratings were designed to capture participants' immediate subjective responses for exploratory within-participant comparison and were not intended to function as validated diagnostic or psychometric scales. In addition to the numerical ratings, participants provide brief written reflections after each auditory intervention. At the end of the experiment, participants directly compared the two interventions by indicating which they perceived as more effective in promoting relaxation and providing a brief written explanation of their choice and perceived differences between the two listening experiences.

Together, the physiological, device-derived, and self-report measures provided complementary perspectives on participants' responses to the singing bowl and binaural beat interventions, allowing preliminary examination of both physiological patterns and subjective experiences of relaxation.

2.7 Statistical Analysis

As this pilot study was exploratory in nature and the sample size was small (N = 6), statistical analyzes focused on descriptive statistics, within-participant change, individual response patterns, and convergence across multiple data sources rather than confirmatory hypothesis testing. Analyzes were therefore primarily based on paired within-subject comparisons, as each participant was exposed to both the BB and SB conditions. The sample size was very small and there was no defensible assumption of normality, so we used two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to examine differences between conditions. These analyzes were considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied since the analyzes were exploratory and aimed at characterizing early response patterns rather than testing a family of confirmatory hypotheses. Single p-values should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Importantly, statistical significance was not treated as equivalent to psychological or clinical importance. The p-values were treated as measures of the compatibility of the observed data with a specified null model, rather than as direct measures of intervention efficacy or probability that a hypothesis was true [23]. Interpretation therefore focused on the direction and consistency of within-participant change, descriptive patterns of effect, convergence and divergence across physiological and subjective measures, and participants' reported listening experiences. EEG alpha and theta activity were not analyzed as continuous quantitative outcomes . Reliable mean alpha or theta power could not be calculated from the raw EEG time-series or calibrated spectral band-power data, so these were not retained in this study. Instead, alpha-and thetarelated patterns were obtained from the single-channel NeuroSky visual display and qualitatively classified. Thus, these observations were considered as descriptive supplemental data and not tested statistically by inferential means.

Participant open-ended reflections were retained verbatim and analyzed in conjunction with physiological and self-report outcomes to provide context for individual responses. As formal independent coding and inter-rater reliability procedures have not yet been completed, these responses are currently treated as qualitative descriptive reflections rather than a formal thematic analysis.

3.1 Physiological and Device-Derived Outcomes 1. Heart Rate

Heart-rate responses varied substantially across participants and conditions. Mean HR change was -5.00 BPM (SD = 12.60) following binaural beats and -2.67 BPM (SD = 7.00) following singing-bowl listening. Median changes were -4.0 and -1.0 BPM, respectively.

The paired difference between conditions was not statistically distinguishable in this small sample, Wilcoxon W = 10.0, p= 1.00. These findings indicate considerable individual variability and provide no evidence that either condition produced a consistently greater short-term HR reduction. Participant-level changes further illustrate the substantial heterogeneity of heart-rate responses across both listening conditions (Figure 1).

2. Heart-Rate Variability

HRV/RMSSD responses were also heterogeneous. Mean change was +2.00 (SD = 4.20) following binaural beats and -2.17 (SD = 9.70) following singing-bowl listening. The paired comparison was not statistically significant, W = 4.5, p = .563. Thus, the current data do not support a condition-specific advantage in HRV response. Given the small sample and short intervention periods, these measures are best interpreted as preliminary indicators of autonomic response variability.

3. Qualitative EEG-Display Observations

Qualitative inspection of the NeuroSky display suggested participant-specific differences in alpha-and theta-related patterns across both listening conditions. Observations related to alpha generally showed stable or increased display activity whereas observations related to theta were more variable across participants and conditions. No consistent qualitative pattern was evident across the available recordings to support theta dominance.

These observations were not quantitatively analyzed, as the raw EEG time-series and calibrated spectral band-power data were not saved, and are not to be interpreted as evidence for or against cortical neural entrainment.

4. NeuroSky-Derived Attention

The NeuroSky-derived Attention index demonstrated substantial between-participant variability. Mean change was 0.00 (SD = 29.66) following binaural beats and +7.50 (SD = 25.45) following singing-bowl listening. The paired comparison was not statistically significant, W = 5.5, p = .375.

Because the Attention score is a proprietary device-derived index rather than a validated direct measure of attentional control, mindfulness, or cognitive load, higher or lower values were not interpreted as inherently more therapeutic.

5. NeuroSky-Derived Meditation

A more consistent descriptive pattern was observed for the NeuroSky-derived Meditation index. Mean change was -0.83 (SD = 24.58) following binaural beats compared with +15.00 (SD = 20.25) following singing-bowl listening.

Five participants demonstrated a more favorable Meditation-index change under the singingbowl condition, while one showed no between-condition difference. The exploratory paired comparison did not meet the conventional .05 threshold, W = 0, p = .063. Given the sample size, this result should not be interpreted as evidence of an established singing-bowl effect. 3.2 Subjective Psychological Outcomes 1. Positive Affect

Positive Affect was descriptively higher following singing-bowl listening (M = 7.75, SD = 0.82) than following binaural beats (M = 7.33, SD = 1.03). The paired difference was not statistically significant, W = 0, p = .125.

2. Negative Affect

Mean Negative Affect was 2.50 (SD = 1.38) following binaural beats and 3.00 (SD = 2.37) following singing-bowl listening, W = 1.5, p = .750.

The greater variability in the singing-bowl condition was influenced by one participant who reported excessive sound intensity and physical auditory discomfort. This participant's response is retained rather than excluded because it provides potentially important information regarding individual differences in perceptual tolerance.

3. Calmness

Calmness was descriptively higher following singing-bowl listening (M = 7.42, SD = 2.73; median = 8.5) than following binaural beats (M = 7.08, SD = 1.69; median = 7.25). The paired comparison was not statistically significant, W = 5.0, p = .625.

The larger variability under the singing-bowl condition again reflects substantial individual differences, including the participant who experienced the stimulus as excessively loud. Mean subjective ratings across the two listening conditions are summarized in Figure 3.

4. Perceived Comparative Relaxation and Qualitative Reflections

All six participants selected the singing-bowl intervention as the intervention they perceived to be more effective in promoting relaxation. (Figure 4).

Under a simple two-choice null model, an exploratory exact binomial test yielded p = .031. This result should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Preference was an exploratory secondary outcome, the sample was extremely small, and participants could not be blinded to the perceptually obvious differences between the two auditory conditions.

Most importantly, preference should not be equated with intervention efficacy. Qualitative reflections from participants help to contextualize this pattern of perceived comparative relaxation. Singing-bowl listening was often characterized as calming, reduced mind-wandering, compatible with breathing, pleasurable, and immersive. Binaural beats induced more heterogeneous responses, with some subjects reporting relaxation, and others describing the stimulation as mechanical, noisy, uncomfortable, cognitively distracting, or difficult to synchronize with their breathing rhythm.

At this stage these reflections are not offered as formally derived qualitative themes. Instead, they offer a phenomenological background against which the large individual differences observed in the physiological and self-report measures can be understood.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Principal Findings

This mixed-methods pilot study explored the immediate psychological, physiological and experiential effects of listening to two different types of sound in adolescents.

The results do not suggest that listening to singing bowls is therapeutically superior to listening to binaural beats, nor do they suggest that either is clinically effective. Due to the low number of participants, the high individual variability, the number of exploratory outcomes and the short duration of the intervention, such conclusions would be beyond the scope of the evidence of the study.

Instead, the findings offer initial evidence for a more complex applied psychological proposition; that sound-based stress regulation may be modulated, not just by the acoustic stimulus, but also by how the individual perceives, attends to and engages in the listening experience.

For at least some participants, both conditions were associated with relaxation. However, physiological responses were heterogeneous and neither HR nor HRV showed a consistent advantage at the condition level. Similarly, there were no statistically significant group differences in PA, NA, and Calmness at conventional levels.

The most consistent descriptive patterns were found for the NeuroSky-derived Meditation index, participants' qualitative experiences, and listening preference. Five of the six participants showed a more favorable change in the Meditation index while listening to the singing bowl, and all six eventually preferred the singing-bowl condition. The observations are hypothesisgenerating, not confirmatory.

4.2 Psychological Importance Is Not Equivalent to Statistical Significance

An important consideration in interpreting these findings concerns the role of statistical significance.

With N = 6, the study has limited statistical power to detect anything other than very large and highly consistent effects. Conversely, an isolated p value below .05 would not establish that an intervention is psychologically meaningful or clinically effective.

Accordingly, the present study does not treat the conventional p < .05 threshold as the primary criterion for interpreting the findings.

For example, the between-condition comparison for the NeuroSky-derived Meditation index yielded p = .063. This should neither be dismissed as "no effect" nor reframed as statistically significant. Instead, the directional consistency across participants constitutes a preliminary pattern worthy of replication.

Similarly, the unanimous preference for singing-bowl listening produced an exploratory binomial p = .031. Although statistically notable under a simple choice model, this result concerns preference, not therapeutic efficacy.

Hence, the more informative question for an exploratory pilot study is not simply whether an outcome crossed a conventional significance threshold but whether multiple forms of evidence display coherent patterns that warrant further investigation.

4.3 Neural Entrainment Cannot Be Established from the Present Data

The EEG-related observations require particularly cautious interpretation.

Binaural beats are often discussed when considering frequency-following or neural-entrainment hypotheses [13, 14, 17]. However, the present study was ill-equipped to provide a definitive test of cortical entrainment.

Raw EEG time-series and calibrated spectral band-power estimates were not saved. Instead, alpha and theta patterns were observed by a single channel NeuroSky visualization and recorded descriptively.

Therefore, terms such as alpha increase, theta fluctuation or theta dominance refer only to the qualitative patterns observed in the visualization of the device. They are not meant to be taken as quantitative evidence for changes in cortical oscillatory power.

Therefore, the present results neither show nor rule out neural entrainment.

The scientifically defendable conclusion is:

There was no consistent qualitative pattern in the available NeuroSky visualization that was sustained and consistent with theta dominance, and the measurement protocol was insufficient to establish or rule out frequency-specific cortical entrainment. This difference is important in that it suggests the theoretical assumptions of binaural beats are not being interpreted as empirical findings.

4.4 NeuroSky Attention and Meditation Are Device-Derived Indices

Similar caution is necessary when interpreting NeuroSky's proprietary Attention and Meditation indices [19,20] . These device-derived scores are not equivalent to validated psychological measures of mindfulness, attentional regulation, meditation depth, cognitive load, or emotional relaxationand published evaluations of consumer-grade EEG devices also report substantial variability in such indices [19, 20].

Therefore, an increased Meditation cannot be directly interpreted as evidence for "deeper meditation" just as a decreased Attention cannot be interpreted as beneficial cognitive disengagement.

These variables should therefore always be referred to throughout the manuscript as the NeuroSky-derived Attention index and NeuroSky-derived Meditation index. In this pilot study, the main value of these measures is to provide an additional device-derived response measure that can be compared with physiological, self-report, and experiential data.

4.5 Autonomic Changes Should Be Interpreted Conservatively

Some individual participants demonstrated substantial HR changes between baseline and postintervention measurements. However, large pre-post differences should not automatically be attributed to the intervention.

Since participants performed two conditions within the same experimental session, baselines in the second condition could have been affected by the preceding intervention, the rest interval, adaptation to the experimental setting, expectancy about the second condition, or spontaneous physiological recovery.

For example, an intervention starting with an unusually high second baseline may seem to produce a particularly large HR reduction partly because of regression to the participant's usual resting state.

Therefore, the current design allows for describing observed within-condition physiological change, but not strong causal statements such as 'the intervention activated the parasympathetic nervous system'. The interpretation of HRV is influenced by factors such as the recording time, respiration, posture, baseline conditions and other methodological factors [21, 22].

Future studies should incorporate longer washout periods, testing on different days, or repeated baseline measurements.

4.6 Acoustic Comfort as a Potential Psychological Moderator

One participant's response to singing-bowl listening is especially informative.

The participant stated that the sound was too loud and physically uncomfortable. This was accompanied by a high negative effect and low calmness. Rather than being treated solely as an outlier, this response may provide important information about individual differences in acoustic comfort and sensory tolerance.

Instead, it demonstrates a potentially important principle in applied psychology: Depending on the comfort of perception and the sensory tolerance of the individual, the same acoustic intervention may have a completely different psychological effect.

Sound intensity, timbre, temporal structure, as well as perceived naturalness and listener comfort [7, 8], may influence the way a sound intervention is experienced. In the wider musicstress literature, context and reason for listening are also suggested to influence stress-related responses [7, 8]. These features should thus not be understood solely as technical features but perhaps as elements of the psychological listening experience.

Future studies should include objectification of sound-pressure level calibration, documentation of listening level, safe and comfortable ranges, and consideration of allowing participants some limited control over playback intensity.

4.7 Preference Is Potentially Meaningful, but It Is Not Efficacy

A clear and consistent finding in the present sample is the unanimous preference for singingbowl listening.

Nevertheless, preference can be the consequence of a variety of psychological processes such as pleasantness, familiarity, novelty, expectancy, perceived naturalness, cultural associations, esthetic preference or sensory comfort.

Preference cannot be equated with stress reduction, therefore." At the same time, preference should not be necessarily treated as a nuisance variable.

It is plausible that sustained attention, expectancy and emotional responsiveness in applied listening interventions could be influenced by willingness to engage with the sound. Research on everyday music listening suggests that stress-related effects may depend on the listening context and motivation [7, 8]. Listener preference may be one of the factors in the process by which sound-based interventions affect subjective experience, but this study is unable to establish that mechanism.

This possibility deserves systematic investigation in larger studies.

4.8 Toward a Preliminary Therapeutic Listening Framework

The present findings, together with the inconsistent literature on binaural-beat entrainment [14, 17], suggest that a purely frequency-based account might not be enough to understand sound-based stress regulation.

Instead of assuming a simple path like: acoustic frequency → pre-determined brainwave state → relaxation. The present findings motivate investigation of a broader therapeutic listening framework.

In this preliminary framework, the psychological effects of sound-based listening may emerge through dynamic interactions among acoustic characteristics, perceptual comfort, attentional engagement, physiological regulation, affective response, and subjective meaning.

The present pilot data do not provide causal evidence for this sequence and, thus, Therapeutic Listening should be offered as a preliminary interpretive framework, and not as a validated theoretical model.

Nevertheless, the framework provides a useful applied psychological lens by shifting attention away from the question: What sound makes the desired brainwave? to ask yourself: What are the acoustic, psychological and contextual conditions for a listening experience that facilitates stress regulation for a particular adolescent?

From the point of view of applied psychology this may be the more important question.

Results suggest that the value of sound-based interventions for adolescent stress regulation may not be in whether or not a particular stimulus induces a pre-determined neurophysiological state, but rather in how adolescents experience, engage with, and self-regulate via the listening experience.

Therapeutic Listening may thus provide a useful initial framework for exploring interactions among auditory features, perceptual comfort, attentional engagement, affective response, physiological regulation, individual preference, and subjective meaning-making.

4.9 Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations severely limit interpretation. First, the sample size included only six adolescents. So this study should be considered exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than an efficacy trial.

Secondly, the crossover design introduces potential order and carry-over effects. Although we counterbalanced the order of conditions, the relatively short time between interventions may not have been sufficient for participants to return to their original physiological and psychological state.

Third, the use of a single-channel consumer-grade NeuroSky system, the lack of storage of raw EEG recordings, and reliance on proprietary indices and qualitative visualization limited the interpretation of EEG. Comparative validation studies have shown that low-cost single-channel systems can capture usable EEG-related signals but are more noise-limited and less reliable than medical-grade systems [18, 19]. When neural entrainment is a primary research question, future research should use research-grade multichannel EEG with prespecified spectral analyzes. Fourth, standardization and documentation of sound intensity should be more rigorous. The negative effect in one participant shows that acoustic comfort can have a significant impact on psychological outcomes.

Fifth, the psychological outcome measures were brief and largely state-based. Future studies with larger samples should include validated adolescent stress, anxiety, emotion-regulation, and mindfulness measures along with the physiological indices.

Sixth, blinding of the participants to the two acoustically different interventions was not performed, which could have led to expectancy and preference effects.

Seventh，although participants were familiarized with both interventions prior to formal data collection, familiarization occurred in different contexts: singing-bowl meditation was experienced in a supervised workshop, whereas binaural-beat familiarization was completed independently at home. This difference may have introduced contextual or expectancy effects.

Finally, although open-ended responses provide valuable context about the experience, the present qualitative component should remain descriptive unless formal analytic procedures are conducted at a later time. For future mixed-methods research, independent coding, either predefined or iterative codebook development, coder reconciliation, and proper assessment of coding reliability could be used.

5. Conclusion

This mixed-methods pilot study provides preliminary evidence that binaural beats and singingbowl listening may support short-term relaxation and stress regulation in adolescents, while also revealing substantial individual variability in physiological, device-derived, subjective, and experiential responses.

The findings did not establish frequency-specific neural entrainment, nor did they demonstrate the therapeutic superiority of either intervention. However, singing-bowl listening showed a more favorable descriptive pattern in the NeuroSky-derived Meditation index and was preferred by all six participants. Participants also more frequently described the singing bowl in terms of calmness, immersion, reduced mind wandering, and compatibility with breathing. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample, potential order and carry-over effects, and limitations of the EEG measurement.

More broadly, the results suggest that sound-based stress regulation may not be adequately explained by acoustic frequency or presumed brainwave entrainment alone. Instead, the psychological effects of listening may emerge through interactions among acoustic characteristics, perceptual comfort, attentional engagement, affective response, physiological regulation, individual preference, and subjective meaning.

We therefore propose Therapeutic Listening as a tentative applied psychological framework to study adolescents' interaction with sound-based interventions. More controlled studies are needed using validated psychological measures, standardized acoustic parameters, and researchgrade physiological methods to see if these preliminary patterns replicate, and to clarify which listening experiences are most effective, acceptable and appropriate for adolescent stress regulation.
