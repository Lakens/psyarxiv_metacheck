study_design_overview Within-Subjects Design. All participants will complete 2 Breathing Tasks measuring Interoceptive Awareness (Task A & Task B), subjective confidence and arousal questions following each trial, and a MAIA questionnaire. Order of performing Task A and Task B will be counterbalanced across participants.

Task A: In this task participants will be asked to match their breath to a circle on a computer screen that expands and contracts rhythmically. Pulse rate will always start at 5-seconds/pulse. Participants will also be asked to indicate through a key press the beginning of every inhale and exhale. This offers a behavioral measure that indicates their ability to match their breathing rate with the pulsing circle. Each trial will consist of 8- circle pulses. As discovered in a previous pilot study (Farb & Logie, 2018) if the pulse of the circle is changed gradually with each breath, on average participants awareness of respiration change is no greater than chance levels even when the overall change is as large as 65% from initial breathing rate. Awareness of change is determined through a 3-way forced choice question after each trial (“On the previous trial did your breathing rate increase, decrease, or stay constant?”). For the current study, we are interested in calculating a threshold of awareness of change in respiration. To do so, a staircase method will be used, such that the overall change in respiration rate over the full trial will either increase or decrease (using a 1-up, 2-down rule) based upon correct or incorrect response on the previous trial. 2 correct responses in a row will lead to a step down in the staircase while 1 incorrect response will lead to one step easier. Step sizes represent total increase or decrease in respiration rate from the previous position on the staircase. The first level of overall respiration rate change will be 50% over the 8 pulses. Speed-up, Slow-down as well as “no-change” trials will be used [1/3 of total trials for each] so that the pulse rate is not changing every time.

Task B. In this task participants will also match their breath to a circle on a computer screen that expands and contracts rhythmically. Pulse rate will always start at 5-seconds/pulse. Participants will also be asked to indicate through a key press when they begin each inhale and exhale. However in this task participants will be asked to press the spacebar once they notice that their breathing rate has changed. “No change” trials, in which the rate of the pulses stays constant the entire duration of the trial, will be randomly interspersed so that the pulse rate is not changing every time. In total there will be 4 “no-change”, 4 speed-up and 4 slow-down trials randomly presented. The maximum number of pulses for each trial will be capped at 12. 		Study Design:
The purpose of the study is to validate a novel task for measuring individual differences in breath awareness, specifically the ability to detect changes in breathing rate and the confidence associated with detection judgments. The study is mostly a within-participant design, featuring the Breath Change Awareness Task (BCAT), a heartbeat counting task, and self-questionnaires assessing interoceptive awareness, self-confidence, and subjective well-being.

Participants will attend a single 1 hour laboratory session, in which they will complete informed consent and be outfitted with respiration and heartbeat monitors. They will then perform the BCAT, the heartbeat counting task, self-report questionnaires, and then the BCAT again to assess test-retest reliability.

The BCAT features a learning phase that has two main factors: (1) direction/magnitude of respiratory change [faster/slower/no change], and (2) the salience of the change [high/low]. Following each BCAT paced breathing trial (see materials for details), participants will engage in a three-alternative forced choice around change detection. The magnitude of change will be determined by a psychophysics staircase to estimate participant’s ability to detect change in respiration rate. The BCAT then features a testing phase in which participants will be assessed at their high salience threshold while manipulating trial salience to verify whether the participant thresholds have been correctly estimated.

Between-Participant Randomization: to determine the extent to which task performance can be explained by visual detection alone, participants will be randomly assigned to one of two conditions: visual and breath. In the [breath] condition participants will receive normal BCAT instructions before each of the BCAT tasks; in the [visual] condition, participants will first be instructed only to view the circle’s changing rate, with no reference to the breath; then, upon the repetition of the BCAT, all participants will be told to breath along with the circle and join their breath.

Within-Participant Randomization: the BCAT features within participant randomization as the order of the 4 different staircase conditions (deceleration low salience, deceleration high salience, acceleration low salience, acceleration high salience) will be counterbalanced randomly by participant. A three-alternative forced choice task that employs a psychophysics staircase to estimate participant’s ability to detect change in respiration rate.

Training Trials: Participants are presented with trials in which their breathing rate either increases or decreases over four breaths, with changes occurring either gradually or quickly. After each trial, participants are asked to identify whether their breathing rate increased or decreased, rate their confidence in the accuracy of their response, and report their level of wakefulness or arousal. Four distinct conditions are used: high salience (rapid changes) and low salience (gradual changes) for both acceleration and deceleration of breathing.

For high salience trials, the change amount is applied to the circle pulse duration in a single multiplication between pulse 2 and 3: New duration = Old Duration * Change Amount

For low salience trials, the local (pulse specific) change amount is applied to the circle pulse duration between every pulse using the following calculation:
LocalChange= ChangeAmount ^ 1/(numBreaths -1)

Test trials: Following the staircase for the four conditions, the high salience thresholds will be used for 16 test trials. The test trials will evenly split direction type: i.e., they will consist of 8 acceleration and 8 deceleration trials. Within each direction, there will be 4 high salience and 4 low salience trials. However, the threshold will be set consistently at the high salience threshold for that direction across all 8 trials. We would therefore expect accuracy to decrease for low salience trials as generally the high salience threshold will be too difficult to detect in a low salience trial presentation.

Accuracy: participant’s ability to correctly assess whether the breathing rate has increased or decreased after each trial by comparing their response to the actual change that occurred.

Confidence: the self-reported certainty a participant has in their assessment of whether the breathing rate increased or decreased after each trial.

Arousal: self-reported level of alertness after each trial to capture the subjective experience of perceived difficulty or the extent to which the change in breathing rate captured their attention.

Detection Accuracy: participant’s ability to correctly assess whether their breathing accelerated or decelerated after each trial compared to the actual change that occurred.

Direction: whether a given trial involves acceleration or deceleration of the respiratory rate. A -1 indicates acceleration, and a 1 indicates deceleration

Level: the proportion change in the circle pulse duration from the start to the end of the trial. For example a 30% acceleration or deceleration is a level of .3

Magnitude of Respiratory Change: the degree of variation in the breathing rate, specifically the extent of acceleration and deceleration, observed across trials. This measures how large or significant the change in breathing rate is as determined by the psychophysical staircase method, which adjusts based on the participant’s correct or incorrect assessment of whether their breathing rate has increased or decreased. It is formally calculated as Direction * Level, so an acceleration of 30% = -1 * .3 = -.3, where as a deceleration of 30% = 1 * .3 = .3; this allows for the total change amount to be calculated as 1 + the magnitude of change.

Salience: how easily noticeable the change in breathing rate is after each trial. Either a gradual change in breathing rate (low salience) or marked change in breathing rate (high salience).

Threshold: the minimum change in respiratory acceleration or deceleration required for a participant to reliably detect a shift in breathing rate. This threshold is determined using a psychophysical staircase procedure, where stimulus intensity is adaptively adjusted based on performance. A lower threshold indicates greater sensitivity, indicating that a participant can detect smaller changes in breathing rates, whereas a higher threshold suggests reduced sensitivity, requiring larger changes for detection. This measure serves as an index of interoceptive precision in the context of respiratory perception. The threshold score is calculated as the average of the last 2 trials in the staircase for any given condition.

Arousal: a participant’s self-reported level of emotional activation in response to each breathing rate trial. Arousal is assessed using a 6-point graphic rating scale featuring emoji representations, allowing participants to gauge their internal state. This measure captures an individual’s perceived intensity of arousal in response to changes in breathing rate.

Confidence: a participant’s self-assessed certainty in their judgement of whether the breathing rate accelerated or decelerated during a given trial. Confidence is measured using a 6-point numeric scale, with higher ratings indicating greater certainty in the accuracy of their response. This measure provides insight into a participants’ meta-perceptual awareness, reflecting their ability to evaluate the reliability of their interoceptive judgements.

Adaptive Interoception: an individual’s ability to accurately perceive and interpret internal bodily sensations in a way that supports effective self-regulation and well-being. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. Abstract

Does bodily change shape self-reported arousal only when it is noticed? In the Breath Change Awareness Task, participants breathe with a visual pacer or follow it by vision alone, report whether its pace changed, and rate arousal. After four development studies (Prolific adults and undergraduates, N = 579), we report a preregistered laboratory study (N = 206) with verified breathing. Arousal followed noticed changes more steeply than missed ones, with or without breathing, and in most studies noticed effects scaled with a change's size beyond the reported direction. In Study 5, missed changes moved arousal up to a third as much although breathing tracked the pacer closely, and a noticed breathed change moved arousal 1.8 [1.3, 2.7] times as much as one followed by vision alone, though not detectably in the online Study 4. Ratings partly followed the preceding detection report. Self-reported arousal mainly follows noticing; breathing adds to it. from the moderate position, part of it: the direction but not the size. Because all three concern bodily change, they predict these effects only when the change is breathed, and a breathed change should move arousal more than an equally large one followed by vision alone (preregistered as a steeper change-arousal slope in the breathing group, H4C); the comparison of noticed changes at the same report and size is exploratory. Table S17 in Section S17.5 lists every preregistered test. A non-interoceptive account, in which the rating follows the report, predicts a noticing effect (a steeper change-arousal slope when a change is noticed) even from vision alone, but little effect of change magnitude on arousal.

Introduction

Your breathing quickens, and you feel on edge. Would the change have moved you if you had not noticed it? Interoceptive signals, including heart rate, respiration, and visceral activity, are continuously integrated with cognitive and affective systems to produce feeling states (Craig, 2002; Sherrington, 1906), but whether a bodily change shapes feeling only when consciously perceived is unclear. Three theoretical positions give different answers. The constitutive position holds that awareness is what makes bodily change felt. James and Lange proposed that emotion is literally the perception of bodily change (James, 1884; Lange, 1885 Lange, /1912)). Contemporary work on interoceptive accuracy is consistent with this view: people who detect their heartbeat more accurately have been reported to rate emotional pictures as more arousing, but not as more pleasant or unpleasant (Pollatos et al., 2007), and their heart-rate reactions track their arousal ratings more closely (Dunn et al., 2010).

By contrast, the constructivist position holds that arousal shapes affect without conscious access to the physiological signal. Excitation transfer paradigms show that residual sympathetic activation amplifies emotional responses in proportion to its magnitude even when attributed to an irrelevant source (Zillmann, 1983), and subliminal threat paradigms show that skin conductance scales with threat level prior to conscious recognition (Öhman & Mineka, 2001). Contemporary accounts propose that bodily signals shape affect continuously and before awareness, which follows as a product rather than a prerequisite (LeDoux & Brown, 2017; Seth, 2013; Winkielman & Berridge, 2004). From this view, arousal magnitude transfers to subjective experience regardless of whether a physiological change crosses the detection threshold.

The moderate position allows arousal to transfer without awareness of its source, but still requires detection of some undifferentiated activation before it can be labeled. Schachter and Singer (1962) proposed that emotion requires two components: physiological arousal and a cognitive label borrowed from context. The source of arousal can be misattributed, but it must reach conscious experience to be labeled as emotion (Dutton & Aron, 1974). In Barrett's contemporary account, interoceptive predictions give rise to conscious core affect, which acquires its emotional character through contextual labeling (Barrett & Simmons, 2015). The moderate position predicts that detection amplifies rather than enables feeling: undetected physiological change may impact subjective arousal, but only noticed changes carry magnitude information.

The three positions have rarely been tested directly, because few paradigms compare noticed and missed changes of the same size in a physiological signal within person and session. Individual-differences approaches comparing people high versus low in interoceptive accuracy establish between-person associations but cannot produce within-person variation in awareness for the same physiological event (Dunn et al., 2010; Murphy et al., 2020).

The Breath Change Awareness Task (BCAT) was built to compare noticed and missed changes of the same size within person. We report five studies, in which participants breathed with a pacing circle whose rate changed, abruptly or gradually, or stayed the same. After each trial, they reported whether the pace had changed, then rated their confidence and arousal. Because change size is set by the design while detection varies from trial to trial, each participant contributes both correctly noticed changes and missed ones, with unchanged trials as a baseline. In Studies 4 and 5, a second group followed the pacer by vision alone, without breathing along; in Study 5, a breathing belt recorded respiration.

Although the pacing stimulus is visual, the BCAT targets the estimate of one's own breathing. Exteroceptive and interoceptive signals routinely co-determine bodily self-models in everyday life (Quigley et al., 2021). An inference is interoceptive because of its target, here one's current breathing rate, not because it relies only on signals from inside the body (Schoeller et al., 2025). Breathing along with the pacer adds an interoceptive source, the felt breath, to the visual one.

The BCAT can compare noticed and missed changes of a given size only if four things hold. First, detection should rise with a change's size, so that changes near threshold are sometimes noticed and sometimes missed. Second, abrupt changes should be detected at smaller sizes than gradual ones (preregistered as Study 5 H2), and more often at a fixed size (H5). Third, breathing should follow the pacer on missed as well as noticed trials, so that a missed change is still a breathed change. Fourth, participants who use vision alone should barely breathe along, so that the vision-alone condition removes the bodily change while keeping the visual one.

All three theoretical positions predict that a noticed change should move arousal in its direction, scaled with its size beyond the reported direction (among trials with the same report, larger changes should move arousal more). Method Overview

The BCAT was refined across studies, so the five studies used different versions of the task (Table 1). Study 5 is the most recent version. Each earlier study had a limitation that a later design removed: Studies 1 and 2 ran online with one staircase for both directions, set in breathing rate, so the same step was a larger proportional change in pulse duration for slowing than for speeding, which made slowing far easier to detect; Study 3 delivered changes that differed from those intended (see Study 3, below); and Study 4 ran each direction in its own block and could not verify breathing. In every version, a circle expanded (inhale) and contracted (exhale) for equal durations, and a change in pace lengthened or shortened both phases equally. One pulse is one full expansion and contraction of the circle, that is, one inhale and one exhale (Figure 1). Here the pulse lengthens by 50% (Change = +0.5) from the 4-s baseline, either abruptly between pulses 2 and 3 (high salience, orange) or gradually across pulses 2-4 (low salience, blue). They breathed along with the circle (in the Visual condition, they followed it by vision alone), then reported whether the pace had slowed, stayed the same or sped up, and rated their confidence ("How confident are you in that judgment?") and arousal ("How awake do you feel right now?") on pictorial 1-6 scales. (C) Session structure; task blocks are drawn to scale, heartbeat counting and questionnaires are not. Study 4: four direction × salience staircase runs of 10 trials, then 8 test trials at fixed change sizes, before which all participants were asked to breathe along. Study 5: Block 1 (Breath or Visual), heartbeat counting and questionnaires, then Block 2 (Breath) if time allowed. Studies 1-2 used eight pulses from a 5-s baseline; Study 3 was designed with 6-s pulses (10/min) but delivered 4.8-s pulses (12.5/min) because of a programming error (Table 1).

Studies 1 and 2 ran online in jsPsych (De Leeuw, 2015). The change unfolded as a gradual geometric ramp in breathing rate, and participants also tracked the circle with arrow keys. The up-down staircase made the next change smaller after two correct responses and larger after each error, and the step size halved every two reversals.

Studies 3-5 ran in PsychoPy (Peirce et al., 2019). Study 3 introduced the salience manipulation (an abrupt step vs. a gradual ramp). In Studies 4 and 5, high-salience changes stepped between pulses 2 and 3 and lowsalience changes ramped across pulses 2-4, and separate QUEST staircases (Watson & Pelli, 1983; 75% correct target) were run for each salience × direction cell, with 20% no-change trials (staircase tracks in Figure S2).

Unit of change.

Change is the signed proportional change across the trial: in pulse duration from the first to the last pulse (final / first -1) in Studies 3-5, and in breathing rate in Studies 1-2, signed so that positive values are slowings in every study. Negative values are speedings; a change of -0.2 is a 20% speeding, and a change of +1.0 doubles the pulse duration. Typical changes were a third to a half of a unit (SD 0.37-0.48 across studies).

Instructions and ratings. In all studies, participants were asked to breathe along with a circle that expanded and contracted (Study 5: "Please breathe along with the circle. Breathe in as the circle gets larger, and breathe out as the circle gets smaller. Try to notice if your breathing speed is changing."). Participants in the Visual (vision-alone) condition were instead told: "Please watch the circle. Notice as the circle gets larger, and notice as the circle gets smaller. Try to notice if the circle speed is changing." After each trial, participants reported whether the pace had slowed down, stayed the same, or sped up (a three-alternative forced choice), then rated confidence and arousal. Arousal was rated as "How much arousal do you feel in your body right now?" (0-50 slider; Studies 1-2), on the Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM; 1-9; Bradley & Lang, 1994; Study 3), or as "How awake do you feel right now?" (six-point pictorial scale; Studies 4-5).

Sample size

Study 1's preregistered target of 180 Prolific participants came from a simulation giving 97% power, at a Bonferroni-corrected α of .05/7, for the Change × Accuracy interaction on arousal. Studies 2-4 were each planned for about 100-120 participants, from a priori power analyses for moderate correlations between BCAT performance and individual-difference measures (90% power); those analyses did not target the arousal effects reported here. Study 5's preregistered target was 125 participants, from simulations (simr; Green & MacLeod, 2016) using Study 4 as pilot data (α = .05/8; the weakest preregistered test reached 86% power), or 147 allowing for 15% attrition. Because many students arrived late and could not complete the full session, and recording physiological and cognitive-task data carried further data-quality risks, we expected attrition of up to 30%. We therefore treated 147 as a minimum and recruited until the end of the recruitment window, which yielded 206 included participants. To show what each study could detect, we computed the smallest Change × Accuracy interaction detectable with 80% power (two-sided α = .05) from the standard error of the interaction in a power model fitted to each study (Table S1). The observed interaction was 1.5-2.6 times this size in Studies 1, 2, 4 and 5, and 0.9 times it in Study 3, which was therefore underpowered for an effect of its observed size.

Study-specific procedures

Full procedures are in Section S17.

Studies 1A and 1B (online, Prolific; N = 181). Participants completed two BCAT versions in a single session (about 45 min), in counterbalanced order. Study 1A used an 8-pulse trial with the up-down staircase; the threshold was the average change over the last 4 trials. In Study 1B the change ramped continuously and participants pressed a key as soon as they noticed it; the threshold was the change at the key press, so its trials have no fixed size and no slope, and it appears in the arousal cells only (Table 2; Figure S3). No-change trials made up about a third of trials.

Study 2 (online, Prolific; N = 166 of 199 who began). It used a single 25-trial staircase without salience manipulation or direction separation, designed to identify the minimum number of trials for staircase convergence; the threshold was the average change over the last 4 trials. No-change trials made up about a third of trials. Thirty-two participants who did not finish the session were excluded.

Study 3 (laboratory, undergraduate pool; N = 103). It used a fixed-size within-person design with two runs of 16 trials, combining high-and low-salience changes. After each trial, participants rated the attractiveness (0-10) of four faces from the Chicago Face Database (Ma et al., 2015), shown in random order: two of the 32 faces rated most attractive in the database's norms and two of the next 32, of a gender set by the participant's reported gender and sexual orientation (Section S17.3). Confidence, arousal and valence were rated on 1-9 scales; arousal and valence used the SAM. The task ran under Python 2.7, whose integer division altered the timing the script computed, so the pacer delivered changes that departed from the design and from the logged values (Section S2). We reconstructed the delivered change on every trial from the task code and the trial order, scored detection against the delivered direction, and analyzed the delivered change throughout; participants' reports follow the delivered rather than the intended change. The smallest nominal speeding delivered no change, which gives Study 3 a no-change baseline. Because most gradual trials delivered an abrupt slowing, salience cannot be tested in Study 3.

Study 4 (online, undergraduate pool; N = 131). Study 4 introduced the QUEST staircase, a shorter 4-pulse trial from a 4-s starting pulse, and the Visual condition. Four conditions (high/low salience × speeding/slowing) each comprised 10 trials, with about 20% no-change trials interleaved, run one at a time as four blocks in random order. A test block of 8 trials (2 per condition) followed, at the mean of each participant's high-and low-salience thresholds for each direction. Condition was randomized in the online survey, which gave the breathe-along or vision-alone instruction and then opened the task. To continue, participants had to select the description matching their instruction. Nine participants restarted the survey and were randomized to the other condition on one attempt; all are analyzed by the condition of the attempt that opened the task (Breath 63, Visual 68). During the 40 staircase trials, the on-screen text was neutral and identical for both groups ("Please try to sense if things are getting faster, slower, or staying the same speed").

The screen introducing the test block asked all participants to "begin breathing along with the circle again", so Study 4's condition comparisons use the staircase trials only. The staircase was run on the proportional change with no lower bound, so after a run of correct responses it could propose a level below zero, which reversed the delivered direction; these 22 trials were excluded. Breathing was not recorded in Study 4.

Study 5 (laboratory, undergraduate pool; N = 206). The preregistered study replicated Study 4 within a single laboratory visit, with the four staircases interleaved. Block 1 (Breath or Visual) was followed by a heartbeatcounting task, questionnaires and Block 2, in which all participants breathed along. Condition order was assigned by approximately alternating sign-up slots (Breath-first 95, Visual-first 111). Because each trial lasted at least four breaths, the design limited per-trial ratings to detection, confidence and arousal, to maximize the number of trials in a one-hour session; valence and arousal were also rated at the start of each block. Of the 206 participants, 157 completed both blocks; the other 49 arrived late and completed Block 1 only. The preregistration excluded participants who did not complete every measure; we kept these 49, a deviation (Table S8b, row 14), and the end of the Results reports the preregistered tests under the preregistered rule.

Heartbeat counting. After Block 1, participants completed a heartbeat-counting task (Schandry, 1981). After a 5-s "get ready" screen, the instruction "Count your heartbeats" appeared for 25, 35 and 55 s (one interval each, random order). Participants silently counted the heartbeats they could feel, without taking their pulse, and then typed their count. No feedback was given. Accuracy per interval was 1 -|actual -reported| / actual (floored at 0), using heartbeats detected in the electrocardiogram (ECG), averaged across intervals.

Physiological recording.

Respiration (breathing belt) and the ECG were recorded with a BIOPAC MP160 and BioNomadix wireless transducers (BIOPAC Systems, Goleta, CA; block-onset alignment: Section S3.2). Breathing-belt signals were processed with the respkit R package (version 0.2.0; Farb, 2026). Synchrony is the correlation between the breathing-belt signal and the pacer over a trial. Breathing analyses used the 145 participants whose breathing belt followed the pacer in a breathing block (median synchrony ≥ .4). Breathing change on each trial was measured from the phase of the breathing-belt signal and compared against a shuffled-schedule chance level; the phase gain is the slope of breathing change on the pacer's change, 1 if breathing followed it fully and 0 if not at all (Section S3).

Online data quality

Studies 1, 2 and 4 were run online; Studies 3 and 5 were run in person. In Studies 1 and 2 (Prolific, one submission per account), procedures were in place that screen out automated responding, although they were designed to check task compliance. Each session opened with four instructed-response items (e.g., "Please select 'Somewhat Agree'"), and any wrong answer ended it. On every trial participants pressed the arrow keys in time with the expanding and contracting circle, whose rate changed within the trial by an amount they could not predict. Tracking accuracy was scored on every frame against the circle's phase, and a practice trial had to be tracked with at least 80% accuracy before the task could begin. In Study 1, as preregistered, trials tracked below 70% were excluded (21 of 2,715), as were participants with more than three such trials (none). Median tracking accuracy was 90% (Study 1) and 91% (Study 2). These procedures exclude auto-clickers, scripted key sequences and form-fillers that do not follow the display. They cannot exclude an AI agent that reads the screen, and the on-screen prompt named the required key. Nor can they show whether the questionnaires, all closed-format rating scales, were completed with AI assistance. The 70% and 80% thresholds are heuristic. Prevention and detection trade off here: the practice gate stops a respondent who does not follow the display before any data are collected, whereas the trial exclusions remove lapses afterwards and depend on where the threshold is set.

We used no CAPTCHA or IP filtering; every participant signed up through a registered account, on Prolific (Studies 1-2) or the university's Sona participant pool (Study 4). In Study 4, we used no procedure specifically designed to prevent or detect automated or AI-generated responses. Participants came from a closed pool: undergraduates enrolled for course credit through the department's participant-pool system. Every participant's task data matched their pool record and questionnaire, and for the pool accounts that submitted the questionnaire more than once, the most complete response was kept (18 accounts). This does not exclude an enrolled student using automation or an AI assistant, and task completion times were not logged.

Exclusions and measures

Participants who gave the same arousal rating on every trial were excluded in Studies 1, 2 and 5 (3, 1 and 3 participants); Studies 3 and 4 had no such rule and kept them, except in Study 4's test-block analyses (Section S17.4). Study 5 also excluded one participant whose detection was at chance (Table S8b, row 10). Section S16 repeats the main analyses under one set of data-quality rules. Participants also completed questionnaires that are not analyzed here; Table S4a lists each with its full name, reference and the studies that included it. Scores on the Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness are reported separately (Farb et al., in preparation).

Analytic approach

Trial-level ratings were analyzed with linear mixed-effects models (lme4, Bates et al., 2015; lmerTest, Kuznetsova et al., 2017), fitted by restricted maximum likelihood with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Accuracy (noticed = 1, missed = 0) was 1 when the three-choice report matched the delivered direction and 0 otherwise. Preregistered tests (Studies 1 and 5; Table S17) are reported with their preregistered models and data, and deviations are listed in Tables S8a-b. Most other models included a random intercept and a random Change slope for each participant; preregistered tests were refitted with this structure as a robustness check.

Arousal models in Studies 1-4 also included a Change² term, which lets the slope differ between speeding and slowing and which Study 5's preregistered models omit; the models behind Table 2's noticed-speeding and noticed-slowing cells and the missed shares used a random intercept only and maximum likelihood (Section S18.4). Effects are reported in rating-scale points per unit change, or as percentages of the rating scale, with 95% CIs. Size effects on noticed trials are reported per unit of log rate ratio, which treats speeding and slowing of equal proportional rate change alike; estimates in pulse-duration units are in Table S5a. Where we report a share of the noticed response carried by missed changes, it is adjusted for arousal ratings on the two preceding trials, because arousal before a trial predicted which changes were detected.

Bayes factors compared models with and without the term of interest. They were fitted in brms (Bürkner, 2017) with a Cauchy(0, 0.707) prior on standardized fixed effects and half-Cauchy(0, 1) priors on standard deviations, and computed by bridge sampling on 40,000 posterior draws (median of 10 seeded repetitions). Person-level comparisons used Jeffreys-Zellner-Siow (JZS) t tests (r = 0.707), and correlations JZS correlation tests (r = 1/3; Rouder et al., 2009). We report BF10 for evidence for an effect and BF01 = 1/BF10 for evidence against it, after the estimate and its CI. Study 5's preregistration lists nine tests of six hypotheses. Seven were frequentist tests, corrected together with the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure as preregistered (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995); the predicted null for interoceptive awareness (H3B) was to be evaluated by a Bayes factor, and test-retest reliability (H6) against a criterion of r ≥ .70. The two hypotheses about interoceptive awareness (H3A-B) are reported separately (Farb et al., in preparation). Study 1's preregistered tests are evaluated against its preregistered Bonferroni α of .05/7. All other analyses were exploratory, including those of Study 5 outside the Preregistered tests section, and no correction was applied to them.

Results

Task validation

All four requirements set out in the Introduction held. Detection rose with the size of the change in either direction in every study (Figure 2; Change² term, all ps < .001; odds ratios in Table S13b). Abrupt changes were detected at smaller sizes than gradual ones in Study 4 (b = -0.061, p = .003) and Study 5 (preregistered H2; Table S17), and in the test blocks, at a fixed size, abrupt changes were detected more often (Study 4: b = 0.72 logits, p = .002; Study 5, preregistered H5; Table S17). In Study 5, breathing followed the pacer at least as closely on missed trials as on noticed ones (phase gain .66 [.62, .70] vs. .56 [.53, .58]; 145 participants with a verified breathing belt).

Participants who used vision alone barely breathed along: median synchrony with the pacer was .07, against .82 among Breath-first participants in Block 1, and their breathing followed the pacer's changes about a fifth as much (phase gain .12 [.04, .19], against .03 expected by chance and .55 among Breath-first participants in Block 1; 34 vision-alone participants with a verified breathing belt). Heartbeat-counting accuracy was unrelated to the breathing-change threshold in Study 5 (r = -.02 [-.18, .15], n = 138; BF01 = 5.0).

Noticing moved arousal

A noticed change moved arousal in its direction (Table 2; Figure S3; model-predicted curves in Figure S1). Noticed speeding raised arousal in every study and group, by 4-10% of the rating scale.

Noticed slowing lowered it by 2-9% in Studies 1A, 1B, 2 and 3 and in Study 5's breathing group, but not reliably in Study 4 or when Study 5 participants used vision alone. The change-arousal relation was stronger on noticed than on missed trials in every study (the noticing effect; Figure 3A; Change × Accuracy: p = .013 in Study 3, p < .001 in the others; Table S10). The noticing effect was present when participants used vision alone. It was about three-quarters as large as when breathing in Study 4 and in Study 5 (77% [26, 174] and 72% [18, 175]; by-group differences in the Table S10 note), and not detectably smaller (BF01 = 20 in each study at the default prior, and at least 2.0 at the narrowest prior scale; Table S14b).

Change size moved arousal beyond the report

On noticed trials, arousal scaled with the size of the change after the reported direction, confidence and trial position were controlled, in Study 1A, in Study 4's breathing group and in both of Study 5's groups (Table S5a): in Study 5, 63% [17, 107] of the noticed-trial size slope survived adjustment for the reported direction and confidence when participants breathed, and all of it (110% [54, 162]) when they used vision alone. It was not detectable in Study 2, Study 3 or Study 4's vision-alone group. These estimates depend on the unit in which change size is expressed: measured in pulse-duration units instead of log rate ratio, Study 5's breathing group no longer reliably scaled beyond the report (59% [-2, 110]), while the other sets kept their pattern. In Study 5, measured breathing added nothing detectable beyond the presented change on noticed trials (+0.03 points per SD of breathing change [-0.00, 0.07]).

What breathing added

In Study 5, where breathing was verified, a noticed change moved arousal 1.78 [1.26, 2.75] times as much when breathed as with vision alone, at the same report and change size (Block 1, between groups; BF10 = 1.4 at the default prior, rising to 9.6 at the narrowest prior scale; Table S14b). This came chiefly from slowing. Exploratory comparisons against unchanged trials reported "same" showed that noticed slowing lowered arousal more when breathed than with vision alone (Breath -Visual: -0.16 [-0.28, -0.04] points, 3.2% of the scale; adjusted for the two preceding arousal ratings, -0.19 [-0.29, -0.10]), whereas noticed speeding did not differ (0.02 [-0.09, 0.14]). In Study 4 the ratio pointed the same way, and its interval included both a null effect and Study 5's estimate (1.30 [0.76, 2.44]; BF01 = 13 at the default prior, 1.6 at the narrowest prior scale; Table S14b); noticed slowing did not differ between groups (-0.01 [-0.16, 0.15]), and noticed speeding tended to raise arousal more when breathed (0.15 [-0.01, 0.30], p = .060). Breathing and noticing did not interact reliably in Study 5 (-0.03 [-0.23, 0.17]). Visual-first participants rated arousal higher overall in Block 1 (Visual -Breath, 0.27 points [0.01, 0.54]), a difference in level rather than in slope.

Heart rate did not track arousal

Trial-level heart rate could not serve as an independent index of arousal. In breathing blocks, heart rate followed the pacing change, but in the opposite direction to rated arousal: it was higher after slowed than after sped-up pacing (0.86 beats per minute, bpm, per unit change [0.54, 1.19]). When participants used vision alone, heart rate followed noticed changes (1.26 bpm per unit change [0.59, 1.94]) but not missed ones (-1.19 [-2.47, 0.10]), again in the opposite direction to the ratings. It did not predict arousal ratings, either on its own or with the pacing change and the participant's measured breathing in the model (within person, -0.01 points per SD of heart-rate change [-0.03, 0.01]). Adding heart rate and measured breathing left the difference between noticed and missed slopes unchanged in the breathing blocks (Section S6). With vision alone the check is inconclusive: physiology could be aligned to trials for only 51 of the 111 participants, and in them the noticed-missed difference was near zero (-0.05 [-0.22, 0.12]) rather than the full group's -0.22 [-0.39, -0.06], so whether physiology accounts for the vision-alone effect is unknown (Section S6.3).

Missed changes moved arousal less

Missed changes moved arousal less than noticed ones (Table 2; Figure 3B): per unit of change, the missed slope was 31% [7, 53] of the noticed slope in Study 5's breathing blocks, and 21% to 53% in the other studies and groups, with 72% in Study 3, whose missed changes clustered at one size (Table 2 note). Bayes factors for a missed direction effect were mixed, supporting the effect in Studies 1A and 2 and in Study 4's breathing group (BF₁₀ > 300), equivocal in Study 4's vision-alone group (BF₁₀ = 0.99), and favoring the null in Study 3 (BF₀₁ = 2.7) and in Study 5 (BF₀₁ = 4.1 breathing, 15 vision alone; Table S14a; at the narrowest prior scale the Study 5 breathing-block value is BF₁₀ = 1.7, Table S14b). In Studies 1A and 2, this effect came from slowing: missed slowings lowered arousal (BF₁₀ > 400 against unchanged trials). For missed speedings, the evidence favored no change from unchanged trials in every study and group (BF₀₁ = 3.7 to 30; Table S14a; Figure S3). Because missed changes were generally smaller than noticed ones, they carried a smaller share of the noticed direction effect: allowing for arousal on the two preceding trials, 15% [2, 30] in Study 5's breathing blocks and 13% to 29% in Studies 1A-3 and 20% with vision alone (Table S9a; Study 4's blocked design prevents an unbiased estimate, Section S7).

In Study 5, which fully interleaved trial types, missed breathed changes moved arousal somewhat more than missed changes followed by vision alone, but not reliably (-0.19 [-0.38, 0.01], p = .062). Whether arousal scaled with the size of a missed change, as the constructivist but not the moderate position predicts, is unclear.

It did in Study 1A and tended to in Study 2, the two studies with a single up-down staircase, in which each miss enlarged the next change, so a missed change's size partly tracked the participant's recent performance. It did not in Studies 4-5, whose QUEST staircases set each change from the whole response history, and Study 3, whose sizes were fixed, was too imprecise to tell (Section S7.4).

Noticed changes, arousal and attractiveness (Study 3, exploratory)

On noticed trials, delivered changes moved arousal (-1.10 SAM points per unit change [-1.34, -0.85]; random intercept only, with the Change² term; Section S2.5), and trial-to-trial arousal went with attractiveness ratings (0.087 points on the 0-10 scale per SAM point [0.037, 0.138]), so the indirect effect of the change on attractiveness through arousal was reliable (-0.096 [-0.152, -0.039] per unit change). The total effect of the change on attractiveness was not detectable (-0.03 [-0.15, 0.10]). On missed trials, arousal went with attractiveness about as strongly (0.063 [0.003, 0.124]), but missed changes moved arousal less, so their indirect effect was 19% [-3, 55] of the noticed-trial one (the ratio of the two indirect effects; Section S2.5). In Study 3, the only study that recorded valence after every trial, noticed changes also moved valence (speeding felt more pleasant: -0.44 SAM points per unit change [-0.55, -0.33]), but whether this differed from missed changes was inconclusive (Change × Accuracy, p = .085; Section S2.4).

Discussion

What participants noticed moved them. Across five studies, a noticed speeding raised arousal and a noticed slowing lowered it, and the same changes, when missed, moved arousal up to a third as steeply in the final version of the task (Table 2). Speeding was the more consistent of the two. Breathing was not required: with vision alone, the noticing effect was about three-quarters as large, with wide intervals.

None of the three positions survives as stated. The constitutive position says a missed change is inert; it was not, though in Studies 3 and 5 the Bayes factors favored no missed direction effect (Table S14a). The constructivist position says a missed change carries the whole response; it carried only a fraction, at best. The moderate position gets the fraction right but assigns it to bodily change, and noticing mattered even when the change was seen but not breathed.

Noticed changes also carried their size. The larger a noticed change, the more it moved arousal, with the reported direction and the participant's confidence held constant. A rating that merely echoed the report could not do this: the report said which way the pace had changed, not by how much. The scaling was clearest in Study 5, came mainly from speeding (Table S5b), and followed the change as presented rather than the breathing it produced. Read together, the two effects say that most of the arousal response arrived with noticing, and that the size of the change then set how large it was; in Study 3, noticed changes also reached attractiveness ratings through arousal.

Felt arousal may draw on whatever signals a change in one's breathing: the seen pacer and, when breathing along, the felt breath, an interoceptive source (Quigley et al., 2021). Noticing mattered regardless of which source carried the change, and breathing added to the response. In Study 5, where breathing was verified, a noticed breathed change moved arousal about 1.8 times as much as an equally large change followed by vision alone (BF10 = 1.4). Breathing added about as much to missed changes (interaction -0.03 [-0.23, 0.17]), though that addition was not itself reliable, so whether the interoceptive addition needs noticing is open.

If noticing drives what moves us, attention habits matter. Speculatively, heightened attention to the body, as in panic disorder (Paulus & Stein, 2010), might raise felt arousal by bringing mild bodily changes to awareness. What would matter is a habit of noticing rather than greater sensitivity. Consistent with this, in a large community sample, mental-health symptoms tracked self-reported interoceptive sensibility but not measured interoceptive performance (Banellis et al., 2026).

Limitations and constraints on generality

Our only measure of felt arousal is a rating given after the detection judgment. A rating that follows the report, or an appraisal of the noticed change, can therefore produce the noticing effect; the size effect rules out only a restated report. Whether noticing changed how aroused participants felt, or only how they rated it, is open. Influence may also have run the other way, with felt activation serving as evidence for the direction judgment: on unchanged trials, arousal was higher after "faster" than after "slower" reports in Study 4's breathing group (0.37 points [0.14, 0.59]) but not in Study 5 (Block 1, -0.02 [-0.28, 0.23]). Part of the noticed-missed difference may thus reflect which trials were judged as changed rather than what noticing added. On misses reported in the wrong direction, arousal leaned toward the report rather than the change, unlike misses reported "same" (difference 0.21 [0.02, 0.40]; Section S7.5).

Detection was measured rather than manipulated: the salience manipulation shifted it only modestly, which limits causal inference about the noticing effect. Trial-level heart rate offered no independent check: with vision alone it responded to noticed but not to missed changes, in the direction of an orienting response rather than of the ratings (Section S6). Rating arousal before the detection judgment, or measuring it outside the report, as with pupil size or skin conductance, would test these accounts directly.

Missed trials are not a random subset. Detection depended on arousal before the trial, in opposite directions for slowing and speeding, and Study 4's staircases were blocked by direction. The shares we report allow for the two preceding arousal ratings, but states that those ratings do not capture could still bias the shares either way.

Breathing along also changed how participants breathed. The pacer imposed a 1:1 inhale:exhale rhythm, which participants adopted while breathing with it (the inhale took 47% of each paced breath, against 41% unpaced). Breathing along thus differed from vision alone in rhythm, effort, proprioception and attention to the body as well as in interoceptive input. Any of these could underlie the larger response to breathed changes, but none can explain the noticing or size effects, which appeared without breathing. Because the pacer was always visible, the task never tests breath interoception on its own. The arousal effects were small. A typical noticed speeding (about 5-7 breaths per minute; Table S7a) raised arousal by 5-10% of the rating scale (breathing groups; Table 2), and the median participant used about half of the scale (42-60% across studies). They arose near the detection threshold, from voluntary paced changes, in sessions over which arousal fell slightly, and may not extend to the large respiratory changes of strong emotion.

Test-retest reliability for individual threshold estimates fell short of the preregistered criterion of r ≥ .70 (Preregistered tests; Table S13g). The ten-trial staircases were built to estimate group thresholds, not stable individual differences, and may require more trials to stabilize. All studies drew from convenience samples (Prolific or undergraduate pools); effect magnitudes may not generalize to other populations or to paces outside the range tested.

Conclusion

Across five studies, the arousal response to a pacer change occurred mainly when the change was noticed, and in most studies a noticed change's size moved arousal beyond the reported direction. Against the constitutive position, missed changes still moved arousal, though less steeply and less consistently than noticed ones. Against the constructivist position, noticing mattered, even with vision alone. Breathing along added an interoceptive source: a noticed breathed change moved arousal about 1.8 times as much as one followed by vision alone, although the online Study 4 did not detect a difference. Self-reported arousal mainly follows noticing a change; an interoceptive source adds to it.
