Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Employees often remain in work states that are blocked, harmful, stagnant, damaged, or otherwise unwanted: chronic workload strain, stalled career progression, ignored voice, unresolved conflict, failed change initiatives, or damaged relationships. In these situations, employees are not only appraising whether work is unpleasant, stressful, or controllable; they are also learning whether the state can still move. Building on cue-appraisal learning, this article develops repairability cue meaning as a movement-specific form of conditioned appraisal meaning that explains how recurring organizational cues come to signal whether unwanted work states remain meaningfully movable. Repairability cue meaning captures whether a recurring organizational cue has come to signal movement, stagnation, increasing changeability, or closure in relation to a particular unwanted work state. The theory specifies how recurring cue-workstate histories organize perceived changeability trajectories and proposes a sadness-mediated pathway from learned lost-movement meaning to organizational demoralization. When recurring cues instead teach that movement has disappeared, workplace sadness and organizational demoralization become more likely. Organizational demoralization is defined as a learned affective-motivational occupational state centered on perceived lost movement. The theory explains why renewed engagement can remain viable before full repair, and why withdrawal, silence, redirection, or exit may become understandable when movement disappears.

Learning Whether Work Can Still Move: Repairability Cues, Hope, and Organizational Demoralization

Organizations do not only expose employees to discrete positive or negative events. They also expose employees to recurring work states that remain blocked, stagnant, damaged, or unresolved over time. A workload problem may persist despite repeated requests for relief. A promotion process may remain opaque despite years of effort. A voice channel may repeatedly absorb employee concerns without visible follow-up. A damaged work relationship may remain tense despite attempts at repair. A change initiative may promise improvement but gradually lose credibility. Research on affective events theory and sensemaking shows that employees interpret work events and patterns over time, not merely as isolated stimuli (Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Weick, 1995; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

In these situations, employees are not merely asking whether the current state is unpleasant. They are asking whether the state can still move. This question is theoretically important because a work state may remain difficult while still seeming movable, or it may become demoralizing before it becomes objectively impossible to change. Hope theory emphasizes agency and pathways toward desired goals, while goal-regulation research shows that perceived movement toward valued outcomes matters for motivation and affect (Carver & Scheier, 1998; Locke & Latham, 2002; Snyder, 2002; Snyder et al., 1991). Hope may be sustained or renewed while an unwanted work state remains unresolved. The present theory predicts that credible cues of increasing changeability make this response more likely. Conversely, demoralization becomes more likely when recurring cues indicate that movement is stalled, symbolic, closing, or no longer credible.

Building on cue-appraisal learning, this article develops repairability cue meaning as a movement-specific form of conditioned appraisal meaning. A repairability cue is a recurring organizational cue tied to a blocked, harmful, stagnant, damaged, or unwanted work state that provides evidence about whether that state remains meaningfully movable. The language of cues draws on sensemaking, conditioning, and social-information perspectives, which emphasize that people use environmental and social cues to interpret what is happening and what action is appropriate or possible (Bouton, 2004; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick, 1995). A repairability cue meaning is a movement-specific conditioned appraisal meaning through which a recurring cue comes to signal whether the relevant work state, or the employee's relation to it, can still be changed, corrected, restored, improved, compensated for, redirected, or moved. Put simply, repairability cue meaning captures what a recurring cue has taught employees about whether the situation can still move.

The theory is designed for recurring work states in which employees have an opportunity to learn from repeated cue histories. Its focal domain includes situations in which a cue becomes connected to a specific repairability question: Does this workload system respond? Does this promotion process create movement? Does this voice channel keep concerns alive? Does this change initiative produce visible progress? Does this relationship remain workable? This focus allows the theory to examine how employees learn movement meanings from organizational experience rather than treating hope, sadness, or withdrawal as reactions to isolated events alone. This emphasis is consistent with research showing that employee affect and behavior unfold across repeated work events and appraisal cycles (Elfenbein, 2023; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

The central claim is that organizational demoralization develops when recurring repairability cues teach employees that blocked or unwanted work states are no longer meaningfully movable. When cues provide credible evidence of movement or increasing changeability, workplace hope becomes more likely (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). When cues provide recurring evidence of closure, stagnation, non-response, or lost movement, workplace sadness becomes more likely. Over time, sadness may become organized into organizational demoralization: a learned affective-motivational occupational state centered on perceived lost movement. Organizational demoralization may make renewed effort, voice, repair, help-seeking, or persistence feel less viable, and may be expressed through resignation, withdrawal, reduced striving, non-attempt, silence, redirection, or exit. This formulation draws on appraisal theory, learned helplessness and hopelessness theory, and demoralization research while translating those insights into an organizational setting (Abramson et al., 1989; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; de Figueiredo, 1993; Lazarus, 1991; Maier & Seligman, 2016; Seligman, 1975).

The article is positioned as an organizational behavior theory paper about appraisallearning, workplace hope, sadness, and organizational demoralization, with implications for occupational well-being. It draws most directly on appraisal theory, hope theory, learned helplessness and hopelessness research, demoralization research, and organizational research on voice, silence, proactivity, job crafting, coping, and change. Appraisal theory explains how emotions depend on perceived meaning, including goal relevance, control, coping potential, certainty, and future possibility (Lazarus, 1991; Moors et al., 2013; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2009; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985). Hope theory explains future-directed striving through agency and pathways thinking (Snyder, 2002; Snyder et al., 1991). Learned helplessness and hopelessness research explain how repeated uncontrollability and negative future expectations can weaken action (Abramson et al., 1989; Beck et al., 1974; Maier & Seligman, 2016; Seligman, 1975). Demoralization research describes states involving helplessness, hopelessness, loss of morale, subjective incompetence, and inability to cope (Woźniewicz & Cosci, 2023; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; de Figueiredo, 1993; Kissane et al., 2004; Tecuta et al., 2015).

Organizational research on voice, silence, proactivity, job crafting, coping, and change provides work-specific domains in which repairability cue meaning may develop (Armenakis & Bedeian, 1999; Carver et al., 1989; Folkman & Lazarus, 1980; Khaw et al., 2023; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023;; Parker et al., 2010; Pinder & Harlos, 2001; Silapurem et al., 2024; Tims & Bakker, 2010; Van Dyne et al., 2003; Wanberg & Banas, 2000; Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001).

The theory builds on established accounts of appraisal, learning, and goal regulation.

Appraisal theory recognizes that stimuli can activate previously learned appraisal outputs, including differentiated appraisal patterns (Moors et al., 2013). Cue-appraisal learning specifies how recurring organizational cues acquire appraisal meanings and how these meanings transfer and change across encounters (Umbra, 2026). The present theory develops this organizational account around a particular relation: what a recurring cue has come to signify about the changeability of a specific unwanted work state.

The contribution concerns the organization and consequences of this learned meaning.

First, the theory specifies the cue-work-state pairing as the focal relation through which employees learn whether a particular difficulty remains changeable. Second, it distinguishes an unresolved state from its perceived changeability trajectory, explaining how organizational evidence can signal improving prospects, persistent stagnation, or closure while the problem itself remains unresolved. Third, it proposes a longitudinal pathway through which learned lostmovement meaning contributes to sadness and subsequent organizational demoralization.

Building on hope and goal-regulation perspectives, the theory connects these processes to renewed engagement, withdrawal, and redirection under conditions that shape whether such responses can be enacted (Carver & Scheier, 1998; Lazarus, 1991; Snyder, 2002).

Theoretical Foundations Hope, Appraisal, and the Question of Movement

Hope theory conceptualizes hope as a goal-directed cognitive-motivational system composed of agency and pathways thinking (Snyder, 2002; Snyder et al., 1991). Agency reflects the perceived capacity to initiate and sustain movement toward goals, whereas pathways reflect perceived routes for reaching those goals. Organizational research has incorporated hope into psychological capital, where hope is treated as a positive state-like resource associated with work attitudes, behavior, and performance (Avey et al., 2011; Luthans et al., 2007; Luthans & Youssef-Morgan, 2017).

This literature is crucial because it frames hope as future-directed movement rather than simple positive affect (Edwards et al., 2025). Employees may be hopeful about a particular workload problem, promotion pathway, voice channel, damaged relationship, change initiative, or role transition. Repairability cue meaning specifies how employees learn whether agency and pathways remain credible in relation to a recurring unwanted work state. A promotion process may support hope if it provides transparent criteria, developmental milestones, and observable progress. The same process may undermine hope if it repeatedly produces silence, shifting standards, and non-response. This distinction is consistent with goal-regulation research showing that perceived progress and viable pathways sustain motivation, while blocked goals and low expectancy undermine striving (Carver & Scheier, 1998; Locke & Latham, 2002; Vroom, 1964).

Appraisal theory explains how emotional responses depend on the meaning individuals assign to events and situations, including goal relevance, goal congruence, control, coping potential, certainty, and future possibility (Lazarus, 1991; Moors et al., 2013; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2009; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985). Organizational emotion research similarly emphasizes that work events produce emotions through interpretive processes and that emotions shape subsequent attitudes, behaviors, and interpersonal dynamics (Ashkanasy & Dorris, 2017; Elfenbein, 2023; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). Repairability cue meaning builds on this tradition by identifying how recurring organizational cues become associated with movement or lost movement across time. It is best understood as a learned cue-specific configuration of appraisals organized around the question of movement.

Perceived control asks whether one can influence an outcome (Skinner, 1996). Selfefficacy refers to beliefs about one's capacity to organize and execute actions needed to produce desired outcomes (Bandura, 1977 (Bandura, , 1997)). Coping potential asks whether one has resources to manage the situation (Folkman & Lazarus, 1980; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Future expectancy asks what is likely to happen or whether effort is likely to lead to outcomes (Lazarus, 1991; Scherer, 2009; Vroom, 1964). Repairability cue meaning asks what a recurring cue has come to predict about whether a specific unwanted work state, or the employee's relation to it, can still move. Its added value lies in level, learning, and trajectory: it is cue-specific, develops over repeated encounters, and distinguishes current non-resolution from increasing or decreasing changeability. Bandura's (1977) distinction between efficacy and outcome expectations allows confidence in one's ability to act to coexist with doubts about the consequences. The present theory draws on this distinction to examine how recurring organizational cues organize expectations about the changeability of particular unwanted work states. Employees may perceive themselves as capable of taking action while learning that relevant organizational actors or processes are unlikely to respond.

Forms of Movement

"Movement" is used as the umbrella term in this theory, but movement can take different forms. The categories below are descriptive labels for forms of repairability evidence rather than established constructs in prior literature. They clarify the boundary of repairability cue meaning and help explain why hope may return before full repair. Their logic is consistent with research on goal progress, coping, job redesign, voice, and exit, all of which treat viable action possibilities as broader than direct problem elimination (Carver et al., 1989; Folkman & Lazarus, 1980; Hirschman, 1970; Snyder, 2002; Tims & Bakker, 2010; Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001). These forms share a common function: they provide credible evidence that the employee is not simply trapped in the same unwanted state. From this perspective, generic reassurance, symbolic communication, reframing without changed options, positivity without changed cue evidence, or promises without capacity for follow-through provide weaker repairability evidence because they do not alter what the cue has taught employees about movement. This distinction parallels organizational-change and psychological-contract research showing that employee reactions depend not only on messages or promises but also on credibility, implementation, participation, fulfillment, and visible progress (Armenakis & Bedeian, 1999; Oreg et al., 2011; Rafferty & Griffin, 2006; Robinson & Rousseau, 1994; Rousseau, 1995; Wanberg & Banas, 2000).

The employee does not merely dislike the current state; the employee experiences the state as closed, stagnant, or no longer meaningfully repairable. Sadness is therefore treated as an emotionally coherent response to perceived lost movement. This claim builds on appraisal theories that link discrete emotions to patterns of goal loss, low control, and low coping potential (Lazarus, 1991; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2009; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985).

Sadness is the focal negative emotion in this theory because it is most directly tied to loss, closure, relinquishment, and diminished future possibility. Stalled movement may also evoke anger when attributed to injustice, obstruction, or mistreatment; anxiety when the future remains uncertain or threatening; shame when the employee interprets failed movement as personal inadequacy; and cynicism when repeated stagnation is attributed to bad faith, hypocrisy, or broken promises (Lazarus, 1991; Reichers et al., 1997; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2009; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985). The theory expects sadness to be most central when employees interpret stalled or lost movement as the loss or closure of a valued future; other emotions may dominate when employees interpret the same pattern primarily as injustice, threat, personal failure, or bad faith (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025).

Learned helplessness research shows that when outcomes are experienced as uncontrollable, later action may weaken even in situations where action might become possible (Maier & Seligman, 2016; Seligman, 1975). Hopelessness theory similarly emphasizes negative expectations about future outcomes and one's capacity to change them (Abramson et al., 1989; Beck et al., 1974). Repairability cue meaning extends these insights into recurring organizational cue histories. Its focus is cue-specific rather than necessarily global; it concerns perceived movability of a recurring work state, not only controllability of outcomes; and it may arise from organizational non-response, symbolic action, shifting criteria, broken repair attempts, or stalled change.

Demoralization research provides a further bridge. Clinical and existential literatures describe demoralization as involving helplessness, hopelessness, loss of meaning, subjective incompetence, and perceived inability to cope (Woźniewicz & Cosci, 2023; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; de Figueiredo, 1993; Kissane et al., 2004; Tecuta et al., 2015). The present article translates this insight into organizational theory while keeping the construct occupationally bounded. Organizational demoralization refers to a learned affective-motivational occupational state centered on perceived lost movement in recurring work conditions. It helps explain why resignation, withdrawal, reduced striving, non-attempt, silence, redirection, or exit may come to feel more viable than renewed effort, voice, repair, help-seeking, or persistence. These responses are behavioral expressions or consequences of demoralization, not its definition.

Work Domains as Repairability Cue Contexts

Organizational research provides concrete domains in which repairability cue meaning may develop. Voice research explains that employees speak up when they believe voice is safe, effective, and worth the interpersonal or political risk, and withhold input when voice seems risky or futile (Detert & Edmondson, 2011; Edmondson, 1999; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023)).

Acquiescent silence is especially relevant because it reflects resignation and low perceived efficacy rather than only fear (Pinder & Harlos, 2001; Van Dyne et al., 2003). From the present perspective, voice channels can become repairability cues when they provide evidence about whether unresolved problems can move.

Job crafting and proactivity research describes employees as active agents who try to change tasks, relationships, resources, and meanings at work (Parker et al., 2010; Tims & Bakker, 2010; Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001). Repeated failed crafting or proactivity attempts may teach employees that a work state is not movable. A person may stop crafting not because they lack initiative in general, but because attempts to move the relevant work state repeatedly fail. Longitudinal job-crafting research further suggests that job crafting unfolds over time rather than as a single static action (Silapurem et al., 2024).

Coping and stress literatures distinguish problem-focused coping, emotion-focused coping, acceptance, and withdrawal when change appears unavailable (Carver et al., 1989; Folkman & Lazarus, 1980; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Repairability cue meaning helps explain why employees may shift among these responses over time. As cues come to signal movement, problem-focused coping or renewed engagement may remain viable. As cues come to signal lost movement, acceptance, withdrawal, or redirection may become more viable.

Organizational change research similarly fits this theory. Employees may initially respond to change initiatives with hope if they signal movement from an unwanted state toward a better one. Repeated symbolic change, delayed implementation, change fatigue, or broken promises may convert change cues into signs of stagnation (Armenakis & Bedeian, 1999; Khaw et al., 2023; Oreg et al., 2011; Rafferty & Griffin, 2006; Reichers et al., 1997; Robinson & Rousseau, 1994; Rousseau, 1995; Wanberg & Banas, 2000). Organizational demoralization may therefore emerge when change itself comes to signal stalled movement.

Core Constructs

The model contains six focal elements: repairability cue, repairability cue meaning, changeability trajectory, workplace hope, workplace sadness, and organizational demoralization.

The model applies the generalization, discrimination, and corrective relearning processes of cueappraisal learning (Umbra, 2026), while response viability operates as a downstream process.

A repairability cue is a recurring organizational cue tied to a blocked, harmful, stagnant, damaged, or unwanted work state that provides evidence about whether that state, or the employee's relation to it, remains meaningfully movable. The cue may be a person, process, event, relationship, role, condition, or situation when it is connected to a specific repairability question. This construct builds on sensemaking, conditioning, and social-information approaches to organizational cues while specifying a narrower movement-oriented cue meaning (Bouton, 2004; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick, 1995).

Repairability cue meaning refers to a movement-specific conditioned appraisal meaning through which a recurring organizational cue comes to signal whether the relevant work state, or the employee's relation to it, can still be changed, corrected, restored, improved, compensated for, redirected, or moved. Repairability cue meaning has a positive pole, when the cue signals movement or increasing changeability, and a negative pole, when the cue signals closure, stagnation, non-response, or lost movement. This definition applies the broader logic of cueappraisal learning to movement-related appraisal content (Umbra, 2026).

Changeability trajectory refers to perceived movement over time in the repairability of a blocked or unwanted work state (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). The key issue is not only whether the state is currently repaired, but whether it appears increasingly movable, decreasingly movable, or closed. This dynamic emphasis is consistent with goal-regulation, affective-events, and organizational-change literatures (Carver & Scheier, 1998; Oreg et al., 2011; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Workplace hope refers to a prospective emotional-motivational response that becomes more likely when a repairability cue provides credible evidence that a blocked or unwanted work state remains movable or is becoming more movable (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). This definition builds on hope theory and psychological capital research while making hope cuespecific and trajectory-sensitive (Avey et al., 2011; Luthans et al., 2007; Snyder, 2002; Snyder et al., 1991).

Workplace sadness refers to a loss-oriented emotional-motivational response that becomes more likely when a repairability cue provides credible evidence that a blocked or unwanted work state is closed, stuck, non-repairable, or decreasingly movable (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). This definition builds on appraisal theories linking sadness to loss, goal obstruction, low control, and low coping potential (Lazarus, 1991; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2009; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985).

Organizational demoralization refers to a learned affective-motivational occupational state in which recurring work conditions come to feel no longer meaningfully movable. In this state, renewed effort, voice, repair, help-seeking, or persistence may feel less viable, and resignation, withdrawal, reduced striving, non-attempt, silence, redirection, or exit may become more likely as behavioral expressions or consequences. This definition draws from clinical demoralization research, learned helplessness and hopelessness, and organizational silence/withdrawal literatures while defining the construct at the occupational level (Abramson et al., 1989; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; de Figueiredo, 1993; Maier & Seligman, 2016; Morrison, 2023; Pinder & Harlos, 2001).

Construct Hierarchy

The constructs operate at four levels. First, cue meaning concerns what the recurring cue predicts about movement or stagnation. Second, emotion concerns how perceived movement or lost movement is affectively experienced, especially as hope or sadness. Third, affectivemotivational orientation concerns whether repeated lost movement becomes organized into organizational demoralization or whether credible movement sustains renewed engagement.

Fourth, behavior or nonbehavior concerns what the employee then does or withholds, such as voice, effort, repair, help-seeking, withdrawal, silence, redirection, or exit. This layered structure follows the appraisal and organizational-emotion view that appraisals, emotional experiences, motivational orientations, and behaviors are related but analytically separable (Elfenbein, 2023; Lazarus, 1991; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Repairability Meaning and Changeability

Repairability cue meaning follows the general acquisition, generalization, discrimination, and revision processes specified by cue-appraisal learning (Umbra, 2026). The present theory does not propose a separate cue-learning mechanism. Instead, it specifies the movement-related content of conditioned appraisal meaning and introduces a trajectory question that is especially important for blocked or unwanted work states: whether the relevant state appears increasingly movable, decreasingly movable, or closed. As employees encounter new evidence of organizational response, non-response, partial response, symbolic response, repair, redirection, or closure, they update the perceived movability of the relevant work state.

Building on hope theory and goal-regulation perspectives, the present theory predicts that credible cues of increasing changeability can support renewed hope while an unwanted work state remains unresolved (Carver & Scheier, 1998; Locke & Latham, 2002; Snyder, 2002; Snyder et al., 1991). The focal question is how employees interpret these cues through their histories with a particular work state. A chronic workload problem may remain difficult but become hope-relevant when credible staffing commitments, workload triage, or revised priorities indicate improving prospects. A blocked promotion process may remain uncertain but become hope-relevant when criteria and milestones provide credible evidence of an available pathway. A damaged relationship may remain painful but become hope-relevant when mediation creates a usable next step. These examples concern changes in what recurring cues signify about future possibilities, even while substantial difficulties remain. Conversely, sadness becomes more likely when changeability decreases. A situation that once seemed movable may become demoralizing if repeated feedback shows that movement is closing. Broken promises, stalled initiatives, symbolic listening, and repeated non-response are consequential because they teach that movement itself is disappearing. This prediction integrates appraisal accounts of sadness and goal loss with organizational research on voice futility, change cynicism, and silence (Lazarus, 1991; Morrison, 2023; Pinder & Harlos, 2001; Reichers et al., 1997; Roseman, 1991; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025; Van Dyne et al., 2003).

From Repairability Meaning to Action

Repairability cue meaning matters because it organizes perceived response viability.

When repairability cue meaning indicates credible movement, future-directed engagement appears more viable (Umbra & Fasbender, 2025). Employees may continue speaking, repairing, asking, crafting, persisting, or seeking help because the relevant work state still appears movable. This pathway is consistent with research on hope, self-efficacy, voice, proactivity, job crafting, and problem-focused coping (Bandura, 1977 (Bandura, , 1997;; Carver et al., 1989; Morrison, 2023; Parker et al., 2010; Snyder, 2002; Tims & Bakker, 2010).

When repairability cue meaning indicates lost movement, sadness-oriented responses become more viable. Employees may stop raising concerns, stop applying, stop crafting, stop repairing, withdraw from change, reduce effort, redirect effort elsewhere, or leave. These responses can be understood as coherent responses to learned immovability. Silence and withdrawal research distinguishes fear-based silence from resignation-based or acquiescent silence, and coping research similarly recognizes disengagement, acceptance, and withdrawal when active problem-focused coping appears unavailable (Carver et al., 1989; Folkman & Lazarus, 1980; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023;; Pinder & Harlos, 2001; Van Dyne et al., 2003). If the situation is genuinely non-repairable or harmful, withdrawal, refusal, redirection, or exit may be realistic and protective. The theory's goal is to explain when hope remains credible and when demoralization becomes understandable.

Repairability meanings do not deterministically produce behavior. They shape whether renewed engagement, voice, repair, help-seeking, persistence, withdrawal, silence, redirection, or exit appears sensible, justified, or worth enacting. Power, perceived safety, voice opportunity, job alternatives, available resources, and exit costs may shape whether the employee actually withdraws, persists, speaks, remains silent, redirects effort, or exits (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; Withey & Cooper, 1989; Edmondson, 1999; Hirschman, 1970; Hom et al., 2012; Hom & Kiazad, 2025; Morrison, 2023).

Propositions

Proposition 1: In recurring unwanted work states, cue-specific repairability meaning will predict later workplace hope beyond broader perceptions of control, coping potential, selfefficacy, trait hope, optimism, psychological capital, and general positive affectivity. This follows from hope theory, psychological capital research, appraisal theory, and expectancy-based motivation (Avey et al., 2011; Bandura, 1997; Lazarus, 1991; Luthans et al., 2007; Snyder, 2002; Snyder et al., 1991; Vroom, 1964).

Proposition 2: In recurring unwanted work states, cue-specific lost-movement meaning will predict later workplace sadness beyond broader perceptions of low control, low coping potential, helplessness, hopelessness, cynicism about change, and general negative affectivity.

This follows from appraisal accounts of sadness and from helplessness, hopelessness, and change-cynicism research (Abramson et al., 1989; Beck et al., 1974; Lazarus, 1991; Reichers et al., 1997; Roseman, 1991; Seligman, 1975; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985).

Proposition 3: In recurring unwanted work states, increases in perceived changeability will predict increases in workplace hope and decreases in workplace sadness before the unwanted work state is fully resolved.

This follows from research on goal progress, hope pathways, and affective events (Carver & Scheier, 1998; Locke & Latham, 2002; Snyder, 2002; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Proposition 4a: Workplace sadness will predict later organizational demoralization beyond baseline demoralization, helplessness, hopelessness, cynicism about change, and general negative affectivity, and will mediate the longitudinal relationship between cue-specific lostmovement meaning and subsequent demoralization. This follows from appraisal accounts of sadness, demoralization research, and learned helplessness/hopelessness traditions (Abramson et al., 1989; Woźniewicz & Cosci, 2023; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; de Figueiredo, 1993; Maier & Seligman, 2016; Roseman, 1991).

Proposition 4b: Workplace hope will predict later renewed engagement beyond baseline engagement, trait hope, self-efficacy, and psychological capital, and will mediate the longitudinal relationship between cue-specific repairability meaning and subsequent engagement.

This follows from hope theory, psychological capital research, and social cognitive theory (Avey et al., 2011; Bandura, 1997; Luthans & Youssef-Morgan, 2017; Snyder, 2002; Snyder et al., 1991).

Proposition 5: Following a history in which a recurring organizational cue has signaled stagnation, credible evidence of increasing changeability will predict greater subsequent workplace hope than equally favorable reassurance that provides no new evidence about the changeability of the relevant work state.

This proposition follows from goal-regulation, organizational-change, and affectiveevents literatures, which emphasize progress, implementation, and event meaning as bases of motivation and affect (Armenakis & Bedeian, 1999; Carver & Scheier, 1998; Locke & Latham, 2002; Oreg et al., 2011; Wanberg & Banas, 2000; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Illustrative Applications

Workload Strain: Increasing Changeability Before Full Repair

Chronic workload strain shows why hope does not require immediate full repair. An employee may face sustained overload, understaffing, or excessive demands. Burnout and job demands-resources research has shown that persistent high demands and insufficient resources are central to employee strain, exhaustion, and impaired functioning (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; Demerouti, 2024; Maslach et al., 2001). If every request for relief produces silence, delay, or symbolic concern without adjustment, the workload system may acquire lost-movement meaning. Sadness and demoralization become more likely because the employee learns that the workload state is not movable.

Hope may return before workload is fully corrected if the organization creates visible triage, temporary relief, staffing plans, prioritization, or concrete timelines. The workload may remain difficult, but the cue begins to signal increasing changeability rather than closure. This application draws on job demands-resources theory and coping research, both of which suggest that resources, prioritization, and problem-focused adjustments can alter employees' experience of demanding work (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; Carver et al., 1989; Folkman & Lazarus, 1980).

Career Progression: Compensation and Redirection When Direct Repair Is Unavailable

Blocked career progression illustrates that movement need not always mean direct repair.

An employee may repeatedly apply for promotion, ask for mentoring, or try to meet advancement criteria, yet receive vague rejection, shifting standards, or silence. Psychologicalcontract research shows that perceived rights and responsibilities matter for how employees interpret organizational treatment and future possibilities (Robinson & Rousseau, 1994; Rousseau, 1995). The promotion process may then signal stalled movement and evoke sadness, resignation, career grief, and demoralization. Direct promotion may not always be possible. In such cases, transparent criteria, developmental milestones, mentoring access, lateral movement, alternative role paths, or compensation for blocked opportunities may still create meaningful movement if they provide credible evidence that the employee is not trapped in the same career state. Mere reframing of the blocked future does not create repairability unless options actually change. This application is consistent with hope theory's emphasis on pathways and coping approaches that distinguish direct problem correction from compensatory or redirection strategies (Carver et al., 1989; Snyder, 2002).

Failed Voice: Symbolic Response Versus Real Movement

Failed voice shows the difference between symbolic response and repairability evidence.

An employee may repeatedly raise concerns in meetings, surveys, or one-on-one conversations, but no visible follow-up occurs. Voice research shows that employees weigh efficacy, safety, perceived impact, and relational risk when deciding whether to speak up (Detert & Edmondson, 2011; Edmondson, 1999; Morrison, 2014 Morrison, , 2023)). The voice channel becomes a repairability cue because it signals whether unresolved problems can move. If voice disappears into silence, sadness, acquiescent silence, and demoralization may become more likely.

By contrast, leaders may preserve repairability by closing the loop, explaining constraints, implementing partial changes, or keeping the issue visibly alive. Listening sessions without authority, surveys without feedback, or acknowledgments without follow-through may be especially consequential because they perform responsiveness while teaching stagnation. This distinction fits research on employee silence, voice instrumentality, and organizational change credibility (Morrison, 2023; Pinder & Harlos, 2001; Van Dyne et al., 2003; Wanberg & Banas, 2000).

Damaged Work Relationships: Bounded Repairability

A damaged work relationship illustrates bounded repairability. After unresolved conflict or failed restoration, the relationship or repair process becomes a cue. Research on workplace relationships, conflict, and interpersonal emotion shows that relational events shape affect, trust, and future interaction patterns (Elfenbein, 2023; Hareli & Rafaeli, 2008; Jehn, 1995). If repair attempts are ignored, punished, or left unresolved, the cue may signal closure, leading to sadness, grief, withdrawal, and demoralization.

Full restoration may not be necessary for hope to return. Mediation, boundary-setting, apology, clarification, or revised interactional norms may make the relationship more predictable, bounded, safe, or workable. Repairability may remain local: one relationship may be closed while other relational or organizational pathways remain movable. This example illustrates the discrimination process described in attribution, conditioning, and sensemaking research (Bouton, 2004; Heider, 1958; Kelley, 1973; Weick, 1995; Weiner, 1985).

Failed Change: Generalization From Repeated Stagnation

Failed organizational change illustrates generalization. A change initiative may initially signal movement from an unwanted state toward a better one. However, if initiatives are repeatedly announced, delayed, relaunched, or disconnected from visible implementation, employees may learn that change itself predicts stagnation. Change research shows that readiness, participation, implementation credibility, change cynicism, and change fatigue influence employee responses to change (Armenakis & Bedeian, 1999; Khaw et al., 2023; Oreg et al., 2011; Rafferty & Griffin, 2006; Reichers et al., 1997; Wanberg & Banas, 2000).

The repairability question is not whether the change message sounds hopeful, but whether the recurring change cue provides credible evidence of increasing changeability.

Transparent constraints, visible progress, and sustained follow-through may preserve hope even when full change is incomplete. Repeated announcement without movement may instead teach that future change efforts are likely to be futile. This application illustrates how repairability cue meaning connects change credibility, progress evidence, and learned futility.

Boundary Conditions and Scope

The theory applies most directly to recurring organizational cues where employees have enough experience to learn whether a blocked, harmful, stagnant, damaged, or unwanted work state is repairable, non-repairable, increasingly repairable, or decreasingly repairable. Its focus is on learned movement meaning in recurring work conditions, especially where employees must decide whether renewed effort, voice, repair, help-seeking, persistence, withdrawal, redirection, or exit remains viable. This scope aligns with dynamic approaches to workplace affect, sensemaking, and repeated-event appraisal (Elfenbein, 2023; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

The theory is also compatible with situations in which hope cannot be credibly restored.

Some work states are genuinely closed, structurally harmful, or non-repairable within the available organization. In such cases, sadness, withdrawal, refusal, silence, redirection, or exit may be realistic and adaptive. Coping research recognizes that withdrawal, acceptance, and disengagement can become more likely when active problem-focused coping appears unavailable, while voice and exit traditions similarly recognize exit as one response to persistent non-responsiveness (Carver et al., 1989; Withey & Cooper, 1989; Hirschman, 1970; Lazarus, 1991; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). This boundary condition is central to the theory's practical and ethical significance: credible hope depends on credible movement.

The normative stance of the theory is therefore dual. It explains how organizations can restore credible hope when movement is possible. It also recognizes that withdrawal may be rational and protective when the work state is genuinely harmful or non-repairable. The theory is not pro-hope in all circumstances. It is pro-accurate repairability evidence.

Organizational demoralization is analytically distinct from clinical depression, suicidality, helplessness, hopelessness, cynicism, distrust, shame, and general negative affect, while allowing meaningful overlap in severe or persistent cases. Entrapment and suicidology literatures indicate that severe, persistent, personally consequential no-way-out states can matter psychologically (Gilbert & Allan, 1998; O'Connor & Kirtley, 2018; Taylor et al., 2011; Williams, 1997). Clinical demoralization research likewise treats demoralization as a meaningful distress construct involving helplessness, hopelessness, loss of morale, subjective incompetence, and inability to cope (Woźniewicz & Cosci, 2023; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; de Figueiredo, 1993; Kissane et al., 2004; Tecuta et al., 2015). Their relevance here is to underscore the occupational importance of perceived non-changeability while preserving the work-contextual meaning of organizational demoralization.

Boundary conditions include the severity and duration of the unwanted work state, credibility of change agents, actual resource constraints, power asymmetry, prior histories of failed repair, individual differences in trait hope or pessimism, depression vulnerability, and whether the organization can realistically increase changeability without misleading employees.

These boundary conditions are consistent and derived from appraisal theory's emphasis on person-environment meaning, job demands-resources theory's emphasis on resources and demands, voice/change research on safety, credibility, and efficacy, as well as demoralization and depression research (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; de Figueiredo, 1993; Edmondson, 1999; Lazarus, 1991; Morrison, 2023; Oreg et al., 2011; Tecuta et al., 2015; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025).

Falsifiability, Measurement, and Empirical Testing

The theory requires evidence of both discriminant and incremental validity. Discriminant validity concerns whether measures of repairability cue meaning, hope, sadness, organizational demoralization, and adjacent constructs capture empirically distinguishable phenomena.

Researchers should assess their measurement structure and relationships while accounting for measurement error, rather than infer separation solely from additional predictive effects (Hinkin, 1998; MacKenzie et al., 2011; Rönkkö & Cho, 2022). Incremental validity concerns whether cue-specific repairability meaning explains subsequent outcomes beyond relevant existing constructs, such as perceived control, coping potential, self-efficacy, voice efficacy, helplessness, hopelessness, cynicism about change, and general affectivity. Additional prediction would support explanatory value, while empirical distinguishability requires separate measurement evidence.

The second test is temporal prediction. Repairability cue meaning should predict later emotional and motivational states, not merely correlate with them at the same time. Longitudinal, diary, and experience-sampling designs can test whether repeated movement strengthens hope, whether repeated stagnation strengthens sadness and demoralization, and whether perceived increases in changeability shift sadness toward hope before full resolution occurs. Temporal designs are especially appropriate because affective-events theory and appraisal-process models emphasize process and change over time (Elfenbein, 2023; Gabriel et al., 2019; Lazarus, 1991; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

The third test compares new evidence following a history of stagnation. The theory predicts that credible evidence of increasing changeability will support subsequent hope more strongly than equally favorable reassurance that supplies no new information about whether the relevant work state can change. This test follows from goal-regulation, organizational-change, and affective-events literatures, which emphasize progress, implementation, and event meaning as bases of motivation and affect (Armenakis & Bedeian, 1999; Carver & Scheier, 1998; Locke & Latham, 2002; Oreg et al., 2011; Wanberg & Banas, 2000; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996). The fourth test is emotional specificity. Sadness should be especially relevant in loss and closure contexts, whereas cynicism/anger, anxiety, or shame may be more central when stalled movement is appraised as injustice, threat, personal failure, or bad faith (Lazarus, 1991; Reichers et al., 1997; Roseman, 1991; Scherer, 2009; Smith & Ellsworth, 1985; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025).

Measurement should separate cue history, perceived cue evidence, emotion, affectivemotivational state, and behavior. Cue history items should assess what repeatedly happened.

Repairability cue meaning items should assess what the cue has repeatedly signaled, such as response, partial movement, stagnation, or closure. Hope items should assess emotional anticipation and energized possibility. Sadness items should assess loss, disappointment, and relinquishment without simply repeating movement language. Organizational demoralization items should assess affective-motivational resignation, reduced perceived point of trying, or relinquishment of effort rather than merely restating that the situation cannot move. Behavioral items should assess voice, persistence, withdrawal, silence, redirection, exit, or repair attempts separately from demoralization itself. This separation follows recommendations for construct development, avoiding common-method bias, and preserving discriminant validity (Hinkin, 1998; MacKenzie et al., 2011; Podsakoff et al., 2003).

For example, repairability cue meaning should be measured with items about what the recurring cue has signaled over time rather than with items such as "I feel hopeful this can change," which would capture hope rather than cue meaning. Similarly, organizational demoralization should be measured through affective-motivational resignation, reduced perceived point of trying, or relinquishment of effort rather than only with items such as "this situation cannot move," which would collapse demoralization into lost-movement meaning.

Studies can strengthen inference through temporal separation: cue history first, cue meaning second, emotion third, and demoralization or behavior later. This sequencing fits the theory's recursive logic and gives researchers a way to test whether repairability cue meaning operates as a learned appraisal process rather than as a concurrent label for emotion or behavior.

Practical Implications

The theory suggests that organizations should not manage hope directly. Rather, they may reduce demoralization when they change the evidence employees receive about movement.

From this perspective, hope is unlikely to return when employees are told to stay positive while the relevant cue continues to signal stagnation. Hope becomes credible when employees observe real movement, credibly developing movement, or honest redirection. This implication follows from hope theory, goal-regulation research, and change research showing that credible progress, participation, and implementation shape employees' responses rather than abstract communication alone (Armenakis & Bedeian, 1999; Carver & Scheier, 1998; Oreg et al., 2011; Snyder, 2002; Wanberg & Banas, 2000).

Leaders and HR systems may use this perspective to identify recurring repairability cues that have acquired lost-movement meaning. These may include workload systems, promotion procedures, voice channels, complaint processes, change initiatives, damaged relationships, resource requests, or feedback systems. The practical question is not only whether employees are dissatisfied, but which recurring cues have taught them that movement is lost. This shift is consistent with sensemaking, conditioning, and social-information perspectives, which emphasize that employees interpret patterns of organizational action and inaction (Bouton, 2004; Salancik & Pfeffer, 1978; Weick, 1995).

Practical responses may be repair-oriented or redirection-oriented. Repair-oriented practices include workload triage, staffing plans, transparent promotion criteria, mediation, voice follow-up, and change implementation. Redirection-oriented practices include alternative role paths, transfer opportunities, compensation for blocked opportunities, support for exiting harmful conditions, or honest communication that direct repair is unavailable. Redirection is one form of movement when direct repair is not credible. This implication draws from coping research, exit/voice traditions, job crafting, and organizational change literatures (Carver et al., 1989; Hirschman, 1970; Khaw et al., 2023; Tims & Bakker, 2010). This perspective also cautions against superficial positivity and symbolic repairability signaling. Encouragement, resilience messaging, listening sessions, optimistic change communication, or career conversations may backfire if the underlying cue continues to signal stagnation. Organizations should not manufacture the appearance of movement. Timelines without resources, listening without authority, career conversations without opportunity, or change messaging without implementation are not repairability evidence. They are potential futility cues. This concern is consistent with research on change cynicism, psychological contracts, and voice futility, which shows that broken promises, low credibility, and nonresponse can shape employee distrust, silence, or withdrawal (Morrison, 2023; Reichers et al., 1997; Robinson & Rousseau, 1994; Rousseau, 1995; Wanberg & Banas, 2000).

If the organization cannot credibly alter the unwanted state, ethical practice may require honesty, compensation, redirection, protection, or support for exit rather than symbolic reassurance. This implication follows from the theory's central claim: hope is constructive when it is grounded in credible movement, and potentially misleading when organizational evidence repeatedly teaches closure.

Future Research

Future research should first establish discriminant validity. Measures of repairability cue meaning, workplace hope, workplace sadness, and organizational demoralization should distinguish cue-specific repairability from perceived control, coping potential, pathways thinking, agency thinking, self-efficacy, voice efficacy, helplessness, hopelessness, cynicism, and general negative affectivity. This is the central empirical burden of the theory and follows standard construct-validation expectations for new theoretical constructs (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; Hinkin, 1998; MacKenzie et al., 2011; Rönkkö & Cho, 2022).

Future research should also examine how repairability cue meaning develops over time.

Longitudinal, diary, and experience-sampling designs could track repeated encounters with workload problems, career processes, voice channels, damaged relationships, and change initiatives. These designs could test whether repeated movement strengthens repairability meaning, whether repeated stagnation strengthens lost-movement meaning, and whether perceived increases in changeability shift sadness toward hope. Such designs align with affective-events theory and appraisal-process models (Elfenbein, 2023; Gabriel et al., 2019; Lazarus, 1991; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Building on cue-appraisal learning's generalization and discrimination propositions (Umbra, 2026), future research should examine how these processes operate specifically for repairability meanings. Studies could examine when lost-movement meaning remains tied to a specific cue-work-state pairing and when it spreads to broader systems, roles, relationships, or organizational futures, as well as how employees discriminate between cues that remain futile and cues that still provide credible movement. Qualitative, critical-incident, diary, and multilevel designs would be useful because generalization likely depends on perceived similarity, authority structures, and organizational pattern recognition (Bouton, 2004; Heider, 1958; Kelley, 1973; Maitlis & Christianson, 2014; Weick, 1995; Weiner, 1985).

Future research should test interventions that make increasing changeability visible. Such interventions might involve closing the loop after voice, providing transparent career milestones, creating visible workload triage, establishing repair processes for damaged relationships, or communicating credible change timelines. The central question is whether these interventions reduce organizational demoralization more effectively than generic reassurance, positive messaging, resilience training, or broad climate interventions. Intervention tests could draw from job crafting, organizational change, and burnout literatures, which emphasize both individual agency and structural work conditions (Bakker & Demerouti, 2017; Demerouti, 2024; Khaw et al., 2023; Oreg et al., 2011; Tims & Bakker, 2010).

Finally, future research should examine the occupational mental-health relevance of organizational demoralization without overextending clinical concepts. Severe, persistent, and personally consequential perceived non-changeability may matter for well-being, but organizational demoralization should remain analytically distinct from depression, suicidality, and clinical demoralization unless empirical evidence supports those links. This research agenda can draw from clinical demoralization, occupational stress, burnout, and organizational emotion literatures (Woźniewicz & Cosci, 2023; Clarke & Kissane, 2002; Demerouti, 2024; Elfenbein, 2023; Tecuta et al., 2015).

Conclusion

Employees do not only appraise whether work is bad, stressful, or controllable. They learn whether recurring unwanted work states still move. A workload system, promotion process, voice channel, damaged relationship, or change initiative can acquire meaning over time, teaching employees whether effort, voice, repair, help-seeking, persistence, withdrawal, redirection, or exit is more viable. This view integrates appraisal theory, affective-events theory, hope theory, conditioning research, and sensemaking by explaining how repeated organizational cues become movement-relevant meanings (Bouton, 2004; Elfenbein, 2023; Lazarus, 1991; Snyder, 2002; Umbra & Fasbender, 2025; Weick, 1995; Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996).

Building on cue-appraisal learning, this article developed repairability cue meaning as a movement-specific conditioned appraisal meaning. Repairability cue meaning captures whether recurring organizational cues have come to signal movement, stagnation, increasing changeability, or closure in relation to particular unwanted work states. When cues provide credible evidence of movement, workplace hope becomes more likely and renewed engagement remains viable. When cues provide credible evidence of lost movement, workplace sadness becomes more likely and organizational demoralization can develop.

The theory connects recurring cue-work-state histories to perceived changeability trajectories and proposes how these learned meanings shape hope, sadness, and organizational demoralization. Credible cues of increasing changeability may support hope before full resolution, while repeated cues of lost movement may contribute to sadness and subsequent demoralization. If supported, the theory directs organizational attention toward the evidence employees receive about particular unresolved conditions: whether improvement remains possible, whether available pathways are becoming more credible, and whether repair or redirection offers a usable next step.
