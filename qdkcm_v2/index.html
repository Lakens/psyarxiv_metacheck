Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Abstract

Psychological essentialism is the tendency to view the members of categories as sharing a hidden essence that explains their observable properties. From a cognitive point of view, essentialist beliefs depend partly on judgments that several properties of objects 'hang together.' As a consequence, humans perceive natural kind categories as having stronger essences than other categories, such as artifact categories. In this paper, we offer a new way to explain essentialism as the result of a process of dimensionality reduction involved in the formation of concepts. More specifically, we propose that a small set of principal components (as understood from Principal Component Analysis) represents patterns of covariation that can support essentialist beliefs about categories. We show that our approach accounts for several empirical findings concerning psychological essentialism. We also discuss how to understand the functional properties in artifact categories, and the connection between generic language and essentialism from a developmental perspective.

1. Introduction

The idea that things have essences goes way back in the history of philosophy. Aristotle (1993) was among the first to propose that things have both essential and accidental properties. He used this distinction to argue that concepts can be defined in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions, since the essence of a concept determines these conditions. Long after Aristotle, Locke (1689 /1975) famously distinguished between 'nominal' essences, which are our mental constructs of objects based on observable characteristics, and 'real' essences, which refer to an object's actual physical constitution, often beyond our sensory perception. In Locke's words, real essences are "the very being of anything, whereby it is what it is. And thus, the real internal, matias.ostavelez@fhce.edu.uy but generally…unknown constitution of things, whereon their discoverable qualities depend, may be called their essence." (III.iii.15).

Beyond the philosophical discussion, a more cognitively oriented question is to what extent humans think of object concepts in terms of essences. Susan Gelman has been a strong proponent of the view that essentialism is a "universal habit of mind" (2003, p. 15). She writes that essentialism has three main components (2003, p. 7): (i) A belief that certain categories are natural kinds.

(ii) A belief that some unobservable property causes observable similarities shared by members of a category.

(iii) A belief that everyday words reflect the real structure of categories.

There exists strong evidence suggests that essentialist thinking develops early in children as a cognitive bias. Gelman (2003, p. 297) writes that "essentialism falls out as a consequence of children being very good at a number of other skills: distinguishing appearance from reality, searching for causes, noting correlational clusters, and understanding how labeling works." Following Gelman, we focus on the results of studies on children since they best reveal to what extent essentialism is "a habit of mind". Their essentialist thinking reveals a powerful capacity to look beyond perceptual features when forming object concepts.

Cognitive scientists working on concepts and their role in categorization, induction and word meaning, often focus on perceptual features (for example, *** reference deleted ***).

However, essentialism presents a challenge for theories of concepts that only rely on perception, since what is thought to be essential for a category is often unobservable. 1 For example, dogs vary a lot in their looks, and there exist mechanical toy dogs and robot dogs that look and behave in ways similar to 'real' dogs. Nevertheless, already small children believe that there are some 'inner' features that constitute the essence of dogs (e.g., Newman et al. 2008). Medin and Ortony (1989, p. 184) introduced the notion of 'essence placeholders' to refer to a "place" in our representation of a category reserved for whatever is assumed to constitute its essence.

Using this notion, it can be said that the main challenge for a cognitive approach to essentialism is to explain which cognitive structures can function as essence placeholders and how these structures contribute to reasoning with concepts.

A further challenge is to explain why the ascription of essences varies across different types of categories. 2 For instance, animal categories are typically seen as having more essential properties (and thus stronger essences) than artifacts (for a review, see Neufeld 2022). As we will show later, an explanation of this is that animals have many properties that 'hang together', while this does not hold for most artifacts.

In this article, our aim is to present a cognitively oriented model of essentialism that focuses on its conceptual foundations. Our proposal is that our essentialist beliefs stem from a process guided by a principle of cognitive economy aimed at reducing the complexity of our concepts by identifying and compressing patterns of covariation among dimensions into new, composite variables. We call these patterns the 'structural blueprints' of categories, and we think that they are plausible candidates for what people represent as essences. Dimensionality reduction allows the cognitive system to represent these blueprints economically via a few new dimensions, which we propose to model using principal component analysis. 3 We use the theory of conceptual spaces as a modeling framework to express these ideas. We show that this approach explains several aspects of empirical investigations concerning psychological essentialism. In particular, we focus on causal cognition. At the end of the article, we discuss the connections between generics and essentialist cognition, and why generics appear so early in linguistic development.

2. Conceptual spaces as a framework

Conceptual spaces (*** reference deleted ***) have been developed as a research program in semantics studying the structure of concepts and their interrelations using geometrical methods.

It builds on two central ideas about the structure of concepts and properties: (i) they are composed of clusters of quality dimensions, many of which are generated by sensory inputs such as shape, color, size, and temperature; (ii) they have a geometric or topological structure that is the result of the integration of the specific structures of the dimensions.

Quality dimensions can be integral or separable. They are integral when you cannot assign a value in one dimension to an object without assigning another value in another dimension (Maddox, 1992). For instance, it is not possible to assign a value to the pitch of a tone without attributing one to loudness. When quality dimensions are not integral, they are called separable.

We define the notion of domain as a set of integral dimensions that are separable from all other dimensions. For instance, human color properties are composed of three fundamental parameters of color perception: hue, saturation and brightness (*** reference deleted ***, Regier et al. 2007). Any color perception is mapped onto specific values to these dimensions.

More generally, different colors can be described as regions of possible values of these three parameters (see Figure 1). The central notion of 'conceptual space' is defined as a collection of one or more domains with a distance function (metric) that represents properties, concepts, and their similarity relations. The distance function can vary; the most common one is the Euclidean, but also Manhattan and polar metrics may be appropriate in different contexts (see Shepard 1964 , Johannesson 2002, *** reference deleted ***).

We represent a property as a convex region within a single domain, whereas concepts are represented as convex regions across a set of domains (*** reference deleted ***). A region is convex when for every pair of points x and y in the region, all points between them are also in the region. An object is represented as a point in a conceptual space. Similarity among concepts and objects is defined as a monotonically decreasing function of their distance within the space (Shepard 1987). This makes our notion of similarity different from Tversky's (1977), which compares the number of properties that two objects have in common with the properties where they differ.

Many predicates in natural language, in particular those expressed by nouns, cannot be defined within a single domain, but as clusters of properties. For example, a dog has a shape, a size, a color, a weight, a temperature, a smell, a sound, etc. This fact leads us to divide predicates into properties and concepts. For most concepts, the domains that compose them can be correlated in different ways. For example, for the concept fruit, properties like size and weight, or ripeness, color, and taste covary. These covariations generate expectations crucial for inferential procedures that exploit semantic properties.

An important advantage of representing concepts in this way is that it allows us to account for the prototypical structure of categories in a natural way (Rosch 1975, *** reference deleted ***, Lakoff 2008). If concepts are defined as convex regions within n-dimensional spaces, a certain point in each region can be interpreted as the prototype for the property or concept. 3.1 The role of covariation

Why are animal categories often seen as having rich essences, while artifact categories are seen as having more arbitrary or "contingent" structures? We think this is because we perceive the former as having many more properties that "hang together" than the latter. For example, birds have beaks, feathers, wings, hollow bones, and they lay eggs. Most of them sing, build nests and fly. On the other hand, a clock can look almost any way, be made of any material, come in any size or color, and so on. The only thing that is common to clocks is that they tell time. So, clocks don't have essences, at least not rich ones. In brief, objects with a multitude of correlated properties are more strongly expected to have essences.

We might not have unbiased contact with the external world, but it is still the world that provides our sensory input. There are only certain clusters of properties that are organized in a stable enough way as to stick together in natural categories enabling us to make inductive inferences. Kornblith (1993, pp. 105-6) expresses the idea as follows:

It is thus safe to say that we have a sensitivity to the features of objects which reside in homeostatic clusters. Indeed, the way in which we detect covariation is precisely tailored to the structure of natural kinds. … [W]e conceptualize kinds in such a way in order to separate the properties of the members of a kind which are projectable from those which are not. We are aided in this task by our ability to detect clustered covariation.

Over recent decades, psychologists have gathered compelling evidence showing that the identification of correlations and covariations among features of different domains is fundamental to concept formation (Medin 1983 , Younger 1990 , Billman and Knutson 1996).

A plausible explanation of this phenomenon is that our perceptual apparatus has evolved to detect covariations across different domains, a competence enhanced by natural selection due to its utility in helping us make sense of our environment. In essence, our ability to detect such interconnected relationships or 'clustered relations' has become a specialized skill, beneficial in navigating and organizing the world around us. In line with this, the basic level categories postulated by the prototype theory of concepts (Rosch et al. 1976) can be characterized by distinctive clusters of covarying properties (Holland et al.1986, pp. 183-4).

In a similar vein, Shipley (1993) convincingly argued that children's knowledge of animals shows a high degree of organization compared to other semantic domains. A key reason for this is that different animal categories show a consistent contrast between clusters of their typical properties. For example, wolves, which eat meat, howl, have thick fur, and live in forests, contrast with parrots that eat seeds, squawk, have feathers, and live in rainforests, and with frogs that eat insects, croak, have moist skin, and dwell in wetlands. This systematic distinction helps identify clusters of properties that covary among similar categories. Billman and Dávila (2001) experimentally demonstrated that this ability to recognize such distinctions speeds up category learning.

Covariations are also important in inductive reasoning. Gelman (2003, p. 316) writes: "The more commonalities you have learned about a category in the past, the stronger your inferences about that category are in the future." For example, the properties of a bird show strong covariation. Kornblith (1993, p. 35) brings out the relation between covarying properties and causality: "It is precisely because the world has the causal structure required for the existence of natural kinds that inductive knowledge is even possible."

3.2 Our proposal

Capturing the ideas discussed earlier is not straightforward for formal models of concepts found in the literature. Consequently, this field of study has overlooked the issue of essentialism. 5 We are going to argue that Conceptual Spaces is the right framework to explicate the elusive idea that categories have hidden properties.

The mechanism we propose functions similarly to Principal Component Analysis (see Jolliffe, 2002): it reduces the dimensionality of the representation of a concept by identifying patterns of covariation among its dimensions and 'compressing' them into a smaller set of new variables corresponding to the directions of greatest variability within the category it represents.

Our idea is that these new variables codify the structural blueprint of the category, that is, the characteristic pattern of 'coordinated variation' among the properties of its members. Because Conceptual Spaces represents concepts as sets of points in multidimensional spaces, they are a suitable framework for modeling dimensionality reduction mechanisms such as PCA.

Among the several principal components this analysis can reveal, the first principal component captures the most variance in the concept. In other words, the principal component is the dimension that yields the best 'explanation' of the data. The second principal component -orthogonal to the first -best explains the remainder of the variation; a third -orthogonal to the first and the second-best explains the remainder of the remainder, etc. Higher components typically have a rapidly diminishing effect on reducing the variance, unless the data set is more or less randomly distributed. The length of a principal component represents the amount of variance in the data that is explained by that component. In other words, the longer the vector is, the more of the variance in the data set is reduced by the component.foot_4

As an illustrative example, let us consider the concept of strawberry. For simplicity, we will focus on three salient dimensions of it: softness, color, and sweetness. As with most fruit categories, these dimensions strongly covary across exemplars: softer strawberries tend to be redder and sweeter, while firmer ones tend to be less red and less sweet. We claim that, from a cognitive point of view, the natural language term "ripeness" refers to a compressed dimension that summarizes this particular pattern of covariation and is part of the structural blueprint of the category and, therefore, of its perceived essence. Moreover, since PC1 captures a much larger share of the total variance than PC2, we could retain only PC1, project the original points onto it, and obtain an even more compressed representation which can be interpreted as the (cognitive) meaning of the adjective ripe. Figure 4 illustrates this possibility. To restate the central thesis of this paper, we claim that the compression of a concept through the identification of a reduced set of principal components allows for the representation of the structural blueprint of a category, that is, the characteristic pattern of covariation among the properties of its members. We believe that this compressed representation can function as an essence placeholder and, consequently, could help explain what cognitive structure sustains our essentialist beliefs. Based on this, we propose that the more efficiently the representation of a category can be compressed, the stronger the essentialist beliefs about it will tend to be.

This idea aligns with the epistemological interpretation of compression made by Chaitin (2002) and Wilkenfeld (2019) that was mentioned in the introduction. Concepts that are more compressible have an epistemic advantage over those that are not because they allow accounting for many 'facts' (in this case, for the diversity of properties of the members of a category) through a few patterns. In contrast, concepts with more arbitrary structures are less intelligible because they require memorizing specific information about their members that does not respond to clear patterns. This helps explain the widespread idea that essences are hidden entities responsible for the observable properties of a category's members (Newman and Knobe, 2019; Gelman, 2013). Indeed, the pattern of covariation in a concept is an abstract structure that makes the observable properties of the members of the category intelligible and predictable.

Another interesting point is that categories that form part of rich taxonomies, such as natural kinds, can be represented in a compressed manner by similar sets of principal components. For example, different subcategories of fruits share compressed dimensions such as ripeness or nutritional value, which capture coordinated changes among more basic properties of their members. This could explain why concepts very close in a taxonomy (such as Bengal tiger and Siberian tiger) are perceived as sharing a very similar nature (Leslie 2017, pp. 405-40). Furthermore, as Devitt ( 2008) argues, an exemplar of a natural kind instantiates the essence of that kind and the essences of the superordinate kinds to which it belongs. Our approach can give a cognitive interpretation to this idea: the compressed superordinate concept can include principal components shared by its subcategories. In this sense, representing an exemplar of an apple through principal components that also form part of the representation of fruit helps explain why we attribute to that exemplar the essence of the latter category.

These ideas may also help explain why we hold essentialist beliefs about people. We often believe that what defines a person is her personality, and we describe personalities using a myriad of dimensions (expressed as gradable adjectives) such as friendliness, reliability, responsibility, moodiness, organization, etc. Many of these dimensions tend to covary, and their patterns of covariation can be represented economically through abstract variables, such as the ones described by the 'Big Five' model of personality. 7 For example, a responsible, organized, and reliable person may score highly on the abstract dimension of conscientiousness. We think that interpreting such variables as intrinsic features that explain behavior may encourage essentialist beliefs about people, analogously to how we construct the essence of natural classes.

It should not be assumed that all concepts have an essence. If the properties of a concept are unrelated and of more or less equal weight, then the principal component will be very small, and therefore the concept will not have any identifiable essence. In the example above, few dimensions (domains) were involved. For most natural kind concepts, the number of domains is very large. The domains are more or less salient for the concept, and most of them are only marginally relevant or not relevant at all. For example, even though all objects have a level of radioactive radiation, this is typically so small that this domain has no influence on the categorization of an object.

In our example, we could interpret the principal component as representing a dimension of ripeness. For other concepts, the principal components might be difficult to identify explicitly, let alone the problem of giving them names. However, principal components always exist for a given data set and are therefore proposed to capture what we intuitively think of as the essence of a concept. The components may, however, reduce the variance of the data set to varying degrees.

3.3 Some formal aspects

In order to make our central hypothesis more precise, we need to get into a little bit of technicalities. Given a data set with multiple variables, each of which represents a specific feature of the data, the standard method for principal component analysis proceeds as follows (Jolliffe 2002): (i) Standardize the range of continuous initial variables.

This step ensures that all variables are on the same scale and that no variable dominates the analysis due to its scale. 8(ii) Compute the covariance matrix to identify correlations. This matrix provides a measure of how much each pair of variables varies together.

(iii) Compute the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the covariance matrix to identify the principal components. The eigenvectors represent the directions of maximum variance in the data, and the eigenvalues represent the magnitude of these variances. The principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, ordered by their corresponding eigenvalues.

Now, the first step would mean that all the dimensions that are relevant for a concept would be given equal weight. This basically involves calculating correlations rather than covariances. In the conceptual space representation of a concept, the dimensions have different salience.

Typically, the salience values of the dimensions are dependent on the context in which the concept is applied. However, when it comes to the essence of a concept, it is conceived of as being independent of the context. In our interpretation, this means that one assumes that there is a 'default' set of salience values for the dimensions of a concept. It is this set of salience values that is to be used when calculating the principal component of the data set associated with a concept. This also means that the ranges of the variables are not standardized. Thus, our proposed method for determining principal components is based on calculating covariations based on given salience weights rather than correlations based on standardized ranges of the dimensions of the data set. Furthermore, it means that if the 'default' salience values should change as a consequence of learning more about a concept, the principal components, and thereby the essence of the concept, would change as well. We will return to this point below.

4. What the principal components explain

In this section we turn to some of the evidence for the hypothesis that children and adults mentally represent essences. Our aim is to show that our hypothesis provides an explanation of a number of inferential patterns that are found in children (e.g., Keil 1989 and Gelman 2003) and adults (e.g., Rips 1989). The inferences are of two kinds: Firstly, categorization is an inference concerning the category of a particular object, for example, recognizing a big cat with a mane as a lion. Second, generalization is an inference about an (unobserved) property of an object; for example, inferring that a lion we see is dangerous. The more the properties of a category are clustered, the more variance will be explained by the first principal component, and the more generalizations will be possible. Gelman (2003, p. 316) writes: "Metaphorically, property clusters are magnets: the more mass they accumulate, the more strongly they pull in new properties." In other words, categories with rich essences will continue to generate more generalizations over time.

4.1 The essence of natural kinds

Essentialist thinking is particularly strong in how children conceive of animals (Gelman 2003 , p 49, Keil 1989). The fact that animals are living things shows up in a large number of common properties: they move, eat, breathe, have offspring, are born, die, etc. As mentioned before, children seem to postulate that there are some underlying dimensions for all these properties, even if these dimensions are neither observable nor possible to express explicitly (Kornblith 1993). This is a characteristic of principal components: While the perceptible properties of an object may change over time, the values for the principal components are typically less varying.

For example, an insect can change from a larva, via a cocoon, to a butterfly. These stages of the butterfly are perceptually radically different, while the essential variables relating to living organisms are constant.

Since the principal components optimize the reduction of the variance of the objects falling under a concept, they contribute more to explaining the similarity of the objects than the observable properties of objects. We submit that these features determine the first principal components. As already mentioned, these components typically do not correspond to observable dimensions. This explains why the essence of a concept is often thought of as being something hidden 'inside' the objects falling under the concept. In our previous example, softness and redness are observable but 'ripeness' is a hidden variable.

The reason why essentialism is stronger for animal and plant categories and weaker for artifacts is that the strong coherence of the properties related to 'living' in animals has no correspondence for artifacts. 9 This entails that animal and plant categories allow inferences based on hidden properties, but artifacts do not. A consequence is that transformations that radically change the appearance of an object result in judgments of category change for artifacts, but animal categorization remains stable.

An example of this effect comes from Keil's (1989) investigation of transformations of objects that change their appearance. For animal categories, such changes do not affect children's categorizations. For example, a skunk that looks like a raccoon, but has the same inside as a skunk is still judged to be a skunk. In contrast, children judge changes in the appearance of artifacts as more important for how the artifact is categorized. For example, a coffee pot that has been made out of a bird feeder is more likely to be classified as a coffee pot than a bird feeder (Keil 1989, pp. 176-181).

The essence of an animal is difficult to make explicit, but it seems to relate to the factors that are necessary for the animal to be living and having agency, being able to act on its own independently of external forces. In contrast, plants are seen as living and growing, but lacking agency.foot_8

4.2 Artifacts, essentialism and affordances

Most artifactssuch as chairs, clocks, and telephonesare categorized on the basis of their functional properties (e.g., Kemler Nelson 1999 , Mandler 2004), that is, which actions you perform in relation to them. Actions also seem to be central to the development of concepts in infants and children. Nelson (1996, p. 110-111) summarizes the position:

The claim was that infants would form concepts of objects that were functional in their own lives, and would therefore learn to name them easily and early. The object's function from the child's point of view (e.g., rolling a ball) would form the core of the concept, and its perceptual appearance would be added as a cue to the identification of instances.

For example, when deciding whether an object is a chair, the perceptual dimensions of the object, like those of shape, color, texture and weight, are largely irrelevant, or at least extremely variable. However, the perceptual features may still play a role when deciding whether the object is a chair.

We propose to analyze functional properties by reducing them to the actions that the objects "afford" (Gibson 1979). To continue with the example, a chair is prototypically an object that affords back-supported sitting for one person, that is, an object that contains a stable flat surface at a reasonable height from the ground and another flat surface that supports the back (see also Jackendoff 1987, p. 105). In contrast, a sofa affords sitting for more than one person, back support, and lying down, but affords less for standing. A stool affords sitting for one person, no back support, but better affordance for standing.

In support of this analysis, Vaina (1983, p. 28) writes: "[T]he requirement for efficient use of objects in actions induces strong constraints on the form of representation. Each object must first be categorized in several ways, governed ultimately by the range of actions in which it can become involved". Following this idea, we propose that function concepts be interpreted with the aid of the action domain. Rosch (1976) points out that some artifacts such as books, pencils, screwdrivers and pipe wrenches have clusters of correlated properties. They may therefore be thought of as having some form of essence. This may be correct, but the set of correlated properties is typically smaller than that for animals, since many properties of artifacts are irrelevant for the function of an artifact. Therefore, the first principal components will explain less of the total variance of the category, which, according to our criterion, predicts weaker essentialist beliefs about them. In accordance with this explanation, Gelman (2003, p. 49) notes that artifacts do not allow category-based inferences to new properties. Another piece of evidence that points in the same direction comes from Kemler Nelson (1999) who investigated how toddler's experience with object function affected their word learning. Our proposal can also explain some aspects of category-based induction. This is a type of inductive inference that exploits information about individual categories for estimating the probability of property projection among them. For instance, "Dogs have sesamoid bones; thus, wolves have sesamoid bones" relies on the conceptual similarities among the categories DOG and WOLF, and not on the logical form of the argument or some other propositionally codified property (Rips 1975 , Osherson et al. 1990). Categories that have similar properties will have similar principal components. Thereby categories with similar essences will support categorybased induction (see Gelman 2003, pp. 143-147).foot_9

4.3 Essences change when the underlying conceptual space changes

A consequence of our characterization of essentialist beliefs as being grounded in principal components is that judgments of essence can change if the knowledge about a category changes.

This consequence goes against a realist view of essences (e.g., Devitt 2008), but is fully compatible with a conceptualist view. Indeed, we think the consequence is desirable. A historical example is the category of whales, which used to be categorized as fish. This means they shared part of their essence with other fish. Nowadays, zoologists tell us that inner properties of whales entail that they should be categorized as mammals. What has happened is that more physiological domains have been added to the concept of a whale, and these additions and their increased salience have drastically changed the principal components of the concept 'whale' and thereby the essence attributed to the category. These kinds of conceptual changes, which appear to be fundamental to children's cognitive development (e.g., Carey 2000), have prompted many psychologists to suggest the existence of intuitive theories. These theories are supposed to organize conceptual knowledge, forming the basis for our judgments of similarity between stimuli. This suggestion is called the 'theory-theory' view.

The crucial point is that learning concepts does not solely involve grouping features into categories. It also requires understanding the relative significance of these features when categorizing and reasoning with concepts. Some psychologists view this as involving some form of higher-order learning, comparing it to the way scientific theories operate (e.g., Gelman and Kalish 1993, p. 110). Scientific theories postulate the existence of unobservable entities and causal structures to account for a range of observable phenomena. If categories are embedded within theory-like structures, the 'superficial' features of their exemplars (such as their shapes, colors, sizes, etc.) would be perceived as being causally dependent on unobservable internal features. Furthermore, changes at the level of these causal relationships would generate changes in the category. From our perspective, essentialism is explained quite naturally from our intuitions about the relevance of the internal features of a category with respect to its external (observable) structure (see Gelman and Wellman 1991).

In our opinion, postulating the existence of intuitive theories seems less cognitively basic than the extraction of principal components that we suggest as an explanation of how people construct and reason with concepts. Introducing cognitive representations of theories is more costly than the problem they attempt to solve. Firstly, even in the philosophy of science it is not entirely clear how to define theories (e.g., Morrison 2007). It is, however, generally agreed that the notion of 'concept' is explanatorily prior to that of theory: While theories might affect the content of their concepts, theories cannot be defined without concepts. Secondly, the central purpose of empirical theories is to determine the causal relationships underlying domains of phenomena. It seems quite hard to explain how children could (implicitly) develop intuitions about causal relations involving features of objects that they cannot perceive. For example, when children think that internal features of animals are essential, they do not rely on biological facts or theories, but express their expectations that these features are immutable (Gelman 2003, p. 240).

Our approach avoids these kinds of obscure assumptions as it explains intuitions about essences as a result of identifying covariance relationships of features in categories. For these reasons, we do not accept theory-theory explanations of essences.

5. Essences and causation

The human mind seeks causes. This is part of what Kant called our Anschauungsformen. We are causal determinists in the sense that we seek causes also for random events like winning the lottery or becoming sick. Children are also causal determinists, actively expecting events to have causes. Authors such as Rehder (2007 Rehder ( , 2017) ) consider that this bias towards the search for causes plays a central role in the explanation of essentialism. At first glance, this idea seems to be in contradiction with our proposal. However, in this section we argue that this is not necessarily the case. We discuss how principal components can serve as the causal variables we desire in order to explain various types of phenomena.

5.1 Essences as inner causes

As previously stated, essences are seen as inner causes of many of the external features of an object (Gelman 2003, pp. 116-128). Since causes are considered to be more important than effects when objects are categorized (Gelman 2003, p. 110), it follows that causal dimensions (or domains) are more salient than the dimensions (or domains) that represent the effects. This explains why some inner variables are those that primarily determine the categorization of an object, even if the variables are not observed but just postulated. For example, in Keil's (1989) studies of the transformations of animal kinds, the inner variables dealing with the living and agency aspects of an animal are the variables that determine a category. Gelman (2003, p. 128) writes that "children may possess a skeletal sense of self-generated cause before they have learned about specific internal mechanisms". We suggest that the child's brain somehow identifies the first principal components of a category and it is these components that serve as causal variables, even if the child has no way of describing them.

Our example of strawberry ripeness will allow us to illustrate this idea. Since color correlates with sweetness in strawberries, knowing a strawberry's color lets us estimate how sweet it will be. However, by representing this covariation through a compressed dimension, we can interpret the association between these properties as the product of the hidden dimension of ripeness. Then, knowing a strawberry's degree of ripeness lets us form expectations about its color and sweetness, interpreting it as the common cause of both properties and screening off the supposed causal relationship between them. In summary, our proposal is that dimensions summarizing covariation patterns can come to be interpreted as underlying states that cause observable properties.

For another example, having unprotected sex may cause AIDS, but the intermediate variable HIV infection screens off sex as the direct cause of AIDS. And there are other ways of transmitting the HIV virus. This means that we view the virus as the direct cause and unprotected sex as only an indirect variable. We submit that this mechanism works more generally also in judgements of essences so that the principal components screen off other variables in causal relations. For example, 'living' and 'being an agent' screen off the biological variables that contribute to these factors. The principal components therefore function as hidden causes.

5.2 Are essences needed for causal reasoning?

As a criticism of the view that essences are central to causal reasoning, Strevens (2000) argues that data on children's causal inferences do not necessarily imply that they are essentialists. He distinguishes three different forms of essentialism, where the version most relevant here is what he calls pure essentialism (see Figure 4), which consists of three tenets: (i) children represent essences; (ii) they define kinds in terms of essences; and (iii) they think essences are causally responsible for the observable properties of kinds. Strevens compares the pure essentialist position to what he calls the 'minimal' hypothesis which denies all three tenets of pure essentialism (see figure 5). Instead, the minimal hypothesis says that "children believe there are causal laws connecting natural kinds and their observable properties, but that they are not committed to any particular view about the implementation of these laws. In particular children do not represent essences as underlying the laws" (Strevens 2000, p. 154). Our main problem with Strevens' hypothesis is that it goes against principles of cognitive economy. A child that represents all the causal relations between natural kinds and their properties (what Strevens calls K-laws) would be much more cognitively burdened than a child that generates the causal connections from the essence of the kind. For example, how can a child combine its knowledge of the feathers, wings, weight, flapping, etc., of a bird to its flying capacity? It is much more cognitively economic to assume that the essence of a bird is what causes it to fly. Again, the essence functions as an intermediate variable between the observable features of a bird and its flying, which screens off the other features from being causes. Since principal components are the variables that most efficiently reduce variation in the properties of a kind, they can play an important role in explaining how people represent causal relations among features.

5.3 The chicken or the egg?

It might be objected that principal components are not 'real' causes, but just mathematical constructs. For a pure essentialist, essences are 'real' so they cannot be just principal components. Against this view, we have already argued that judgments of essences may vary with the knowledge of the agent, which indicates that essences are, after all, cognitive constructs and nothing inherent in a natural or artifactual kind. In our opinion, the question whether principal components are 'real' belongs to metaphysics. From our instrumentalist (conceptualist) position, the principal components have the properties that are normally associated with causal variables, and they are therefore suitable to serve as representations of essences. Nevertheless, the chicken or the egg problem remains: Which is cognitively prior, essences or causes?

There exists some evidence that essences rely on causal knowledge more than in covariational learning. In a set of experiments, Malt and Smith (1984) and Ahn et al. (2002) showed that typicality judgments about exemplars of a category were stronger for those that included pairs of causally correlated properties. In their experiments, they provided descriptions of exemplars of the category bird. Beyond sharing general properties like having wings, some exemplar had the properties living near the ocean and eating fish, while others had being white and eating fish. Although both property pairs showed a similar degree of covariation, subjects judged the exemplars with the pair lives near the ocean and eats fish as more typical because they saw these properties as causally related. They concluded that semantic judgments depend not only on pairwise covariations, but also on causality judgments, which is consistent with theory-theory and has implications for what counts as the essence of a concept.

There is, however, an alternative explanation (see also Rogers and McClelland 2004).

Participants might give more weight to properties they perceive as causally linked because they coherently covary with multiple other properties, unlike the other property pair. For instance, living near the ocean and eating fish apply to many shorebirds sharing other common traits like diving and swimming. Conversely, being white and eating fish could apply to various birds, including swans and ducks, making this combination less coherent.

Furthermore, our perception of the connection between property pairs within a category may be influenced not only by their covariation but also by the relations among properties across similar categories in the same domain. For example, people may perceive the pair being white and eating fish as less correlated because they know that color and feeding habits are not consistently (statistically) related across different animal categories. In contrast, living near the ocean and eating fish may be perceived as more correlated because habitat and feeding habits are often related across various animal categories. This broader perspective suggests that the essence of a concept is shaped by the statistical structure of the environment, not just within a single category but across related categories. This view would support that essences are cognitively prior to causes.

In a similar line of argumentation that takes causality as the central factor behind essence, Rehder (2007) explored a 'generative' theory of categories that is based on causal networks of properties that belong to a category (see also Rehder, 2017 and Rehder and Kim 2006 , 2010). For example, bird DNA causes birds to sing, have wings, have light bones and to eat seed. Having wings and being light causes the bird to be able to fly and the ability to fly and being light in turn is a cause of their building nests in trees (see Rehder 2007, Figure 12-1).

Some of these causal links are probabilistic and Rehder uses Bayesian calculations to determine the strength of various causal relations. Such generative causal networks are then seen as the basis for the essential properties of a category. This theory thus views causes as cognitively prior to essences.

In order to construct such causal networks, the relevant causal variable must first be identified. In the example above, 'having bird DNA' is seen as the basic cause of all the other properties and thus an essential variable of the bird concept. However, knowledge about bird DNA is historically recent and dependent on education, but people have assigned an essence to birds long before this knowledge was available. As a matter of fact, children (and adults) often ascribe essences to natural phenomena without knowing anything explicit about the underlying variables. Therefore, we do not believe that causal models of the kinds studied by Rehder (2007) are helpful in answering the question of whether essences or causes are cognitively more fundamental.

To be clear, we are not claiming that causality plays no role in ascriptions of essences.

But we believe that, if we set aside expert knowledge, most of our causal knowledge about inter-feature relations is probably 'shallow' (Strevens 2008). For example, we know that the curved shape of a boomerang or the size of a bird's wings play a causal role in (or are enabling conditions of) the respective flight patterns, but few competent users of those categories know the physical mechanisms underlying this causal relation.

We believe that much of our knowledge of the causal structure of categories has been learned from causal generics (e.g., "birds can fly because they have wings") that do not include explanations of underlying causal mechanisms but that still determine our expectations about the patterns of correlations between properties in a category. The causal generic "A causes B in category X" is a shortcut to a type of statistical knowledge that should have been learned by observing a large volume of exemplars of a category, something that is often difficult.

In summary, we believe that causal knowledge is certainly crucial for the perception of the essence of a category, but that this is a phenomenon limited to expert knowledge, whereas in our everyday knowledge (i.e., for 'folk' categories), the perception of essence is based on statistical mechanisms that identify sets of candidate causes or enabling conditions for an effect.

6. Essentialism and language

There are two main connections between essences and language, both of which are central to how children learn concepts. Firstly, when a label is used for a category, children implicitly assume that the objects that fall under the label share an essence. For example, even though dogs are perceptually rather dissimilar, applying the label "dog" to all variants suggests that they have several central properties in common, and it induces children to focus on these properties.

Given the limited mind-reading of small children (see Wellman et al., 2001), it is likely that they believe that labels reflect what objects are called rather than what people call objects.

This can be interpreted as a form of essentialist thinking (Gelman 2003). Second, generics are used to inform children about properties of a concept that help identify the principal components that represent its structural blueprint. 12

This would explain why parents normally choose basic level labels (in the sense of prototype theory), the level that is informationally richer, when they introduce a new category. Piaget (1929, p. 61-62) writes that "in learning the names of things the child at this stage believes it is doing much more. It thinks it is reaching to the essence of the thing and discovering a real explanation". In contrast, these features do not hold of adjectives, since an adjective only refers to a single domain (*** reference deleted ***) and can therefore neither contain correlations between properties, nor be the only components in generics. Gelman (2003, p. 187) also points out that children "assume that a novel noun refers to a class of like object, whereas a novel adjective refers to a single property".

6.2 Generics

Generics are quite frequent in adult child-directed speech. Gelman et al. (1998) estimate that more than 3% of mothers' statements are generics (more than 5% in statements about animals).

This percentage may seem low, but it still represents a substantial part of the input to children.

We submit that the main function of generics is educational. They provide an efficient way to convey conceptual knowledge that is the foundation for a rich system of expectations in children. Their minds seem adapted to efficiently incorporating them into a system of beliefs and understanding the relations between generics relating to different levels of categories. For example, when a child sees a cow, the mother may express "A cow says moo". Even though there is only one cow in the focus of attention of the child, he or she will interpret the sentence as a generic rather than about the particular cow (see also Brandone et al., 2012). This tendency to generic interpretation supports that the child (implicitly) understands that language is generally not about the here and now, but conveys new information. Even a single instance of a category can trigger a generic utterance. This indicates that parents as well as children conceive of objects both as individuals and as examples of a category.

Gelman (2003, (174) (175) argues that generics have three functions when teaching children. Firstly, to teach children category-wide generalization. Secondly, a generic implies that members of a category are alike in important ways. The child implicitly presupposes that the parents present generics that contain the most relevant information (a Gricean maxim), especially that the properties mentioned are the most salient ones. In other words, parents provide information that helps children establish the principal components of a concept, that is, its essence. There is evidence suggesting that parents prefer to use generics about richly structured categories (Gelman 2003, p. 194-195). Thirdly, generics foster essentialist thinking (see also Foster-Hanson et al. 2016). Gelman (2003, p. 294) writes that "from the beginnings of language children seem to assume that language directly reflects essentialist structure in the world, and they seem to make use of words to bootstrap essentialism to new categories and to new category instances, seemingly without effort".

The fact that generics exist in all languages suggests that this understanding is a universal human feature with deep cognitive underpinnings. In support of this, Gelman and Roberts (2017, 7905) write: "In the case of learning categories, we suggest that cumulative cultural evolution is enhanced by labels and generics, which provide a simple yet powerful means of passing along the wisdom (and prejudices) of prior generations."

7. Conclusion

Within philosophy, essentialism has often been associated with a realist view of the world, in particular with so-called modal realism (see Cowling 2013). In this article, we adopt a cognitivist view of essences. We believe that such a position can better explain the data that have been collected by psychologists concerning essential thinking in children and adults.

Our contribution is to propose a new interpretation of psychological essentialism in terms of statistical mechanisms that support informational efficiency while allowing us to capture patterns in the data. Our model is based on the theory of conceptual spaces. We propose that a small set of principal components allows us to represent the structural blueprint of a category, which functions as an essence placeholder when interpreted as reflecting an intrinsic organization of properties shared by its members.

We have shown that this proposal can explain several of the empirical findings concerning how humans, in particular children, conceive of essences. We have also highlighted the connections between essential thinking and how labels and generics are used to convey information about categories.

It is important to acknowledge that our approach is not unique in suggesting PCA-like mechanisms underlying cognition. In face recognition research, several models have used principal component analysis to explain the identification of faces and expressions, highlighting the psychological plausibility of such approaches (Calder et al. 2001, Tsao and Livingstone 2008). While our paper does not explore these specific relationships, there may be conceptual overlaps between these models and our framework.

Even though it may be difficult to establish a psychologically valid similarity measure as distances in domains of conceptual space to test our model, our proposal can lead to new types of empirical investigations concerning essences. In particular, our model predicts that when individuals learn more about a category, their judgments concerning the essence of the category will change. This prediction goes against a realist view of essences, and support for the prediction would thus be support for a cognitivist view.
