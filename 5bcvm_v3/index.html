Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Color science has spent most of its history explaining one thing: the conscious sensation of hue, saturation, and brightness. Yet beside that success sits a growing list of inconvenient findings.

Examples include ambiguous colors (one photograph seen as white-and-gold by some and blueand-black by others); memory colors (a gray banana that looks faintly yellow); color before form (newly sighted patients who see color before objects); imagined or dreamed colors (chromatic experiences generated without light or eyes); aphantasia (people who cannot summon a color in imagination); synesthesia (a printed letter flooded with color); and large, stable individual differences in the colors people report. These are not marginal curiosities or isolated anomalies. They are the ordinary output of a system that the percept-centered framework leaves out. A chromatic stimulus does not simply produce a color percept; it initiates a broad physiological and psychological event, of which the percept is only one part.

That percept is constructed at the moment of seeing by memory, language, attention, affect, and culture, and it shapes them in turn, a reciprocal determinism of percept and event. I develop this framework at three levels, as two nested theories: a new theory of color contained within a constructivist model of the entire chromatic event. The account retains trichromatic and some opponent-process theory rather than replacing them, but extends them by predicting phenomena the traditional framework treats as noise. Chief among these are the individual differences that, on this view, become the primary evidence for how color is constructed.

1. Introduction: color is not a property waiting to be found

Ask most people what color is and they will point to the world. The apple is red; the sky is blue; the eye simply reads these facts off the world. This intuition is so familiar that it has become almost invisible, yet it has organized an entire science. For more than three centuries, color research has treated its task as explaining how the visual system detects and represents object color, and it has succeeded brilliantly within that domain. We understand the retinal cone classes, chromatic opponent coding, and much of the cortical circuitry underlying color vision.

We can predict when two physically different lights will appear identical to a standard observer. What we have rarely asked is whether the thing all this explains, the conscious sensation of hue, is the phenomenon itself, or only its most visible part.

The central claim of this paper is simple: color science has been explaining the right phenomenon at the wrong level. The evidence is that the percept-centered framework keeps producing anomalies. The same photograph of "the dress" splits observers into camps who see it as white-and-gold or blue-and-black, an ambiguous color they cannot talk each other out of.

A photographed banana on a neutral background looks faintly yellow even though it is physically gray, an effect known as memory color. Patients blind from birth and then surgically sighted, after removal of congenital cataracts, report color almost immediately but cannot yet tell a cube from a sphere by eye; color arrives before form. Most of us can summon a vivid color with the eyes closed or in a dream, from no light at all; some people, those with aphantasia, cannot generate one even in imagination; and others, those with synesthesia, see a fixed color every time they read a particular letter. And when careful measurements are made, even observers with normal color vision differ widely and reliably in the colors they report from identical light. Each of these, and others like them, has been studied and filed as a special case, a curiosity, or a source of noise. They are none of those things. They are exactly what a constructed system should produce, becoming visible only when the construction is, for once, caught in the act. A theory that predicts them explains more than one that treats them as exceptions.

The insight is old. Writing in eleventh-century Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham already treated seeing as active inference rather than passive reception, anticipating by centuries the unconscious inference usually credited to Helmholtz (Sabra, 1978 (Sabra, , 1989;; I. P. Howard, 1996; Howard & Peterzell, 2013). His experimental, inferential account of perception has led some historians to regard him as the first perceptual psychologist (Hatfield, 2009; Sedgwick, 2022; Smith, 2015).

The other candidate for that title is Newton (Wooten & Hammond, 2026), who knew color was not simply in the world: the rays, he wrote, are not colored, and the color is something the mind supplies. But the field kept the sensation at its center anyway, because the sensation is what seems given, and because a science needs a stable object. This paper takes Newton's remark to its conclusion: the sensation is not merely mind-supplied but constructed, assembled at the moment of seeing out of far more than the incoming light, and once we see how much goes into it, the sensation stops looking like the bedrock of the science and starts looking like one stratum among several. Color is not a property of the world. It is an event, a construction of which the percept of hue is only the most visible layer.

The proposal has two distinct claims, either of which can stand independently. The first concerns construction. Color science has mapped a chain of processing from the retina to the cortex, but that chain is not the whole story. The percept of hue, saturation, and brightness is also built, at the same moment, by memory, language, affect, attention, prediction, and cultural knowledge. And, remarkably, the two shape each other, the influence running in both directions. The second concerns taxonomy, meaning where a science draws the boundary of the thing it sets out to explain. Because so much goes into the percept, it is the wrong unit to place at the center: it is one part of a larger event, not the event itself. Color science has been carving the subject at the percept; the natural joint lies around the whole chromatic event. The first claim is already a theory of color perception. The second, redrawing the boundary of the explanatory object, is the more consequential, and the reason for the title: to understand color we must study not the colored patch but the whole happening it belongs to.

The two horns this account means to escape are old. On one side is naïve realism, the view that color is simply out there in objects and the eye reports it. On the other is dissolution, the view that since color is not out there it must be an illusion, a mere convention, or a private invention.

Democritus stated the second when he said color exists only by convention, while by nature there are only atoms and void, and Plato's Theaetetus already located color in the meeting of eye and object rather than in either alone. This account rejects both alternatives. Color is neither a property lying in the world waiting to be detected nor an illusion to be explained away. It is a real event, reliably produced, measurable, and lawful, that is constructed in the encounter between a structured observer and a structured world.

None of this rejects the achievements of classical color science. Trichromatic theory, and coneopponent coding, are correct at their level, which is why the account keeps them; the classical perceptual-opponent stage, the red-green and blue-yellow axes of color appearance, is under increasing empirical pressure, and the account keeps the parts that remain well supported while treating its role in appearance as an open question. What follows is not another competing theory of color. It is a framework within which the existing theories acquire their proper scope, the containment mapped in Figure 2: a picture in which the well-mapped machinery of the eye is one contributor to a larger, constructed event. The frame is built to hold not only the classical psychophysics but the strongest programs of the present generation, the predictive-processing and Bayesian accounts of appearance, the cortical multiplexing of color with form and object vision, the work on memory color and cognitive penetration, and the individual-differences program, giving each a level and a role within one phenomenon while adding commitments none of them made on its own. A reader who does not recognize all of these terms need not worry: each is introduced and given its place in the sections that follow.

Each successfully describes one level of a larger whole; the frame's task is to hold them together as parts of a single phenomenon. The argument is addressed first to color and vision scientists, and its ask is concrete: to treat color as the whole physiological and psychological event a chromatic stimulus initiates, not only the percept at its center, and to widen accordingly what the science counts as its object of explanation.

2. The chromatic event: three levels

The account rests on a simple distinction the single word "color" hides: it names three different things, and ordinary language, with much of the science, runs them together.

Level 1 is the physical stimulus. Light of a given spectral composition arrives at the eye. This is a matter of physics and optics, describable without mention of any observer, and it is the same whether or not anyone is looking. It is the only level at which "color" names something wholly outside the person, and, strictly, it is not colored at all; it is a distribution of energy across wavelengths and space.

Level 2 is the color percept. This is the conscious experience: this particular red, that particular blue, with its hue, its saturation, and its brightness. It is what people almost always mean by "seeing a color," and it is the thing color science has worked hardest to explain. It is genuinely there; the account does not dissolve it, only insists it is one part of a larger structure.

Level 3 is the whole event the stimulus sets off. A chromatic stimulus does not merely produce a percept and stop. It initiates a cascade that is at once physiological, perceptual, cognitive, affective, and behavioral, sorted here into four families. The perceptual family binds color to form, groups the scene, and delivers recognized objects. The physiological family runs from chromatically selective cortex down to autonomic arousal, pupil size, alerting, and circadian timing. The cognitive-semantic family engages memory, language, category, semantic knowledge, and mental imagery. The affective-social-behavioral family engages emotion, aesthetic preference, social signaling, and decision and action. Color, on this account, is this Level 3 event, of which the conscious percept is the most conspicuous part, not the whole. This is the paper's ontology, and it reconciles the claims made along the way: color is not a property of the world because it does not live in the Level 1 stimulus; it is an event because it is the whole Level 3 cascade; and the percept is only one part of it because the percept is Level 2, inside the event rather than identical to it. How these families and their members actually partition is, at present, an open empirical question, and an important one. The four families are less a finished chart than a periodic table still being filled in, its real columns to be found in the covariation across observers. The list above is a first sorting by function, not a claim that each element is independent. Some determinants may prove to be facets of a single underlying factor, rising and falling together across observers and conditions; others may turn out to be genuinely separable; and the most consequential Level 3 factors may be ones no current taxonomy names, discoverable only once the covariation among these systems is measured directly. Establishing that factor structure, which elements share a common cause and which stand apart, is a principal task the account sets for itself. The method suited to it is the individual-differences analysis of Section 7.

The model can be drawn two ways, and Figure 1 shows both. Panel A draws it as a causal architecture: the well-mapped feedforward chain from eye to brain delivers the color percept, and the Level 3 determinants reciprocally construct it, a constructivist theory of color. Panel B draws the same model as the whole event, a wheel with the stimulus at the hub, the percept as the ring, and the four families around it, a constructivist theory of the physiological and psychological event that color names. Three consequences follow, because most confusion here comes from crossing the levels. First, the levels are distinct, and a finding at one does not transfer to another without argument: an association is not an induction, a percept is not an event, a reportable experience is not the same as a physiological response, and much apparent inconsistency in the literature is the residue of level-crossing inferences made without warrant. Second, the natural kinds, if there are any, likely live at Level 3. If we want categories that predict how a chromatic stimulus will move attention, affect, memory, physiology, and action, we will not find them by refining our description of hue; we will find them by characterizing the dimensions of the event itself.

Third, most of Level 3 is, at present, poorly mapped, because the field has spent its effort on Level 2. That gap is the research frontier this account opens.

3. How the percept is constructed: the Level 3 determinants

The constructive claim has a single heart: the conscious percept is not received but made, and made at the moment of seeing, by systems the standard framework has filed as downstream of color or set aside as secondary. I take the main determinants in turn, with the strongest evidence for each.

Memory and object knowledge. The clearest demonstration is memory color, a phenomenon

Hering named more than a century ago (Hering, 1874 (Hering, /1964)): a gray banana looks not neutral but faintly yellow, because the visual system knows what bananas are (Hansen, Olkkonen, Walter, & Gegenfurtner, 2006). Observers asked to set a picture of a banana to gray reliably overshoot toward blue, cancelling the yellow the object's identity has added, and the effect is well modeled as the visual system combining the incoming signal with a learned prior about the object's typical color (Witzel, Valkova, Hansen, & Gegenfurtner, 2011). This is not a judgment made after seeing; it is present in the appearance itself, and it is exactly the sort of thing a percept-centered framework cannot treat as central, since on that view the gray banana should look gray.

Language and category.

The words a language provides for color appear to inflect perception near category boundaries. Russian, which forces its speakers to distinguish lighter goluboy from darker siniy, gave those speakers a discrimination advantage across that boundary that English speakers lacked (Winawer et al., 2007). The effect was once attributed to a lefthemisphere, language-based source (Gilbert, Regier, Kay, & Ivry, 2006) and taken to show that Whorf (1956) was "half right" (Regier & Kay, 2009). The strong form of this claim has since been contested: reanalysis shows the cross-boundary advantage does not survive rigorous treatment of the response-time data (Martinovic, Paramei, & MacInnes, 2020), and the effects that do survive look like influences on attention and judgment rather than a rewriting of early sensory coding (Witzel & Gegenfurtner, 2018). Nor does lexical richness fix perceptual boundaries: the number of blue terms a language provides does not determine where its speakers place the green-blue border (Uusküla & Bimler, 2026). The developmental picture complicates it further: category-like responding is present in prelinguistic infants but lateralized to the right hemisphere, shifting to the left, language-dominant hemisphere only in adults (Franklin et al., 2008). That is informative rather than damaging: it places language exactly where this account expects it, among the Level 3 determinants that shape the percept from above, not among the sensory mechanisms that deliver it.

The deeper linguistic point is that the very inventory of color words is a construction, and its variation across cultures makes our own "color" concept look parochial. Since Berlin and Kay (1969), the cross-linguistic study of basic color terms has documented striking regularities across unrelated languages, including the recurring naming motifs that emerge when hundreds of languages are compared (Lindsey & Brown, 2009). But it has also documented languages that carve the space quite differently, with different numbers and boundaries of terms, and with terms whose basic status tracks culture rather than the spectrum (Paramei, 2005; Wierzbicka, 1990; Dedrick, 1998). Even within a single language the interface between perception and name is not fixed: individual differences in hue scaling imply a non-uniform perceptual prior behind color naming (Peterzell, 2026b), and corpus evidence shows the lexical boundary between blue and brown still shifting in American English (Peterzell, Albers, Johnson, & Paramei, 2026).

The sharpest version is what may be called the Kala paradox: some languages appear to lack a general, abstract word for "color" at all, naming instead the visible qualities of particular things, so that the very category the science takes as given, "color," is itself a culturally specific abstraction rather than a natural kind (Levisen, 2026). If a language can do without the concept, then "color" is not the joint at which the phenomenon divides; it is one culture's way of bundling a stimulus, an experience, and an event under a single, convenient word. And it is a bundling that spread: the abstract category traveled with colonial languages and with the mass-produced goods of industry, the shirts, cars, and flags that differ in color alone where little in the older material world did, so that "color" as a free-standing dimension is not merely provincial but exported, an artifact of commerce and empire as much as of cognition (Levisen, 2026). That is a claim of interest well beyond vision science, to linguists and anthropologists, and it is a claim the percept-centered framework cannot even state, because it has quietly assumed the category it should be questioning.

Affect.

Color and feeling are coupled in both directions. The ecological valence account shows that liking for a color tracks the average affective value of the objects that color belongs to, so preferences are learned from a lifetime of colored things rather than fixed at the retina (Palmer & Schloss, 2010; Schloss & Palmer, 2017). In the other direction, color carries and modulates emotional and motivational states, and the same stimulus can look and function differently in different affective contexts (Elliot & Maier, 2014). The color-emotion associations this produces are robust and partly universal across dozens of countries, yet further shaped by language and geography, so the mapping is neither arbitrary nor fixed (Jonauskaite et al., 2020; Jonauskaite & Mohr, 2025). These are not associations laid over a fixed sensation; they belong to appearance and behavior, and they are dimensions of the event that a science of hue leaves on the table.

Attention, binding, and perceptual construction. Attention, binding, and grouping do not merely select among finished colors; they help determine which color is seen and where.

Binding color to form requires attention, the founding claim of feature-integration theory (Treisman & Gelade, 1980), and without attention color and form can misbind into illusory conjunctions (Treisman & Schmidt, 1982); manipulations of attention and visual search can in turn measurably shift perceived hue and salience. This matters structurally. In the cortex, color is carried largely by double-opponent cells that are also sensitive to space and orientation (Shapley & Hawken, 2011). The pathway encodes color and spatial information together: single cortical cells can extract form from the combined chromatic and achromatic signal (Billock, 1995), and most cells in the primate visual system carry color and form jointly, color vision inseparable from form and object vision (Werner & Webster, 2002). Color is a kind of object vision, not an attribute painted onto objects after they are seen. And brightness, the least "cognitive" of the percept's three dimensions, is no exception: it is not read off the light but computed from edges and context, which is why the same luminance can look light or dark depending on what surrounds it (Rudd, 2014).

Prediction. Much of the above can be phrased as prediction: the visual system combines incoming chromatic evidence with prior expectations, and the percept is where the two settle, the territory of Bayesian and predictive-processing accounts (Brainard, 2009; Clark, 2013; Rao & Ballard, 1999), which the model does not rival but contains, as the top-down half of the architecture (Section 5). Memory color, constancy, and the dress are all, in this vocabulary, cases of a strong prior doing visible work.

Physiology beyond the image. Some of the event never becomes a hue at all. Light also drives circadian timing, alerting, pupil size, and autonomic arousal through retinal pathways that do chromatic work without ever producing a conscious color. Chief among them are the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, which contain the photopigment melanopsin (Provencio et al., 2000); they help set the circadian clock (G. C. Brainard et al., 2001) and shape a range of other non-visual and visual responses (Spitschan, 2019). These non-image-forming responses are genuine, lawful dimensions of the chromatic event, and they are rendered invisible by a science that defines its subject as the reportable percept.

Culture. Culture shapes several of these determinants at once: it supplies the learned object associations, the language categories, and the affective and social conventions through which a stimulus is construed. Those categories are not arbitrary but are shaped by what a community needs to communicate, so that color naming across languages partitions the space in nearoptimal yet culture-specific ways and tracks how useful each color is to talk about (Regier, Kay, & Khetarpal, 2007; Gibson et al., 2017). Culture is less a separate arrow onto the percept than the source of many of the others, and it is why two observers with identical eyes can inhabit different chromatic worlds.

Taken one at a time, each of these can be waved away as a modulation of an underlying sensation. Taken together, they describe a percept with no stable, determinant-free core to modulate. That absence of a bare sensory given is the constructive claim. A percept is not found in the stimulus. It is forged in the encounter.

4. Reciprocal determinism

The influences in Section 3 are usually imagined as one-way: the world causes the percept, and everything else decorates it afterward. The account's central structural claim is that the traffic runs both ways. The percept at Level 2 shapes the Level 3 systems, and those systems reach back and shape the percept, continuously, so that what a person consciously sees is partly constructed by the memory, language, attention, and affect the stimulus engages. This is reciprocal determinism: the relation between the levels is a loop, not a pipeline, and the color you experience is its settling point, not a fixed input. The term is borrowed from social-cognitive psychology, where Bandura (1978) named reciprocal determinism the mutual causation among behavior, cognition, and environment. On his account, a person is neither the passive product of circumstances nor their autonomous author, but both at once. Here it names the mutual causation between the percept and the systems of the event, and it is itself an instance of this account's method, conscripting a powerful idea from a field that never studied color. The principle reaches well beyond color. The cerebral cortex is wired for it: the great majority of its pathways are reciprocal, a feedforward projection almost always matched by a feedback one (Felleman & Van Essen, 1991), and cortical responses are shaped as much by recurrent and top-down signals as by the feedforward sweep (Lamme & Roelfsema, 2000).

Bidirectional, mutually determining interaction appears wherever the nervous system has been examined closely: the descending modulation of pain in gate-control theory (Melzack & Wall, 1965), the assimilation and contrast by which each stimulus's appearance depends on its neighbors, the continual traffic between cognition and emotion (Pessoa, 2008). In resting color perception on reciprocal determinism, the account grounds it in a principle the brain uses everywhere, not a mechanism devised for color alone: a venerable idea that here becomes the royal road from a science of the color percept to a science of the whole chromatic event.

Reciprocal determinism is what separates this account from a merely sophisticated feedforward story. A purely bottom-up model can accommodate top-down "biases" as corrections applied to a sensory estimate; the estimate remains primary. It cannot easily accommodate the claim that the percept and the systems that shape it are constituents of one event, each partly making the other, with neither prior. 5. The constructivist model of color: an architecture that contains the classics

Beside the taxonomic claim stands a second, and a reader who rejects the first can still accept it.

Figure 1A draws it as an architecture. A feedforward chain runs from the physical stimulus, through the eye's optics, to the retinal image. From there it passes through the receptors (cones, rods, and the intrinsically photosensitive ganglion cells), the single-and double-opponent cells, and the ventral color areas, to a percept of hue, saturation, and brightness. That chain is the bottom-up account trichromatic and opponent-process theory built. Onto the percept, the Level 3 systems do their constructive work, and this top-down half is the territory predictiveprocessing and Bayesian accounts have mapped. The model is not either half by itself but the two joined: the percept is the single Level 2 point where the whole system meets, fed forward from the receptors on one side and constructed from memory, language, and the rest of Level 3 on the other.

The relationship to existing theory is not rivalry but containment (Figure 2). Trichromacy (Lomonosov, 1756 (Lomonosov, /1970;; Young, 1802; Helmholtz, 1867) describes how Level 1 is transduced, and cone-opponent physiology how it is first recombined; both are adopted whole. The classical perceptual-opponent stage, the claim that red-green and blue-yellow organize color appearance (Hering, 1874 (Hering, /1964)), is now seriously contested, and the account keeps what remains well supported while questioning whether these axes form a fixed organizing frame for appearance.

Cortical color proves to be tuned to a far broader set of directions than the two opponent axes (Conway, Moeller, & Tsao, 2007). A recent and prominent case holds that Hering's opponentcolors theory has reached the end of its usefulness as an account of appearance (Conway, Malik-Moraleda, & Gibson, 2023). The empirical challenge, though, was pressed earlier by Lindsey and Brown, whose cross-cultural naming data had already shown that the world's languages do not privilege the Hering primaries as landmarks (Lindsey & Brown, 2009; Lindsey, Brown, & Lange, 2020). And under retinal stabilization, observers can even be brought to see the reddish-greens and bluish-yellows that a hard opponent stage forbids (Billock, Gleason, & Tsou, 2001). Unconscious inference (Helmholtz, 1867), predictive processing, retinex constancy (Land, 1977), and Gestalt organization (Koffka, 1935; Köhler, 1929; Wertheimer, 1923; Wertheimer & Peterzell, 2010) describe parts of the construction; they are imported and given chromatic work to do. The account does not overturn these; it houses them, assigns each a level and a role, and adds the claim they lacked: that the assembled system, not any one layer of it, is what color perception is. The classical theories are not rivals to be defeated but the parts this account arranges, included and, in being arranged, transcended.

6. What the account is not

Because the account rejects naïve realism, it is easily mistaken for one of the several ways of dissolving color into construction, convention, or illusion, of which the philosophy of color offers a full menu (Brown & Macpherson, 2020). It is none of them. Figure 3 marks the distance: the account takes the middle path between two pills, neither the blue pill of realism nor the red pill of dissolution. It is not eliminativism. On the view that nothing in the world is really colored, color is a systematic error and the honest conclusion is that colors do not exist. The present account holds the opposite: color exists, as an event, reliably produced and open to measurement. What it denies is only that color is a mind-independent property of surfaces, the sophisticated physicalist realism on which colors are reflectance-types waiting to be detected (Byrne & Hilbert, 2003). On the present view, color is real but constructed in the encounter, not a property the surface possesses on its own. Perception buries naïve realism in every waking moment.

It is not the interface theory of perception. On that view, perceptual experience is a speciesspecific user interface, tuned by evolution for fitness rather than truth, so that color, like a desktop icon, reveals nothing about what lies beneath (Hoffman, Singh, & Prakash, 2015). The account shares the rejection of naïve realism but stops short of that metaphysics. It stakes nothing on the world being hidden or unreal; it holds only that color is constructed upon a real stimulus, and that the construction is itself a proper object of study, not a veil over one. The move is old: the interface theory revives, in the language of evolutionary game theory, Bishop Berkeley's idealism, on which sensible qualities exist only in being perceived (Berkeley, 1710 (Berkeley, /1949 (Berkeley, , 1713 (Berkeley, /1949)). This account declines that idealism rather than refuting it; where Berkeley dissolved the mind-independent world, it keeps the world's contribution as a real, measurable stimulus, one pole of the reciprocal relation.

It is not a color-is-only-convention relativism. That the inventory of color words varies, and that construction draws on learned and cultural materials, does not make color arbitrary. The construction is lawful, constrained at every turn by the physics of the stimulus and the structure of the observer, and its regularities are exactly what the science can study. Reciprocal determinism has two poles, and one of them is a real, measurable stimulus.

7. Individual differences as the key to the structure

There is a deep irony in this subject's history. The variability color science has worked hardest to eliminate is the very signal a constructivist theory most needs: the scatter between observers, the person for whom red is calming, the synesthete for whom a letter is crimson, the aphantasic for whom imagined blue does not exist. If a chromatic stimulus initiated a single fixed percept, between-observer variation would be noise to average away. If it initiates a constructed, multidimensional event, then different observers occupy different regions of the event's space, and the pattern of their differences is a direct readout of the space's dimensions (Figure 4). The same conclusion has been argued from the philosophy of color: there can be color variation without any objective color that some observers get right and others get wrong (Brown, 2022). A minority of investigators have always treated that variation as signal, not nuisance. Pickford catalogued individual differences in color vision at mid-century (Pickford, 1951) and later took up the color vision of artists and the psychology of visual aesthetics (Pickford, 1965 (Pickford, , 1972)); Guilford charted how color preference varies across observers (Guilford, 1934); and the modern factor-analytic program, developed by Webster, Bosten, Peterzell, and others, treats inter-observer covariance as its primary data. The same dimensional, individualdifferences logic has been carried into empirical aesthetics, where the structure of beauty and aesthetic response, of which color preference is one strand, resolves into a stable set of factors that still vary from observer to observer (Marković, 2012; Marković, Bulut Allred, et al., 2026). The programmatic case has been made forcefully from within vision science. Reviewing the field, Bosten (2022) argues that variability arises at every level of the color system, that each of us therefore inhabits a distinct perceptual world, and that individual differences are not only worth studying in their own right but usable as a methodological tool for exposing the mechanisms of perception (see also Mollon, Bosten, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017). That review builds the case within color vision and, but for a closing gesture toward ecology and philosophy, keeps it there. The account here puts the same variability to a different use, as evidence about the explanatory object itself: the percept whose scatter these programs measure is not the phenomenon but one dimension of a larger constructed event.

The variability is real and structured, not error. Settings of the unique hues, the reds and blues that observers call pure with nothing of a neighboring hue in them, differ widely between people and yet are stable within a person across sessions. That combination is the signature of a real individual parameter rather than measurement noise. The photograph of "the dress" split observers into groups seeing white-and-gold or blue-and-black, a difference now traced to divergent assumptions about the illuminant lighting the scene (Lafer-Sousa, Hermann, & Conway, 2015; Brainard & Hurlbert, 2015): the same pixels, different priors, different colors, a natural experiment in construction that made the point to millions before it made it to the journals. Carriers of anomalous cone genotypes, and the small number of likely tetrachromats, may command color distinctions the rest of us cannot make at all, so that a stimulus that is one color for most observers is two for them (Jameson, Highnote, & Wasserman, 2001; Jameson et al., 2020; Jordan, Deeb, Bosten, & Mollon, 2010).

Treated as noise, these facts are an embarrassment to be pooled away. Treated as signal, they are a method. Dimension-reduction applied to the covariation of many chromatic measures across many observers can recover the latent channels that underlie color perception, because traits that rise and fall together are likely served by a common mechanism (Emery, Volbrecht, Peterzell, & Webster, 2017; Peterzell, 2016). The differences between people are not the obstacle to understanding the mechanism. They are how the mechanism shows itself, and a framework that makes them central rather than marginal is, for that reason alone, worth adopting. This is not to claim that individual differences are the one right way to study color; group methods, physiology, and computational modeling remain indispensable. The point is narrower, and harder to dismiss: variation between observers has been systematically undervalued, and a mature science of the chromatic event will have to weight it far more heavily than a century of averaging has allowed.

8. Neither necessary nor sufficient: the percept comes apart from the stimulus

If color were simply the visual system's report of a wavelength, the percept and the stimulus would travel together. They do not, and the cases where they come apart are among the clearest evidence that the percept is constructed (Figure 5). The stimulus is not sufficient. The same light yields different colors depending on the constructive context, most dramatically in the dress but routinely in every color-constancy phenomenon. There, a surface keeps its apparent color across large changes in the light it reflects, because the visual system discounts the illuminant it infers, drawing on strong, structured priors about natural daylight (Bloj, Kersten, & Hurlbert, 1999; Pearce et al., 2014; Hurlbert, 2019). The coupling runs the other way too: changing the inferred illumination shifts not only apparent color but judgments of an object's quality, such as the ripeness or freshness of food (Cebioglu, Mistry, Vasey, & Hurlbert, 2025). Appearance tracks the inferred cause, not the raw input.

And the stimulus is not necessary. The most ordinary demonstration is available to most readers at this moment: close the eyes and picture a red apple, and for most people a color appears, vivid for some and faint for others, with no red light anywhere in the room. That everyday fact is already decisive, because it shows the color-generating machinery running with the stimulus removed, which is only possible if the color was being constructed rather than merely received. Two populations sharpen the point from opposite directions. People with aphantasia lack voluntary visual imagery; asked to picture that apple, they report that nothing colored appears. Vivid imagers, at the far end of the same continuum, describe imagined color almost as saturated as the real thing (Zeman, Milton, Della Sala, Dewar, et al., 2020). The capacity to construct color internally is itself an individual-differences dimension. Synesthetes make the complementary point. For a grapheme-color synesthete, a printed letter or numeral reliably and involuntarily triggers a specific color that no wavelength on the page can account for, and these associations are consistent within a person over years and idiosyncratic across people (Simner, 2013; Ward, 2013). The differences between synesthetes themselves are grounded in measurable differences in brain structure and function (Rouw & Scholte, 2010).

Hallucination makes the point most starkly. In migraine, flicker, and psychedelic states the visual system generates vivid color and structured form with nothing external to see. The recurring geometry of these visions, the lattices, spirals, tunnels, and cobwebs catalogued as Klüver's form constants, takes its shape from the architecture of primary visual cortex rather than from any stimulus (Bressloff et al., 2001; Billock & Tsou, 2012). In each of these cases the Level 3 machinery produces, or fails to produce, a Level 2 color with the stimulus removed. Only a constructed color could behave this way.

The developmental and sight-restoration cases make the same point across time. When a person blind from birth has sight restored, the color percept arrives almost at once, while the object, its meaning, and its uses have to be constructed over days and weeks. Cheselden's (1728) young patient, couched for congenital cataract, could tell colors apart almost immediately but could not tell a cube from a sphere by sight. Gregory and Wallace's (1963) patient S.B., grafted after roughly fifty years of blindness, showed the same pattern, color given among the first things and least useful. And the newly sighted children of Project Prakash cannot immediately match an object seen to one felt, acquiring the cross-modal mapping only with days of experience (Held et al., 2011), the very failure Berkeley predicted in his New Theory of Vision (Berkeley, 1709 (Berkeley, /1948)). Why hue should arrive so early while the object lags has been argued to reflect a developmental coupling in which color cues help scaffold luminance-grounded object representations (Peterzell, 2026a. Hue is given early and cheaply. The chromatic event, the web of object, meaning, and value a color belongs to, is assembled. That dissociation between the quick percept and the slow event is exactly what a levels account predicts and what a single-percept account struggles to explain.

9. That the substrate itself is plastic

One assumption has so far survived untouched, and it too gives way: that Level 1 and its transduction are fixed, and only the higher machinery is malleable. The color-vision substrate is itself adjustable. Adult observers recalibrate their sense of what looks neutral after sustained exposure to a chromatically biased environment, and this renormalization is a standing feature of the mature system (Webster, 2015). The relative numbers of the cone types, which vary by more than an order of magnitude between people, have surprisingly little effect on color appearance, implying a normalizing process that tunes perception to the prevailing input rather than reading it off the receptor counts (Neitz, Carroll, Yamauchi, Neitz, & Williams, 2002). Most dramatically, gene therapy that added a third cone type to red-green colorblind adult primates produced new chromatic discriminations, showing that even the receptor complement is not a fixed given that the rest of the system must simply accept (Mancuso et al., 2009). If the substrate itself is tuned by experience, and can be altered and then exploited, the neat division between a fixed physical-physiological base and a malleable interpretation does not hold. Construction goes all the way down.

10. Seeing the construction directly

The construction of color need not always be inferred; it can sometimes be brought into view, and painters and certain contemplative traditions are where the science can, cautiously, learn how. Painters learn to unsee the object and attend to the patch of colored light in front of them, a trained reversal of the ordinary priority of object over appearance. Their difficulty in doing it is a report on how automatic the construction normally is, and the curiosities of color vision that painters exploit have themselves become an object of study (Jameson, 2026; Werner, 2026). Van Gogh's late landscapes push this to the edge (Figure 6): a sky rendered in a yellow no evening sky emits, a field in complementary contrasts that belong to the painter's construction rather than the meadow's spectrum, and yet read as intensely, almost oppressively real. The painting works precisely because color is not a readout of the stimulus; the painter is manipulating the construction directly, and the viewer's Level 3 systems finish the job. The heightened seeing can also arrive unbidden. Emerging in 1843 from a breakdown so severe that light had become unbearable, Fechner stepped into his garden and saw the plants blaze with an inner radiance, the vision that moved him to write his book on the soul-life of plants (Fechner, 1848; Heidelberger, 2004). Whether deeper sight or a convalescent's transfigured mood, it was seeing changed from within. Visionary painters such as Alex Grey set out to externalize that intensified color deliberately, a heightened seeing, not a truer one (Grey, 1990). Offered as illustration rather than evidence, several contemplative traditions describe, in their own vocabularies, the same distinction between bare perception and the conceptualized perception that ordinarily overwrites it, and between the seer and the seen. It bears emphasis that the classical Indian schools treated this as a theory of perception, not a metaphysics. Nyāya analyzed the passage from bare, preconceptual sensing (nirvikalpaka) to the conceptualized percept (savikalpaka) that follows within a moment, the very layering this account draws between the incoming signal and the construction placed upon it (Matilal, 1986). Buddhist epistemology pressed a parallel analysis of how the mind supplies what perception seems merely to find (Coseru, 2012). The Advaita D g-D śya-Viveka ṛ ṛ turned the separation of witnessing awareness from its constructed object into a discipline a trained observer can learn to notice rather than merely infer (Nikhilananda, 1931). The parallels are structural, not mechanistic: convergent first-person testimony, from people who spent lifetimes attending to perception, that what we normally take as a simple given is in fact assembled, so long as the testimony is not mistaken for proof.

11. Falsifiable commitments and a research program

A constructivist account risks sounding unfalsifiable, a frame elastic enough to absorb any result. It is not, and it is worth stating the commitments that could come back the wrong way.

First, if attention and binding are constituents of the chromatic event rather than machinery beside it, the covariation of chromatic tasks across observers should yield a binding-andattention factor under dimension reduction; it either emerges or it does not. Second, if a conditioned or otherwise learned color is a Level 3 construction, the generalization gradient around it should bend at the learned category boundary rather than follow the low-level coneopponent metric; the two predictions are distinguishable. Third, the method the program rests on, recovering latent chromatic structure from deliberately small and extreme samples chosen for their informativeness rather than their representativeness (Peterzell, 2026d), either replicates or fails. Fourth, the account sides with the challenge to perceptual opponency, and is therefore committed to the red-green and blue-yellow axes not behaving as a fixed organizing frame for color appearance; should the classical opponent structure prove robust after all, that commitment is wrong. None of these is rhetorical.

The positive program follows from treating color as a Level 3 event. It means measuring the event, not only the percept: characterizing the dimensions along which chromatic stimuli move memory, affect, physiology, and action, even shaping ostensibly non-chromatic judgments such as numerosity (Peterzell, Arrighi, et al., 2026). It means designs that treat individual differences as the primary signal rather than error to be pooled away. Central to this is mapping the factor structure of Level 3 itself: recovering, from the covariation of these systems across observers, which determinants are facets of a common factor and which are separable, and whether important factors remain that no current taxonomy names. The predicted binding-andattention factor is the first test case. Recovered and validated, such dimensions become candidate computational phenotypes: stable, reproducible patterns of visual processing that characterize an individual observer at an intermediate explanatory level between behavior and biology, a construct a companion paper develops for vision more broadly (Peterzell, 2026c). It means larger and more varied samples than color psychophysics has typically used, and analyses that mine existing datasets for the variability they discarded. And it means asking, of any proposed regularity, at which level it lives, so that we stop mistaking a fact about the percept for a fact about the event, or a property of one observer's construction for a property of color itself. The reward is a color science that can speak to the questions the percept-centered framework cannot: why a color calms or alarms, why it means what it means, why two people looking at one light can honestly disagree about what they see.

12. Conclusion

To understand color as a constructed event is to change what the science is about. If two people can differ in the color they see from the same light, then perception is not a faithful mapping of an objective world but a selective, active construction, and in measuring and explaining only the average we may be studying something with no real existence. A science that mistakes the surface for the phenomenon can measure it forever without understanding it. The percept of hue, saturation, and brightness that has stood at the center of color science for three centuries is real and important, but it is one part of a larger happening, constructed at the moment of seeing out of memory, language, attention, affect, and culture, and reaching back into all of them. The account offered here keeps everything the classical science established and adds what it was missing, a model of the whole event and of the construction of the percept within it, and it earns its keep not by complaint but by explanation: the dress, memory color, the newly sighted, aphantasia and synesthesia, and the reliable differences between observers stop being anomalies and become the evidence. The ambition is correspondingly large: to be the frame in which this generation's best color science, together with ideas imported from fields that never studied color, can sit as parts of a single phenomenon, held together by what is genuinely new here, the three-level taxonomy, the reciprocal determinism of percept and event, and the use of individual differences as the primary evidence for how color is constructed. To keep calling one part "color," and to keep it alone at the center, is to carve the subject at the wrong joint. The proposal here is to look beyond it, to the whole chromatic event, and to let the differences between us, so long treated as noise, become the evidence for how the seeing is done. Color is not discovered. It is assembled.

The same model as one event: the stimulus at the hub generates the percept (inner ring; "color experience" denotes the same Level 2), the visible core of a larger Level 3 event in four families, each reciprocally related to the percept.

Figure 2.

Figure 2. Theories subsumed by the constructivist account. Every theory the account adopts, modifies, contests, rejects, or originates, tagged by origin (color science, or a general theory of mind imported and given a chromatic role) and by level. The account is a container: most of what it holds was built elsewhere and is here assigned a level and a job.

Figure 3.

Figure 3. Neither pill. Naïve realism (blue pill): color is given in the world. Radical dissolution (red pill): color is nothing but construction or convention. The account takes neither, a constructed event anchored to a real cause. Rejecting realism does not require embracing unreality.

Figure 4.

Figure 4. One stimulus, many constructed events. One physical stimulus (Level 1) becomes markedly different events (Level 3) across observers who differ in receptors, wiring, language, memory, imagery, affect, and culture. Pooled across many observers, the variation is not noise: its covariance collapses onto a few latent dimensions, the target of the individual-differences program. The scatter is schematic.

Figure 5.

Figure5. Where the three levels come apart. Every pairwise dissociation among the physical stimulus (Level 1), the color experience (Level 2), and the constructed event (Level 3) is attested; were the three one thing, the figure could not exist. A stimulus is neither necessary for color (imagery, dreaming, and synesthesia produce it without one) nor sufficient (the same light yields different colors).

Figure 6.

Figure6. Seeing the construction. In van Gogh's sower, color is manifestly constructed, not transcribed: the chromatic relations belong to the painter, not to any spectrum a field and sky emit, yet the scene reads as vividly real. Painters and several contemplative traditions offer convergent testimony that the assembly can, with effort, be partly observed, structural parallels, not mechanistic claims.
