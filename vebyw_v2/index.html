How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Introduction

Artificially-Generated Non-Consensual Intimate Imagery (AI-NCII) sometimes referred to as deepfake pornographyfoot_0 , is a form of image-based sexual abuse (IBSA) in which a person's likeness is embedded into sexually explicit images or videos without their consent, and often without their awareness, using artificial intelligence (AI). AI-NCII represents a relatively new form of gender-based violence, and estimates suggest that 99% of all AI-NCII victims are women (Hero, 2023). AI-NCII has not only become more accessible (Murphy et al., 2025) with many applications and websites dedicated to its generation (Winter and Salter, 2020) and sharing (Morgan et al., 2026), but the growing sophistication of AI also enables the creation of highly realistic content making it difficult for individuals to prove the fraudulent nature of the AI-NCII content (Gockel, 2024).

In England and Wales, the non-consensual sharing and creation of AI-NCII have been illegal under the Sexual Offences Act 2003 (Government, 2026) since 2024 and early 2026, respectively. This legal recognition establishes AI-NCII as sexual abuse; however, the criminalisation of the act does not necessarily determine how individuals perceive AI-NCII victims. Victim characteristics, including identity, occupation, and perceived conformity to social norms can influence how individuals are judged or blamed (Flynn et al., 2023). Through an increased understanding of how victim characteristics, unrelated to the crime, alter judgements of victim blame and perceived harm, we can gain insight into how social perceptions may be contingent upon who the victim is and how much they conform to expectations of a legitimate or "ideal" victim.

An ideal victim is a person or group who, when victimised, fit the perceived characteristics of a complete and legitimate victim (Christie, 1986).

To be afforded ideal victim status, a victim should meet the following criteria. They should: be perceived as weak in relation to the perpetrator; be engaged in a respectable activity; be situated in a location where they could not reasonably be blamed for being; have been victimised by a perpetrator portrayed as "bad"; and have no prior relationship with the perpetrator. Victim characteristics, particularly when they may be considered as belonging to or defining victims as minority or stigmatised groups, can result in victims falling outside common social narratives or norms, undermining their ideal victim status. This may be because these characteristics violate expectations concerning who is vulnerable to victimisation and how a victim is expected to behave. Expectancy violations theory (Burgoon, 2015) suggests that when expectations are violated, attention is drawn to the element that is perceived as atypical, which in the context of victimisation, can lead to an increase in blame. The ideal victim framework provides a means to describe who is likely to be perceived as a genuine and credible victim, whereas expectancy violations theory offers a way to interpret why individuals or groups may fall outside the ideal victim framework.

In related research into contact sexual violence, rape victims are blamed more when scenarios align with preconceived stereotyped beliefs that the victim was somehow responsible for the rape or could have prevented the perpetrator from raping them (Sleath and Bull, 2010). Some people expect certain behaviours from rape victims (such as screaming, shouting, or crying) which do not necessarily align with actual reported behaviours (Sleath and Woodhams, 2014), indicating an incongruency between the expected narrative and the truth. Despite non-expressiveness being a commonly reported behaviour in victim-survivor accounts, research shows that when the expected narrative is violated (that is, rape victims depicted as non-expressive), they are rated as less credible by people who expect high expressiveness from the victim (Hackett et al., 2008). This increased negative perception is likely driven by the violated expectation and suggests that by not fulfilling the expected narrative of high expressiveness the victim is not perceived as an ideal victim.

The present study seeks to understand how individual perceptions of stigmatised and minority groups can influence the perceived blame and harm for victims of AI-NCII using the ideal victim framework and expectancy violations theory. Using vignettes -short descriptions of hypothetical scenarios, followed by capturing participant judgements -this preregistered study explores how stigmatised and minority groups fall outside ideal victim designation.

One stigmatised group particularly relevant to AI-NCII is sex workers.

Despite their prevalence among those targeted by AI-NCII, their victimhood is often overlooked; AI-NCII frequently appropriates and manipulates the sexual content featuring sex workers to be used as the bodies upon which faces are superimposed. For example, Maddocks (2020) describes sex workers whose bodies are appropriated in AI-NCII as invisible victims, noting that public attention tends to centre on the individual whose face has been superimposed rather than the sex worker. More recently, it has been demonstrated that individuals whose bodies, rather than faces, were used in AI-NCII sexual abuse were attributed greater blame and less anticipated harm, with these effects exacerbated when the victim was identified as a sex worker (Fido et al., 2026).

As a result, the first of three research questions is, how does professional identity change public perceptions of victims in AI-NCII scenarios? By manipulating the professional identity of the victim portrayed in a vignette, as either a sex worker who posts consensual sexually-explicit content to OnlyFans, or as an office worker who posts casual non-sexuallyexplicit images to social media platforms, the role of stigma can be assessed.

Related research with IBSA and sharing of NCII has examined how a number of factors influence perceptions of victim blame and harm. One study examined relationship status, finding that greater blame is attributed to victims who are portrayed as being in a casual relationship than those in committed relationships (Fido et al., 2025). Additionally, a victim of NCII sharing may not be considered a victim if the creation and initial sharing of intimate images is not deemed a respectable act (Call, 2021). Other research has shown that victims who are considered more sexually promiscuous (operationalised as those perceived to not being in a committed relationship, Fido et al., 2025) may not meet many people's expected level of respectability and so cannot be considered an ideal victim.

Further evidence indicates that victims of non-consensual sharing of intimate images are also judged more negatively when the original sexual content was created consensually by the victim (Naezer and van Oosterhout, 2021; Zvi, 2022). The stigma associated with perceived sexual promiscuity may violate individual expectations of modesty which leads to victims being perceived as having engaged in less-than-respectable acts or as having placed themselves in circumstances for which they are not considered blamelessthereby violating the criteria required for ideal victim status.

Greater sexual objectification can occur when individuals are perceived to be engaged in more sexual jobs or activities (Kellie et al., 2021), and sexual objectification is a factor within sexual abuse that can result in victims of sex-based violence being judged more negatively than victims of other crimes (Alemi et al., 2025; Desroches et al., 2025). Victims of both AI-NCII and NCII scenarios are objectified more than scenarios describing the non-consensual use of images in a non-sexual manner (e.g., for marketing) (Shepherd et al., 2026), highlighting how sexual objectification is not only dependent on victim characteristics, but the context as well.

Another way in which individuals may not meet the criteria of an ideal victim is through their identification as sex workers, as societal perceptions may characterise their work as inconsistent with the expectation that an ideal victim should be engaged in a respectable activity. For example, sex workers portrayed as victims of rape are blamed more than non-sex workers (Sprankle et al., 2018). In modern society, sex work remains a heavily discriminated and stigmatised identity (Grittner and Walsh, 2020), with sex workers being negatively portrayed by the general public and the media (Bowen et al., 2021; West, 2025). Online sex workers, such as those who distribute content via sharing platforms (e.g., OnlyFans or Fansly), have been reported to experience similar levels of stigma to those engaging in in-person sex work (Stutz et al., 2024). Therefore, sex workers can be considered a stigmatised group, as individuals involved in sex work are often perceived negatively by the public and more widely.

The second of the three research questions focuses on minority groups and how they may fall outside common social narratives, thereby challenging expectations surrounding ideal victimhood. Specifically we examined transgender individuals as a minority group in comparison with cisgender individuals-this comparison is important given evidence that gender minority groups such as transgender individuals are more likely to experience digital sexual harassment than cisgender individuals (Powell et al., 2020).

And so we asked, how does gender modality change public perceptions of victims in AI-NCII scenarios? As with Powell et al. (2020) we use cisgender to identify individuals whose experience and/or expression of gender aligns with that assigned at birth, specifically using this term to distinguish between the experimental manipulation of gender modality.

A common thread across victim blaming is not necessarily linked to the type of crime or violence, but rather to the perpetuation of gender stereotypes and sexist beliefs. Indeed, gender is a common predictor of victim blaming, with men blaming victims more (Flynn et al., 2023; Hayes et al., 2013) and perceiving victims as experiencing less harm (Eaton et al., 2026).

The gender of both victim and perpetrator can contribute to violations of expectations. Scenarios involving female victims are perceived as more criminal and more harmful than those involving male victims (Fido et al., 2022).

These gender-based violations are specifically violations of the common narratives frequently described in public spheres; examples of gender violence typically portray it as a heterosexual act between a man as the perpetrator and a woman as the victim (Donovan and Barnes, 2018). As a result, if these expectations form the common narrative for gender violence, deviations in further examples (such as changes in victim or perpetrator gender) can result in expectancy violations leading to increased victim blame.

Building upon these findings, research on AI-NCII has not yet examined perceptions of transgender victims, and work in this area still largely relies on binary gender conditions (Kira, 2024), despite transgender and genderdiverse individuals being at a higher risk of sexual abuse than cisgender individuals (Abern et al., 2023; Powell et al., 2020).

In research regarding gender, it is necessary not just to consider the influence of victim gender but also the gender of the judgement-maker. Research has highlighted clear gender differences in responses to victims, for example, (Fido et al., 2022) found complex gender interactions within AI-NCII perceptions, indicating that male participants attributed greater blame to celebrity victims whereas female participants placed greater blame on non-celebrity victims, and male participants consider sharing NCII to be more acceptable (Umbach et al., 2024).

Finally, the third research question explores the extent to which victims experience a perceived loss of control upon discovering the existence of AI-NCII depicting them, either passively through the notification from an anonymous source or actively through an online service. That is, how does the way a victim finds out about the existence of AI-NCII change how a situation is perceived?

The impacts of AI-NCII upon an individual can be characterised in terms of blame and perceived harm-victims of IBSA have reported increased anxiety, as well as a loss of control and agency (Aborisade, 2022; Bates, 2017; Murça et al., 2023), which echo those reported by victims of contact sexual assault (Frazier, 2003). In IBSA cases, victims highlight how the nonconsensual sharing undermines their bodily autonomy and deprives them control over with whom representations of their bodies are shared (Nygård et al., 2025). IBSA victims have made clear their desire to reclaim control over the images, along with their lives (Rackley et al., 2021). As a consequence, it is beneficial to determine how perceived victim control is considered in the context of AI-NCII and how individuals become aware of the illicit material.

Attitudes and beliefs towards sexual violence provide an important framework for understanding victim blaming and evaluated victim harm. Of particular interest are myths, where myths refer to false or prejudiced beliefs that excuse perpetrators, minimise the abuse, or increase victim blaming.

Research on contact sexual assault has identified rape myths as being linked to increased acceptance of interpersonal violence and acceptance of broader sex-role stereotypes (Burt, 1980). These beliefs shape judgements of victims, particularly when future scenarios or cases deviate from an individual's expectations (Frese et al., 2004; Hackett et al., 2008). Similar findings arise in IBSA, with increased acceptance of IBSA myths (Powell et al., 2019) linked to greater self-reported perpetration of IBSA. This is echoed specifically within AI-NCII being positively correlated with watching, sharing, and creating AI-NCII (Twomey et al., 2025).

Taken together, the literature indicates significant gaps in understanding how AI-NCII victims are perceived, particularly among minority groups (e.g., transgender individuals) and stigmatised groups (e.g., individuals who create consensual sexual content). The present research therefore examines how AI-NCII victim characteristics influence judgements related to victim blame, perceived harm, acceptability of perpetrator actions, and victim control of the situation.

Vignettes

Participants read one vignette. Each vignette outlined an AI-NCII incident involving a victim who posted images of themselves online, which were subsequently stolen by an unknown perpetrator and used to generate AI-NCII without the victim's consent or knowledge. The perpetrator was described as having shared the videos online, with the victim subsequently discovering their existence. The four vignettes differed as a function of the victim's professional identity (an office worker posting images to social media or a sex worker posting to OnlyFans), the means the victim found out (whether they were alerted anonymously, or used an online service to be alerted), as well as the victim's gender modality (cisgender woman, transgender woman, cisgender man, or transgender man). Unisex names were used in the vignettes for both victims (Taylor, Sam, Alex, Charlie) and perpetrators (Rowan, Elliot, Robin, Jordan). By providing as little identifying information about the perpetrator as possible, the scenarios reinforced the perception that the perpetrator was a stranger to the victim. The experts read the four base vignettes (showing possible gender modality as [trans/cis]gender [wo]man throughout) and rated each vignette on a 1 -4 scale for the following questions, "is the vignette clear and easy to understand?" (unclear, somewhat clear, clear, very clear), "is the vignette relevant to the intended research aims?" (irrelevant -very relevant), and "does the vignette present a realistic and plausible scenario?" (unrealisticvery realistic). The experts were able to provide qualitative feedback on the text. Following the feedback, vignettes were modified in line with ratings and comments before being sent back to the expert panel for a second review. This content validity approach is similar to that endorsed by Lynn (1986).

Both rounds of expert review were assessed using an item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and a scale-level average (S-CVI/Ave). The I-CVI was calculated as the proportion of experts rating each vignette as 3 or 4 across three dimensions (realism, clarity, and relevance), with unanimous agreement required for an item to be considered suitable (Polit and Beck, 2006; Lynn, 1986). The S-CVI/Ave was calculated as the mean I-CVI across all items, with a threshold of .90 required (Polit and Beck, 2006).

Pornography Use

The Brief Pornography Screen (BPS) (Kraus et al., 2018 (Kraus et al., , 2020) was designed to screen for problematic pornography use, and here it is used as a metric for general porn consumption. The BPS includes five-statements each rating using a three-point Likert response scale of Never, Sometimes, and Very Often (scored as 0, 1, and 2). The questions address porn consumption with statements such as, "you find yourself using pornography more than you want to." BPS scores are summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 10. Questions were presented in a consistent order on the same page.

OnlyFans Awareness

To assess participants' awareness and usage of OnlyFans (or similar services), they were first given a description of OnlyFans, highlighting its popularity with pornography creators, the basic structure of the platform, as well as statistics on the number and demographic characteristics of its creators:

OnlyFans is an Internet content paid subscription platform. It is widely known for its popularity with amateur and professional pornographers although it also hosts other content creators including athletes, musicians, and comedians. Creators can share videos and images with subscribers who can typically pay monthly, in one-time tips, or on a pay-per-view basis.

About 70% of all OnlyFans creators post adult content or pornography which is over 3 million creators.

Over 305 million accounts exist on OnlyFans, of both creators and subscribers. In terms of demographics, 84% of creators are women, male creators account for about 14%, and non-binary or other creators make up 2%.

Participants were asked if they had previously heard of OnlyFans (or other platforms). If yes, participants were asked about their usage in the last six months and whether they had "viewed adult content on OnlyFans (or a similar platform, e.g., Fansly)?", "subscribed to a new adult content OnlyFans (or similar platform) account?", or "created or posted adult content to OnlyFans (or similar platforms)?" with possible responses of Never, Sometimes, or Very Often (scored as 0, 1, and 2). Scores were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0-6.

If Sometimes or Very Often was selected for viewing, participants were asked "on average, how many days a week do you access OnlyFans to view content?". If Sometimes or Very Often was selected for creating content, participants were also asked "on average, how often do you post content to OnlyFans (or similar platforms)?" with possible responses of less than once a month, 1-2 times a month, 3-4 times a month, and more than 4 times a month.

Just World Belief Scale

Just World Beliefs refer to a cognitive bias characterised by the assumption that people generally get what they deserve and that the world is fundamentally fair (Lucas et al., 2011). This questionnaire consists of two eightitem subscales that measure the perception of a just world for the self (e.g., I feel that I generally earn the rewards and punishments that I get in this world) and a just world for others (e.g., I feel that people generally earn the rewards and punishments that they get in this world). Responses were recorded on a seven-point Likert scale from Strongly disagree to Strongly agree. Items were randomised within each subscale, with presentation of the two subscales also randomised.

Assessing AI-NCII judgements

Presented below the vignette, participants were asked to respond to 15 questions. Nine of these questions were taken from (Fido et al., 2022), originally used to measure victim blaming in rape scenarios by (Krahé et al., 2007), and altered for AI-NCII scenarios. Four of these questions form a subscale of victim blame (e.g., How much do you think [victim] is to blame for the incident?), and five form the subscale of victim harm (e.g., Do you think [perpetrator]'s actions will cause mental harm to [victim]?). Three additional questions were included to measure perpetrator acceptability (e.g., How reasonable were [perpetrator]'s actions in this situation?), and the remaining three questions formed a subscale of perceived situation control (e.g.,

To what extent does the way [victim] found out make them seem in control of the situation?). Responses were on a seven-point Likert scale with only endpoints labelled (Not at all -A great extent) and the midpoints numbered.

The relevant subscale items were mean-scored creating a single composite variable for victim blame, victim harm, acceptability of perpetrator actions, and victim situation control.

AI-NCII Awareness

To gauge participants' awareness of AI-NCII, they were asked whether they had previously heard of deepfake pornography. The term deepfake pornography was used because it is likely to be more familiar to individuals outside of AI-NCII research. Participants who responded yes were asked to write a brief explanation of what they understood deepfake pornography to be. This was followed by a standardised description to ensure that all participants, regardless of whether they had previously heard of AI-NCII, had the same information: Participants were asked about their engagement with AI-NCII over the previous six months, specifically whether they had: "knowingly searched for deepfake pornography", "shared deepfake pornography", or "created or asked someone to create deepfake pornography". Responses were recorded as Never, Sometimes, or Frequently (scored as 0, 1, and 2). Scores were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0-6.

Participants were then asked about the legality of AI-NCII in England and Wales, specifically, they were asked whether the following four actions were legal; viewing, storing, sharing, or creating deepfake pornography.

Deepfake Myth Acceptance Scale

The Deepfake Myth Acceptance Scale is a six-item scale designed to measure false beliefs about deepfake pornography ("when deepfake videos are just jokes, they aren't harmful"). Participants responded using a five-point Likert scale from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree. The scale shows moderate correlation with past behaviours and future intent to engage with or create AI-NCII (Twomey et al., 2025).

Procedure

The study design included a non-anonymised wrapper survey and a fully anonymised separate survey linked within. Participants entered the wrapper where they saw the information sheet and provided informed consent before being redirected to the separate anonymised survey -participants were explicitly informed that their subsequent responses would be fully anonymised and could not be individually linked to them. Once in the anonymous survey, they saw a second statement confirming their anonymity and respond "yes" to questions asking whether they understand their information is completely anonymous and separate to any identifying information. Participants were informed that when responding to any free-text questions they should not include any personally identifiable information within their responses.

Participants then completed basic demographic information including age, gender identity, sexual orientation, and ethnicity.

Participants saw the measures in the order listed above in the Methods and Materials section. Upon completion of the survey, participants were asked to confirm whether they had given the study their full attention and answered all questions honestly. They were explicitly informed that their response to this question would have no impact on their compensation and would not result in any negative consequences or non-compensation.

To ensure that all participants concluded the study with an accurate understanding of AI-NCII, they were provided with information explaining that although the vignettes were fictional, the underlying behaviours and associated harms were based on real-world experiences. This provided an explanation of AI-NCII and outlined its current legal status in England and Wales. Participants were also provided with guidance on appropriate steps if they encountered or suspected AI-NCII was given, such as not circulating the content, reporting it to the hosting platform, or, where appropriate, contacting law enforcement. To ensure that participants read and understood the information provided, they were subsequently required to answer three comprehension questions correctly before finishing the study. This comprehension check was intended to minimise the risk of participants leaving the study with an inaccurate understanding of AI-NCII. For the comprehension check, participants were asked to: • Select the statement that best described a deepfake from the following three options: "A sexual video of consenting adults shared online with permission"; "A digitally manipulated video in which someone's face is placed into sexual content they did not perform or consent to" (correct); or, "A still photograph of someone from a social media profile".

• Respond true or false to the statement, "AI-NCII can cause harm to those depicted, resulting in feelings of violation, anxiety, reputational damage, loss of trust, and even long-term psychological distress" (true), • Respond true or false to the statement, "In England and Wales, it is illegal to share or threaten to share AI-NCII content" (true).

This concluded the anonymous survey section. Participants were then given a code to enter the wrapper survey. Following submission of the code, participants were provided with the debrief sheet, marking the conclusion of their involvement in the study. The study was designed to require responses to all questions (except for optional free-text comments in response to the vignettes), and as such all participants submitted complete responses.

Analysis

The Just World Belief scales (self and other) and the deepfake myth acceptance scale were each subject to exploratory factor analysis to verify the items loaded appropriately onto the proposed factor structures. Confirmatory factor analysis was then used to produce a single score per participant based upon all relevant items.

The two Just World Belief scales (self and other) were combined, and a two factor structure was considered appropriate based upon a parallel analysis scree plot and theoretical expectations. All "self" items loaded on one factor with a minimum item loading of .51, and all "other" items loaded to the second factor with a minimum loading of .45. A confirmatory factor analysis using weighted least squares resulted in a TLI value = .70, RMSEA = .05, and SRMR = .09. This 'inadequate' model fit is possibly due to correlation between the two factors resulting from the common phrasing used between the subscales. Despite the less than adequate model fit, based upon the available metrics, the existing support for a two factor model indicates the use of factor analysis is sufficient. The full process is shown in Appendix A.

The deepfake myth acceptance scale was tested using a parallel analysis scree plot, indicating suitability for computing a single factor. All items loaded to the single exploratory factor with a minimum loading of .59. The confirmatory factor analysis yielded an acceptable structure, TLI = .94, RM-SEA = .02, and SRMR = .06. For the full results from the factor analysis for both Just World Belief and deepfake myth acceptance, see Appendix A.

The modelling of the four dependent variables: perceived victim blame, perceived victim harm, perceived acceptability of perpetrator actions, and perceived victim situation control, all used the same predictor (independent variable) structure. This included primary fixed effects of participant gender (man/woman), victim professional identity (sex worker/office worker), victim gender modality (transgender/cisgender), victim gender identity (man/woman), the means of finding out (anonymously/via an online service), participant age, participant pornography use, participant Just World Belief subscales of self and other, and deepfake myth acceptance. It also included interactions between participant gender and victim professional identity, and participant gender and victim gender modality. More complex models were assessed however they offered no significant improvement upon the model described above.

When asked about the acceptability of perpetrators' actions, responses were strongly skewed towards zero indicating a high level of intolerance.

Discussion

In this study, participants were presented with a vignette describing a scenario involving a victim of AI-NCII and were asked to respond to questions concerning perceived victim harm, victim blame, acceptability of perpetrator actions and victim control. The vignettes were manipulated across three conditions: the victim's gender identity with victims described as either cisgender or transgender men or women; the victim's professional identity described as either office workers or sex workers; and how they discovered the AI-NCII, from an anonymous source or proactively via an online service.

The first research question asked: how does professional identity change public perceptions of victims in AI-NCII scenarios? Our findings revealed that victims described as sex workers who post sexually explicit content online were blamed more, perceived as experiencing less harm from the creation and sharing of AI-NCII, and the perpetrator's actions were perceived as more acceptable than when victims were described as office workers who posted content on social media. Professional identity therefore influenced judgements across multiple dimensions of AI-NCII victimisation, with the sex worker framing consistently producing less sympathetic evaluations of the victim.

Importantly, the professional identities used in the vignettes should not be considered as mutually exclusive categories. An individual may work in an office while also producing sexually explicit content on a subscription-based platform. Indeed, there have been reports that some creators use platforms such as OnlyFans as a secondary source of income alongside their primary employment (The, 2022). Consequently, the distinction between an office worker and a sex worker in the present study represents a manipulation of the victim's described professional identity, rather than a distinction between two inherently discrete populations. This is important because it demonstrates that relatively limited information about a victim can substantially alter perceptions of the same underlying act of victimisation.

The finding that sex workers were blamed more and perceived as experiencing less harm is consistent with previous evidence that sex workers are subject to higher levels of victim blaming following sexual victimisation (Velasco and Sanmartín, 2026). The present findings extend this literature to AI-NCII, suggesting that these patterns of judgement may also apply when the sexual material has been artificially generated or manipulated rather than genuinely produced by the victim. This raises the question of what might account for these similarities in judgements?

Research by Fido et al. (2026) found that participants attributed greater blame to victims whose bodies, as opposed to face, were portrayed, particularly when the victims were described as sex workers perhaps due to expectations that body imagery is typically (but not always) obtained via consensual pornography and it is these preconceptions that influence the victim blaming attitudes. It was also reported by Fido and colleagues that sex workers were blamed more regardless of whether they were depicted as the face or the body compared to non-sex workers.

The present findings support the work by Fido et al. (2026) as participants suggested that the victim would have been less likely to be targeted had they not posted explicit content online in the first place, and that "[the victim] should have never started in sex work to begin with, so in a sense it's [their] own fault" (Participant 381). This encapsulates our findings that victim blaming is far greater for sex workers than office workers.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the sex worker identity violates conventional expectations of who is typically perceived as a victim, resulting in sex workers failing to meet the criteria of an ideal victim. Participants might have perceived these victims as not being wholly blameless because of the pre-existence of sexual or intimate content, leading them to be seen as more responsible for subsequent victimisation. This interpretation is supported by research demonstrating that victims of shared NCII are judged more negatively when victims have existing sexual content available online (Naezer and van Oosterhout, 2021; Zvi, 2022). The present findings suggest that this effect extends to AI-NCII, despite the crucial distinction between consensually produced intimate content and AI-NCII created or disseminated without consent. In other words, consent to one form of sexual material appears capable of altering perceptions of a subsequent non-consensual actpotentially because consent to sexual self-presentation is incorrectly conflated with consent to other forms of sexual representation.

A similar pattern has been observed in other forms of IBSA. Previous research has demonstrated that victims described as being in casual relationships are more likely to be blamed for the sharing of NCII than those described as being in committed relationships (Fido et al., 2025). This has been interpreted in terms of perceived sexual promiscuity and the assumptions associated with individuals who engage in particular forms of sexual behaviour. The present findings may reflect a related process of sexual objectification, whereby a victim's existing sexualised presentation is incorporated into judgements concerning their subsequent victimisation. Sexual objectification has been demonstrated in both interpersonal contexts (Loughnan et al., 2013) and online forms of IBSA (Serpe and Brown, 2022). Again, discrimination against those who make their sexuality public may contribute to perceptions that the victim was acting inappropriately by sharing intimate content, placing sex workers outside the framework of the ideal victim.

The second research question to be answered was: how does gender modality change public perceptions of victims in AI-NCII scenarios? It was found that transgender victims -regardless of whether they were described as men or women -were perceived as significantly more blameworthy by men than women. No statistically significant differences in gender modality were ob-served for perceptions of harm, acceptability of perpetrator actions, or perceived victim control. The effect of gender modality was comparatively specific to victim blame and was moderated by participant gender.

The ideal victim framework proposes that social judgements of victimhood are shaped by expectations concerning characteristics such as innocence, vulnerability, and absence of responsibility for the perpetration (Sleath and Woodhams, 2014). However marginalised individuals may be less easily accommodated within these expectations, particularly when aspects of their social identity are perceived as inconsistent with conventional norms of victimhood. From this perspective, describing a victim as transgender may introduce an additional social identity that interacts with existing expectations about who constitutes a "typical" victim. The present findings provide some support for this interpretation through the increased victim blame observed.

Transgender victims were not perceived as experiencing less harm, nor were perpetrator actions considered more acceptable. Instead, men specifically attributed greater blame towards transgender victims. This finding suggests that the expectancy violation associated with transgender identity may operate primarily through judgements concerning blame as opposed to a total denial of victimhood. Therefore, despite the pattern of increased blame and decreased perceived harm observed for sex workers, the findings for gender modality demonstrate that harm and blame are not equivalent judgements. Victims can be perceived as experiencing comparable levels of harm while being judged differently in terms of blamelessness. That is, perceptions that a victim bears greater responsibility for being targeted do not victimhood.

Implications

The primary implication of this research is that, for victims of AI-NCII, third-party judgements of harm and blame are not determined solely by the nature of the act itself. Instead peripheral information concerning the victim's identity and circumstances through which they encountered the abuse may shape perceptions of harm, blame, acceptability, and control. This finding may be particularly consequential for victims whose identities or behaviours are subject to social stigma, such as sex workers and gender minority groups.

The effect of professional identity is especially important. The findings evidence the increased victim blaming attributed to stigmatised groups, such as sex workers (Grittner and Walsh, 2020; West, 2025; Stutz et al., 2024).

This was not only seen in the differences in harm and blame attributed to the two professional identities, but also in the free text responses, for example where participant 327 said "I can't feel sorry for someone who posts sexually explicit images of themselves then complains if someone else makes more explicit imagery of them". This comment encapsulates the idea of victim blaming well, the participant suggests that because the victim posts (consensual) explicit imagery, others should also be allowed to do the same (with or without the victim's consent). The victim's voluntary production of sexual material is treated as evidence that they should have less objection to the creation of further sexual material without their consent. Such reasoning effectively extends consent beyond the specific act to which it was originally given. These changes in judgements based upon whether an individual engages in sex work or not highlights the importance of ensuring that the representation of victims within support services and interventions (including legal) does not reinforce victim-blaming attitudes and instead promotes equitable access to appropriate support regardless of the victim's identity or circumstances.

Specifically, interventions designed to strengthen recognition of victimhood should challenge the misconception that a victim's prior actions or existing consensual material legitimises their subsequent victimisation or diminishes their status as a victim. Guidance for practitioners, platforms, and the wider public should emphasise that, for all individuals, consent to create or distribute intimate material for a particular purpose does not constitute unrestricted consent for others to modify, redistribute, or generate additional material depicting that person-all these things constitute forms of NCII. This principle is particularly important when supporting members of stigmatised groups, including sex workers, who may be disproportionately affected by assumptions that their occupation or prior consent to sexual content diminishes their right to control how intimate material depicting them is used.

When victims were described either as transgender or cisgender, transgender victims were found to receive greater blame from men than by women.

This suggests that judgements of victims might be shaped by an interaction between victim characteristics and observer characteristics. Importantly, and as noted previously, this increased attribution of blame was independent of perceived harm. Therefore interventions aimed at reducing victim blaming may need to address the specific beliefs underlying victim blame rather than assuming negative judgements of marginalised victims reflect a general failure to recognise their victimisation. That is, even when the harms experienced by marginalised victims are recognised, interventions may need to challenge the values and assumptions that prevent these individuals as being perceived as 'ideal victims'.

Across all four dependent variables-blame, harm, acceptability, and control-greater acceptance of deepfake myths emerged as a significant predictor. Greater acceptance of deepfake myths was associated with increased victim blame, reduced perceptions of victim harm, greater acceptability of perpetrator actions, and increased perceptions of victim control. A dominance analysis (see Appendix B) indicated that deepfake myth acceptance was a substantial conditional predictor across all four models, accounting for a large proportion of the explained variance. This finding further supports the utility of the deepfake myth acceptance scale (Twomey et al., 2025) as a measure of perceptions of AI-NCII.

Limitations and Future Work

The research topic examined in the present study-how individuals perceive and judge victims of AI-NCII-is a sensitive topic. Questions concerning the extent to which a person is to blame for sexual abuse might be especially susceptible to socially desirable responding, potentially resulting in artificially low ratings of victim blame and high ratings of perceived victim harm. Indeed, the dependent variables of harm, blame, and acceptability were not normally distributed. Gamma distributions were therefore used to account for pronounced skew in these variables. If social desirability influenced participant responses, then the findings might be subject to this limitation. To mitigate this potential limitation, however, the study was intentionally designed to reassure participants that their responses were fully anonymous and unlinked to their Prolific account. Additionally, although some of the findings may have been mediated by social desirability, the responses still revealed significant relationships between perceptions and experimental manipulations. If social desirability played a large role, then the real and unreported judgements could potentially be even more negative for stigmatised and minority groups.

The general-population sample provides an important baseline for understanding perceptions of AI-NCII victims. Future research could build on these findings by examining practitioners who regularly interact with victims, such as therapists, counsellors, solicitors, and law enforcement, whose professional experience might shape perceptions and judgements differently.

Comparing these groups with the general population would help establish whether the patterns identified here extend to practitioner contexts.

Future research should examine the mechanisms through which victim characteristics influence judgements. In particular, experimentally assessing perceived responsibility, sexual objectification, ideal-victim expectations, and perceived agency could confirm whether the effects observed in the present study reflect perceptions of marginalised or sexualised victims as less deserving of victim status. This would allow explanations based on ideal victim theory and expectancy violations theory to be tested more directly rather than inferred from observed patterns of victim judgement.

Conclusion

This study examined how victim characteristics and the circumstances of discovery influence bystander or third-party perceptions of AI-NCII victimisation. The findings indicate that perceptions are shaped by how victims are socially characterised. Sex workers were blamed more and perceived as less harmed than office workers, while transgender victims received greater blame from men as opposed to women. These findings suggest that existing stigma surrounding sex work and marginalised gender identities can extend to judgements of AI-NCII victimisation, influencing attributions of responsibility and harm. Additionally, victims who were anonymously notified of AI-NCII were perceived as having less control than those who discovered their victimisation proactively through an online service, suggesting that perceptions of victim agency may also depend on how victimisation is discovered.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that AI-NCII does not occur within a social vacuum; judgements of victims are shaped by pre-existing beliefs about sexuality, gender, and stigma. Recognising these influences is important for understanding why some victims may receive less sympathetic responses than others despite experiencing the same form of non-consensual abuse. Addressing misconceptions surrounding consent and deepfake sexual abuse may support more equitable responses to AI-NCII victimisation. Interventions should therefore focus not on changing or concealing aspects of victim's identity, but on addressing the underlying processes or means by which these perceptions are formed and perpetuated. Victims should not have to fit an ideal to be believed, protected, or supported.
