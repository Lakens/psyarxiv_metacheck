Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

This research presents the development and validation of a Scale of Epistemic and Aleatory Uncertainty (SEAU), a novel measure designed to assess individual differences in two distinct forms of uncertainty intolerance: aleatory uncertainty (risk aversion) and epistemic uncertainty (perceived lack of information). Existing measures of intolerance of uncertainty (IU) focus predominantly on aleatory uncertainty (risk aversion) and tend to conflate emotional responses with cognitive perceptions, failing to differentiate between these dimensions. Across two studies (N = 550 and N = 250), exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses identified a four-factor structure for the SEAU: aleatory uncertainty, social epistemic uncertainty, and positively and negatively worded epistemic uncertainty. The SEAU demonstrated strong internal consistency and convergent validity with established measures of psychopathological traits. Notably, epistemic uncertainty was more strongly associated with intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety traits than aleatory uncertainty, suggesting distinct cognitive mechanisms may underpin different forms of IU. Gender and age effects were observed, with females and younger individuals reporting higher IU. The SEAU offers a theoretically grounded, psychometrically robust tool for disentangling cognitive and affective components of uncertainty intolerance, with implications for understanding transdiagnostic risk factors in psychopathology. Future research should explore its clinical utility and predictive validity across diverse populations.

Introduction

Numerous measures have been developed to index different aspects of the intolerance of uncertainty (IU) across different contexts, such as decision-making research, healthcare, and psychopathology. Existing measures include items regarding emotional and behavioural responses to uncertainty and higher-order thoughts regarding uncertainty (e.g. aversion to uncertainty or confusion when situations are uncertain) (Hillen, Gutheil, Strout, Smets, & Han, 2017). While different definitions and conceptualisations of IU exist, in the field of psychopathology IU is typically defined as the tendency to find uncertain or probabilistic events aversive, while acknowledging that events may be inherently unpredictable (Carleton, 2012). IU has been linked to numerous psychopathologies, with the strongest evidence supporting the role of IU as a transdiagnostic risk for emotional disorders (Carleton et al., 2012; Grupe & Nitschke, 2013; McEvoy & Mahoney, 2012; Rosser, 2019; Shihata, McEvoy, Mullan, & Carleton, 2016).

Typical approaches to IU are emotion-based, they assume that individuals differ in the extent to which they find uncertain, or probabilistic events, aversive. Implicit in this assumption, however, is that people do not differ in a meaningful way in how they understand the likelihood of a probabilistic event, including their capacity to estimate the probability of an event accurately and the attribution of uncertainty about the event (e.g. to unknowable circumstances, or their own ignorance). In other words, it is generally assumed that it is not the judgement of uncertainty that differs between individuals, rather it is the interpretation and response to that uncertainty which defines individual differences in IU. When an event has an uncertain potentially negative outcome, IU may be referred to as risk-aversion.

It has recently been suggested that, due to differences in the rate at which people update their predictions about the world based on experience, there may also be individual differences in one's perceptions of outcome variability (Moul, Don, & Livesey, 2023). On experiencing a variable outcome, one individual may conceive this variability as being owed to the natural stochasticity of events which are nevertheless determined by a relatively stable state of the world. For instance, most people are happy to accept that there is a stable 50% chance of a flipped coin landing on heads even though we cannot predict the outcome on any single coin flip. Similarly, the probability of winning a game of roulette can be calculated in advance while accepting that the precise outcome cannot be known. Another individual may conceive variability as due to rapidly changing and unpredictable underlying states. This individual may experience a day in which there are periods of rainfall as one that fluctuates between two states; being fine and being rainy, as opposed to viewing the whole day as being slightly wet. For this individual, the outcome is volatile and uncertain because the world is volatile and uncertain. This cognitive account of individual differences in the ability to determine stability may lead to differences in an individual's ability to make predictions or decisions based on the (un)available information (Moul et al., 2023). In the cognitive decision-making literature, this is described as 'epistemic uncertainty'; that is, knowledge an individual lacks, or perceives to lack, about the likelihood of an event.

Despite being hypothesised to arise from fundamental cognitive processes, epistemic uncertainty can be strongly associated with a negative affective state since a lack of individual knowledge may be internalised as a personal shortcoming (Fox & Tversky, 1995; Moul et al., 2023). Thus, individual differences in both affective responses to uncertainty (risk-aversion) and in cognitive processing of uncertainty (epistemic uncertainty) can lead to a negative emotional state and the avoidant behaviours associated with anxiety disorders.

It is important to note, however, that these two forms of uncertainty intolerance (aleatory and epistemic) do not necessarily covary. An individual could be poor at determining the likelihood of an event and find this perceived lack of knowledge unsettling (high epistemic uncertainty aversion) but be happy to make decisions and take risks if they feel that they know the likelihood of the outcomes (low aleatory uncertainty aversion). This individual would likely generate a low score on a measure of risk-aversion but may experience high levels of uncertainty intolerance via an epistemic pathway. Alternatively, uncertainty itself may be regarded as aleatory by one individual but epistemic by another. For example, two individuals given information regarding the likelihood of a medical diagnosis (e.g. cancer recurrence) may find this statistic unbearable but due to different forms of uncertainty intolerance. One individual might interpret this as purely aleatory and dislike the outcome being uncertain. The other may experience this as epistemic uncertainty due to their lack of access to, or inability to interpret, expert advice and medical information.

A further distinction can be made between aleatory and epistemic forms of uncertainty with respect to negative affect. While aversion is inherent in the definition of intolerance of aleatory uncertainty -a dislike of outcomes that are uncertain as opposed to certain -there is only an assumed link between the experience of epistemic uncertainty and the induction of negative affect. In other words, it is possible that an individual may experience high levels of epistemic uncertainty -they do not feel that they have enough information to make an informed decision -but be unphased by that state and willing to make a behavioural choice regardless. To date, there is no validated measure which assesses individual differences in both aleatory uncertainty (i.e. risk) aversion and in the experience of epistemic uncertainty (the perception of having insufficient information to form an estimate of the likelihood of an event).

The present study aimed to develop and validate a measure that is able to do two things; 1) assess both an individual's intolerance of risk (aleatory uncertainty) and their experience of feeling that they lack sufficient information (epistemic uncertainty), and 2) assess aleatory and epistemic uncertainty intolerance independently from the negative emotions they may induce in some individuals. The development and validation of such a measure would represent an important nexus of cognitive and clinical psychology approaches to uncertainty, with implications for future theoretical and clinical understandings of IU.

Study 1: Scale Development and Exploratory Factor Analysis

The aim of study 1 was to use exploratory factor analysis to identify self-report items that captured aleatory and epistemic uncertainty intolerance. Boateng et al.'s (2018) recommendations for best practice for scale development were followed.

Method: Item Development for the Scale of Aleatory and Epistemic Uncertainty (SEAU)

Conceptual definitions of two domains of aleatory and epistemic uncertainty were made a priori. Aleatory uncertainty definition: stochastic uncertainty derived from the likelihood of an event, where that event is recognised as being probabilistic and not deterministic given the causal factors that one could, in principle, know in advance. Epistemic uncertainty definition: uncertainty derived from the ignorance, or perceived ignorance, of the individual about the likelihood of an event, or the "knowable" causal factors that determine that likelihood. Item generation included both deductive and inductive methods. As aleatory anxiety aligns with current conceptualisations of IU, we adapted pre-existing items by removing references to feeling anxious or any other negative affective state. As the epistemic scale was entirely novel, this was generated predominantly from first principles using inductive methods. For example, at the crux of epistemic uncertainty is the experience of having insufficient information to make a decision. Thus, items that referred to experiencing this state were generated, e.g. "I often feel as though I don't have enough information to decide whether to take a risk."

The preliminary item pool was generated by one author and contained 54 items, featuring high levels of redundancy, as suggested by DeVellis (2003). Both positively and negatively worded items were intentionally included to avoid response biases. These preliminary items were then reviewed by the two other authors. Consensus was reached regarding exclusion of irrelevant items, rewording of unclear items and inclusion of other important items to capture the content domain more holistically. This resulted in expanding the content domain of epistemic uncertainty to include items relating to lacking information in social contexts. Social items were added as they should load onto the same epistemic construct even though the context is different from typical decision-making. Following Boateng et al. (2018), it is recommended to include items that do not perfectly fit the domain identified as undesirable items will be removed through later evaluation steps. A five-point Likert scale (strongly disagree -strongly agree) was used for all items.

Following the review, the initial SEAU scale consisted of 51 items: 15 aleatory items, 19 epistemic items, and 17 social-epistemic items (these items were considered part of the epistemic subscale for theoretical and analytical purposes). See supplementary materials (S1) to view all original scale items.

Method: Scale Development

Readability parameters in Microsoft Word produced a Flesch-Kincaid grade-level score of 6.9 and a Flesch reading ease score of 64.9, suggesting all the items were suitable for reading at or beyond a 7th grade level and easy for individuals at and above 12-15 years to understand. This was informally confirmed by a small convenience sample of young adult volunteers to check for item clarity.

Following Comrey and Lee's (2013) graded scale of sample sizes for scale development and the 10:1 ratio scale (Boateng et al., 2018), we recruited a sample of 550 participants. For the 51-items included, this met both the criteria for the 10:1 ratio and "very good" sample size for scale development and included a buffer zone of 40 participants in case data were lost due to poor attention or incomplete responses.

In addition to the 51-item SEAU described above, existing measures of intolerance of uncertainty, and anxiety disorder symptoms were included in the questionnaire battery to allow for convergent validity checksfoot_0 . The measures used were as follows:

Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale 12 (IUS-12; Carleton et al., 2007).

The IUS-12 is a 12-item self-report measure of intolerance of uncertainty. Respondents are asked to rate how characteristic given statements are of them on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all characteristic of me) to 5 (extremely characteristic of me). The IUS-12 has been associated with a range of anxiety disorders, and shows a two-factor structure, consisting of inhibitory uncertainty and prospective uncertainty (Carleton, 2012; Carleton et al., 2007). Internal consistency for the IUS-12 in the current study was excellent, α = .90.

Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (BFNE; Leary, 1983).

The BFNE is a 12item self-report measure assessing anxiety associated with social judgement, used in research and clinical settings as a measure of social anxiety disorder. Respondents rate their endorsement of fear or worrying about negative social evaluations on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely), with four reverse-coded items. Scores range from 12 to 60, with higher scores representing higher levels of anxiety about negative social evaluations. Internal consistency in the present sample was excellent, α = .94.

Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory -Revised (OCI-R; Foa et al., 2002).

The OCI-R is an 18-item self-report measure of common obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) symptoms. Respondents are asked to rate their distress over these symptoms over the past month on a 5-point Likert scale of 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much). Total scores therefore range from 0 to 72. The OCI-R is widely used in research as a broad indicator of OCD symptomatology and showed excellent internal consistency in the current sample, α = .93.

Participants

A representative community sample (N = 550) were recruited through the Prolific online database. Participants were eligible to participate if they were aged over 18, fluent in English, and registered in Prolific.

Procedure

The study was administered using the Qualtrics Survey Software platform, and approval was gained from The University of *** Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval Code: 2023/716) prior to data collection. Once informed consent was provided, participants completed an online battery of questionnaires. This included demographic questions, the SEAU items (order randomised), and a short battery of questionnaires described previously.

Three attention check questions were included in the questionnaire battery (one in the SEAU and two in other scales). Upon completion, participants received payment through their Prolific account.

Results

Of the 550 recruited, 548 participants (male = 272, female = 262, prefer to self-identify gender = 14, mean age = 38.6 (SD = 13.7), ethnicity = 54% white/European, 19% black/African American, 7.5% Asian, 8% Latin American/Hispanic, 11% mixed race, 0.5% other), completed the questionnaire battery. Participants (N = 8) who answered 1 or more attention check questions incorrectly were removed from analyses. Participants were also removed from analyses if they gave uniform answers to all 51 SEAU items (N = 4) or who completed all test items in less than 7 minutes or more than 60 minutes (N = 17) as each of these criteria were considered indicative of insufficient engagement. This resulted in 519 participants included in the item reduction analysis.

Item reduction analysis:

The items were first refined through iterative item-total and item-item analyses. Items with item-total correlations of less than 0.3 (N = 10), or greater than 0.7 (N=1), and items with more than 80% of item-item correlations less than 0.3 (N = 12) were removed.

Exploratory factor analyses (EFA) of the remaining 28 items, using principal axis factoring and Oblimin rotation with Kaiser normalisation, were conducted. Following each iteration, items with communalities less than 0.3 and items with factor loadings less than 0.4 on any factor were identified and excluded from the next EFA round. This process was continued until all items met inclusion criteria for communality and factor loading. Next crossloadings of items between factors were examined. Items which loaded (>0.3) onto two or more factors but had less than 0.2 difference between factor loadings were excluded and the EFA was conducted again. This process was continued until all items met these cross-loading criteria. Once these criteria were met the factor loadings were reviewed.

The result of the EFA revealed a 4-factor solution comprised of 19 items in total. The factors comprised; 1) 7 aleatory items, 2) 4 social epistemic items, 3) 4 negatively worded epistemic items, and 4) 4 positively worded epistemic items. For the final factor structure (19 items), the Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ2 [171] = 4619.75, p < 0.001) and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy was .904. The four extracted factors explained 54.88% of the total variance. The final factor structure of the Scale of Epistemic and Aleatory Uncertainty is shown in table 1. Reliability ranged from good to very good for the factors (aleatory α = 0.896; social epistemic α = 0.854; positive epistemic α = 0.739; negative epistemic α = 0.760).

Discussion

The EFA identified 4 factors. Social epistemic items were hypothesised to load onto a general epistemic factor but the results from the EFA identified these items as forming an independent factor. Similarly, two epistemic factors, distinguished by the affective framing of the items, were identified rather than the single epistemic factor hypothesised. Associations with demographics revealed a small, negative correlation between the epistemic factor (driven by the positively framed items which were reverse coded) and age suggesting that as people age they experience less epistemic uncertainty (they are more likely to endorse feeling as if they have sufficient information to make decisions). Females scored higher than males on all factors of uncertainty with the exception of social epistemic uncertainty which supports the EFA identifying this as a factor separable from decision-making epistemic uncertainty. While all factors of the SEAU were significantly positively associated with IUS-12 and BFNE scores, only the negatively-worded epistemic factor and the social-epistemic factor were significantly correlated with OCIR scores. The formation of two epistemic factors (differentiated by the framing of the question) was not predicted but the fact that only one of them was associated with OCIR scores suggests that these two factors may differentiate meaningfully between aspects of IU relevant for psychopathology. It was noted that the IUS correlated more strongly with the BFNE and the OCIR than all but the negatively-worded epistemic factor of the SEAU but that this was expected given the inclusion of the endorsement of negative affect common across these three clinically-relevant scales.

Study 2: Confirmatory Factor Analysis and Validity

The aims of study 2 were to 1) test if the factor structure generated by the EFA was consistent with the theorised underlying structure, 2) test alternative models of factor structure to determine best fit, and 2) to confirm the associations demonstrating construct validity found in the EFA in an independent sample.

Participants

A representative community sample (N = 250) was recruited through the Prolific online database. Participants were eligible to participate if they were aged over 18, fluent in English, and registered in Prolific.

Procedure

As for study 1, this study was administered using the Qualtrics Survey Software platform, with ethics approval gained from The University of *** Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval Code: 2023/716) prior to data collection. Once informed consent was provided, participants completed the online battery of questionnaires comprising demographic questions including a question asking of any current or past mental health diagnoses, the SEAU items, and a battery of questionnaires. Three attention check questions were included in the questionnaire battery. Upon completion, participants received payment through their Prolific account.

Measures

In addition to the SEAU items identified by the EFA in study 1, participants completed a battery of questionnaires indexing symptoms and trait measures of common psychopathologies to allow for convergent validity checks. These questionnaires included: the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale 12 (IUS-12; Carleton, 2007), the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (BFNE; Leary, 1983), and the Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory -Revised (OCI-R; Foa et al., 2002) as per study 1. Additional measures were as follows:

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-Trait (STAI-T; Spielberger, 1970).

The STAI-T is a 20-item self-report measure which indexes enduring, trait-based levels of anxiety. Respondents are asked to rate how frequently they endorse certain items relating to trait anxiety on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always), with seven reverse-coded items. Total scores on the trait measure range from 20 to 80. In the present study, the internal consistency of the STAI-T was excellent, α = .945.

UPPS Impulsive Behavior Scale (UPPS; Whiteside & Lynam, 2001).

The UPPS is a 45-item scale designed to measure facets of impulsivity using four subscales: urgency, premeditation, perseverance and sensation seeking (Whiteside & Lynam, 2001). Respondents are presented with statements about their affect, behaviour and cognition, e.g. "When I am upset I often act without thinking", and are asked to rate their agreement with each item on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Agree Strongly") to 4 ("Disagree Strongly") with some items reverse-coded. Total scores range from 45 to 180. The scales have demonstrated high internal consistency (Cα's = .82-.91, in this study α = .837). Impulsivity is a transdiagnostic feature of psychopathology, most notably featured in ADHD, conduct disorder and substance use disorders (Beauchaine, Zisner, & Sauder, 2017) and has been argued both to be positively associated with intolerance of uncertainty due to its potential as a mechanism to reduce anxiety (Sadeh & Bredemeier, 2021) and to be negatively associated due to the greater likelihood of risk-averse behaviours in those with high intolerance of uncertainty (Carleton et al., 2016).

Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995).

The DASS-21 is designed to assess symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress among adults. The original 42-item measure has high construct validity and internal consistency for each subscale (α = .91 Depression, .84 Anxiety, .84 Stress; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995). The brief version, used in this research, has been found to have similar properties across samples (Henry & Crawford, 2005). Respondents are presented with short statements and are asked to indicate the extent to which the statement has applied to them over the past week on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 ("Did not apply to me at all-Never") to 3 ("Applied to me very much, or most of the time-Almost always"). Three subscale scores are generated by adding scores from all items in each subscale and a total distress score is calculated by adding all items. Higher total scores represent greater levels of negative emotional symptoms. In the present study, the internal consistency of the DASS-21 was excellent, α = .944.

Death Anxiety Beliefs and Behaviour Scale (DABBS; Menzies, Sharpe, & Dar-Nimrod, 2022).

The DABBS is an 18-item scale assessing aspects of death anxiety. It has been shown to be a valid and reliable measure of affect, beliefs and behaviours relating to death anxiety (Menzies et al., 2022). A measure of death anxiety was included as death is a very good example of a situation in which insufficient knowledge can be gained to alleviate uncertainty (e.g. When will I die? How will I die?). As such, we predicted that it should correlate positively with epistemic uncertainty. In the present study, the internal consistency of the DABBS was good, α = .886

Inventory of Callous Unemotional Traits (ICU; Frick, 2003), as cited by (Essau, Sasagawa, & Frick, 2006). The ICU is a 24-item measure of psychopathic traits, specifically callousness, uncaringness and unemotionality, that has been validated in community samples.

Respondents rate how well statements describe themselves on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not at all true) to 3 (very true). This study used the abbreviated ICU-11, which uses 11 of the original ICU items to produce a scale that is invariant across genders and a better fit across cultures (Allen, Shou, Wang, & Bird, 2021). Total scores range from 0 to 33, with higher scores indicating higher levels of psychopathic traits. In the present sample, internal consistency for the ICU-11 was good, α = .773. The ICU-11 measure was included as psychopathic traits have been associated with elevated risk-taking behaviours (Maes, Woyke, & Brazil, 2018) and diminished risk-perception (Hosker-Field, Molnar, & Book, 2016) and scores were hypothesised to correlate negatively with aleatory uncertainty.

Results

Of the 250 recruited, participants (N = 7) who answered 1 or more attention check questions incorrectly or who completed all test items in less than 7.5 minutes or more than 60 minutes (N = 12) were removed from analyses as each of these criteria were considered indicative of insufficient engagement. This resulted in 231 participants included in study 2 analyses (male = 112, female = 117, prefer to self-identify gender = 2, mean age = 35.3 (SD = 11.7), ethnicity = 57% white/European, 14% black/African American, 10% Asian, 7% Latin American/Hispanic, 11% mixed race, 1% other/prefer not to disclose). Of these participants, N = 88 (39.5%) reported having either a current or past diagnosis of an anxiety disorder, N = 8 (3.5%) preferred not to answer this question.

Confirmatory Factor Analyses

Given that the results of the EFA identified 4 factors, as opposed to the 2 hypothesised (epistemic and aleatory), three potential models were generated to determine the best model fit. The three models generated were as follows: 1) 4 factor model -no second-order (intolerance of uncertainty) general factor, 2) second-order model -each of the 4 factors loaded onto a single "intolerance of uncertainty" factor, 3) third-order model -the positive and negative worded epistemic factors were loaded onto a second order "cognitive epistemic" factor which, alongside the social epistemic and aleatory factors were loaded onto a general "intolerance of uncertainty" general factor.

The models were generated using JASP (Version 0.98.0, 2026). Given the relatively large sample size, Chi Square non-significance was not used as an indicator of model fit (Gatignon, 2010). Required indices for acceptable model fit were, CFI > 0.9, and RMSEA < 0.08. Model 1 (4 factor model: CFI = 0.951, RMSEA = 0.082) and Model 3 (third-order model, 20 CFI = 0.952, RMSEA = 0.081) failed to achieve adequate fit. However, Model 2 (second-order model, CFI = 0.955, RMSEA = 0.078) achieved adequate fit.

Association with demographics

Total SEAU scores and all of the factors, with the exception of the aleatory scale, were significantly associated with participant age (N = 230, ps <= 0.021). Except for aleatory uncertainty, for which there was no significant association, as age increased, self-reported intolerance of uncertainty decreased. To test mean differences in total and factor scores according to gender, those participants who preferred to self-describe (N = 2) were removed from analyses. There were no significant differences in mean total score, nor between the aleatory, social, or negative epistemic factors according to gender. The pattern of correlations between the SEAU factors and the OCIR and the DABBS was similar. This suggests that for OCPD and death anxiety, uncertainty caused by the perception of having insufficient information may be more phenotypically relevant than an intolerance of risk itself.

The correlations shown in table 4 found that UPPS scores were positively (albeit weakly) associated with aleatory uncertainty (risk aversion) but negatively associated (weak-moderate) with social epistemic uncertainty. Given that all factors of the SEAU were significantly positively correlated with intolerance of uncertainty as measured by the IUS-12, it is possible that the SEAU factors may be able to provide insight into the conflicting evidence from prior research regarding the directionality of the association between IU and different aspects of impulsivity.

General Discussion

The aim of this research was to validate a new measure assessing aleatory and epistemic components of intolerance of uncertainty in independent community samples. The final measure, the Scale of Epistemic and Aleatory Uncertainty (SEAU), demonstrated very good internal consistency. Overall, gender effects were unclear with different patterns of gender differences in the two independent samples. However, all significant differences were in the direction of higher reported SEAU scores for females than for males (see Panchyshyn, Tekok-Kilic, Frijters, and Tardif-Williams, 2023, for research looking at different mechanisms to relate IU with anxiety between males and females). Overall, younger people reported higher levels of IU as measured by the SEAU which is in accordance with prior IU research (Yu et al., 2025).

Convergent validity was demonstrated via moderate to strong correlations with current measures of intolerance of uncertainty (IUS-12) and measures of anxiety (FNEB, STAI). The SEAU also demonstrated sensitivity to clinical severity with significantly higher scores in those with a current, or history of, anxiety disorder.

The different subscales of the SEAU demonstrated associations with specific psychopathological traits. The differentiation of the subscales in this way is a strength of the SEAU and supports the suggestion that different forms of IU are important to consider in the aetiology of psychopathology. Most notably, the three epistemic factors of the SEAU were more strongly correlated with current measures of intolerance of uncertainty and anxiety than the aleatory factor. In addition, taking the results from both the EFA and the CFA together, there is some evidence for a moderate correlation between the negatively-worded epistemic factor and OCIR scores suggesting that obsessive-compulsive traits may be associated specifically with this factor. As such, the SEAU offers promise as a tool that demonstrates good convergent validity with current measures of IU and anxiety but can also elucidate potential mechanisms that contribute to different transdiagnostic traits.

This research had some clear strengths; the use of two independent community samples totalling over 780 participants, a large age range and gender-balanced samples, quality control and attention checks used to ensure the reliability of the data, data regarding mental health history to allow for a proxy case-control comparison, and data on a range of psychopathological traits to allow for assessment of expected patterns of convergence.

Despite these strengths, some limitations should be noted. Exploratory factor analyses identified four factors and this was supported by confirmatory factor analyses. However, the reliability estimate for the negatively framed epistemic factor in the CFA was borderline. While this index may be an underestimate due to the small number of items in this factor (N=4), this warrants caution. While no single factor item was identified as being problematic, either in terms of wording or of lowering the Cronbach's alpha, future research will need to determine if this factor frequently displays sub-optimal internal consistency and whether this is associated with sample characteristics. The split of the epistemic item into two separate factors that differed in terms of their framing (positive versus negative) was also unexpected but consistent across the EFA and CFA. Rather than forming two ends of the same factor, as expected, these two factors appear to be measuring distinct concepts. It is possible that the comparative framing of three out of four of the positively worded items (e.g. I don't need as much information as other people to decide whether to take a risk) means that these items are tapping into a construct such as confidence in decision-making, in addition to experiences of epistemic uncertainty.

This possibility should be tested in a future study. It is also worth reminding the reader that the motivation for the development of the SEAU was theoretical -the idea that a different form of IU may be present in individuals but currently unidentified. As such, the items developed for the epistemic factors could not be derived from prior measures and, instead, were created based on theoretical principles.

This research provides a solid basis for the introduction of the SEAU as a valid and reliable measure of aleatory and epistemic uncertainty. In addition to those outlined previously, there are many directions that future research should and could take. For example, assessing the stability of the SEAU using a test-retest reliability study, and determining if scores on the SEAU, or individual factors, can be used to identify clinical versus control participants with adequate sensitivity and specificity. Indeed, these further psychometric properties should be discerned before the scale can be reliably used to identify the types of uncertainty intolerance associated with psychopathologies. Once this has been achieved, the SEAU may be used to identify potential psychological mechanisms that have been, as yet, untapped in understanding individual differences in psychopathology.

This is an important consideration as current measures that align IU with risk-aversion, by definition, include the endorsement of negative affect and anxiety. For example, in the short version of the Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12; Carleton, Norton, & Asmundson, 2007) items include, "I can't stand being taken by surprise", and "Unforeseen events upset me greatly". As such, some items ask individuals to endorse items that equate being uncertain with negative emotions. While some measures of IU include individual items that refer to having insufficient knowledge (e.g. "It frustrates me not having all the information I need." (IUS-12)), current measures neither discriminate between different types of uncertainty (aleatory versus epistemic) nor between the cognitive experience of uncertainty and an aversive response to uncertainty. An individual could have a strong aversion to experiencing epistemic uncertainty but feel relatively comfortable with risk / aleatory uncertainty and so produce a low score using current measures.
