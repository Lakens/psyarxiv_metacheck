How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Despite the heightened need of connectedness in adolescence, the association between instant messaging (IM) and its psychosocial outcomes remains ambiguous. In line with three existing theories on this topic, ecological momentary assessment (one week, 8 prompts/day) was used to link within-person IM behavior and between-person dispositions with the social outcome perceived IM social support and the psychological outcomes loneliness, depressive feelings, and non-suicidal selfinjury (NSSI) behavior. Furthermore, the interplay between the two was taken into account. Belgian adolescents (n = 181; Mage = 15.04; 63% women) who reported higher-than-usual IM connection behavior consistently showed better momentary psychosocial outcomes. Robust differences related to the dispositions could only be identified for depressive feelings. Women and participants with higher scores on neuroticism, fear of negative evaluation, social anxiety and distress in new situations, and social comparison orientation proved more vulnerable for these feelings. A tentative association related to loneliness was found for higher neuroticism. Moreover, age (marginally) strengthened the negative association between IM behavior and the outcomes IM social support and depressive feelings. In general, these findings highlight the importance of the perceived message content, the potential vulnerability of more extreme dispositions, and the value of embedding psychosocial correlates in a developmental framework.

Introduction

To this day, the question "Do adolescents benefit from social media?" cannot be answered unambiguously. Social media are, because of their persistently changing nature, inherently a moving target (Valkenburg et al., 2016). While scholars previously believed that youngsters displaced offline interactions by online interactions and thus strong friendship bonds by superficial contacts (i.e., displacement hypothesis; Kraut et al., 1998), we now know that these claims should be approached in a more nuanced way (Valkenburg et al., 2022). Indeed, social media nowadays represent an integral part of adolescents' social life (Winstone et al., 2021). The increased social use of social media (e.g., sending messages) around the end of primary school, which likely entails a displacement of a-social media (e.g., watching tv, reading books) by social ones (Hall & Liu, 2022), has become a typical part of maturation (Vanwynsberghe et al., 2024) that adds to the social and identity development of the adolescent (Sun & Subrahmanyam, 2017; Vanwynsberghe et al., 2024). In the same vein, youngsters perceive, compared to other age groups, the highest levels of connectedness through social media, a trend that has crystallized over the last years (De Marez et al., 2025). Accordingly, adolescents primarily use social media to get in touch with friends and family (e.g., Valkenburg & Peter, 2007), which in turn coincides with increased offline contacts and increased friendship strength (Valkenburg & Peter, 2007; Valkenburg & Peter, 2009). Hence, despite the risks of social media (e.g., cyberbullying, interference with other activities; e.g., Tsai et al., 2019), scholars presently acknowledge the psycho-social benefits as well (e.g., Nick et al., 2018).

A type of social media use that has a generally positive connotation is instant messaging (IM; e.g., Best et al., 2014). These private online conversations (e.g., through Snapchat) typically take place between two individuals or in small groups and are mainly used to share, anytime and at any place, daily affective experiences (e.g., Frison & Eggermont, 2015). Within this intimate atmosphere, a culture of openness and even frankness holds: personal thoughts can be shared genuinely without worrying about implicit politeness (Winstone et al., 2021). This being the case, instant messaging with friends has been closely linked to social support (e.g., Timmis, 2012). For example, IM conversations with friends nearly result in the same level of perceived social support than in-person conversations (Holtzman et al., 2017). Analogous to the well-supported offline association (Cohen, 2004), experiments (e.g., Teoh et al., 2015), and observational studies (e.g., Dolev-Cohen & Barak, 2013; Frison & Eggermont, 2015) have shown that IM social support co-occurs with mental health. That is, distressed individuals experience less negative affect after IM conversations with a friend. In case the IM interactions do not convey a sense of social support or takes place between two acquaintances, the latter mood-enhancing effect is absent (e.g., Teoh et al., 2015) or even reversed (e.g., more depressive symptoms; Frison & Eggermont, 2015).

More than other social media, IM creates an ideal context to nourish social relationships (e.g., IM social support; Taylor et al., 2022). Reversibly, while less common, negative social exchange (e.g., waiting a long time to respond to a message) might be present, hampering social bonds (Taylor et al., 2022; Winstone et al., 2021). According to scholars of the interpersonal-connection-behaviors framework (Clark et al., 2018), these (dis)connection behaviors explain the inconsistency in the previously found negative and positive associations between IM and mental health (Clark et al., 2018; Taylor et al., 2022). Others take this claim a step further and state that only the skills of the individual to acquire social support matter, a vision that is shared in the form of the rich-get-richer and the poorget-richer hypothesis (Kraut et al., 2002). While "rich" individuals (e.g., with a low social comparison orientation (low likelihood to evaluate themselves against others) or highly extraverted persons) possess the skills to attract social support in offline and online contexts, "poor" individuals (e.g., socially anxious adolescents) are insufficiently able to gain face-to-face social support. Given that IM communication is less threatening (e.g., less non-verbal communication; e.g., Dolev-Cohen & Barak, 2013) and thus offers "poor" individuals the opportunity to "catch-up", the poor-get-richer hypothesis expects that poor individuals will benefit socially (e.g., high perceived IM social support) and psychologically (e.g., good mental health) from IM. By contrast, the rich-get-richer hypothesis postulates that only "rich" individuals will profit from online communication.

However, all three frameworks do not seem to disambiguate the (dis)advantageous nature of instant messaging completely. For example, although IM interference (i.e., feeling interrupted by IM and often also forced to respond immediately; Winstone et al., 2021) is associated with less loneliness compared to undisturbed online interactions (Achterhof et al., 2022), phubbing adolescents perceive more loneliness than their peers (Hutabarat et al., 2024). Furthermore, according to the meta-analysis of Cheng et al. (2019), socially anxious individuals do not benefit socially from online communication (i.e., poor-get-poorer effect), despite feeling more relaxed and in touch with themselves (e.g., Vanwynsberghe et al., 2024). However, Desjarlais and Willoughby (2010) and Desjarlais (2022) did find poor-get-richer effects for socially anxious youngsters, both in terms of IM social support accrual and wellbeing. Likewise, discrepancies were found for extraversion. While extravert individuals profited psycho-socially from IM conversations according to Cheng et al. (2019) and Lee (2020), a poor-getricher effect for upset introvert adolescents was found in the study of Dolev- Cohen and Barak (2013).

Overall, these findings remain mixed, thereby making it difficult to advance towards a uniform conclusion.

"Moving beyond the false dichotomy of social media being good or bad" (Taylor et al., 2022, p. 1), the Differential Susceptibility to Media Effects Model (DSMM; Valkenburg & Peter, 2013) states that media outcomes depend on the joint interaction of the dispositional traits of the individual, his/her social-developmental context (e.g., influence of peers) and his/her repetitive ephemeral media response states (e.g., perceived IM social support). This inherently means that media effects take place within and not between individuals. Accordingly, the between-subject premise that media effects vary (mostly) across adolescent groups, on which most (abovementioned) studies were based, does not hold (Parry et al., 2022). Moreover, between-subject effects often (partially) mismatch within-subject effects. For example, although more frequent IM users reported less wellbeing than their fellow peers, adolescents could be divided in three groups based on their intrapsychic media response: stable (ca. 65%), increased (ca. 18%) and decreased wellbeing (ca. 17%) after IM use (Beyens et al., 2021). Hence, research should move away from the dominant cross-sectional between-subject designs and reinterpret the abovementioned frameworks in an (average-)within-subject context (Johannes et al., 2024). However, the present ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies have predominantly focused on frequency-based (e.g., How much time have you spent on social medium X since the last beep? ; e.g., Beyens et al., 2020) and contact-based measures (e.g., Have you chatted with others since the last beep? ; e.g., Achterhof et al., 2022; Pouwels et al., 2021). Scientific progress can be made by including momentary content-based indicators (Valkenburg et al., 2022), which plausibly are more directly related to mental health outcomes (Verbeij et al., 2022), and inter-person moderators (Beyens et al., 2021), that allow to extrapolate conclusions to other populations.

The Present Research

Taking the abovementioned research gaps into account, this EMA study approaches the social and psychological (dis)advantages related to IM conversations between peers from a content-based within-person and a trait-based between-person view. With this study, we aim to test the accuracy of the interpersonal-connection-behaviors framework, the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypothesis in an adolescent sample. Accordingly, we will focus on perceived momentary IM social support (i.e., connection behavior) and momentary IM negative social exchange (i.e., disconnection behavior) as proxy for the time-varying content of IM conversations between adolescent peers. Furthermore, the dispositional traits extraversion, neuroticism, social anxiety and social comparison orientation (SCO) will be taken into account. Two research question series will be answered, addressing the social respectively the psychological outcomes of IM. Regarding the social outcomes of IM, we focus on associations related to the same chat conversation. First, we intent to discover if momentary IM negative social exchange predicts momentary IM social support (i.e., RQ 1A). We will additionally clarify if the dispositional traits predict between-person differences in the mean level of momentary IM social support (i.e., RQ 1B). Third, we focus exploratively on the interplay of the mechanisms of the discussed theories. That is, we aim to unravel whether the dispositional traits predict the strength of the within-person association between momentary IM negative social exchange and momentary IM social support (i.e., RQ 1C).

Concerning the psychological outcomes of IM, three complementary questions will be answered with regard to the mental health outcomes (a) loneliness, (b) depressive feelings, and (c) the presence or absence of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) behavior. First, we address whether IM social support predicts mental health (i.e., RQ 2A). In addition, we intend to clarify if the dispositional traits predict between-person differences in the mean level of momentary mental health (i.e., RQ 2B). Lastly, we focus on the explorative question whether the dispositional traits predict the strength of the within-person association between IM social support and mental health (i.e., RQ 2C). Bearing in mind the time frame of the corresponding items, we study contemporaneous within-person associations in case of the former two outcomes and concentrate on the temporaneous association in the latter case.

As we aim to evaluate the three aforementioned conflicting frameworks, several conflicting hypotheses are formulated. These will enable us to establish which of the three is reflected in our data.

Building on the interpersonal-connection-behaviors framework, we anticipate that momentary IM negative social exchange will detract from momentary IM social support. Moreover, we expect that momentary IM social support will enhance momentary or future mental health. Regarding the dispositions of the adolescents, the mean level of momentary IM social support and mental health might be predicted by higher (i.e., rich-get-richer effect) or lower levels (i.e., poor-get-richer effect) of extraversion and lower (i.e., rich-get-richer effect) or higher levels (i.e., poor-get-richer effect) of neuroticism, social anxiety, and SCO. With regard to the exploratory research questions, no explicit hypotheses are put forward. Given the similarity to the story of Goldilocks, in which the interplay between her personal preferences (i.e., her dispositions) and the available options (i.e., the IM behavior she is confronted with) matter, evidence for the latter will be referred to as a goldilocks effect.

Methods Procedure

The data of this study were acquired in three data collection periods (July 2024, August 2024, October 2024) with a similar structure. After a period of recruitment via a follow-up research mailing list (based on previous studies of the lab), distributing flyers to youth movements and mouth-to-mouth advertising, interested adolescents and their parents were invited, during the week before the EMA assessment, to one of the information sessions. During these online preparatory sessions, all facets of the study were thoroughly explained (e.g., answer surveys as quickly as possible; in case you need to turn off the sound of your phone, make sure you can see its screen). All items were clarified in order to make sure participants grasped their meaning correctly. Adolescents who were absent during the online information sessions received the presentation with audio clips. Afterwards, participants signed an informed assent whereas parents gave permission through an informed consent. Furthermore, youngsters filled in a cross-sectional baseline questionnaire before entering the EMA phase of the study.

For the one week EMA assessment, adolescents installed the app m-Path (Mestdagh et al., 2023) on their smartphone. They received EMA questionnaires eight times a day from Monday till Sundayfoot_0 and were asked to complete as much surveys as possible. To avoid that adolescents were only able to answer questionnaires outside the school hours (i.e., smartphone bans), the data collection always took place during a holiday. Based on recommendations of Van Roekel et al. (2019), the surveys were structured according to a semi-random sampling scheme with push notifications between 10 am and 10 pm, time blocks of 75 minutes and a minimal between-prompt interval of 15 minutes. After the initial push notification, participants received reminders after 5 and 10 minutes. After 15 minutes, the survey became inaccessible. Each questionnaire took about two minutes to complete. Participants were followed up on every day by a member of the research team. They received a motivational text message in case they did not complete three or more surveys of that day. At Tuesday, the second day of the data collection, all youngsters were contacted to remediate possible difficulties. A large part of the questionnaire focused on experiences related to the latest IM conversation adolescents had with their peers (if, since the last beep, a new IM interaction occurred). After the study, adolescents received 45€ in case they had completed 80% or more of the EMA questionnaires. Participants who filled in less than 80% received a proportional monetarily reward. The research protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (1432023000129). The post-registration of this study can be found on https://osf.io/ahjxb/.

Participants

This study focused on the general adolescent population. All Dutch-speaking adolescents who attended classes in secondary school could participate, regardless of their study track or age. Note that we did not verify in advance whether or not youngsters used IM or not. Indeed, previous research showed that adolescents who do not integrate IM in their daily life, are (almost) non-existent (De Raeymaecker et al., 2025). A sample size estimation was made with the analytic R package "EMAtools" (Kleinman, 2021), that takes the repetitive nature of EMA assessments into account. Given the novel scope of this study, we could not reliably estimate the parameters of the hypothesized population model. In addition, the executed weekend pilot study with 11 participants could not be used either for the purpose of a simulation-based power analysis. According to the power analysis, a minimum of 110 participants (at a 50% "beep" response rate) was necessary to achieve a power of .80, given α = .05 and a small effect size.

We ultimately recruited 181 Belgian adolescents in secondary school (Mage = 15.04; SD = 1.74), of which 114 women (63%), 66 men (36.50%) and 1 non-binary individual (0.60%). The majority of the sample (41.99%) followed classes (i.e., for the part of the autumn data collection) in or had just completed the academic year of (i.e., for the part of the summer data collection) the second stage of secondary education (15.47% in grade 9; 26.52% in grade 10). About one in three pupils (34.81%) were in the third stage of secondary school (17.68% in grade 11; 15.47% in grade 12; 1.66% in grade 13). A little less than a fourth of the participants (23.20%) were in the first stage of secondary school (9.94% in grade 7; 13.26% in grade 8). Information of the ethnicity of the participants was not available.

Snapchat was the most commonly used messaging app: 64.64% respectively 17.68% indicated to use it "a lot" or "pretty often" to chat with other people. In addition, participants regularly made use of WhatsApp (38.67% respectively 38.12%), Instagram (17.68% respectively 22.65%), TikTok (17.13% respectively 20.44%) and, to a lesser extent, SMS (6.63% respectively 14.36%). This study is part of a more extensive project. Below, only the variables that were relevant for this study are discussed. An overview of the complete EMA state and trait questionnaire can be found on https://osf.io/ahjxb/.

State Measures

In order to maximize the content validity of the items, the EMA questions were grafted on existing trait questionnaires and, whenever relevant, on the Experience Sampling Methodology Item Repository (Kirtley et al., 2024). The items were ordered in line with the Critical Incident Technique, which aims for unbiased memory retrieval in case assessment does not immediately take place after the targeted event (i.c., the last IM conversation with peers) (Flanagan, 1954). This being the case, questions about emotions and perceptions were preceded by situational questions (which are not part of this study). Moreover, items with the same time frame (e.g., "since the last beep") were grouped (Myin-Germeys & Kuppens, 2022). All items were pilot tested by 11 adolescents for one weekend after which the wording of some items was slightly altered. The sampling frequency (i.e., 8 times/a day) was deemed feasible. The average compliance of the EMA questionnaire in general was 79.89%. That is, of the 56 surveys adolescents received during the study, participants completed on average 44.74.

IM Social Support.

The amount of social support adolescents perceived during their last IM conversation with peer(s) was studied with four items. Inspired by the Online Social Support Questionnaire (Nick et al., 2018) and its four subscales, the social support aspects emotional social support (i.e., "I felt connected to the person(s) I was chatting with."), social companionship (i.e., "I had the impression that the person(s) I was chatting with was/were willing to spend time with me."), informational social support (i.e., "I think I got useful information or advice from the person(s) I was chatting with."), and instrumental social support (i.e., "I think I received the help I needed from the person(s) I was chatting with.") were taken into account. The statements could be rated on a sevenpoint scale ranging from "I completely disagree" (0) to "I totally agree" (6). All scores of a row were averaged to create a person-specific mean at each assessment. The between-person reliability (ꙍ between = .93) and the within-person reliability (ꙍ within = .77) were acceptable to good.

IM Negative Social Exchange.

Momentary perceptions of negative social exchange during the last IM conversation with peer(s) were assessed with a hostility item (i.e., "I perceived the messages as being hurtful."), an interference item (i.e., "The messages distracted me from what I was doing."), an insensitivity item (i.e., "I had to wait a long time before I received a response from the person(s) I was chatting with."), and a ridicule item (i.e., "The person(s) I was chatting with made fun of me.").

The construction of these four statements was based on the subscales of the IMTENSE questionnaire of Tsai et al. (2019), who adapted the TENSE questionnaire of Ruehlman and Karoly (1991) to specifically evaluate negative interactions in an IM context. Similar to the questions about IM social support, we calculated a person-specific mean score by computing the mean of the separate items between 0 (i.e., "I completely disagree") and 6 (i.e., "I totally agree") on each row. While the betweenperson reliability (ꙍ between = .66) was acceptable, the within-person reliability (ꙍ within = .26) was poor.

Striving for an as optimal balance between the latter low value and the complexity of the statistical design of this study, the averaged index was, in line with the post-registration, retained.

Loneliness. The item "Since the last beep, I have felt alone in the world." was used as an indicator for momentary feelings of loneliness. Identical to the previous items, loneliness was scored on a seven-point scale between 0 and 6.

Depressive Feelings. The extent to which adolescents showed momentary signs of depressive feelings was measured with the item "Since the last beep, I have felt down.". The same scoring applies as above.

Presence or Absence of NSSI Behavior. Derived from the NSSI screening questionnaire of Baetens & Van Hove (2021), the yes-no question "Since the last beep, have you intentionally hurt yourself without wanting to die (e.g., cut, scratched, or hit yourself)?" assessed the presence respectively the absence of NSSI behavior.

Social Comparison Orientation.

The extent to which adolescents had a social comparison orientation was taken into account by the Iowa-Netherlands Comparison Orientation Measure (INCOM; Gibbons & Buunk, 1999). Using 11 statements with a five-point Likert scale ("totally disagree"

-"totally agree"), the INCOM gauges individuals' tendency to compare the own abilities (i.e., Comparison of Abilities subscale) and opinions (i.e., Comparison of Opinions subscale) with those of others. Sum scores at the subscale and overarching level can be calculated by adding up the relevant components (i.c., between 0 and 4). Previous studies have shown an acceptable reliability and validity (e.g., Gibbons & Buunk, 1999). However, based on research identifying an overall SCO factor (i.e., regular items) and an "indifference for comparison" factor (i.e., reverse scored items), we decided to exclude the two reverse coded items (Schneider & Schupp, 2014; De Raeymaecker et al., 2025).

Accordingly, only a total SCO of 9 items was computed. The corresponding internal consistency was high (α Total = .76).

Data Analysis Plan

In order to establish the social and psychological outcomes of IM conversations with peers, we used Residual Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (RDSEM) with Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation (Asparouhov et al., 2018; McNeish & Hamaker, 2020). This technique allows lagged predictors, cross(-lagg)ed effects (cf. time-series modeling), and latent variables (cf. structural equation modeling) to be incorporated in the analysis while still being able to uncover individual differences (cf. multilevel modeling) (Asparouhov et al., 2018; McNeish & Hamaker, 2020). Specifically for RDSEM, the autoregressive estimates are calculated based on the residuals rather than on the observed variables (i.e., regular DSEM), meaning that the temporal aspect in the studied association is removed. RDSEM is the recommend analysis method for contemporaneous associations and data with a time trend (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2019). Bayesian estimation, which often prevents nonconvergence issues, was primary chosen for its computational power rather than its epistemology (Asparouhov et al., 2018; Levy & McNeish, 2023).

Before running our main RDSEM models, we tested whether the assumption of stationarity McNeish & Hamaker, 2020) held. Given that the stationarity assumption was violated for all outcomes (positive association for loneliness and depressive feelings; negative association for IM social support and NSSI behavior), the variable "days since the start of the study" was incorporated in the analyses (within-person part). Afterwards, several multilevel vector autoregressive (MVAR) models with 2500 iterations of the MCMC algorithm were conducted. The TINTERVAL statement "time_h (1.5)", instructing the data to be divided in intervals of 1.5 hours (i.e., survey blocks of 1h15 + betweenprompt interval of 15 minutes) and filling in missingness based on a Kalman filter, was included in every model, reducing bias related to missingness and semi-random sampling. In line with our postregistration, completed surveys of participants with a low compliance rate were not excluded (Jacobson, 2020). However, given that (R)DSEM is in essence based on momentary deviations of the person-specific mean (Asparouhov et al., 2018), participants without within-person variation in the MVAR-predictors or outcomes were excluded. Moreover, individuals with nearly constant time series (i.e., within-person variance ≤ .02) were removed as well. The latter approach provides a conservative estimate of the size of the differences among recurrent observations of a person over time (i.e., autoregression; Scott et al., 2021), thereby guaranteeing a minimally informative dynamic process (McNeish & Hamaker, 2020).

For all research questions covering the social outcomes of IM, we tested two series of contemporaneous RDSEM models based on Bayesian linear regression. In order to answer RQ1A, we focused on a RDSEM model that included the time-varying constructs IM social support and IM negative social exchange. At the within-person level (level 1: repeated assessments), this model contained IM social support, its autoregressive effect, the covariate IM negative social exchange, its autoregressive effect, and the random unique variances of both variables. All parameters were latent person-mean centered, meaning that a latent "true" person-mean is calculated after which the adolescents' observations are centered (Asparouhov & Muthen, 2019). Subsequently, six RDSEM models addressing the effect of the dispositional traits were run with regard to RQ 1B and 1C. In these models, the dispositional traits extraversion, neuroticism, SCO, and the social anxiety components "Fear of Negative Evaluation" (FNE), "Social Avoidance and Distress-General" (SAD-G), and "Social Avoidance and Distress in New Situations" (SAD-N) were individually introduced in the above-described model. In all of these models, age and sex 2 were included as covariates. All dispositions, except sex, were standardized and thus centered. Furthermore, the latent residual variances were allowed to covariate. In line with our post-registration, covariation between the random slopes was precluded to reduce the complexity of the model. To answer our research questions, we were interested in (a) the effects of the dispositional traits on the person-specific mean of IM social support and (b) the effects of dispositional traits on the association between IM negative social exchange and IM social support. In case several dispositions were significant, we planned to run a supplementary RDSEM model to test the combined effect of these characteristics. Hereby, we recognize that dispositions might cluster within persons. For more information, see post-registration.

Considering each of the three psychological outcomes of IM, the same research structure was followed. Note that for loneliness and depressive feelings, Bayesian linear models were applied, while in case of the binary presence or absence of NSSI behavior, Bayesian probit modeling was required. In addition, acknowledging the time frame of the corresponding survey items, we focused on contemporaneous associations for the former two and on a temporaneous one in case of NSSI behavior. For NSSI behavior, the abovementioned time trend prompted modeling both the autoregressive and the temporaneous cross-lagged within-person effects at the level of the residuals (Fang et al., 2023; McNeish et al., 2023). Before interpreting the results, model convergence was ascertained. In line with Hamaker et al. (2018), we considered a Gelman-Rubin Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) value near 1 as an indication of model convergence (Gelman & Rubin, 1992). To reduce the possible effect of autocorrelation on the estimated parameters, we applied a thinning factor 20 in every RDSEM model (i.e., every 20 th sample from the MCMC algorithm was used for statistical interference). All estimates were interpreted on the basis of 95% Bayesian credible intervals.

Parameters with a 95% posterior distribution without zero were declared significant.

Descriptive statistics and intraclass correlations (ICCs) were calculated for all study variables (see Table 2). Most adolescents reported moderately high IM social support, little IM negative social exchange, almost no loneliness and a negligible amount of depressive feelings. Discussion

Based on the presumed working mechanisms of two large groups of theories, this study aimed to delineate the social and psychological impact of instant messaging. While the interpersonalconnection-behaviors framework (Clark et al., 2018) prioritizes the power of (dis)connection behavior, the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypothesis (Kraut et al., 2002) highlights the importance of dispositional traits that remain present across situations. In addition, the interplay between the two, in which we analyzed whether the dispositions altered the strength of the association between the IM behavior and the outcome, was taken into account. In the following text, each of the three mechanisms is discussed.

The Power of IM Behavior

We robustly found evidence for the predictive power of IM behavior in the prediction of both social and psychological outcomes of instant messaging. Adolescents who perceived higher levels of IM connection/disconnection behavior compared to their own average, reported better respectively worse social and psychological health. Only NSSI behavior could not robustly be predicted by the within-person level of IM social support after taking the temporal dynamics of the model into account.

However, these inconsistencies are probably due to the small sample size and the low prevalence rate, which are not surprising in community samples. Model changes might shift the limited information about infrequent acts of NSSI across parameters.

Although this study is, to the best of our knowledge, one of the first studies that provides formal evidence for the interpersonal-connection-behaviors framework (Clark et al., 2018), these findings are congruent with studies that did not explicitly use this terminology. Research has repetitively shown that the implicit requirement to be continuously at your peers' disposal through IM, and the corresponding pressure this creates (Winstone et al., 2021; Yau & Reich, 2018), lowers the perceived IM social support (Tsai et al., 2019). Relatedly, conversations with friends provide a reciprocal understanding of each other's daily lives (Lenhart, 2015) and create an atmosphere of IM social support (Winstone et al., 2021), which ultimately has been linked to better psychological adjustment (Cole et al., 2017). Furthermore, a recent within-person study concluded that about 50% of the social media outcomes can be attributed to dynamic factors and/or measurement error, thereby explaining the limited the consistency of effects over time (Pouwels et al., 2025).

Given that we controlled for the autoregressive effects of the covariates and the outcomes, we can assume that IM social support counteracted possibly disadvantageous effects of the previous on the present mental state (except for NSSI behavior) (i.e., average within-subject effect). These effects were present despite the low levels of loneliness and depressive feelings across time. Notably, this finding does not correspond to other IM research (Verbeij et al., 2024): youngsters who received more positive messages compared to their own average did not describe higher affective wellbeing.

However, analogous to the well-known differences in the effects of received and perceived social support (e.g., Frison & Eggermont, 2015), we believe this discrepancy might be attributed to objective (i.e., based on deep-learning analyses of archived IM conversations) vs. subjective (i.e., based on a questionnaire about experiences) estimates. Similarly, Shawcroft et al. (2025) reported that the perceived feelings that social media initiated, not the content itself, was elementary for the individualspecific psychological outcomes. This reiterates the conclusion that the same (digital) content might give rise to dissimilar perceptions depending of the characteristics of the person involved (van der Wal et al., 2024).

The Power of Dispositional Traits

The importance of the dispositions was dependent on the outcome under inquiry. For the studied social outcome of IM (i.e., IM social support), no significant disposition-related betweenperson differences could be established. For IM social support, a part of the explanation might lie in the generally low presence of IM negative social exchange. This low rate mimics, at least for the aspect IM ridicule, the recently confirmed finding that even in IM conversations a positivity bias exists (Verbeij et al., 2024). Regarding the psychological outcomes (i.e., loneliness, depressive feelings, NSSI behavior), neuroticism just not reached significance with regard to the outcome loneliness (but did in the sensitivity analysis). Considering depressive feelings, several dispositional traits mattered. People with higher scores on (a) neuroticism, (b) social anxiety and distress in new situations, (c) fear of negative evaluation, (d) social comparison orientation, and (e) women showed more depressive feelings. Similarly, although the different between-person variables might have "competed" to explain the limited variance in the model, adolescents with higher scores on (a) neuroticism and (b) social anxiety and distress in general and (c) younger individuals (in three of the six models) performed more NSSI behavior. Yet, as the NSSI sample was very small, the latter should be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, this means that we did not find evidence for the rich-get-richer hypothesis, nor for the poorget-richer hypothesis (Kraut et al., 2002). Instead, our results only underscored poor-get-poorer effects.

Rooted in the meaningful grouping of adolescents by their combination of dispositional traits, previous research hypothesized the existence of a general vulnerability factor, regardless of the offline or online context one is in (De Raeymaecker et al., 2025). This study seems to endorse this claim, (at least) with regard to depressive feelings. Sex was only "significant" (i.e., zero not in the 95% credible interval) in the models where the dispositions were "not significant", while the dispositions were "significant" in the models where sex was "insignificant". In accordance with an exploratory correlation analysis and an independent samples t-test, we suspect multicollinearity. A similar phenomenon might have taken place in the model that combined all formerly significant dispositions, of which ultimately none reached significance. Indeed, the shared variance of the dispositions makes it more difficult for the individual predictors to become significant. Based on their research, De Raeymaecker et al. (2025) stress the importance of bringing the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypothesis back to its starting point: the skills to attract social support in the offline and/or online modality. In this case, this would mean that the combination of "poor" characteristics like neuroticism, social anxiety, and/or social comparison orientation, and/or "rich" characteristics like extraversion determines whether and to what extent one experiences poor mental health, both in offline and online environments. This intuitively matches with the recurrent explanatory hypotheses that online risk factors coincide with offline vulnerability factors (Fassi et al., 2025), that the preference for offline and online support seeking is person-specific (Mackenzie et al., 2020), and that individuals with a psychiatric condition react with an overall intensified emotional responsiveness (Janssen et al., 2025). Additional initial evidence can be found in the study of Steinsbekk et al. (2024), who followed up a Norway cohort for several years, and demonstrated that the social skills of these adolescents were (apart from those with high social anxiety) not affected by their social media use.

Nevertheless, in this dataset, individual differences frequently existed with regard to the residual variances of depressive feelings. Depressive feelings were in several models less predictable for women and adolescents with higher levels of neuroticism, fear of negative evaluation (FNE), social anxiety and distress in new situations (SAD-N), and social comparison orientation. Again, these characteristics align with the ones discussed above. This means that, even though they reported higher mean levels of depressive feelings, their depression levels fluctuated more over time compared to males and youngsters with lower levels of these dispositions. These fluctuations might be triggered by situational (IM) factors. For example, while posting personal messages towards a small (one-on-one) and a large audience (a group) were positively linked to wellbeing in case of high socially anxious young adults, Desjarlais (2022) found an insignificant and a negative association for individuals with low social anxiety. Furthermore, similar to the study of Anthony et al. (2023), the relationship (strength) adolescents have with their interaction partner might be important. For instance, the social and mental health of individuals with a high social comparison orientation might better when interacting with a best friend compared to a regular classmate.

A Goldilocks Effect: the Power of Development

Both for the social and one of the psychological outcomes of instant messaging, a cross-level interaction of age was found. Age marginally to significantly strengthened the negative association between IM negative social exchange and IM social support, and clearly reinforced the negative association between IM social support and depressive feelings. These goldilocks-like findings highlight the importance of interpreting IM-related outcomes in a developmental framework. As the mean age of the adolescents in this sample was 15.04 years old, the established advantageous effect can be understood from the developmental perspective of middle adolescence and beyond. Middle adolescence, of which the starting point is placed around 14 years old, is typically characterized by increased interest in peers, with whom they start spending more time (De Goede et al., 2009). This shift brings along an increased tendency to compare themselves with peers (Van der Aar et al., 2018), and thus a developmentally normal rise in social comparison orientation (Buunk et al., 2020), but also allows more emotional intimacy in friendship relationships (De Goede et al., 2009). By comparison, late adolescents have fewer friends with whom they retain closer connections, thereby emphasizing emotional intimacy (Neal, 2023). Analogously, the type of social media use, supported by progressive emotion regulation capacities, changes throughout development (Brown et al., 2026). While young individuals are predominantly interested in identity exploration and peer approval, older persons focus more on maintaining contact with meaningful others and use social media in a value-driven way (Brown et al., 2026). Accordingly, the association between instant messaging frequency and relationship strength is stronger in late adolescence and young adulthood than at later ages (Vauclair et al., 2023). Furthermore, the rate of liking, opining, and broadcasting behaviors lowers during late adolescence (Steinsbekk et al., 2024). This evolution seems to go hand-in-hand with the progressively reduced number of agreements adolescents have with their parents (e.g., about whom they chat with; about what they share) as they grow older (Vanwynsberghe et al., 2024). Concluding, positive mechanisms may start to gain the upper hand throughout adolescence, while mechanisms that are traditionally linked to disadvantageous social media effects (e.g., social comparison; Le Blanc-Brillon et al., 2025) lose, at least in an instant messaging context, their importance. In line with the Dynamic Systems Theory (Smith & Thelen, 2003), one changing element might set into motion an advantageous change for the system(s) (i.c., the adolescent (and his friends)). As a remark, we acknowledge that this effect might not generalize to adolescents' media use in general, which underscores the value of looking at more specific functional aspects as instant messaging.

Which Theory was Supported? Implications

Even though the importance depended on the outcome studied, we found evidence for each of the three frameworks that were discussed in this paper. In line with the Differential Susceptibility to Media Effects Model (DSMM; Valkenburg & Peter, 2013), perceived (dis)connection behavior (i.e., social factors in the DSMM), dispositions (i.e., dispositional factors in the DSMM) and the developmental interplay between the two (i.e., developmental factors in the DSMM) all mattered. In this model, all three differential susceptibilities are able to impact the immediate media response states and the more long-term media effects directly (i.e., as a predictor) or indirectly (i.e., as a moderator) in a parallel way. Although our data supported both the direct and indirect effects at the immediate to short-term level (i.e., the last IM conversation adolescents had), not all factors returned in all models. Given that perceived (dis)connection behavior consistently proved to be relevant, except for NSSI behavior, we advocate for a continued scholarly interest in the content of instant messaging and a withdrawal from the overly general time-based measures. From a clinical perspective, behavior is easier to change than the by definition stable dispositions. Based on this study, reducing negative IM social exchange and boosting IM social support (e.g., by limiting IM conversations to the ones with pleasant people) seems to be relevant for one's social and mental health. With the implementation of the Digital Service Act, these goals should become more easy to achieve (e.g., guideline to impede downloading minors' information or adding them involuntarily to a group). Regarding the dispositions, we found significant differences related to depressive feelings and loneliness. This underscores that mental health support or preventive courses should be attuned to the dispositions of the person involved. Third, in some analyses, we established a protective effect of age: age strengthened the advantageous negative association between IM behavior and social or psychological outcomes. Hence, supportive or preventive courses about IM should be mainly directed at early to middle adolescents.

Yet, optimistically, perceived IM social support and depressive feelings might even show progress in the absence of intervention.

Limitations

In this study, we focused on momentary effects. This does not allow to claim causality. However, similar to other studies (e.g., Beyens et al., 2021), we aimed to make the time between the IM conversation and the social and psychological outcomes as small as possible. Accordingly, we deliberately chose for the smallest unit of inference. In case of the social outcomes, this means that both IM negative social exchange and IM social support were measured with regard to the same time frame: the last IM conversation adolescents had with their peers. This guaranteed that both perceptions were assessed in relation to the same conversation and conversation partner. Although one predominantly has conversations with individuals one feels at ease with (Lieberman & Schroeder, 2020), differences have been showed when IM-ing with best friends vs. with cyberfriends (Anthony et al., 2023). Moreover, we assume that relationships will be affected by the accumulated previous offline and online interactions, thereby making certain interpretations more likely (e.g., He always writes concise messages. I shouldn't interpret this as rude or unfriendly behavior.). Note that in this study, we took the autoregressive effects of both time-variant variables into account. Hence, it is not sure that the controlled for previous conversation was held with the same person or a person with an equally strong relationship.

Regarding the psychological outcomes, there was a difference in the indicated timeframe of the predictor (i.e., during the last chat conversation with peers) and the outcomes (i.e., since the last beep). According to McNeish and Hamaker (2020), this discrepancy should be taken into account to decide which of the explorable associations comes closest to temporal precedence. Given that individuals tend to prioritize recent perceptions when assessing past information (i.e., recency effect; Yoo et al., 2025), we assume that adolescents mainly evaluated their loneliness and depressive feelings based on their most recent experiences. Hence, we focused on contemporaneous associations. For the binary outcome NSSI behavior, an intentional and thus easily identifiable act, we concentrated on a cross-lagged association. However, for the former two outcomes, we acknowledge that we cannot confirm that the instant messaging conversation took place before (what mostly mattered for) momentary loneliness and depressive feelings.

Closely related to the previous limitation, this study concentrated on the semi-random immediate to short-term effects of instant messaging. Although this reduces memory effects, we did not use objective registration tools to determine when the IM conversation took place exactly. Both older (e.g., Bayer et al., 2018) and newer studies (Klingelhoefer et al., 2025) have demonstrated that, depending on the outcome, different time intervals might produce different results. For example, Bayer et al. (2018) determined that shared Facebook content could be linked to significantly increased positive feelings after 10 minutes, but not after 30 minutes. In addition, Klingelhoefer et al. (2025) demonstrated that, after objectively measuring the time lag and the time window till the EMA prompt, the extent to which outcomes reach significance is largely time-specific. Scholars have not yet enough knowledge about the time-(in)variant nature of these kind of social media outcomes (Pouwels et al., 2025). Building on the typical EMA-design, extending the study duration or complementing the EMAsurveys with cross-sectional questionnaires over time would be relevant to understand the more distal and cumulative effects.

Third, although we took the autoregressive effects of the outcome variables and the covariates into account, we only included one crossed within-person effect. This implies that we did not test the reversed effect, which might have been equally plausible. Indeed, youngsters with high levels of depressive symptoms perceive social media in another way than non-depressed adolescents (Janssen et al., 2025). Besides the fact that they are overly responsive to potential interpersonal treats, they also experience less online social support (Janssen et al., 2025). Nevertheless, in line with what we explained above, one can wonder whether these individuals possess a vulnerable dispositional profile.

For example, more neurotic individuals have a higher probability to be depressed as well (e.g., Navrady et al., 2017). Considering that individuals with these symptoms experience offline and online social difficulties as well (Mcbride & Preyde, 2022), we again arrive at the foundational thesis of the rich-getricher and the poor-get-richer hypothesis: the ability to obtain social support regardless of the communication modality. Looking at the broader picture, Radovic et al. (2017) presume a vicious cycle in which (a) feeling more depressed builds up to perceiving less social support and (b) the absence of perceived social support precipitates depressive feelings. Yet, one can ponder over cause and consequence. Future studies could consider a mediation approach which allows identifying how variables follow up on one another throughout time.

Additionally, linked to the aspect of generalization, our sample might be, for several reasons, less representative for the general adolescent population. First, the youngsters in our sample reported quite high IM social support and (quite) low IM negative social exchange, loneliness, depressive feelings and NSSI behavior. Considering IM negative social exchange, extreme observations were rarely present in this sample (i.c., the scores 4.75, 5, 5.25 were each only once present in the dataset). Vanwynsberghe et al. (2024) notice that "expose groups", in which gossip and unflattering pictures of bullied persons appear, are one of the newer ways to harm others. Second, most participants (63%) were women. Furthermore, all data were collected during the holidays, meaning that participants might have been more relaxed. Alternately, it might have created more opportunities or topics to discuss with peers.

Lastly, we highlight two questionnaire-related remarks. First, IM negative social exchange, which was calculated as the mean of four questions per EMA prompt, showed a less optimal betweenand within-person reliability. Additionally, all EMA surveys needed to be answered with regard to peers. This was highlighted during the information sessions and was explicitly mentioned in several questions (i.e., "conversation with peers"). However, some questionnaires were filled in with regard to other groups (i.e., by choosing the answer option "other: please specify: …"; e.g., parents, family, boss). Although these interactions were excluded before analyzing the data, it remains possible that some non-identifiable non-peer chats were taken into account in this study.

Conclusion

Based on the rich-get-richer hypothesis, the poor-get-richer hypothesis, the interpersonalconnection-behaviors framework, and the interplay of these perspectives, this study analyzed the social and the psychological outcomes related to instant messaging. While we found robust evidence for the within-person association between (dis)connection behavior and all outcomes (except for NSSI behavior), consistent between-person differences pertaining to the dispositional traits could only be identified for depressive feelings. More specifically, adolescents with higher scores on (a) neuroticism, (b) fear of negative evaluation, (c) social avoidance and distress in new situations, and (d) social comparison orientation, and (e) women perceived more depressive feelings. For loneliness, a tentative association was found for neuroticism. Third, we found (tentative) between-person differences in the association between IM behavior and two study outcomes. Age strengthened goldilocks-wise the spillover effect both in case of IM social support (marginal findings) and depressive symptoms (significant results in four of the six models). These findings fit well within the Differential Susceptibility to Media Effects Model that emphasizes the intertwined impact of social, dispositional, and developmental factors.
