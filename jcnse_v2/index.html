Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Research on psychological concepts rooted in historical intellectual traditions has attracted growing attention, yet studies in this field may investigate fundamentally different psychological objects. The present article conceptualizes the modern and traditional approaches to such research and systematically examines their methodological implications. Whereas the modern approach constitutes its objects primarily through contemporary manifestations within a population, the traditional approach specifies psychological targets through theoretical formulations grounded in classical accounts. Such targets may have theoretically specified internal relations and need not already be realized in the populations available for research. Drawing on existing studies of traditional Chinese psychology, the analysis identifies potential methodological mismatches and demonstrates their implications for empirical investigation. Ordinary participants may not possess the understanding or degree of realization required to investigate a target mentality; cultivation may be a methodological precondition rather than merely an intervention to modify existing tendencies; and satisfactory psychometric results do not necessarily establish that a measure represents the target's intended meanings and internal relations. Moreover, findings from partial realizations cannot simply be generalized to fully cultivated forms. These analyses provide principles for selecting participants, determining the role of intervention, evaluating measurement, and delimiting the scope of inference according to the intended research object and question. The article also considers how empirical evidence can inform the evaluation and possible revision of classical psychological claims, while distinguishing empirical investigation from the normative justification of cultivation. Beyond traditional psychology, these methodological insights may inform empirical research on psychological possibilities specified through other theoretical sources.

Researching Traditionally Rooted Psychological Concepts: Methodological Implications from

Traditional Chinese Psychology

Historical intellectual traditions have become important sources of concepts for contemporary psychological research. Scholarship engaging Chinese, Indian, and Islamic traditions illustrates how psychological ideas articulated in earlier intellectual systems can enter modern theoretical and empirical inquiry (Li et al., 2026; Rao et al., 2008; Rassool, 2023). In China, research on traditional Chinese psychology has also received attention amid efforts to develop an autonomous psychological knowledge system drawing on Chinese intellectual resources (Luo & Lin, 2026; Ye & Su, 2023). Scholars have investigated concepts rooted in Confucian, Daoist, and Buddhist thought, including filial piety (Yeh & Bedford, 2003), Zhongyong thinking (中庸思维; Yang et al., 2016), junzi personality (Ge et al., 2021), Confucian oneness (Zhou et al., 2021), and appreciative joy (Gu et al., 2023).

Within this line of research, studies differ in how they define and operationalize traditionally rooted psychological concepts. This variation matters because contemporary understandings and manifestations of these concepts may differ from their classical articulations, as illustrated by filial piety (Bedford & Yeh, 2019). Some studies derive conceptual definitions or measurement content primarily from classical texts.

For example, the initial items of the Junzi personality measure were developed from descriptions of the junzi (君子) in the Analects (Ge et al., 2021). Other studies incorporate contemporary participants' understandings into construct development. Wang and Wang (2010), for instance, used an open-ended survey to collect university students' descriptions of ren (仁) alongside their theoretical analysis, while Ge (2021) generated filial-piety items through both adaptation of classical texts and participants' free generation.

Some existing work has begun to recognize the distinctions and methodological tensions underlying these different practices. For example, Xie et al. (2017) differentiated between "canonic culture" and "popular culture." They argued that canonic culture is abstract and articulated in classical texts, primarily inspiring interpretation and theoretical creativity, whereas popular culture manifests in everyday experience and can be directly measured. Their distinction therefore made clear that classical articulations and popular cultural manifestations should not be treated as interchangeable, but it did not directly address whether and how mentalities articulated in classical texts could themselves become objects of systematic psychological investigation. At the same time, ideal psychology, an approach developed from research on traditional Chinese psychology, explicitly focuses on mentalities articulated in classical texts rather than on those already prevalent among people influenced by the corresponding traditions (Zeng, 2025).

Methodological work associated with ideal psychology has further criticized the assumptions that ordinary participants already possess traditionally articulated ideal mentalities and that findings obtained from limited realizations can be generalized to their more fully realized forms (Liu et al., 2022). Together, these discussions raise a broader methodological question: when a mentality is specified through engagement with classical theory rather than defined by its contemporary manifestations in a population, under what conditions can empirical findings obtained from contemporary participants be interpreted as evidence about that mentality? Yet these discussions have not provided a systematic account of the methodological requirements for investigating classically specified mentalities or the potential mismatches between such targets and conventional empirical research practices.

To address this gap, the present article distinguishes two research orientations toward traditionally rooted psychological concepts. Under the modern approach, the research object is constituted primarily through its contemporary understandings and manifestations within a population. Under the traditional approach, it is specified through theoretical formulations grounded in classical accounts. Importantly, such theoretically specified targets may differ from psychological phenomena already manifested in contemporary populations-a common starting point in empirical psychology-raising particular methodological questions about their investigation. To examine these questions, the article first analyzes how the two approaches differ in the constitution, internal organization, and realization status of their research objects. It then examines the methodological implications of these differences for participant selection, intervention, operationalization and measurement, and the scope of inference. Drawing on examples from research on traditional Chinese psychology, this analysis highlights potential mismatches between research targets and empirical methods or inferences, and provides principles for selecting appropriate methods and interpreting findings. Finally, building on the preceding analyses, the article considers the justification for cultivating traditionally articulated mentalities in contemporary practice and the relationship between research on these mentalities and the continuation of intellectual traditions.

Together, these analyses aim to provide a clearer methodological basis for research on traditionally rooted psychological concepts, with potential implications for empirical inquiry into theoretically articulated psychological targets beyond the historical traditions examined here.

Fundamental Differences in Research Objects

Constitution of the Research Object

The most fundamental distinction between the modern and traditional approaches lies in how the research object is constituted. Because both approaches may address the same historically rooted concept, the distinction does not lie in whether the concept itself originated in antiquity. Rather, it concerns what primarily determines the identity of the object under investigation: its contemporary manifestations among a population, or a theoretical formulation grounded in classical accounts.

Under the modern approach, a historically rooted concept is constituted primarily through its contemporary psychological manifestations. Although the concept originates in an inherited tradition, what defines the object of inquiry is how it is currently understood, experienced, or enacted among the population under investigation. In this sense, the approach follows a population-grounded logic familiar from indigenous and cultural psychology (Kim & Berry, 1993; Markus & Kitayama, 1991): empirical patterns observed among contemporary participants can contribute directly to defining, operationalizing, and revising the construct. The research object is therefore the contemporary form of the historically rooted concept as manifested in the population under study.

By contrast, under the traditional approach, the research object is constituted primarily through engagement with classical texts and their interpretive traditions. These provide the initial theoretical basis for specifying the meanings and boundaries of the target, but do not require recovering a fixed or uncontested original meaning. As hermeneutic philosophy emphasizes, modern interpreters cannot fully reproduce the historical horizons of classical authors (Gadamer, 1975). Therefore, contemporary psychological inquiry necessarily involves interpretation of specific classical texts and, in some cases, deliberate development of traditional concepts. For example, debates over mindfulness often examine how modern interpretations depart from Buddhist accounts or ask what would constitute "authentic" mindfulness in relation to Buddhist traditions (Dreyfus, 2011; Van Gordon & Shonin, 2020). A more deliberate form of theoretical development is illustrated by research on the Five-Pattern account of personality derived from traditional Chinese medicine. As discussed by Yang and Xue (2006), the inherited classification was reformulated to allow individuals to exhibit features of multiple patterns to varying degrees, rather than belonging exclusively to a single type. These examples illustrate how inherited concepts can be reinterpreted or reformulated through engagement with classical accounts, rather than redefined primarily according to how they are understood or manifested in contemporary populations.

The distinction becomes particularly clear when the same historically rooted term is examined under the two approaches. Filial piety (xiao, 孝) provides an illustrative example. Under the traditional approach, filial piety is examined as a construct articulated within a broader moral-relational framework involving hierarchical obligations, ritual propriety, affective attunement, and moral cultivation, as reflected in Confucian classics such as the Analects and the Classic of Filial Piety (e.g., Angle, 2009; Ebrey, 2014).

Under the modern approach, by contrast, filial piety may be reconceptualized primarily in terms of its contemporary psychological forms. The Dual Filial Piety Model, for example, draws on historical and cultural analyses but primarily seeks to explain reciprocal and authoritarian psychological orientations in contemporary parent-child relationships, rather than to examine whether individuals realize filial piety as formulated in a particular classical account (Bedford & Yeh, 2019; Yeh & Bedford, 2003). The difference here is not merely that ancient and modern individuals understand filial piety differently. Rather, the divergence reflects distinct ways of constituting the research object: the modern approach constitutes the object as a contemporary psychological phenomenon instantiated in a population, whereas the traditional approach constitutes it as a theoretically articulated construct. The same historically rooted term may therefore designate different research objects under the two orientations, rather than simply different interpretations of a single object.

Internal Organization of the Research Object

Beyond differences in what constitutes the research object, a further distinction concerns the relations that organize its constituent elements. Classical texts may specify theoretically meaningful relations among these elements that are not exhausted by the covariance among parallel dimensions commonly modeled in conventional psychometric analyses. For example, theoretical accounts of the Daoist construct of wang (忘, forgetting/self-forgetting) specify a sequential organization: coexistence cognition serves as a foundational process, whereas self-forgetting experience and realization represent later or resultant states (Ye & Mao, 2021). Daoist personality illustrates a different kind of whole-part dependency: individual dimensions have been argued not to be fully interpretable in isolation, because their theoretical meaning depends partly on their relations to the other dimensions and to the construct as a whole (Tu & Guo, 2016).

Under the traditional approach, classical accounts specify the meanings and constitutive relations that define the target, although claims about how its elements develop or function together remain open to empirical investigation.

By contrast, under the modern approach, because the target is the contemporary psychological phenomenon as instantiated in a population, its internal organization concerns how that phenomenon is actually organized among contemporary individuals. Classical texts may still inform initial conceptualization and hypotheses about its possible structure, but the contemporary construct need not retain the conceptual distinctions and constitutive organization articulated in those sources. It may depart from the classical organization without thereby misrepresenting the modern research object. At the same time, even when the contemporary organization closely corresponds to the classical specification, the correspondence does not make the two research objects identical: under the modern approach, the organization describes a psychological pattern instantiated in a contemporary population, whereas under the traditional approach, the classical account specifies the target whose organization is being investigated.

Realization Status of the Research Object

A further property of the research object concerns its realization status. Under the modern approach, because the target is identified through its contemporary manifestations in a population, some degree of empirical realization is presupposed. In this respect, the approach follows the familiar logic of most empirical psychological research, including mainstream cultural psychology, which takes already instantiated psychological phenomena as its objects of investigation. Individuals may vary substantially in the extent to which they exhibit the relevant mentality, and the mentality itself need not be highly prevalent, but it is nevertheless understood as a psychological phenomenon that is already instantiated to some extent in the population under investigation. Its realization is therefore part of what makes it an object of inquiry in the first place.

By contrast, under the traditional approach, the research object is specified through classical articulation, so empirical realization is not required for constituting the object. The possibility that a target may not yet be empirically realized is especially salient in traditional Chinese psychology for several reasons. First, psychological constructs articulated in classical traditions may no longer be widely instantiated in contemporary populations, particularly when everyday understandings and practices have changed over time. Second, many traditional mentalities are explicitly associated with learning, selfcultivation, or disciplined practice, and therefore cannot be assumed to arise merely through cultural membership. The Confucian junzi, for example, represents a cultivated form of personhood achieved through sustained education and self-discipline rather than an average psychological type (Angle, 2009; Ivanhoe, 2000). More fundamentally, some constructs are articulated as ideal or aspirational mentalities that may be only rarely, or not yet fully, realized in empirical populations. Recent work has argued that the deliberate pursuit of such ideal mentalities is a distinctive feature of traditional Chinese psychology (Li et al., 2026). Accordingly, the realization status of a traditionally articulated construct is itself an empirical question, distinct from the specification of what the construct is.

Methodological Consequences and Mismatches

Participant Selection

The differences in research objects identified above have several methodological consequences, beginning with participant selection. Under the modern approach, because the research object is the contemporary psychological phenomenon as manifested in a population, sampling members of that population generally provides an appropriate empirical basis for investigating it. This population-based logic is familiar in cultural and indigenous psychology, where participants are recruited as members of the cultural population in which the target psychological pattern is distributed (Kim & Berry, 1993; Markus & Kitayama, 1991). Cultural membership can therefore provide a reasonable starting point for sampling, although the suitability of particular participants still depends on the manifestations or degree of realization relevant to the research question.

By contrast, under the traditional approach, cultural membership alone may not provide a sufficient basis for participant selection. As discussed above, many traditionally articulated mentalities are not assumed to be widely realized and may depend on relevant learning, cultivation, practice, or experience.

When this is the case, the appropriateness of participants depends more directly on whether they possess the knowledge, experience, or degree of realization required to meaningfully instantiate the target mentality. Participant selection therefore follows a more competence-or realization-sensitive logic: individuals with relevant textual familiarity, practical training, or sustained cultivation may provide a more appropriate empirical basis than members of the general cultural population.

A methodological mismatch may arise when a classically articulated construct is investigated among ordinary contemporary participants without establishing that the target mentality is sufficiently realized among them. Research on junzi personality provides an illustrative example. Ge et al. (2021) explicitly sought to clarify the structure and meaning of the junzi in Confucian thought: their theoretical conception was derived from descriptions in the Analects, and the initial questionnaire items were adapted from classical passages concerning the junzi. The research object was therefore constituted primarily through classical articulation, as in the traditional approach. The resulting scale, however, was developed and subsequently applied among ordinary contemporary participants, including university students (e.g., Ge & Hou, 2021). The relevant tension is that the junzi in the Confucian tradition is a cultivated form of personhood attained through sustained education and self-discipline rather than an ordinary personality type (Ames & Rosemont, 2010; Angle, 2009), which raises the question of whether ordinary contemporary participants adequately understand, let alone instantiate, the classically articulated junzi. This creates two related issues. First, because item retention and the final factor structure were partly determined by responses from these ordinary contemporary participants, it is questionable whether the resulting fivefactor solution can be taken to reflect the internal structure of "Junzi personality in Confucius' thought," as Ge et al. (2021) intended. Second, and more importantly, subsequent research used scores on this scale to draw conclusions about the psychological implications of junzi. For example, Ge and Hou (2021) explicitly related their findings to Confucian claims about the junzi and mental health, interpreting the positive associations of "Junzi personality" as empirical support for these classical propositions. They also acknowledged that cultural change may have shifted the meanings captured by their classically derived scale. Yet if scale scores obtained from ordinary participants have not been shown to represent the degree to which they realize the classically articulated junzi, it remains unclear to what extent these findings provide evidence about the psychological consequences of junzi itself rather than about the contemporary psychometric construct measured by the scale. Similar participant-target concerns have also been raised in research on ideal mentalities. For example, Liu et al. (2022) criticized what they termed the realistic assumption, referring to the unwarranted assumption that participants have already acquired a target mentality. These discussions are not revisited in detail here because they were not framed around the distinction between the traditional and modern approaches developed in the present article.

The potential methodological mismatch illustrated by the junzi case does not imply that generalpopulation samples are inherently inappropriate for research on classically articulated constructs. For example, Mencius maintained that all people possess an incipient feeling of compassion (ceyin zhi xin, 恻 隐之心; Mengzi, 2A6). General-population samples may therefore be appropriate for examining whether and under what conditions such compassion is manifested. By contrast, Mencius regarded this incipient compassion as only the beginning of benevolence (ren), whose full realization required cultivation.

Ordinary participants may therefore be appropriate for investigating whether and how ren develops through cultivation, but not necessarily for examining its characteristics or psychological consequences in a fully realized form. Thus, participant selection should be guided by what the articulated tradition specifies about the target mentality and what the study seeks to establish, rather than by a categorical preference for general-population or specialized samples. When the intended inquiry requires a degree of realization not found among available participants, a further methodological question arises-whether the target mentality must first be cultivated before it can be empirically investigated.

The Role of Intervention

The methodological role of intervention depends in part on whether the target mentality is already realized to the extent required by the research question. Under the modern approach, the target is a psychological phenomenon already manifested in a contemporary population, and thus researchers can begin by observing its existing manifestations. As in psychological research more generally, researchers can also intervene to further cultivate or modify psychological tendencies already present in the population and evaluate the effects of such interventions (e.g., Yang et al., 2020). Under the traditional approach, intervention may serve a further methodological role. Some classically articulated mentalities depend on deliberate learning or cultivation for their realization. Investigating their characteristics or psychological consequences therefore requires participants who have attained the degree of realization relevant to the inquiry. For such targets, cultivation may constitute a methodological precondition for obtaining relevant evidence, whether undertaken before recruitment (as in studies of experienced meditators, e.g., Engen & Singer, 2015) or through an intervention implemented as part of the study. This requirement is especially evident for ideal mentalities whose full realization has not yet been empirically established, as noted above. According to ideal psychology, research on these mentalities follows a cycle of design, achievement, and evaluation. That is, researchers use interventions to cultivate the target mentality and establish a basis for subsequent empirical investigation (Zeng, 2025). For example, Zhou et al. (2021) sought to cultivate the Confucian ideal mentality of oneness through meditation practice and psychoeducation in mindfulness-based positive psychology. Beyond ideal mentalities, some mentalities articulated in classical traditions are already empirically realized, but their acquisition by individuals still requires training. For example, mindfulness as an emotion regulation skill is cultivated through meditation practice. However, dispositional mindfulness refers to the tendency to be mindful in everyday life and is often assessed among participants without such training. Drawing conclusions about cultivated mindfulness from these responses has been criticized as potentially misleading (see Zeng, 2025).

However, conducting an intervention does not by itself establish that the empirical requirements of the study have been met. Zhou et al. (2024) noted that interventions targeting ideal mentalities had not demonstrated their full realization and often left the expected degree of attainment unclear. For the present analysis, interventions involving either ideal or existing mentalities should be evaluated against the specific requirements of inquiry into the classically articulated target. For example, conceptual instruction may suffice when the study examines participants' understanding of a classical concept, provided that the relevant understanding has been established. In contrast, research on the psychological consequences of a stable cultivated mentality may require evidence that participants have attained the relevant state and can sustain it in everyday life.

Operationalization and Measurement

In addition to participant selection and intervention, differences in object constitution have implications for operationalization and measurement. Under the modern approach, because the target is a contemporary psychological phenomenon, standard psychometric analyses, such as factor analysis, can provide evidence about the organization of its indicators in the population being studied (Flake et al., 2017). Under the traditional approach, however, the target is specified through theoretical formulations grounded in classical accounts, so psychometric evidence must be considered in relation to the target's specified meanings and internal relations.

First, construct development and item formulation should be guided by the meanings specified in the selected classical account. Bian et al. (2019), for example, drew on Mencian thought in developing a conscience measure for contemporary Chinese adolescents. Their filial piety and fraternal duty dimension combined adolescents' own attitudes and behaviors towards family members with perceived parental care, as reflected in the item "I think my parents care about me." In the Mencian account, however, filial and fraternal concern involves one's own love for parents and respect for elder siblings (Mengzi, 7A15). If scores on this dimension are interpreted as representing adolescents' own filial and fraternal orientation in the Mencian sense, the inclusion of perceived parental care requires further justification. Even when the theoretical account makes the relevant conceptual distinctions, the items may not preserve them. Li and Hou (2012) distinguished Confucian understanding of fate from fatalism, emphasizing self-cultivation and an active response to circumstances, whereas their fate-thinking items focused on beliefs in predetermined outcomes. Researchers should therefore specify which defining features their construct and its items represent, clarify any deliberate narrowing or reconstruction of the classical target, and interpret the resulting scores within that stated scope.

Even when the items express the intended classical meanings, participants may understand them differently, particularly when the target involves unfamiliar knowledge or cultivated experience. Zhou et al. (2024) highlighted this difficulty in assessing ideal mentalities: participants may not understand concepts such as emptiness and oneness before training, yet explaining these concepts during preintervention assessment may itself constitute an intervention. When the inquiry concerns participants' understanding and experience following cultivation, post-intervention qualitative interviews offer one possible alternative to pre-post self-report assessment (Zeng, 2025). For research on a classically articulated target, such interviews could ask participants to explain the concept in their own words and describe concrete experiences relevant to it. Researchers could then examine how these accounts correspond to the meanings and defining features specified in the selected classical tradition. This would help clarify whether participants' responses reflect the intended understanding and experience of the target, although post-intervention interviews alone do not directly quantify pre-post change.

Second, measurement should provide a basis for examining the internal relations articulated in classical accounts, which may extend beyond covariance among parallel dimensions. For example, mindfulness scales commonly distinguish several facets, whose separate associations with psychological symptoms are then examined (e.g., Baer et al., 2006). Awareness-related facets have been associated with greater depressive symptoms at very low levels of nonreactivity, and with greater distress after controlling for acceptance (Desrosiers et al., 2014; Walach et al., 2025). If these facet-level associations are attributed to mindfulness as a coordinated process of awareness and acceptance, researchers may misleadingly conclude that the classically specified mentality itself is associated with greater distress. As a potential solution, Zeng et al. (2015) suggested pairing items about the same emotional situation, with one assessing whether the person notices emerging emotions or impulses and the other assessing whether they respond with acceptance. These paired measures could support analyses of whether the association between awareness and distress varies with acceptance.

The preceding example concerns how distinct elements function together. In some cases, internal relations may also help determine what an element means within the target. For example, Tu and Guo (2016) argued that yielding within Daoist personality must be understood in relation to naturalness, genuineness, and other features of the whole. They noted that superficially similar retreat might instead reflect strategic calculation. Where such dependencies form part of the target's specification, identifying distinct factors does not establish that each dimension score retains its intended classical meaning when interpreted in isolation. Tu and Guo also discussed situational assessment as a possible way to examine behavior alongside its context, underlying intentions, and the person's overall characteristics. Such assessment could provide information that isolated dimension scores omit. Further methodological work is needed to clarify and validate how this information supports interpreting a measured tendency in the intended Daoist sense.

Notably, attending to classical accounts of conceptual organization does not mean that measurement results must reproduce a predetermined statistical structure. As discussed above, the selected classical account may specify the constituent elements to be represented and the conditions under which they can be interpreted as parts of the whole. However, these specifications do not by themselves determine the appropriate factor structure, the correlations among component scores, or whether a total score is justified.

Measurement development should therefore provide an empirically supported basis for examining classical claims about how these elements are associated or develop in sequence. Such claims are hypotheses to be tested, rather than outcomes that measurement must guarantee.

Scope of Inference

Under the modern approach, findings about a traditionally rooted concept primarily concern its contemporary psychological form and thus do not directly substantiate claims about its classically articulated form. By contrast, under the traditional approach, researchers investigate how mentalities One inferential problem concerns simplification: findings about a construct sharing only some features with a classical mentality are taken to characterize that mentality as a whole. For example, research on general oneness beliefs examines the view that everything is fundamentally one (Diebels & Leary, 2019), whereas Neo-Confucian accounts of oneness also incorporate ethical commitments concerning care for other people, creatures, and things (Tien, 2012). Findings about the ontological belief may inform discussion of that aspect of the classical account, but do not by themselves establish the psychological consequences of the mentality incorporating those ethical commitments.

Another associated inferential problem has been termed the linear assumption (Liu et al., 2022): findings obtained at partial realization are extended to fuller realization as though the observed relationships must remain unchanged. This issue warrants particular care where classical Chinese accounts of cultivation describe qualitative transitions between stages. If such transitions occur, fuller realization may involve changes in psychological functioning rather than simply a greater magnitude of an established relationship. For example, Ge and Hou (2021) reported positive associations between junzi personality scores and mental health. Even if those scores reflect partial realization of relevant qualities, the findings do not establish that the same relationships hold for cultivated junzi personhood. Similarly, work on ideal mentalities such as oneness emphasizes distinguishing degrees of mastery rather than generalizing across them indiscriminately (Zeng, 2025). Research at lower degrees of realization remains informative, particularly when higher degrees or ideal forms are not yet available for empirical investigation.

Nevertheless, conclusions should distinguish findings about the degrees examined from claims about fuller realization.

A further inferential issue concerns how contemporary findings bear on classical claims across historical contexts. Even when a study adequately investigates a classically specified mentality, the relationships observed may depend on the historical and social conditions under which it is realized. For example, even if contemporary research adequately captures the Confucian conception of filial piety, its association with psychological well-being may depend on family roles and obligations that differ from those in historical settings. Therefore, findings under contemporary conditions can inform the present applicability of a classical claim without necessarily establishing whether it held in its original context. At the same time, where the classical claim is intended to hold more generally, contemporary evidence may also support or challenge it. For example, Liu and Guo (2016) drew on animal research to reassess the factual premises of classical accounts of human-animal differences. Empirical findings may provide grounds for supporting or revising classical claims, but the conclusions warranted depend on both the scope of the classical claim and the conditions actually investigated.

Broader Implications of the Traditional Approach Normative Justification of Cultivation

Building on the preceding methodological analysis, the traditional approach also raises questions about the cultivation of classically articulated mentalities in contemporary psychological practice. As discussed above, research on classically articulated mentalities may require deliberate cultivation.

Moreover, classical traditions often justify the pursuit of ideals such as the Confucian junzi within broader ethical or religious frameworks (Angle, 2009; Ivanhoe, 2000). Although these frameworks may have carried normative authority in their historical settings, such authority cannot simply be presumed in contemporary pluralistic societies. Adopting a classically articulated mentality as a desirable goal of contemporary cultivation thus requires further justification.

One possibility is to argue that a mentality is worth cultivating in its own right, which requires reasons that remain defensible in the contemporary context rather than reliance on traditional authority alone.

Alternatively, researchers may recommend its cultivation for anticipated benefits, such as improved wellbeing or mental health. This instrumental justification requires theoretical and empirical support for the expected effects. Evidence of such benefits can support cultivation for those purposes, but does not by itself establish the mentality's value as an ethical or spiritual ideal. Related concerns about the justification of traditional cultivation goals have been raised in discussions of mindfulness-based interventions and ideal psychology (Harrington & Dunne, 2015; Liu & Zeng, 2023; Zhou et al., 2024). Accordingly, researchers should clarify the grounds on which they recommend cultivation and provide justification appropriate to their claims.

For ideal mentalities under the traditional approach, this problem is particularly acute. There may be insufficient evidence of their benefits to recommend cultivating them on empirical grounds. Yet obtaining such evidence may first require cultivating them. To address this dilemma, the present article suggests distinguishing exploratory cultivation for research from recommending these mentalities as goals of psychological practice. The former can proceed with a defensible research rationale and appropriate ethical safeguards, without assuming that the targets are beneficial. The latter requires further justification for why they are worth pursuing, drawing on normative arguments, empirical evidence, or both.

Research and the Continuation of Tradition

Another implication concerns how the traditional approach relates to inherited traditions themselves.

Because this approach constitutes its research objects through classical accounts, it may appear to require fidelity to those accounts in their entirety. However, studying a classically articulated mentality does not require accepting all the associated theories, practices, or normative claims. As discussed above, empirical claims made in classical theories remain open to examination and revision, while traditional normative ideals require further justification for contemporary cultivation. Moreover, traditional cultivation methods need not be the only or most appropriate means of realizing a classically articulated mentality. For example, Hao et al. (2026) developed a new Daoist meditation method to address difficulties in traditional contemplative operations while retaining an inherited cultivation aim. Thus, grounding research in classical traditions does not entail an obligation to preserve all their inherited responses.

More broadly, by engaging directly with classical accounts, the traditional approach may contribute not only to contemporary psychological knowledge but also to the inheritance and further development of intellectual traditions. Cultural continuity may itself be an important practical goal, sometimes associated with broader cultural and political concerns. Nevertheless, it is not a defining requirement of the traditional approach, whose primary scientific purpose remains the generation of valuable psychological knowledge.

Recent work on continuative development has argued that traditions can be further developed by reopening inherited inquiries and developing new answers, practices, and methods, rather than merely preserving existing ones (Zeng, 2026). Such continuation of inherited inquiries also need not aim at preserving a tradition's culturally distinctive identity. Thus, the traditional approach can contribute to cultural continuity without requiring uncritical preservation of inherited knowledge.

Conclusion

Research on psychological concepts rooted in historical intellectual traditions has attracted growing attention, especially in China. The present article conceptualizes the modern and traditional approaches in this field, bringing into focus a fundamental distinction in their research objects and systematically analyzing its methodological implications (see Table 1 for a summary). At the most fundamental level, the modern approach constitutes its research objects primarily through contemporary understandings and manifestations within a population, whereas the traditional approach specifies them through theoretical formulations grounded in classical accounts. The analysis further clarifies differences in how the internal organization of these objects is specified and whether their empirical realization is presupposed. These distinctions provide a more differentiated understanding of the psychological objects that research on traditionally rooted concepts can investigate.

More importantly, distinguishing these approaches provides a basis for identifying potential methodological mismatches in existing research and guiding future empirical investigations. Drawing on existing studies of traditional Chinese psychology, the analysis shows how findings from contemporary participants may fail to support claims about classically specified mentalities in research adopting the traditional approach when participants lack the relevant understanding or degree of realization, when measures do not adequately represent the target's meanings and internal relations, or when findings about partial realization are generalized to fuller forms. It also highlights that cultivation may sometimes be a methodological precondition for investigating a target, rather than merely a means of modifying an existing psychological tendency. These analyses provide principles for selecting participants, determining the role of intervention, developing appropriate measures, and delimiting the scope of inference according to the intended research object and the specific research question.

Beyond these methodological contributions, the analysis also clarifies how contemporary psychological research can critically engage with inherited theories and practices. It shows how empirical evidence can inform the evaluation and possible revision of classical psychological claims, while emphasizing that recommending the cultivation of classically articulated mentalities requires separate justification. In this sense, the present article contributes a methodological perspective to discussions of the inheritance and further development of traditional psychology, without treating cultural continuity as a defining requirement.

More broadly, the methodological significance of this analysis extends beyond research on traditionally rooted psychological concepts. Although the traditional approach specifies its research objects through engagement with classical accounts, the analysis points to a broader methodological possibility: psychological inquiry can begin with a theoretically specified target rather than a psychological phenomenon already observed in a population. Research on ideal mentalities has emphasized that psychological ideals may be newly designed rather than necessarily derived from classical traditions (Zeng, 2025). Similarly, the methodological considerations developed here may inform empirical investigations of psychological possibilities arising from other theoretical sources, illustrating how research on traditional psychology can contribute to contemporary psychology beyond the particular concepts it examines.
