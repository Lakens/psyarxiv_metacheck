Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Marginal Significance works

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

People may experience large language models (LLMs) as therapeutically allied, emotionally close, or socially substitutive, but whether these relationships become visible in conversations is unknown. We distinguished what users discussed, what unfolded relationally, and how they experienced the AI relationship. We linked self-reports from 94 adults to donated, personally meaningful ChatGPT conversations using 21 outcome-blind frozen topics, blinded triple 1 annotation of 500 sequences, and participant-grouped machine learning frozen before outcome access. Digital Therapeutic Alliance (DTA) and AI Attachment were related but showed different conversational correlates. Validation (R² CV = .423) and uptake (R² CV = .260) were recoverable out of sample, yet, neither formed a generalizable DTA signature. Conversely, a post-registration multivariate analysis identified a distributed Social Substitution fingerprint beyond conversation length and 21 topics (∆R² CV = .070) and improved prediction in 98% of repetitions. Ruptures were rare and consistently repaired; their association with DTA was fragile. Thus, different human-LLM relationships leave different-and sometimes no readily observable-conversational traces.

1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, have rapidly become embedded in everyday life. This development is particularly pronounced among younger users; recent representative data from Germany indicate that ChatGPT use has become widespread among adolescents [1]. Their psychological relevance, however, extends beyond ubiquitous adoption and instrumental use. Although affective engagement appears concentrated among a subset of users, naturalistic LLM interactions include emotional expression, support seeking, self-reflection, and relational cues [2]. Users seeking emotional or mental-health support from ChatGPT report using the system not only for information and psychoeducation, but also for companionship, relational guidance, decision-making, and emotional support [3]. Longitudinal research with conversational agents similarly shows that users may disclose increasingly personal experiences and develop heterogeneous forms of perceived closeness over time [4, 5].

Such responses are consistent with longstanding evidence that people apply interpersonal expectations and social rules to technological systems [6, 7]. Contemporary conversational AI intensifies these possibilities because interaction is sustained through natural language. Social presence, anthropomorphism, perceived authenticity, and repeated interaction can contribute to relational significance [8, 9]. Importantly, relational significance is not simply an inherent property of the technology: it emerges through interactions between system characteristics, users' perceptions and motivations, and the communication that unfolds between them. Human-AI relationality is also neither uniform nor necessarily positive. That is, users may experience closeness and support alongside frustration, uncertainty, disruption, or continued awareness of the system's artificial nature [4, 10]. Relational experiences with AI are therefore better understood as heterogeneous and dynamic than as a simple continuum from absence to presence of a "relationship."

Despite growing evidence that users experience conversational AI relationally, considerably less is known about whether-and how-different forms of relational experience become visible in interaction itself. Existing research has predominantly examined what users report about their relationship with AI. Yet, subjective relational experience and observable interaction are not interchangeable levels of analysis. More specifically, similar subjective evaluations may arise from different interactional processes, while different forms of relational experience may leave different conversational traces. The central question is therefore not simply whether people can experience a relationship with AI, but when different relational experiences become visible in conversation and what linguistic or dyadic processes make them observable. In the present study, we address this question using a novel/innovative approach naturalistic, personally meaningful conversations with ChatGPT.

1.1 Digital Therapeutic Alliance and AI Attachment

One framework for characterizing supportive human-AI interaction is the Digital Therapeutic Alliance (DTA). DTA builds on the traditional working-alliance framework, in which therapeutic collaboration is organized around agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and an interpersonal bond [11, 12]. In human psychotherapy, alliance is robustly associated with positive treatment outcome across therapeutic approaches and populations [13] [14] [15]. Digital interventions raise the question of whether analogous collaborative experiences can emerge when support is mediated partly or entirely by technology.

Evidence suggests that users can indeed report alliance-like experiences with digital interventions and conversational agents [16] [17] [18]. However, directly transferring a construct developed for therapist-client relationships to interactions with artificial systems is conceptually problematic. One key issue is that goal and task agreement may translate relatively readily to human-AI interactions, whereas bond, empathy, reciprocity, trust, and personalization may take different forms when no human therapist is present [19, 20]. Further, qualitative and integrative work suggests that digitally mediated alliance may involve accessibility, autonomy, perceived non-judgment, personalization, system competence, engagement, and the experience of being recognized, understood, and supported [21] [22] [23]. Accordingly, we use DTA to refer to the userperceived quality of therapeutically oriented collaboration with ChatGPT, without assuming that human-AI interaction is ontologically equivalent to a therapist-client relationship.

A related but distinct construct is AI Attachment. Whereas DTA concerns the perceived quality of therapeutically oriented collaboration, AI Attachment concerns the broader psychological and relational significance that AI acquires for the user. Attachment-related experiences with artificial agents can include proximity seeking, safe-haven and secure-base functions, and anticipated separation distress [24, 25]. Recent scale development has operationalized AI Attachment as a multidimensional construct comprising Emotional Closeness, Social Substitution, and Normative Regard [26]. Emotional Closeness captures familiarity, comfort, personal significance, and emotional connection with AI. Social Substitution concerns the use of AI as a substitute or supplement for social interaction and support, whereas Normative Regard captures the extension of interpersonal norms of consideration and respect toward AI [26]. The German adaptation used in the present study retains corresponding dimensions of Emotional Closeness, Social Substitution, and Respectful Treatment of AI [27].

The distinction among these dimensions is psychologically relevant. In the original scale-development work, Social Substitution was particularly related to socioemotional motives for AI use and showed associations with social vulnerabilities including loneliness and social anxiety [26]. Attachment-like experiences are also not restricted to dedicated companion systems. They have been documented in interactions with broadly used conversational AI, whose users may move between instrumental, supportive, and relational modes of interaction [28]. At the same time, attachment to AI should not be equated with beneficial interaction. Relational significance may coexist with dependence, loneliness, guilt, or distress [29]. Thus, the extent to which an AI becomes emotionally or socially significant is conceptually distinct from whether interaction with that AI is experienced as therapeutically collaborative.

DTA and AI Attachment can therefore be expected to overlap while capturing different forms of relational experience. This distinction parallels psychotherapy research showing that attachment orientations are associated with, but not equivalent to, working alliance [30]. In human-AI interaction, DTA emphasizes collaborative processes such as understanding, responsiveness, and constructive engagement, whereas AI Attachment captures the emotional, social, and normative significance of the AI itself. Some conversational processes-including self-disclosure, personalization, and perceived responsiveness-may nevertheless contribute to both.

This conceptual distinction raises a behavioral question that has received considerably less attention: whether different relational experiences are expressed through the same conversational processes. Put differently, a user's appraisal of therapeutically oriented collaboration need not have the same behavioral correlates as emotional closeness, the use of AI as a social counterpart, or the extension of interpersonal norms toward the system. Examining these constructs alongside observable conversation therefore offers a way to test whether related self-reports correspond to a common relational process or to distinguishable forms of human-AI engagement.

1.2 From Self-Report to Observable and Dyadic Communication

Self-report is indispensable for assessing subjective relational experience, but it does not directly reveal the interactional processes through which that experience is enacted. Language provides a complementary behavioral level of analysis. In psychotherapy, linguistic characteristics of sessions have been associated with client-rated working alliance. For example, natural language processing (NLP) analyses have identified associations between alliance ratings and features such as pronoun use, non-fluency, and syntactic patterns [31]. Machine-learning approaches further indicate that alliance-relevant information can be recovered from psychotherapy transcripts, although prediction remains imperfect [32]. More recent computational work likewise suggests that multidimensional information concerning therapeutic bond, tasks, and goals can be modeled from counseling transcripts [33, 34]. These findings are situated within a broader development in computational mental-health research. A systematic review of NLP applications to mental-health interventions found that research has predominantly focused on patients' clinical presentation, whereas relational dynamics such as therapeutic alliance, ruptures, affective processes, and linguistic coordination have received substantially less attention [35]. At the same time, written supportive interactions demonstrate that complex interpersonal processes can be identified computationally from text. Expressed empathy, for example, can be detected in online mental-health support interactions [36], and linguistic characteristics of crisis-text conversations have been associated with users' perceptions of counselors' concern [37]. Together, this literature supports conversational language as a complementary source of information about relational processes while cautioning against treating linguistic features as direct equivalents of subjective relational experience.

This distinction is central to the present study. Conversational features are treated as behavioral correlates of relational experience rather than as direct measures of DTA or AI Attachment. Self-report captures how users experience their relationship with ChatGPT; conversational analysis captures what can be observed in the interaction through which relational processes may be expressed. If relational experience becomes behaviorally visible, however, its signature may not reside in either conversational partner alone. Instead, relational information may emerge from how user and AI respond and adapt to one another across conversational turns.

Human dialogue is inherently interactive. The interactive alignment account proposes that conversational partners adapt to one another across lexical, syntactic, semantic, and situation-model levels [38]. In psychotherapy, interpersonal synchrony and linguistic coordination have likewise been linked to therapeutic relationship processes [39, 40]. Importantly, coordination is not a unitary phenomenon. Style-based and content-based matching, for example, may capture different aspects of collaboration and alliance [41, 42]. Linguistic coordination should therefore be conceptualized as a family of potentially distinct dyadic processes rather than as a single proxy for relational quality.

Several forms of coordination may be particularly informative in human-AI interaction. Language-style matching captures coordination in patterns of linguistic style, whereas lexical entrainment refers to the emergence of shared lexical choices across conversational partners [43]. Semantic alignment, in turn, captures similarity in meaning or content across adjacent contributions. Emerging evidence suggests that accommodation also occurs in human-AI dialogue. Analyses of naturalistic GPT-4o conversations indicate bidirectional linguistic accommodation, with model-side adaptation and increasing user convergence across interaction [44]. Complementary evidence suggests that different forms of linguistic alignment may relate to distinct aspects of relational engagement with conversational agents [45]. These findings motivate examining language-style matching, lexical entrainment, and semantic alignment as complementary dyadic characteristics rather than treating linguistic similarity as a unitary marker of relational quality.

Coordination alone, however, does not establish that an AI is responding appropriately to a user. A complementary process is contextual responsiveness: whether an AI response is contingent on and specifically tailored to the preceding user contribution. Related supportive behaviors include validation, such as acknowledging, legitimizing, normalizing, or affirming a user's expressed experience. Therapist responsiveness has been associated with therapeutic alliance [46]. Yet, empathic, validating, or reassuring language does not by itself establish context-sensitive responsiveness [47, 48]. General supportive language and genuine adaptation to the user's specific contribution therefore represent related but conceptually distinguishable interactional properties.

A dyadic account must additionally consider what happens after the AI responds. We use uptake to describe whether a subsequent user contribution acknowledges, incorporates, elaborates on, questions, or otherwise meaningfully engages with information introduced by the AI. Analyses of donated mental-health chatbot conversations suggest that stronger perceived relationships are characterized not simply by selfdisclosure, but by sustained disclosure, engagement with empathic responses, constructive use of suggested tools, and conversational continuity [49]. Relational information may therefore reside less in isolated words than in patterns unfolding across successive contributions. Contextual responsiveness and uptake capture complementary directions of this process: how the AI responds to the user and how the user subsequently responds to the AI.

Taken together, these perspectives motivate a multilevel account of observable relational behavior. User-level characteristics describe how users communicate with the AI; AI-level characteristics describe how the system responds; and dyadic characteristics describe coordination emerging between them. This distinction allows subjective relational experience to be examined against conversational behavior without assuming in advance that its observable correlates must reside in one particular interactional level.

1.3 Beyond Harmonious Interaction: Rupture, Repair, and Critical Engagement

Relational processes may become particularly visible when coordination fails. In psychotherapy, alliance ruptures refer to episodes of tension or breakdown in collaborative interaction, while repair concerns the subsequent restoration of collaboration [50, 51]. Rupture episodes can leave observable linguistic traces and have been identified computationally from psychotherapy transcripts [52, 53]. Conversation content may also carry information about rupture occurrence [54], further illustrating that relational disruption can become observable at multiple levels of interaction. Extending these ideas to human-AI interaction, we conceptualize misattunement as a local failure of conversational fit, rupture as a more pronounced disruption of collaborative interaction, and repair as subsequent interactional moves that restore coordination. Such disruptions may occur when an AI misunderstands a user's intention, fails to address an expressed need, responds inappropriately to emotional context, or otherwise breaks the emerging coordination of an exchange. A user may then correct, reject, disengage from, or request clarification of the AI response; the system may in turn acknowledge the mismatch and adapt its subsequent response. Extending rupture concepts from psychotherapy to human-AI dialogue is a theoretical hypothesis rather than an assumption that therapist-client processes transfer unchanged to artificial systems.

Crucially, disagreement should not automatically be interpreted as rupture. A user may question, correct, qualify, or reject an AI response while remaining actively engaged with it. Research on appropriate reliance shows that source verification and recognition of inconsistencies can reduce reliance on incorrect LLM responses [55]. Research on critical thinking in generative-AI use similarly emphasizes verification, reflection, and understanding the limitations of AI systems and links these processes to more extensive verification behavior and greater accuracy in evaluating AI-generated information [56]. We refer to such behavior as critical engagement: questioning, verifying, qualifying, correcting, or resisting an AI response while remaining substantively engaged with the conversation.

The converse is equally important: agreement does not necessarily indicate high-quality relational functioning. LLMs may produce sycophantic responses that excessively affirm users' views. Experimental evidence indicates that sycophantic AI responses can increase trust and system preference while reducing responsibility taking and willingness to repair interpersonal conflict [57]. High agreement, trust, or affirmation therefore cannot by themselves establish adaptive relational quality. A behavioral account of human-AI relational processes must distinguish constructive disagreement from relational disruption, just as it must distinguish responsive support from uncritical affirmation.

1.4 Conversation Content as Context

Relational behavior does not occur independently of what users and AI systems are discussing. Naturalistic interactions with general-purpose LLMs span informational, practical, personal, and emotional concerns. Large-scale analyses of real-world LLM use illustrate this thematic heterogeneity, including in conversations concerning health and psychological concerns [58]. Conversations about emotionally salient or personally sensitive topics may, for example, provide different conditions for self-disclosure, validation, responsiveness, or linguistic coordination than predominantly informational exchanges.

Evidence from psychotherapy similarly suggests that conversational content and relational quality constitute related but non-equivalent levels of interaction. Topicmodeling analyses of psychotherapy transcripts have identified associations between conversational topics and therapeutic alliance, while topic information alone provides limited information about alliance [59, 60]. Thus, what interaction partners discuss may shape the conditions under which relational processes emerge without itself constituting relational quality.

This distinction is important when linking observable conversational behavior to subjective relational experience. An apparent association between a linguistic or dyadic characteristic and DTA or AI Attachment could partly reflect differences in conversational content. Users who donate conversations concerning highly personal or emotional topics may, for example, also disclose more, receive more supportive language, or engage differently with the system. We therefore treat conversation topics as contextual features rather than direct indicators of relational quality. Examining thematic structure alongside relational behavior allows us to ask whether linguistic and dyadic characteristics provide information beyond differences in what users and AI systems happen to discuss.

1.5 The Present Study

The present study examines when different relational experiences with AI become visible in naturalistic conversation. We link self-reported Digital Therapeutic Alliance and AI Attachment to self-selected ChatGPT conversations that participants considered personally meaningful. Rather than eliciting standardized interactions under experimental conditions, we analyze conversations that had already occurred during participants' ordinary use of ChatGPT. This provides an opportunity to study relational processes as they unfold in naturally occurring human-LLM interaction.

We consider multiple forms of subjective relational experience. DTA captures users' perceptions of therapeutically oriented collaboration with ChatGPT. AI Attachment captures the broader relational significance attributed to AI and is examined both overall and through the dimensions of Emotional Closeness, Social Substitution, and Respectful Treatment of AI. These constructs range from perceived therapeutic collaboration to emotional connection, the use of AI as a social counterpart, and the extension of interpersonal norms toward the system. Examining them jointly allows us to ask whether different forms of relational experience are accompanied by common or distinguishable conversational patterns.

To move beyond self-report, we examine observable interaction at three complementary levels. User-level characteristics capture linguistic and engagement-related properties of participants' contributions. AI-level characteristics capture properties of ChatGPT responses, including validation and contextual responsiveness. Dyadic characteristics capture processes emerging across adjacent conversational contributions, including language-style matching, lexical entrainment, semantic alignment, and user uptake. We additionally examine misattunement, rupture, repair, and critical engagement as interactional processes that may become visible when coordination is challenged or disrupted.

Conversation content provides a separate contextual level. We therefore characterize what participants discuss using computational topic modeling supplemented by independent human topic annotation. Selected relational processes are additionally assessed through blinded multi-rater human annotation, providing an independent reference for evaluating the reliability and computational operationalization of theoretically proposed conversational constructs.

This multimethod framework distinguishes three questions that are often conflated in research on human-AI relationships: what people talk about with AI, what relationally occurs within the interaction, and how users subjectively experience their relationship with the system. Integrating these levels allows us to test whether different forms of relational experience share common observable correlates or are differentially reflected in naturalistic human-LLM interaction, and whether such associations extend beyond basic conversation characteristics and thematic content.

Consistent with the preregistered analysis plan, we address four research questions:

RQ1.

What thematic structures characterize personally meaningful human-ChatGPT conversations?

RQ2.

To what extent are Digital Therapeutic Alliance (DTA) and overall AI Attachment, as well as the AI Attachment dimensions Emotional Closeness, Social Substitution, and Respectful Treatment of AI, associated? RQ3. Which user-level, AI-level, and dyadic conversational characteristics are associated with DTA and AI Attachment, and do these associations persist beyond basic conversation characteristics and conversational content? RQ4. How are misattunement, rupture, repair, and critical engagement expressed in human-AI interaction, and do relational disruption and critical engagement show distinguishable associations with DTA and AI Attachment?

Based on the theoretical framework outlined above, we specified three directional hypotheses. First, DTA was expected to be positively associated with overall AI Attachment (H1). Their conceptual non-equivalence was additionally examined through differential associations with conversational features. Second, higher DTA was expected to be associated with stronger contextual responsiveness and greater user uptake (H2). Associations between DTA and specific forms of linguistic coordination, including language-style matching, lexical entrainment, and semantic alignment, were examined exploratorily. Third, greater relational disruption (misattunement/rupture) was expected to be associated with lower DTA (H3). Repair processes and associations of critical engagement with DTA and AI Attachment were examined exploratorily.

Associations of conversational markers with overall AI Attachment and its dimensions were additionally examined to investigate whether DTA and AI Attachment show differential behavioral correlates. These analyses are particularly relevant to the broader question motivating the study: whether forms of relational experience that overlap at the level of self-report are also expressed similarly in observable interaction.

Together, this approach tests when different relational experiences with AI become visible in naturalistic conversation, whether their observable correlates reside in user, AI, or dyadic behavior, and whether these associations extend beyond basic conversation characteristics and what people discuss with the system. In doing so, we distinguish the subjective experience of relating to AI from the relational processes that can be observed in interaction itself.

2 Methods

2.1 Participants and Procedure

2.1.1 Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited in Germany through advertisements distributed at universities and via social media. Eligibility criteria were (a) minimum of 18 years, (b) current use of ChatGPT, (c) self-reported German language proficiency at a native-speaker level, and (d) a minimum of one previous personally meaningful interaction with Chat-GPT. Personally meaningful interactions were described as conversations concerning personal, emotional, or psychologically relevant topics, such as difficult situations, self-reflection, loneliness, interpersonal conflict, stress, or important decisions.

The analytical sample comprised 94 participants with complete Digital Therapeutic Alliance (DTA) and AI Attachment data and one corresponding analyzable donated conversation. Participants had a mean age of 24.87 years (SD = 5.17, range=18-49); 73 (77.7%) identified as female, 19 (20.2%) as male, one (1.1%) as diverse, and one (1.1%) preferred not to disclose gender. Most participants were students (n = 78, 83.0%). Seventy-six participants (80.9%) had used AI chatbots for at least one year, and 83 (88.3%) used them at least several times per week. Further demographic and ChatGPT-use characteristics are reported in Table 1.

2.1.2 Procedure

After providing informed consent and completing eligibility screening, participants selected one previously existing ChatGPT conversation that they considered personally meaningful. Thus, conversations arose during ordinary ChatGPT use rather than being elicited experimentally. Participants donated the selected conversation through a conversation-specific ChatGPT share link and were informed that the link provided access only to the conversation they chose to share. They also reported the ChatGPT model used, whether default settings or personal instructions had been active, why the conversation was meaningful, and its perceived personalness on a five-point scale from 1 (not personal ) to 5 (extremely personal ). The survey additionally assessed demographic characteristics, ChatGPT-use patterns and motives, DTA, and AI Attachment. Questionnaire responses were subsequently linked to the corresponding donated conversation.

2.1.3 Ethics and data protection

Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained before participation. No direct identifiers were requested in the survey. Before data analyses was conducted, a trained research assistant manually screened each donated conversation for anonymity and removed or replaced any names, locations, contact information, and other potentially identifying details with generic placeholders. A subsequent rule-based quality-control screen flagged residual email addresses, telephone-number-like strings, URLs, postal codes, and related patterns for manual review. All conversational analyses used the resulting de-identified corpus. The study is in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethical board of the LMU medical department.

2.2 Registration and Analytical Transparency

The study and primary conversational analysis plan were retrospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) on August 28, 2026, after completion of data collection and initial data preparation, quality-control procedures, questionnaire scoring, descriptive and psychometric analyses, and initial bivariate analyses of the self-report measures, but before the primary conversational analyses (https://osf.io/54frt/overview?view only=6686da56daa24af8b9b17694132d1f25).

At the time of registration, no topic-modeling analyses, automated linguistic or dyadic analyses, or multivariable analyses linking conversational features to DTA or AI Attachment had been conducted. In particular, contextual responsiveness, user uptake, linguistic coordination, semantic alignment, lexical entrainment, misattunement/rupture, repair, and critical engagement had not been analyzed in relation to the study outcomes. The registered plan prespecified the research questions, directional hypotheses, core conversational constructs, primary covariates, statistical models, and planned sensitivity analyses. Human raters were blinded to participants' DTA and AI Attachment scores throughout annotation. Analyses or methodological extensions introduced after registration are explicitly identified as post-registration exploratory analyses or sensitivity analyses below.

2.3 Measures

2.3.1 Digital Therapeutic Alliance

DTA was assessed using the six-item Conventional Therapeutic Alliance subscale of the eHealth Therapeutic Alliance Inventory (ETAI-CTA) [61, 62]. The ETAI-CTA is a digital adaptation of the Working Alliance Inventory [12] and retains its conceptual foundation in goals, tasks, and bond. Items assess perceived understanding and support of personally relevant goals, alignment of content and tasks with those goals, helpfulness of information or advice, and perceived effort to provide support. Item wording referred specifically to the participant's interaction with the chatbot.

Items were administered on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), rather than the original seven-point ETAI response format, to harmonize the relational measures. Psychometric equivalence with the original format was therefore not assumed, and internal consistency was evaluated in the present sample. The six items were averaged, with higher scores indicating stronger perceived DTA.

2.3.2 AI Attachment

AI Attachment was assessed with the German version of the 15-item AI Attachment Scale [26, 27].

AI Attachment

The scale comprises Emotional Closeness, Social Substitution, and Respectful Treatment of AI (termed Normative Regard in the original scale). Emotional Closeness captures familiarity, emotional connection, and personal significance; Social Substitution captures turning to AI for conversation, disclosure, or emotional support as an alternative or complement to human interaction; and Respectful Treatment captures the extension of interpersonal norms such as politeness and consideration to AI.

Participants rated all items on five-point Likert scales. Each dimension was calculated as the mean of its five items (Emotional Closeness: Items 1, 5, 7, 10, and 12; Social Substitution: Items 2, 4, 8, 11, and 14; Respectful Treatment: Items 3, 6, 9, 13, and 15). Overall AI Attachment was the mean of all 15 items. Higher scores indicated stronger attachment, and internal consistency was evaluated for the total score and each dimension.

2.4 Conversation Data and Preprocessing

The final de-identified corpus comprised 94 conversations and 1,516 individual messages: 758 user messages and 758 assistant messages. Exact duplicated rows identified during quality control were removed. Each message retained its conversational role, order, and de-identified text. Basic characteristics included total messages and words, user and assistant messages and words, mean words per user and assistant message, and their relative contributions.

Adjacent user messages and assistant responses formed 758 user-assistant exchanges. Analyses requiring a subsequent user contribution used three-turn sequences of the form User t -Assistant t -User t+1 . The corpus contained 664 eligible sequences from 85 conversations; nine conversations had no complete three-turn sequence and therefore did not contribute to these analyses. No conversation was excluded solely because of length. Because length was strongly positively skewed, log(1 + total words) was the primary length covariate and log(1 + total turns) was used in sensitivity analyses.

2.5 Conversation Content and Topic Analysis

2.5.1 Human and independent LLM topic references

All 94 complete conversations were independently annotated by three human raters using a predefined topic codebook. Raters were blinded to DTA and AI Attachment and assigned a free-text primary topic, an optional secondary topic, one of ten broad domains, indicators of multiple substantial topics, and uncertainty. Broad-domain reliability was assessed using nominal Krippendorff's α, exact three-rater and pairwise agreement, and rater-specific distributions. Two-of-three majority classifications were used where available; cases without a majority were adjudicated against the codebook while preserving all original ratings and reliability estimates. Open-ended topic labels were harmonized separately for semantic comparison. As a post-registration exploratory benchmark, GPT-5.6 Sol independently annotated the same conversations using the same framework without access to human ratings, participant characteristics, or outcomes. Complete annotation, adjudication, LLM-prompt, agreement, and semantic-comparison procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods S12.

2.5.2 Computational thematic structure (RQ1)

The thematic structure was estimated using a data-driven semantic pipeline developed without access to human topic annotations, independent LLM annotations, DTA, or AI Attachment. Because complete conversations could contain multiple themes, the primary unit was a semantically coherent segment constructed from adjacent user-assistant exchanges. Segmentation specifications were compared using internal fragmentation and segment-length criteria; the selected specification yielded 150 frozen semantic segments across 94 conversations. Segment embeddings were then reduced with UMAP and clustered with HDBSCAN. Model selection considered cluster number and size, outlier burden, within-cluster semantic coherence, silhouette width, and stability across fixed initializations. The selected solution was frozen before external comparison and comprised 21 clusters, with 15 segments classified as outliers.

Clusters were characterized from class-based term representations and representative segments and assigned concise descriptive labels and qualitative coherence ratings without reference to external annotations or outcomes. After the solution was frozen, convergence with independent human and LLM segment annotations was evaluated using categorical and semantic metrics. User-only and unsegmented wholeconversation analyses assessed dependence on assistant language and segmentation. Thresholds, embedding and chunking procedures, UMAP/HDBSCAN specifications, seed-stability diagnostics, cluster representations, full validation methods, and sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplementary Methods and Results S12-S13 and Supplementary Tables S12-S23.

2.6 Associations Between DTA and AI Attachment (RQ2)

H1 predicted a positive association between DTA and overall AI Attachment. The preregistered primary analysis used Pearson's correlation with a two-sided 95% confidence interval. Pearson correlations between DTA and Emotional Closeness, Social Substitution, and Respectful Treatment were preregistered secondary analyses; corresponding Spearman correlations were preregistered robustness analyses. Tests were two-sided with α = .05, and Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) adjustment was applied across the three dimensional tests. A post-registration exploratory simultaneous regression examined the dimensions' unique associations with DTA after shared variance was controlled. This model was not used to evaluate the preregistered H1. Further details are reported in Supplementary Section S14 and Tables S24-S25.

2.7 Human Annotation of Relational Processes

A predefined validation sample of 150 three-turn sequences was independently coded by three human raters who were blinded to DTA and AI Attachment. User t , Assistant t , and User t+1 constituted the annotation unit; Assistant t+1 was available only when needed to evaluate repair. A fixed codebook defined validation (0/1), contextual responsiveness (0-2), user uptake (0-2), misattunement/rupture (0-2), critical engagement (0/1), repair (NA/0-2), and uncertainty (0/1). Validation referred to acknowledgement, legitimization, normalization, or affirmation of the user's experience; contextual responsiveness to contingency on the preceding user contribution; and uptake to the subsequent user's meaningful engagement with the assistant response. Rupture denoted disrupted conversational or relational fit, repair denoted restored coordination following disruption, and critical engagement denoted substantive questioning, qualification, correction, or resistance while remaining engaged.

Consistent with the preregistered analysis plan, reliability was evaluated before consensus formation using construct-appropriate Krippendorff's α, exact agreement, prevalence, and rater-specific distributions. Binary consensus labels used two-of-three majority and ordinal labels used the median. Original ratings and reliability estimates were preserved when ambiguous or boundary cases were adjudicated against the codebook; repair was applicable only following identified disruption.

Post-registration, 350 additional eligible sequences from 79 participants were selected without outcome access using participant-balanced sampling, with no more than 12 new sequences per participant. The same three-rater, blinded protocol produced an extended corpus of 500 sequences. The original 150-sequence sample retained its role as the preregistered reliability and validation sample; the additional 350 annotations constituted a post-registration extension for computational-marker development. Strong ordinal disagreements and rare-event boundary cases underwent targeted, outcome-blind adjudication, while original ratings were retained for reliability analyses and soft-target modeling. Sampling, codebook, reliability, consensus, and adjudication procedures are reported in Supplementary Sections S1-S11 and S15.

2.8 Independent LLM Benchmark of Relational Annotation

As a post-registration exploratory benchmark, GPT-5.6 Sol independently annotated the same 500 sequences using the fixed human codebook and structured response categories. The model had no access to human ratings or adjudication, participant characteristics, outcomes, or subsequent NLP predictions. Its ratings were not used to calculate human reliability, construct supervision targets, or train the humansupervised computational models. Full prompts, model settings, agreement metrics, and results are reported in Supplementary Sections S5, S10, S15.3, and S15.4.

2.9 Human-Supervised Computational Relational Markers

Human annotations provided theoretically anchored but potentially noisy supervision for scalable relational markers. Target definitions were fixed construct by construct before outcome linkage: validation used the proportion of the three raters coding validation; uptake used their mean ordinal rating, with median/adjudicated labels reserved for sensitivity analyses; and contextual responsiveness was treated as measurement-constrained because of its ceiling distribution. Rupture and critical engagement were evaluated as exploratory rare-event targets, whereas repair remained conditional and was not modeled unconditionally.

Theory-driven, semantic-embedding, and combined representations were compared against a mean-only baseline using elastic-net regression. Theory-driven features captured contribution length, reference and reuse of partner content, validation phrases where applicable, question asking, lexical overlap, language-style matching, semantic alignment, affective characteristics, and matching in the theoretically relevant turn direction. Semantic representations encoded the User t -Assistant t relation for validation and responsiveness and the Assistant t -User t+1 relation for uptake.

Model development used nested participant-grouped cross-validation, ensuring that sequences from one participant could not enter both training and held-out folds. Ten outer folds evaluated generalization; model tuning occurred within each outer training set using five participant-grouped inner folds. Performance was calculated from out-of-fold predictions using RMSE, MAE, R² , and Pearson's r. Model-family selection occurred without outcome access. The combined representation was retained for validation and theory-driven representations for uptake and contextual responsiveness. Rare-event models used training-fold-specific class weighting and emphasized precision-recall area under the curve; neither rupture nor critical engagement achieved sufficient performance for promotion to a scalable participant-level NLP marker. Exact feature definitions, embedding construction, tuning grids, performance comparisons, and rare-event evaluation appear in Supplementary Sections S16-S18 and Tables S26-S28.

2.9.1 Pre-outcome freeze and full-corpus application

Feature definitions, target choices, model-family selection, tuning procedures, and aggregation rules were frozen before any computational marker was linked to DTA or AI Attachment. The frozen pipeline was then applied to all 664 eligible sequences from 85 participants. The 500 development sequences retained participant-grouped out-offold predictions; frozen final models trained on those sequences were applied without further tuning or feature selection to 164 previously unseen sequences. Sequence predictions were averaged within participant. No conversational marker was subsequently redefined or reoptimized on the basis of an outcome association. Further details are reported in Supplementary Section S19.

2.10 Additional Conversational Features

Additional directly computed markers were specified independently of outcome associations. User-level features captured self-reference, verbosity, and affective valence. AIlevel features comprised the human-supervised validation and responsiveness markers together with assistant verbosity, reference to preceding user content, validation-phrase use, question asking, and affective valence. Dyadic features included human-supervised uptake, lexical overlap, directional content reuse, language-style matching, semantic alignment, and affective matching across adjacent contributions. Sequence-level features were averaged within participant. Complete operational definitions are reported in Supplementary Sections S16-S17 and Table S27.

No features were constructed or selected using outcome associations. Complete specifications and results are reported in Supplementary Section S20.2 and Table S33.

2.11.3 Topic-adjusted and sequence-level sensitivity analyses

To test whether conversational fingerprints reflected fine-grained thematic differences, the independently frozen 21-topic solution was converted to conversation-level proportions without refitting. Nested ridge analyses for DTA and Social Substitution were repeated with length and all 21 topic proportions as the baseline. Selected markers were additionally analyzed across all 664 sequences using participant-clustered heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, thereby avoiding treatment of sequences as independent participants. These analyses were complementary sensitivities (Supplementary Section S20.3 and Tables S34-S35).

2.12 Relational Disruption, Repair, and Critical Engagement (RQ4)

Because rupture and critical engagement were rare and their computational classifiers were not promoted to scalable markers, final RQ4 analyses used the humancoded/adjudicated 500-sequence corpus. Participant-level rupture exposure was represented by the proportion of coded sequences containing rupture and, complementarily, by mean rupture severity. Critical engagement was summarized as the proportion of coded sequences classified positive. Models retained the number of annotated sequences per participant to account for unequal annotation coverage. H3 predicted lower DTA with greater rupture exposure. The primary participantlevel regression adjusted for log(1 + total words), parsimonious human Broad Domain, and number of coded sequences. Mean rupture severity was a complementary operationalization; associations with AI Attachment and Social Substitution were secondary or exploratory. Sequence-level rupture models used participant-clustered robust standard errors. Repair was characterized descriptively because it was conditional on the small number of rupture events.

Rare-event robustness analyses comprised rank correlations, a binary ever-rupture indicator, leave-one-rupture-participant-out analyses, participant bootstrap resampling, and participant-level permutation testing. These were interpreted as robustness assessments of H3 rather than alternative primary hypothesis tests. Full RQ4 specifications are reported in Supplementary Section S21 and Tables S36-S42.

2.13 Statistical Software and Reproducibility

Analyses were conducted in R. Penalized models used glmnet, and participantclustered robust inference used sandwich and lmtest. Relational-marker embeddings were generated locally with intfloat/multilingual-e5-large; the RQ1 pipeline used text-embedding-3-large, UMAP, and HDBSCAN. Participant identifiers defined all grouped cross-validation folds. 3 Results

3.1 Sample and Conversation Characteristics

The final corpus comprised 94 donated ChatGPT conversations containing 1,516 individual messages, equally divided between user (n = 758) and assistant messages (n = 758). Conversations contained a median of 10 individual messages, corresponding to five user-assistant exchanges, although conversation length varied substantially (M = 16.13, SD = 33.14, range = 2-276 messages).

The donated conversations contained a median of 1,339 words (M = 3241.83, SD = 8040.46). Assistant responses accounted for the majority of conversational text: participants contributed a median of 100 words per conversation, compared with 1,223 words generated by the assistant. Conversation-length measures showed pronounced positive skew, reflecting a small number of substantially longer conversations.

3.2 RQ1: Thematic Structure of Personally Meaningful Conversations

The frozen data-driven solution identified 21 fine-grained thematic clusters across the semantically segmented conversations (Figure 2). The thematic landscape was broad and extended substantially beyond explicitly psychological content. The most widely represented coherent topics concerned romantic relationships, separation, and relationship uncertainty (12 conversations), study and examination stress (10 conversations), acute anxiety, panic, and emotion regulation (7 conversations), and health symptoms and medical interpretation (7 conversations). Additional recurring themes included loneliness and relational loss, psychological symptoms and diagnostic interpretation, career and life planning, exercise and training, religion and spirituality, family and parenthood, practical support, celebrations, and interpersonal or social self-reflection. Several themes illustrated how psychologically salient processes were embedded within everyday concerns. Academic conversations frequently centered on stress and coping, relationship conversations involved uncertainty, separation, loneliness, or a need for connection, and future-oriented decisions were embedded within concerns about work, family, or personal well-being. Thus, psychologically meaningful content was not confined to conversations explicitly framed as mental-health discussions.

Segment-level human and LLM validation.

Independent human annotation of the same 150 frozen semantic segments showed moderate agreement for Broad Domain (Krippendorff's α = .50). Among the 147 segments with complete ratings from all three raters, exact three-rater agreement was 41.5%. A two-of-three majority classification was available for 132 of the 150 segments (88.0%); the remaining 18 segments were adjudicated without access to the computational or LLM topic assignments.

The independently generated LLM Broad Domain classifications showed substantial correspondence with the final human segment-level reference, with an accuracy of 70.7%, Cohen's κ = .65, macro-F1=.70, and weighted F1=.69.

The frozen data-driven cluster structure also converged with the independent segment-level references. Among the 135 non-outlier segments, correspondence with the adjudicated human Broad Domains was NMI=.509, AMI=.347, V-measure=.509, and purity=.644. Correspondence with the independent LLM Broad Domains was NMI=.463, AMI=.297, V-measure=.463, and purity=.659. The stronger adjusted mutual information observed in the unit-matched human comparison relative to the complementary conversation-level cross-granularity comparison suggests that part of the lower cross-level correspondence reflected differences in the units and granularity of thematic representation. Full segment-level and complementary whole-conversation validation results are reported in the Supplementary Information.

Fine-grained Primary Topic correspondence.

At the level of free-text Primary Topic descriptions, independent LLM and human annotations showed clear semantic correspondence. Mean cosine similarity between the independent LLM Primary Topic and the segment-specific centroid of the available human Primary Topic representations was .631 (SD = .115; median=.652), compared with a mean similarity of .360 for unmatched segment pairs. The matched correspondence was greater than expected under random pairing (p = .0001, 10,000 permutations).

The segment-specific human topic was the closest-matching human Primary Topic for the corresponding LLM annotation in 61.3% of all 150 segments and appeared among the five closest human topics in 88.0% of segments; mean reciprocal rank was .727. These findings indicate substantial segment-specific semantic correspondence between the independently generated human and LLM topic descriptions.

As expected given their different levels of specificity, correspondence between the general frozen computational cluster labels and segment-specific Primary Topic descriptions was more modest. Across the 135 non-outlier segments, mean cosine similarity was .306 (SD = .104; median=.312) between the computational cluster label and the human Primary Topic centroid and .277 (SD = .113; median=.285) between the computational cluster label and the independent LLM Primary Topic. These crossgranularity comparisons were therefore treated as complementary to the direct Broad Domain convergence analyses rather than as one-to-one classification tests.

Sensitivity analyses.

The thematic structure remained evident when the analysis was restricted to user language. At minPts=3, the user-only whole-conversation sensitivity analysis identified 12 clusters with 16.0% outliers and showed comparable or stronger correspondence with the human reference (NMI=.518; AMI=.343; purity=.608) and the independent LLM reference (NMI=.531; AMI=.365; purity=.608).

In contrast, representing each complete User+Assistant conversation using a single embedding produced a coarser 10-cluster solution with 16.0% outliers and weaker correspondence with the human reference (NMI=.371; AMI=.191; purity=.519). Correspondence with the independent LLM reference was also lower than in the user-only solution (NMI=.482; AMI=.329; purity=.595).

Together, the primary and sensitivity analyses indicate that personally meaningful human-ChatGPT conversations contain a broad, fine-grained thematic organization that is already evident in user language and is partly obscured when long, potentially multi-thematic conversations are compressed into a single representation. The convergence of the frozen data-driven solution with both independent human and LLM annotations further supports the interpretability of the recovered thematic structure while also indicating that the data-driven clusters capture finer-grained distinctions than the prespecified Broad Domains.

3.3 RQ2: Associations Between DTA and AI Attachment

The six-item ETAI-CTA showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = .84). Internal consistency was also satisfactory for overall AI Attachment (α = .89) and its three dimensions: Emotional Closeness (α = .77), Social Substitution (α = .90), and Respectful Treatment of AI (α = .89).

Consistent with H1, DTA was moderately positively associated with overall AI Attachment, r(92) = .41, 95% CI [.23, .57], p < .001 (Table 3; Figure 3). At the dimensional level, the strongest association with DTA was observed for Emotional Closeness, r(92) = .47, 95% CI [.29, .61], p < .001. Smaller positive associations were observed for Social Substitution, r(92) = .27, 95% CI [.07, .45], p = .009, and Respectful Treatment of AI, r(92) = .26, 95% CI [.06, .44], p = .013. All three dimensional associations remained statistically significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (p FDR < .001, .013, and .013, respectively; Table 3).

The registered Spearman robustness analyses yielded a comparable pattern (Table 3; Supplementary Table S24). DTA was positively associated with overall AI Attachment (ρ = .37, p < .001), Emotional Closeness (ρ = .44, p < .001), Social Substitution (ρ = .26, p = .011), and Respectful Treatment of AI (ρ = .22, p = .034).

The broader correlational structure is shown in Figure 3. The three AI Attachment dimensions were themselves positively interrelated, with particularly strong correlations between Emotional Closeness and Social Substitution and between each of these dimensions and the overall AI Attachment score.

In a post-registration exploratory analysis, the three AI Attachment dimensions were entered simultaneously into a linear regression model predicting DTA. The model explained 23.8% of the variance in DTA, R² = .238, adjusted R² = .212, F (3, 90) = 9.36, p < .001. Emotional Closeness showed a unique positive association with DTA (B = .47, SE = .12, β = .51, 95% CI [.23, .71], p < .001), whereas Social Substitution (β = -.12, p = .349) and Respectful Treatment of AI (β = .13, p = .199) did not show unique associations after accounting for the shared variance among the three dimensions (Supplementary Table S25).

Taken together, these findings support H1 and indicate that DTA and AI Attachment are positively related but non-equivalent constructs. Their overlap was most pronounced for the Emotional Closeness dimension of AI Attachment.

3.4 RQ3: Observable Relational Processes and Conversational Fingerprints

3.4.1 Human Measurement and Independent LLM Benchmark

Following the registered 150-sequence reliability assessment, the human annotation corpus was extended post-registration by 350 sequences, yielding 500 sequences with three independent human ratings each. Across the full corpus, Krippendorff's α was .238 for validation, .002 for contextual responsiveness, .252 for user uptake, .175 for misattunement/rupture, and .176 for critical engagement. Exact three-rater agreement was 45.6%, 83.0%, 31.6%, 92.6%, and 92.8%, respectively. The combination of high raw agreement and low chance-corrected reliability for several constructs reflected pronounced category imbalance. Human ratings were therefore treated as theoretically anchored but noisy supervision rather than an error-free behavioral gold standard. The independent post-registration LLM benchmark across the same 500 sequences also showed construct-dependent convergence. Exact Human-LLM agreement was 60.2% for validation (κ = .287), 96.2% for contextual responsiveness (weighted κ = .060), 64.4% for user uptake (weighted κ = .358), 88.8% for rupture (weighted κ = .424), and 85.8% for critical engagement (κ = .231). The LLM applied more permissive thresholds than the human reference, particularly for validation and rare-event constructs. Full rater and Human-LLM results are reported in the Supplementary Information.

3.4.2 Human-Supervised Computational Recoverability

Despite noisy human supervision, validation and user uptake could be recovered from conversational language out of sample (Table 4). The selected combined theory-plusembedding model predicted validation with R² CV = .423, RMSE=.260, MAE=.209, and r = .655. The selected theory-driven user-uptake model achieved R² CV = .260, RMSE=.463, MAE=.364, and r = .510. Contextual responsiveness showed substantially weaker recoverability (R² CV = .075, RMSE=.153, MAE=.104, r = .276), consistent with the severe ceiling constraint of the human target. The theory-driven coefficient profiles suggested that the three constructs were associated with different linguistic and dyadic patterns (Figure 4). These coefficients are descriptive predictive contributions rather than causal effects.

3.4.3 Preregistered H2: Direct Relational Markers and DTA

H2 was not supported (Table 5). That is, user uptake was not associated with DTA, r = -.037, 95% CI [-.248, .178], p = .737, and contextual responsiveness likewise showed no significant Pearson association, r = -.116, 95% CI [-.321, .100], p = .291. FDR correction across the two prespecified H2 tests did not alter this conclusion (p FDR = .737 and .582, respectively).

The corresponding length-adjusted models were also null (uptake: B = -.037, SE = .095, p = .693; responsiveness: B = -.113, SE = .096, p = .244). Validation similarly showed no length-adjusted association with DTA (B = -.059, SE = .128, p = .647). The registered Spearman sensitivity for responsiveness was negative and nominally significant (ρ = -.223, p = .040), but the direction was opposite to H2 and was not supported by the registered Pearson or adjusted analyses. Sensitivities using log(1+turns) and influence diagnostics likewise provided no evidence for the predicted positive H2 associations.

3.4.4 Different Relational Experiences Showed Different Multivariate Fingerprints

The absence of direct H2 associations did not imply that conversational behavior was unrelated to all forms of relational experience. Post-registration repeated nested cross-validation revealed a markedly different pattern across outcomes (Figure 5). For DTA, none of the frozen marker families provided reliable incremental out-of-sample value beyond the covariate-only baseline: mean ∆R² CV was -.001 for user-level, -.004 for AI-level, -.005 for dyadic, and -.009 for all-levels models. Overall AI Attachment and Emotional Closeness likewise showed no stable multivariate improvement.

In contrast, Social Substitution showed a distributed conversational fingerprint. Relative to the covariate-only baseline, mean incremental cross-validated R² was .039 for user-level markers, .043 for AI-level markers, .050 for dyadic markers, and .065 for the combined all-levels representation. The corresponding 95% repeat-distribution intervals were [.005,.070], [.011,.078], [.017,.083], and [.004,.113], respectively; the alllevels model improved on baseline in 98% of repetitions. Respectful Treatment showed smaller positive mean increments for the all-levels (∆R² = .012) and dyadic (∆R² = .011) representations, but their intervals included zero.

The descriptive marker-outcome correlation profile was consistent with this differentiation (Figure 6). Social Substitution showed positive correlations with validation (r = .29), validation-phrase rate (r = .25), assistant question rate (r = .25), languagestyle matching from Assistant t to User t+1 (r = .25), semantic alignment in the same direction (r = .24), and subsequent-user verbosity (r = .24), alongside negative correlations with user affect valence (r = -.28) and subsequent-user affect valence (r = -.22). These correlations are descriptive and were not used to select or redefine the frozen markers.

3.4.5 The Social-Substitution Fingerprint Extended Beyond Conversation Topics

The Social-Substitution pattern persisted when the broad content adjustment was replaced by the independently frozen 21-topic representation. The all-levels marker family added a mean ∆R² CV = .070 beyond conversation length and all 21 topic proportions (median=.064; 95% repeat-distribution interval [.008,.235]) and outperformed the topic-only baseline in 98% of repetitions. The dyadic, AI-level, and user-level families showed smaller positive mean increments (.034, .028, and .024, respectively), although their intervals included zero. In contrast, DTA again showed essentially no incremental value after fine-grained topic adjustment; the all-levels model yielded mean ∆R² CV < .001 in absolute magnitude (mean=-.0004; interval [-.042,.027]). Complementary sequence-level models with participant-clustered standard errors converged with this pattern. Social Substitution was positively associated with the frozen validation marker (B = .446, SE = .144, p = .002) and language-style matching from User t to Assistant t (B = .224, SE = .109, p = .040), whereas corresponding DTA associations were not significant. These analyses were sensitivity analyses and did not treat the 664 sequences as independent participants.

Taken together, RQ3 showed a dissociation between computational recoverability and subjective relational outcomes. Validation and uptake could be predicted from language, but the registered direct markers did not explain DTA. Conversely, Social Substitution-a distinct dimension of AI Attachment-showed a distributed, outof-sample conversational fingerprint that persisted beyond fine-grained conversation content.

3.5 RQ4: Relational Disruption, Repair, and Critical Engagement

Across the final 500-sequence human-coded corpus, 12 sequences were adjudicated as rupture-positive, involving nine participants; 14 sequences showed critical engagement, involving 12 participants. Thus, both processes were uncommon. Notably, all 12 rupture-positive sequences for which repair was evaluated received a repair score of 2, indicating clear repair in the subsequent interaction.

3.5.1 Registered H3: Rupture Exposure and DTA

Consistent with H3, the registered primary participant-level analysis showed that greater rupture exposure was associated with lower DTA, B = -.235, SE = .086, t = -2.72, p = .008, R² = .121. A complementary model using mean rupture severity yielded the same pattern, B = -.229, SE = .086, p = .010, R² = .118. Thus, the primary registered analysis initially supported H3. The association was not evident for overall AI Attachment (B = -.136, p = .131) or Social Substitution (B = -.087, p = .509). Critical engagement was likewise not significantly associated with overall AI Attachment (B = -.155, p = .091) or Social Substitution (B = -.137, p = .304). Sequence-level rupture models with participantclustered standard errors did not show significant associations with DTA, overall AI Attachment, or Social Substitution (Supplementary Information).

3.5.2 Rare-Event Robustness of the H3 Association

Because rupture occurred in only nine participants, the H3 association was subjected to extensive rare-event sensitivity analyses (Figure 7). The negative coefficient remained highly similar when eight of the nine rupture-positive participants were removed one at a time (Bs = -.222 to -.268; ps=.002-.012). However, removing participant T062 eliminated and reversed the association (B = .051, SE = .091, p = .579), demonstrating sensitivity to one influential rupture-positive participant.

Alternative exposure definitions provided further evidence of fragility. A binary ever-rupture indicator was not associated with DTA (B = -.067, p = .489), and rank-based associations were essentially zero (Spearman ρ = -.002, p = .988; Kendall τ = -.002, p = .983). The participant bootstrap retained a negative median estimate (B = -.226) but its 95% interval included zero, [-.496,.218], with 74.2% of successful bootstrap estimates negative. In contrast, participant-level permutation testing supported an association of the observed magnitude (two-sided p = .0093; directional negative p = .0087).

Accordingly, the registered primary H3 test showed the predicted negative association between rupture exposure and DTA, and the estimate was stable across most leave-one-rupture-participant-out analyses. However, the result was sensitive to one influential participant and was not reproduced by all alternative rare-event operationalizations. The evidence for H3 should therefore be interpreted as suggestive but fragile rather than as a robust participant-level rupture effect.

4 Discussion

This study examined whether different ways of experiencing a relationship with a large language model become visible in naturalistic conversation. By linking self-reported Digital Therapeutic Alliance (DTA) and AI Attachment to donated, personally meaningful ChatGPT conversations, we distinguished three levels that are often conflated: what people discuss with an AI, which relational features occur within the interaction, and how users experience their relationship with the system. Overall, results revealed that these levels were connected, but not interchangeable. DTA and AI Attachment were positively associated at the level of self-report, particularly through Emotional Closeness; yet, they did not share a common conversational signature. Human-perceived validation and user uptake could be recovered from language out of sample, demonstrating that relational processes were computationally observable. Contrary to H2, however, these processes were not positively associated with DTA, and the broader set of frozen conversational markers provided essentially no incremental out-of-sample information about DTA. In contrast, Social Substitution showed a distributed conversational fingerprint spanning user, AI, and dyadic behavior, which remained informative beyond conversation length and 21 independently frozen topics. Relational ruptures were rare and consistently followed by clear repair; although the preregistered primary H3 model yielded the predicted negative association with DTA, sensitivity analyses showed that this result was fragile. In sum, the central finding of this study is therefore not that relational experience can simply be read from language, but that different relational experiences with LLMs become visible in conversation to different degrees and in different forms.

The thematic findings further show that psychologically meaningful human-LLM interaction cannot be identified solely by isolating conversations explicitly framed as mental-health discussions. Psychological concerns were frequently embedded within ordinary topics: academic conversations involved anxiety, coping, and performance pressure; relationship conversations included uncertainty, loneliness, and needs for connection; and career or practical decisions became occasions for future-oriented reflection. What people discuss thus provides the context in which relational processes unfold, but does not by itself capture what relationally occurs or how the relationship is experienced.

4.1 Subjective Relational Experience Is Not Equivalent to Observable Interaction

The positive association between DTA and overall AI Attachment supports H1 and is consistent with therapeutically collaborative and attachment-like experiences sharing a broader relational component. This overlap was strongest for Emotional Closeness, which remained uniquely associated with DTA when all three attachment dimensions were considered simultaneously, whereas Social Substitution and Respectful Treatment of AI did not. Feeling therapeutically allied with ChatGPT therefore appeared to be particularly related to feeling emotionally close to the system, rather than to using it as a social substitute or extending norms of respectful treatment toward it. Yet, the bivariate associations were moderate rather than redundant, and the attachment dimensions differed in their unique and behavioral correlates. This supports the conceptual distinction developed in the Introduction: DTA concerns the perceived quality of therapeutically oriented collaboration, whereas AI Attachment concerns the broader emotional, social, and normative significance of AI [11, 19, 26]. Related self-reports should therefore not be treated as interchangeable indicators of a single human-AI relationship construct.

The conversational results sharpen this distinction. H2 predicted that greater contextual responsiveness and user uptake would accompany higher DTA. Neither association was supported, validation was likewise unrelated to DTA in the adjusted analysis, and no marker family improved out-of-sample prediction of DTA beyond baseline covariates. This absence is theoretically informative because validation and uptake were nevertheless recovered from conversational language with meaningful outof-sample accuracy. The null DTA pattern therefore cannot be reduced to a general inability of language-based methods to identify relational behavior.

Instead, the findings indicate a boundary between observer-identifiable properties of an exchange and a participant's global evaluation of the relationship. DTA may depend on expectations, prior experiences, perceived helpfulness, goal attainment, and the personal meaning assigned to an interaction, none of which must be fully expressed in a single donated conversation. This is compatible with work showing that digital alliance includes accessibility, autonomy, personalization, competence, and non-judgment alongside conventionally interpersonal features [21] [22] [23]. It also qualifies a straightforward transfer from psychotherapy research, where transcript features can carry information about working alliance [31] [32] [33]. A marker may validly represent validation or uptake without serving as a proxy for DTA. Computational measurement of an interactional process is therefore not equivalent to measurement of relationship quality; the relevant question is which subjective experience a feature predicts, for whom, and under what conversational conditions.

4.2 Social Substitution Leaves a Distributed Conversational Fingerprint

The clearest conversational differentiation concerned Social Substitution. Unlike DTA, overall AI Attachment, or Emotional Closeness, Social Substitution showed incremental out-of-sample information across user-, AI-, and dyadic-level marker families. The combined representation produced the largest improvement, suggesting that the signal was distributed across the interaction rather than concentrated in either conversational partner. Descriptively, the pattern combined more validation and assistant questioning with greater subsequent user engagement and several forms of dyadic alignment, alongside lower user affect valence. No single coefficient defines Social Substitution; it was associated with a configuration of how users communicated, how ChatGPT responded, and how the conversation continued. This pattern fits the psychological meaning of Social Substitution: turning to AI for conversation, disclosure, or support as an alternative or complement to human interaction, an orientation linked to socioemotional motives and social vulnerabilities [26]. It may therefore be more behaviorally enacted within conversation than a global judgment of therapeutic collaboration. Repeated engagement with validating responses, sustained elaboration after an AI contribution, and coordination across turns may characterize a mode in which the AI functions as a social counterpart. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that relationships with conversational agents develop through repeated self-disclosure and interactional continuity [4, 5, 49], and that chatbot questioning can elicit disclosure and shape continued interaction [63]. Social Substitution should not, however, be interpreted as inherently beneficial or harmful: relational significance can coexist with support and comfort as well as dependence, loneliness, or distress [29]. The present study identifies an observable correlate of a social use orientation; it does not establish its consequences.

Two features strengthen this result. First, marker definitions and computational models were frozen before access to relational outcomes, so the pattern was not produced by redefining features after observing their associations. Second, the all-level fingerprint remained informative after adjustment for the 21 independently frozen topic proportions and conversation length. It therefore cannot be explained simply by participants high in Social Substitution donating longer conversations or discussing different topics. Although content can covary with relational processes [54, 59, 60], the observed signal extended beyond a fine-grained representation of what was discussed.

The complementary sequence-level findings point in the same direction: validation and user-to-assistant language-style matching were positively associated with Social Substitution, whereas corresponding DTA associations were absent. Although these analyses were sensitivities and the multivariate fingerprint analysis was developed post-registration, convergence across participant-level out-of-sample prediction and sequence-level models supports the broader inference that relational constructs overlapping in self-report can have distinguishable behavioral expressions. The absence of a corresponding DTA signature also qualifies evidence from dedicated mental-health chatbots, in which stronger relationship evaluations accompanied sustained disclosure, uptake of empathic responses, constructive engagement with suggested tools, and conversational continuity [49]. Such processes may be more closely coupled to alliance in structured therapeutic systems than in a general-purpose LLM, where users move flexibly between informational, supportive, and relational modes. System purpose and interactional context may therefore be boundary conditions for the behavioral expression of DTA.

4.3 What Relational Language Models Can Measure

The human-supervised analyses contribute methodologically to research on human-LLM interaction. Validation and uptake were recoverable from naturalistic threeturn sequences, whereas contextual responsiveness was substantially harder to recover under a pronounced ceiling distribution. Computational observability is thus construct-dependent. Validation often has recognizable linguistic realizations, and uptake is defined partly by what a user does with the preceding AI contribution. Contextual responsiveness instead requires judging whether a response is specifically contingent on the user's needs and was rarely rated as absent. Because severe imbalance can yield high raw agreement alongside weak chance-corrected reliability, adjudicated annotations should not be treated as an error-free gold standard.

At the same time, the recoverability results show that noisy supervision can contain usable signal. They extend demonstrations that empathy and supportive processes can be detected in text-based mental-health interactions [36, 37] and that alliance-relevant information can be modeled from psychotherapy transcripts [35]. Our findings add a dyadic perspective: the unit of interest is not only what the user or AI says in isolation, but how one contribution responds to another and shapes the next turn. This accords with interactive alignment and linguistic coordination [38, 43] and emerging evidence of accommodation in human-AI dialogue [44, 45]. Participant-grouped out-of-sample evaluation and separation of model development from outcome testing are essential safeguards against psychologically plausible associations that do not generalize beyond the analyzed corpus.

More generally, the findings support a dynamic rather than purely attitudinal account of human-AI relationality. An AI may acquire relational significance through repeated disclosure, response, and continued engagement, while a user's global evaluation remains shaped by expectations and experiences not fully observable within one conversation [4, 5]. Relational experience and interactional behavior may influence one another over time without becoming equivalent levels of analysis.

4.4 Rupture, Repair, and Critical Engagement

Relational disruption provided a different view of the interaction. Only 12 of the 500 human-coded sequences were classified as rupture-positive, involving nine participants, and critical engagement was similarly uncommon. All 12 rupture-positive sequences were followed by clear repair. Conspicuous breakdowns were therefore unusual in the donated conversations and, when they occurred, coordination was restored locally. In human psychotherapy, rupture and repair are dynamic processes rather than simple opposites of alliance [50, 51]. Correspondingly, relational quality in human-AI interaction may depend less on the complete absence of misattunement than on whether coordination can be re-established.

This interpretation must remain cautious. The preregistered primary H3 analysis found the predicted association between greater rupture exposure and lower DTA, and the estimate was similar when eight of the nine rupture-positive participants were removed individually. Yet, exclusion of one influential participant eliminated the association, and binary, rank-based, bootstrap, and sequence-level analyses did not converge uniformly. The evidence is therefore consistent with H3 but too fragile to establish a stable participant-level rupture effect. Moreover, all ruptures being repaired does not show that repair caused a favorable experience: there was no unrepaired comparison group, and the three-turn structure necessarily foregrounded interactions that continued after an AI response.

Distinguishing rupture from critical engagement remains important even without robust outcome associations. Questioning, correcting, or resisting an AI can reflect appropriate scrutiny rather than relational failure. Verification and sensitivity to inconsistencies can support appropriate reliance [55, 56], whereas harmonious agreement may arise from sycophantic model behavior and have undesirable consequences [57]. Relational quality should therefore not be defined as frictionless agreement. Larger prospective corpora enriched for errors, corrections, and disagreement are needed to distinguish constructive challenge from breakdown and to identify forms of repair that restore coordination without promoting uncritical reliance.

4.5 Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

Several features strengthen the study. The conversations arose during ordinary ChatGPT use rather than from standardized prompts, and participants selected interactions they regarded as personally meaningful. The design linked subjective measures to the conversations on which relational judgments could plausibly be based. It combined independently frozen topic modeling, blinded multi-rater annotation, human-supervised computational modeling, participant-grouped cross-validation, and explicit separation of feature development from outcome access. User, AI, and dyadic features were modeled separately, and relational information was tested beyond conversation length and thematic content. Finally, preregistered hypotheses were retained despite the unsupported H2 and fragile H3 findings, while post-registration extensions were identified as such.

These strengths coexist with important limitations. The modest, predominantly female and largely student sample comprised German-speaking, experienced ChatGPT users who reported at least one personally meaningful interaction, limiting generalizability to other populations, languages, cultures, models, and use contexts. Each participant donated one self-selected conversation. This increased psychological relevance but introduced selection, did not represent participants' full ChatGPT histories, and may not capture stable interactional style; model versions and personal instructions also varied naturalistically. The observational, cross-sectional design precludes causal or temporal conclusions about whether Social Substitution shapes behavior, particular interactions strengthen it, or both reflect unmeasured characteristics and contexts.

The annotation targets were theoretically anchored but sometimes rare, imbalanced, and only modestly reliable, and computational predictions inherit uncertainty from these human references. Repeated nested cross-validation estimates internal outof-sample performance within this sample, not performance in an independent cohort. The Social-Substitution finding should therefore be regarded as a robust exploratory pattern within this dataset, not a deployable individual assessment tool. Controlling 21 topics reduces an important alternative explanation but cannot remove all semantic, situational, or person-level confounding. Finally, the study assessed relational experience and conversational correlates, not clinical benefit, social displacement over time, or safety; neither high DTA nor Social Substitution implies effective or healthy AI use.

Future work should follow users across multiple conversations and combine momentary interaction-level ratings with repeated measures of DTA, attachment, loneliness, social connectedness, and well-being. Such designs could separate stable person-level orientations from within-person change and test whether conversational fingerprints precede, follow, or reciprocally reinforce relational experience. Cross-cultural and multilingual replication, comparisons across model versions and system designs, and external validation of frozen marker pipelines are needed. Experiments could manipulate contextual responsiveness, validation, disagreement, and repair while measuring both perceived relationship quality and appropriate reliance. Potential benefits and harms should be evaluated together rather than treating stronger relational engagement as an unqualified design goal.

4.6 Conclusion

Human relationships with LLMs cannot be understood from self-report or conversational behavior alone. In these naturalistic ChatGPT interactions, DTA and AI Attachment overlapped subjectively but were not behaviorally interchangeable. Validation and uptake were computationally observable without forming a generalizable conversational signature of DTA. Social Substitution, by contrast, was reflected in a distributed user-AI-dyadic fingerprint extending beyond conversation length and thematic content. Ruptures were rare and consistently repaired, while their association with DTA remained suggestive but fragile. Research on human-LLM relationships must therefore distinguish what people discuss, what unfolds between human and AI, and how the relationship is experienced. Being therapeutically allied with an AI, feeling emotionally close to it, and using it as a social substitute are related experiences-but they do not become visible in conversation in the same way.
