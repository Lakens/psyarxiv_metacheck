Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Beyond the Screen: Self-Reported Accounts of Escalation Crossover in a Darknet Selfidentified Pedophile Community

The problem of child sexual exploitation and abuse has been declared to be a global public health emergency, with 300 million children estimated to be affected globally by technology facilitated abuse (Fry et al., 2025). The Australian Centre to Counter Child Exploitation (ACCCE) Child Protection Triage Unit recorded 41 per cent jump in reports of online child sexual exploitation in the 2024/2025 financial year (ACCCE, 2025). Similarly, in 2025, INHOPE recorded over four million suspected online records of CSEM which was more than 450% increase compared to 2024 (INHOPE, 2025). The exponential growth of child sexual exploitation and abuse has been facilitated largely by the advent of technology creating new modalities to facilitate online child sexual exploitation and abuse, including new offenses that have disrupted traditional understandings of child sexual abuse (Finkelhor et al., 2022). This includes viewing, trading, and collecting CSEM (Krone, 2004; Quayle & Taylor, 2002; Steel et al., 2021); online solicitation or grooming; nonconsensual sexting; sextortion; self-produced child sexual abuse material; commercial child sexual exploitation (Finkelhor et al., 2022; McCartan & McAlister, 2012; Ramiro et al., 2019; Wolak et al., 2018); viewing of "shotacon" or "lolicon", Japanese media forms that include sexualized and young looking characters (Galbraith, 2011; Sluzhevsky, 2022); generating, viewing, and trading artificial intelligence (AI) generated CSEM (Thiel et al., 2023; Wolbers et al., 2025); and, commercial child sexual exploitation such as livestreaming child sexual abuse (Brown et al., 2020; ECPAT International, 2017; Napier et al., 2021). These emerging forms of abuse present new frontiers of offending that research is yet to understand. One particular need for empirical research is to understand the scope and diversity of online offenses (Finkelhor et al., 2022), particularly in the context of understanding how different offending types and profiles can contribute to increasing risk of contact offending. Another need is to understand the mechanisms and drivers that cause people with online offenses to understand their progression from online to contact offending (Ahmad et al., 2025).

Escalation Crossover Offending

Although sexual attractions to children can be a driver towards contact offending, many individuals do not make that transition (Cantor & McPhail, 2016). Understanding why some people move from online offenses to contact child sexual abuse is of concern for developing valid applied risk assessments as well for public safety; however, research in this area is limited given the known numbers in the cohort of individuals that have made the transition and been charged. A lack of understanding creates challenges for offender detection and the complex social processes involved in the trajectory of crossing over to the real world (Eke et al., 2011; Fortin et al., 2018; Seto et al., 2011). The escalation crossover group may be a subset from those in the "dual" and "mixed" offending groups. Dual and mixed refer to those that engage in multiple offending categories without regard for the temporal order of offending, while escalation crossover presents a unique developmental pathway characterized by a temporal sequence where the online offending behaviors precipitate the contact behaviors (Ahmad et al., 2025). However, little is known about the mechanisms and drivers that influence an individual's possible progression on this developmental journey (Fortin et al., 2018).

The motivation-facilitation model (Seto, 2019) conceptualizes sexual offending as a product of the combination of motivating and facilitating factors. Motivating factors are those that increase the likelihood of an offender seeking out deviant sexual stimuli and facilitating factors are those that can increase the likelihood of the sexual offense to occur. Motivating factors include paraphilia, high sex drive, and intense mating effort. Facilitators can be trait (e.g., antisociality, offense supportive attitudes and beliefs), state (e.g., mood, substance abuse) and situational (e.g., vulnerable victims, presence of guardians). Based on this theoretical framework, research has found differences between individuals with CSEM only offenses, contact only offenses, and both CSEM and contact offenses on motivational, facilitative, and situational dimensions for sexual offending (Babchishin et al., 2015; Elliott et al., 2019; Henshaw et al., 2018).

Prevalence and Risk Factors of Escalation Crossover Offending

A recent systematic review conducted by Ahmad et al., (2025) assessed the rates of escalation crossover offending, finding that rates were low for people with CSEM offenses (range: 0.8% to 12%), and varied for those with online solicitation offenses (range: 22% to 96%) and commercial offenses (range: 13% to 57%). Babchishin et al. (2015) undertook a meta-analysis to compare the characteristics between the three groups of individuals with online offenses (including CSEM-only, contact-only, and mixed), finding that the key transitional variable leading to escalation crossover was opportunity. Motivated offenders who had access to children were the most likely to commit contact abuse. This is consistent with other research suggesting that crossover offenders tend to have greater access to their child victims or the nature of their crimes reflect an opportunistic nature (McManus et al., 2015; Paquette & Brouillette-Alarie, 2024).

The presence of deviant sexual fantasies has been identified as a risk factor in committing a sexual offense, where individuals become motivated to enact the imagery they see within their fantasies (Bartels & Gannon, 2011; Deu & Edelmann, 1997; MacCulloch et al., 1983; Turner-Moore & Waterman, 2017; Ward & Hudson, 2000; Willis & Bartels, 2024).

Deviant sexual fantasies are types of mental imagery that elaborate a sexual scenario or script containing illegal experiences, such as acts of inflicting harm on someone (Harvey & Jeglic, 2020); and its repeated use can increase the risk of the fantasizer committing a sexual offense (Bartels & Gannon, 2011).

Emerging evidence has suggested that viewing CSEM may be an antecedent to contact abuse for some individuals (Ahmad et al., in press; Insoll et al., 2021). It has also been theorized that engaging in the various forms of CSEM can act as a facilitator to sexual offending by the internalization of rationalizations and offense-supportive beliefs (Lievesley et al., 2023). Offense-supportive beliefs are a key etiological factor to sexual offending and a risk factor for recidivism (Helmus et al., 2013; Szumski et al., 2018), underpinned by implicit theories of sexual offending (Ward & Keenan, 1999). Implicit theories increase risk of sexual offending through biasing social information processing. For example, the implicit theory of "children as sexual beings," suggests that children are sexual beings capable of having sexual agency; internalization of which can lead to supporting offending behavior. Paradoxically, CSEM has also been identified to function as an inhibitor for contact offending as a means of sexual gratification (Ahmad et al., in press; Quayle & Taylor, 2002; Walker, 2021); however, these studies note that CSEM should not be considered a prosocial strategy for harm reduction due to its inherently exploitative nature.

Pedophilia Forums on the Darknet

Communities of online self-identified pedophiles are increasingly housed on the darknet, where they cultivate environments of social cohesion and openly share their interests, experiences, and desires relating to pedophilia (Ahmad et al., in press; Beech et al., 2008; Durkin, 1997; Holt et al., 2010; Jenkins, 2001; Quayle & Taylor, 2002; Taylor et al., 2001). As the darknet is an encrypted part of the internet accessible only by specialized software, such as The Onion Router (Tor) browser, these communities provide an opportunity for social science research to access uncensored and typically inaccessible data and understand the behaviors, attitudes, values, and motivations of self-identified pedophiles (Ahmad et al., in press; Ball et al., 2024; Chiang et al., 2021; Durkin, 1997; Durkin & Bryant, 1999; Holt et al., 2010; Insoll et al., 2022; Jimenez-Arista & Reid, 2023; McManus et al., 2016; O'Halloran & Quayle, 2010; Steel et al., 2024; van der Bruggen & Blokland, 2021a , 2021b , 2022; Wolbers et al., 2024; Woodhams et al., 2021). Research access to these communities is problematic as participants often have reasonable concerns about disclosing information to researchers, which if known widely, could lead to social isolation or criminal penalties (Barratt & Maddox, 2016).

Darknet pedophilia communities can facilitate both pro-and anti-offending ideologies within their social discourse. Recently, Ahmad et al. (in press) explored the concept of selfidentified "conservative pedophiles" who abstain from sexual relations with children, despite their attractions, due to a self-constructed moral framework. These self-imposed moral constraints varied widely. Some individuals adopted conservative views due to a recognition of inherent harm caused to children; others believed in a concept called "discovery trauma", where societal reactions to the sexual relationship upon discovery cause harm to the child rather than the sexual relationship itself. Other reasons for self-identified conservativism included fear of legal or social consequences; and simply lack of opportunity to offend. Ahmad et al.'s (in press) study also identified "pro-contact" pedophiles, who are supportive of sexual relationships with children under self-constructed conditions of consent. These selfidentities of pedophiles show that a conservative identity can function as an inhibitor to contact offending; however, only when it is rooted in recognition of inherent harm caused to children. Other conservative identities and all pro-contact identities may present elevated risk profiles for escalation crossover. Ahmad et al. (in press) also identified inhibitors (CSEM, self-imposed non-offending stance, fear of detection or societal exposure, avoidance of situations with access to children, use of virtual material, and engagement in fantasies) and drivers (CSEM and opportunity) of escalation crossover, although these were limited to a subforum dedicated to conservative pedophiles and its limited generalizability to other types of self-identified pedophiles was noted.

The Current Study

In the current study, critical gaps in understanding the self-reported rates and drivers of escalation crossover offending within a darknet community of self-identified pedophiles are explored. Despite the growing recognition of the importance of understanding escalation crossover, there are significant limitations in the extant research that we aim to address in this paper, including: a) The self-reported diversity of online offending behaviors and activities, particularly in relation to newly emerging modalities of offending; b) the self-reported rates and drivers of escalation crossover from a broad sample of selfidentified pedophiles not limited to conservatives via obtaining uncensored insight into their behaviors; and c) the self-described accounts and pseudo-moral frameworks perpetuated by selfidentified pedophiles, building on the work of Ahmad et al. (in press).

We hope this exploration will help build current understanding of the psychological mechanisms underlying escalation crossover to inform tailored risk assessment and intervention.

Method

Data Source

Data for this study were obtained from a darknet forum 1 hosted on the Tor network for individuals self-identifying as pedophiles, with variations of the term "pedophile" utilized in the forum name. The forum was initiated in 2011 as a platform for sharing "child-love" 2 1 The darknet forum utilized for this study has not been named to maintain the anonymity of the community. 2 The terms "child love", or "boy love", or "girl love" refer to sexual attraction to children that is described holistically with non-sexual elements, including emotional, romantic, and moral guides and media; however, it has since evolved into an anonymous "therapeutic community" to provide advice on fostering "loving, caring, consensual relationships" with children.

Media sharing is strictly not permitted. This ongoing forum has been active for over 14 years, with approximately 1,425 active members, 277,822 posts, and an average of eight members online per day. The page has received over 7.5 million total views to datefoot_0 , with 4.2 million views received in 2024. The forum is composed of three main sections with 14 subforums.

Each subforum contains threads, which are a collection of posts. Threads begin when a member posts a question or shares an experience to open a conversation. Other members then generate the conversation on the topic by responding to the initial post and replying to each other.

To our knowledge, this forum has been used for research into pedophilia in only three other peer-reviewed studies (Ahmad et al., in press; Ball et al., 2024; Wolbers et al., 2024) and one PhD thesis (Verrijdt, 2019). This is a valuable data source that offers insight into a community of self-identified pedophiles (including offending and non-offending) who have been largely undetected by law enforcement.

The darknet is different from the "clearnet" or "surface web", as it cannot be accessed by mainstream software and includes hidden sites only available through specialized browsers (Gehl, 2016). Those accessing the darknet are also able to completely maintain anonymity, where most visitors will never know the identity of the server host, and the hosts will never know the identity of their visitors (Gehl, 2016). To maintain access to a hidden, highly anonymous, and stigmatized community, we drew from (Ahmad et al., in press).

Pseudonymous and Deceptive Approach

The study was conducted under an approved ethics protocol in partnership with the Australian National University's (ANU) Cybercrime Observatory. The Observatory provided access to an existing pseudonymous forum account and to the relevant forum data. Similar to Gehl (2016), we believed we had "no choice but to study the dark web social network on its own terms", both due to the technical reasons by nature of the darknet, as well as the culture of the present darknet forum itself. According to the forum's registration agreement, members are not permitted to provide any personally identifying information, specifying "no real-life personal details" and instructing members to provide a fake e-mail address "with absolutely NO connections to your real-life information at all". Gehl (2016) suggests that a pseudonymous approach is culturally appropriate for darknet domains, where members value dissociation from their real-world identities. We therefore utilized this approach to be anonymous within the community.

The methodological and ethical complexities of the darknet, particularly in researching highly sensitive and stigmatized behaviors, justified our use of covert deception for this study. Pickering et al. (2021) suggests that the use of deception in crime or cybercrime research must be justified to observe spontaneous responses. The Belmont Report (National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical Behavioral Research, 1978) further states that deception is permissible when risks to the subjects are minimal, and if informing subjects of aspects of the research are likely to impair its validity.

Previous discussions between users within the forum observed by the research team indicated that direct engagement without deception can decrease member engagement and increase suspicion. For the present study, we believed the study would be compromised if participants were informed of the research, an effect particularly pronounced among hidden populations who are actively evading detection and belong to groups where researcher identification "may not always be welcome", and is rather inadvisable (Pickering et al., 2021). Noting these complexities, we utilized an established account within the forum using a pseudonym in partnership with the account owner, the ANU Cybercrime Observatory, to embed ourselves within the community, and engage with forum discussion through an established pseudonymous account while concealing the researchers' institutional identity and research purpose and under the guise that a forum member was initiating conversations for their own interest. Engagement was limited to voluntary discussion of prior experiences and hypothetical views, using questions framed to align with the forum's existing conversational norms. Notably, our research involved analysis of user responses to structured polls and posts; we did not seek to identify participants outside of the forum and no identifying information was collected. Participants could not be debriefed after engagement because disclosure of the research purpose would compromise the established account and its ongoing credibility within the community. Data were accessed through the ANU secure laboratory environment. Where data were downloaded for offline analysis, storage was on an encrypted and locked USB device accessible only to authorized research personnel, in accordance with the data-access arrangements. The dataset used for analysis comprised textual material in response to posts, poll responses, and associated comments. No CSEM, including in textual, image, or videobased forms was collected, downloaded, or retained for this study. We also recognize that forum activity and engagement in response to our posts could contain information suggesting serious or immediate harm; however, because forum users operated pseudonymously and the study did not collect identifying information, it was impossible for the research team to identify or report individual users.

Darknet Active Engagement Ethnography

A darknet active engagement ethnographic approach was employed following the methodologies identified in previous studies (Barratt & Maddox, 2016; Gehl, 2016). This included an initial period of observation where the technical, ethical-legal, and social sensitivities were observed, and preparations were made to understand the operations of the forum. This also included linguistic immersion by learning and adopting the forum's vernacular and terminology to assist in the fieldwork. For example, this aided in the conceptualization of escalation crossover for this study using the community's established lexicon of "crossing the line". This was a common phrase used within conversations to describe behaviors relating to progression from online to contact behaviors. We also learnt that the community's terminology for CSEM was "child pornography" or "CP".

Consequently, we adopted the phrase "crossing the line" to refer to escalation crossover. The second stage entailed interactive fieldwork where we focused on monitoring dynamics in response to our presence and establish legitimacy as a fellow self-identified pedophile.

During this stage, we mirrored an agentic nature of ethnography for an exploratory, flexible practice similar to Barratt and Maddox (2016). We utilized common conversational strategies observed among other forum members to generate discussions, including open ended questions in free-text posts to invite conversation, as well as the forum's native function of structured polls.

Three structured polls were posted on the forum inviting respondents to vote for i) the type of online behaviors they engage in, ii) whether they had crossed the line from online to contact behaviors and what were their reasons doing so, and iii) their self-identified pedophile identity. Open ended questions were also employed in these posts to invite commentaries from respondents based on their responses. It should be noted that these three polls were posted in three separate posts within the forum as the features of the forum only permitted one poll per post. As a result, the respondents for each poll may not be the same. This variation in respondent base for each poll should be considered when interpreting results.

Data were collected from 11 July 2025 to 25 October 2025, after which no more votes or responses were received.

Participation in each poll was voluntary, and the study could not determine whether individual respondents participated in more than one poll. The resulting data should therefore be interpreted as anonymous, self-reported forum accounts.

Poll 1: Online Behaviors

The development of the first poll to understand the diversity of respondents' online behaviors was based on online activities identified in existing literature and defined further below, including: viewing softcore or hardcore, or "hurtcore" CSEM, viewing non nude content, reading or writing fictional erotic stories involving children, collecting or trading CSEM, viewing self-produced CSEM, viewing hidden cam content, viewing artificial intelligence (AI)-generated CSEM, viewing "shotacon" or "lolicon", online solicitation of children, "Daddy Dom Little Girl" role playing online, and livestreaming child sexual offending. This was a multi-select poll, where respondents were able to choose more than one item.

Softcore and Hardcore CSEM. CSEM can be differentiated in two types. "Softcore" CSEM features non-penetrative imagery, such as nudity or children in sexualized poses, whereas "hardcore" or "hurtcore" features graphic depictions of abuse including rape, torture, sadism, or bestiality (Ahmad et al., in press).

Collecting or Trading CSEM. The collecting habits of offenders have been noted to be important characteristics of their offending behaviors (Krone, 2004; Quayle & Taylor, 2002; Seigfried-Spellar, 2014; Steel et al., 2021). Krone (2004) noted that a subset of CSEM offenders become obsessive collectors, such that their collections become an end in itself.

Viewing Non-Nude Content.

Non-nude content refers to visual material that depicts children in sexually suggestive poses or dressed in provocative clothing but does not depict full nudity (Ahmad et al., in press).

Viewing Self-Produced CSEM.

Self-produced CSEM refers to content that is selfproduced or livestreamed sexually explicit material by children themselves (Bloxsom et al., 2024) through platforms such as Omegle (Salter & Sokolov, 2024).

Viewing Voyeuristic Content.

Voyeuristic or hidden-cam content refers to content that is covertly recorded to capture children in vulnerable settings without their knowledge, such as in bathrooms, change rooms, and public pools (Lister & Gannon, 2024).

Viewing AI-Generated CSEM. AI generated CSEM refers to synthetic CSEM that is created using AI or deepfake technology. The production of AI generated CSEM is easily accessible as offenders can legally download the software they need and produce an unlimited number of images (Thiel et al., 2023).

Viewing Lolicon or Shotacon. Lolicon and shotacon are Japanese genres of anime or manga featuring cartoon children in an erotic or sexually suggestive manner. This material is often used as a substitute to CSEM to help individuals manage their urges (Ahmad et al., in press; Jones et al., 2021).

Online Solicitation of Children. The online solicitation of children, also known as sexual chatting or online sexual grooming, is a common form of online child sexual exploitation where an offender creates an opportunity to exploit a child through a premeditated process of fostering a relationship of trust with the child prior to manipulating them into online sexual activities (McCartan & McAlister, 2012; Ramiro et al., 2019).

Reading or Writing Fictional Erotic Material.

Fantasy or fictional sexual materials can include written erotic stories that are written or read for the purpose of sexual arousal or fulfilment (Lievesley et al., 2023). Although this material may include real people or children, its content is not based on real events (Ahmad et al., in press). This material is readily available online, such as in darknet forums, including the present forum, where members share their own content and read others'. It is notable, however, that while such material may be fictional, it may still draw upon real people or experiences, or combine both fictional and autobiographical elements.

"Daddy Dom Little Girl" Role Playing. "Daddy Dom Little Girl" (DDLG) refers to a sexual age regressive role play that is conducted in online spaces, similar to bondage, discipline, dominance and submission, sadomasochism (BDSM), in which one member plays the role of the "daddy dom" or the caretaker, and the other member plays the role of the "little girl" (O'Donovan et al., 2022; Wolbers et al., 2024). DDLG has been previously noted being utilized as a role-play by self-identified pedophiles (Wolbers et al., 2024).

Livestreaming Child Sexual Abuse.

A newly emerging form of online child sexual exploitation is the livestreaming of child sexual abuse. This involves individuals virtually but actively participating in the sexual abuse of a child by requesting, ordering, paying, and dictating how the sexual activity will be carried out (ECPAT International, 2016; Hillman et al., 2014).

Poll 2: Crossing the Line

The development of the second poll was to understand the self-reported rates and driving mechanisms of escalation crossover or crossing the line. The poll specifically asked respondents whose first online pedophile-related activity was engaging with CSEM, whether they had later progressed to contact offenses.

The options provided in the poll for crossing the line were based on previous research identifying risk factors, namely; opportunity, desensitization to CSEM and fantasies (Ahmad et al., in press; Babchishin et al., 2015; Bartels & Gannon, 2011; Deu & Edelmann, 1997; Insoll et al., 2021; MacCulloch et al., 1983; Quayle & Taylor, 2003; Ward & Hudson, 2000).

An "other" option was also provided in the poll, and respondents were encouraged to elaborate on these reasons using the free text reply feature. This was a single-select poll, where respondents were able to choose only one item and respondents were encouraged to select the option that best represented the primary driver for them crossing the line.

Qualitative Structured Tabular Thematic Analysis

Responses were analyzed using the procedures and guidelines of Smith et al. (2021) and Robinson (2022). The posts were downloaded into Microsoft Excel where a structured tabular analysis was carried out using Robinson's (2022) inductive analytical framework, specifically developed to analyze short open-ended responses and online posts. The qualitative approach used to develop the themes was a sequential idiographic thematic content analysis, where themes are developed in the data as they arise, as discussed in the phenomenological approach of Smith et al. (2021).

The first stage entailed deep immersion in the data, including repeated reading and note-taking for developing the codes. The second stage entailed the development of the codes and initial coding framework, which was completed by the first author based on the tabular analysis and by identifying recurring themes emerging from the data. The themes identified represent the operation of the forum and discussions around contact and non-contact child sex offending. This coding frame was verified by the third and fourth authors (both senior researchers in either clinical psychology or law and criminology). At this stage, the coding frame, together with the entire dataset was given to an independent coder not involved in the data collection. The inter-coder is the fifth author, a PhD researcher in psychology. 6 The word "conservative" was intentionally utilized in the respondent-facing survey as it is operationalized as a legitimate and frequently negotiated pedophile identity within the forum itself. Its use in this article does not imply moral legitimacy or protection from harm. The label is retained only when describing the forum's own discourse. Both third and fourth stages included the development and tabulation of themes, which was conducted based on semantically consistent clusters. We followed the pattern coding approach, which involves collapsing codes generated in the initial framework into fewer analytical concepts (Miles et al., 2019); the first and fifth authors conducted this stage independently.

Intercoder Reliability

As qualitative analysis is open to bias, it is essential that identified themes and the coding are verified by independent coders (Miles et al., 2019). In the fifth stage, both the first and fifth authors discussed codes and checked agreement for themes to determine clarity and accuracy. We found that our independently completed codes and themes were highly consistent, and so our discussions primarily focused on resolving semantic ambiguities in theme labelling rather than addressing substantial inconsistencies. After these refinements, we calculated intercoder reliability (ICR).

ICR is a numerical measure of agreement between two independent coders regarding the coding of a dataset and is an important step in reducing bias risk in qualitative research (O'Connor & Joffe, 2020). We utilized both percent agreement and Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) measures of the extent to which the two independent coders made similar decisions regarding coding (MacPhail et al., 2016) for an indication of the degree of agreement. Percent agreement in this study was >90% on first calculation, indicating significant consistency in coding and theme development and meeting ICR requirements for robust analysis (Miles et al., 2019; Roaché, 2017). To obtain a more reliable and robust index that controls for chance agreement, ICR was additionally evaluated using Cohen's kappa, which indicated substantial agreement (κ = 0.67) according to Landis and Koch benchmarks (Landis & Koch, 1977).

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the online behaviors of self-identified pedophiles, as well as the self-reported rates and drivers of escalation crossover from CSEM offending to increase our empirical understanding of the links and potential transitions between online and contact offending among this group. We confirmed and expanded upon the growing literature relating to the diversification of online child sexual exploitation behaviors (Finkelhor et al., 2022).

The results indicated that the engagement rates among the behavior types vary considerably depending on both preference (e.g., for fictional/artistic material) and associated perceived risk of detection, ranging from 4% for livestreaming sexual abuse to 68% for viewing softcore CSEM. The topmost common behaviors included viewing softcore CSEM, reading fictional stories and viewing non-nude content; these findings suggest that forum members may prefer material that they are able to frame as "victimless," likely stemming from a perception that its creation does not involve the harm of a child. For example, fictional stories or non-nude content can be rationalized as not causing any tangible harm to children, while softcore CSEM may be considered by some who view it as harmless due to its nonpenetrative, apparently non-violent nature. The marked popularity of fictional and artistic materials is consistent with recent research that has identified fantasy sexual material as a perceived outlet for sexual gratification that does not entail physical contact with a real person (Al-Alosi, 2018; Lievesley et al., 2023) and may therefore be perceived as victimless. Ahmad et al. (in press) similarly found that those who identify as conservative pedophiles selectively engage with types of CSEM that displays children who appear to be willing participants, and in doing so, construct a moral boundary based on levels of perceived harm.

Considering the nature of the forum is to provide advice on fostering "loving, caring, consensual relationships" with children and that this poll was open to both conservative and non-conservatives, it is unsurprising that the most engaged activities are those that may be considered acceptable based on this pseudo-moral framework. However, noting the significant presence of pro-contact conservatives in the third poll's sample, being almost 80 percent, it is also unsurprising that hardcore CSEM was the third most commonly engaged activity.

Fictional stories and non-nude content being among the top engaged activities, with trading CSEM, online solicitation, and livestreaming sexual activities being among the lowest engaged may suggest a hierarchy of risk (i.e., risk of detection), where most respondents appear to engage in low-risk consumption whereas a small subset engage in high-risk actions.

The low engagement of interaction-based behaviors such as online solicitation and livestreaming may also indicate that most respondents consider risk mitigation to be an important aspect of their choice of activities and would steer away from interaction-based behaviors due to their perceived increased risk of detection. This is consistent with respondent commentary stating that the heightened nature of modern surveillance prevents them from engaging in riskier behaviors. Another explanation of this distribution of behaviors could be related to the availability of the material, where fictional stories and non-nude content might be relatively easy to find, while less common behaviors might be less accessible.

One of the most important findings of this study is that almost half of all the first poll's respondents reported they had crossed the line to contact offending. This percentage exceeds previous research. For example, (Ahmad et al., 2025) found the rates of CSEM escalation crossover offenders to be between 0.8 to 12 percent. The high rate found in this study may be due to several factors, including the nature of the sample being a highly anonymous community, allowing members to discuss their experiences and behaviors candidly, which may facilitate more honest admissions of escalation crossover than would have been observed in a convicted or clinical sample. However, it must be noted that forum's anonymity and focus on sexual relationships with children may have shaped who participated in the polls and how they presented themselves. Anonymity may reduce some barriers to disclosure, online pedophilic forums provide social contexts and hierarchies which may facilitate exaggeration or status-seeking for credibility (Durkin & Bryant, 1999). The current data cannot distinguish candid accounts from performative claims.

Access to this forum also provided the opportunity to tap into a typically inaccessible cohort of both convicted and undetected individuals, enabling us to cast a wider net and be able to describe the rates of escalation crossover among this hidden population, some of whom have not come to the attention of law enforcement. Nonetheless, the small sample size of this study cannot be ignored when noting the significantly high rate of escalation crossover.

Consistent with previous research, our findings indicate that opportunity, or a situation providing immediate access to a child, stood out as a critical driver of escalation crossover (Ahmad et al., in press; Babchishin et al., 2015; McManus et al., 2015; Paquette & Brouillette-Alarie, 2024), appearing synergistically with other pre-existing psychological and behavioral risk factors, such as CSEM engagement and deviant fantasies (Bartels & Gannon, 2011; Insoll et al., 2021; Ward & Hudson, 2000). These findings reiterate our current understanding of opportunity being an important factor in escalation crossover, particularly when combined other external or internal disinhibitory factors. The motivation-facilitation model (Seto, 2019) can be applied within the current sample with intrinsic motivation in the form of pedophilia (as it is reasonable to infer that all members have pedophilic attractions considering the nature of the forum) establishing the baseline risk, and facilitating factors such as impulsivity, development and/or strengthening of offense-supportive beliefs through CSEM engagement and access to an unsupervised child increasing risk of escalation crossover. These findings reinforce the multifactorial nature of the motivation-facilitation model, highlighting that opportunity or situational factors in isolation may not be sufficient for escalation crossover, however they can act as the catalyst when intersecting with other preexisting motivation and facilitating factors.

Our findings are broadly consistent with the interaction account proposed by the motivation-facilitation model, particularly the concept of a person-environment interaction, which suggests that strongly motivated individuals, such as those with sexual attractions to children, are more likely to create opportunities to offend. Opportunity was the most frequently selected explanation among participants who reported having crossed the line, indicating that it could be an important situational factor, often operating synergistically with other factors such as disinhibition. Retrospective accounts commonly linked access to a child with factors such as heightened sexual arousal, intoxication, and impulsivity. This interpretation is also consistent with other research suggesting that disinhibitory factors such as cognitive impulsivity have a greater link to contact offending among online offenders while a greater degree of self-control may be a distinguishing factor between those who crossover to contact offending and those who do not (Ahmad et al., in press; Babchishin et al., 2015; Elliott et al., 2009; Seto et al., 2012). Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as our data was self-reported where respondents retrospectively constructed the intersection of opportunity, motivation, and disinhibition in explaining their own accounts of crossing the line.

Our findings also provide indicative support for indulgence in fantasies as being another potential risk factor for escalation crossover, with approximately 20% of respondents from the third poll reporting that indulgence in fantasies was their primary driver of crossing the line. This is consistent with past research suggesting the use of sexual fantasies involving children can function as a predictor and etiological factor of contact offending (Bartels & Gannon, 2011; Seto, 2019). A large online study that reported a pattern of findings to support applicability of the motivation-facilitation model found that the risk of contact offending increased for participants who scored highly on time spent engaging in sexual fantasies involving children (Klein et al., 2015).

Although less common, desensitization to CSEM and wanting to produce CSEM were also identified as indicative factors to consider in the escalation crossover pathway. Research has found that the desensitization and normalization of offense-supportive attitudes that result from CSEM engagement may increase risk of escalation crossover (Ahmad et al., in press; Quayle & Taylor, 2003). A recent review found that although there tend to be a lower proportion of escalation crossover offenders, a common feature across all those who had crossed over appeared to be the desire to enter into direct contact with the objects of their collection: children (Fortin et al., 2018). Such desire may enable escalation crossover due to a pursuit of desiring direct, sexual experiences with children, or wanting to produce CSEM themselves. Similar to Ahmad et al. (in press), an equally interesting and important contribution of this study is the apparent dual function of CSEM as both a behavioral inhibitor and potential risk factor for escalation crossover. Whether CSEM may represent a component to divert individuals from, or alternatively complement contact offending has been debated in literature (Ahmad et al., 2025; Beech et al., 2008; Quayle & Taylor, 2002; Riegel, 2004; Seto et al., 2001); however, it is understood that while CSEM users form a largely heterogenous population, a small subset exists that poses a real risk for future contact offending (Ahmad et al., 2025; Babchishin et al., 2015; Insoll et al., 2021; Krone & Smith, 2017; Quayle & Taylor, 2002). Most respondents described offense-supportive cognitions and implicit theories relating to sexual activity with children that were developed or reinforced because of engagement with CSEM; notably, such cognitions involved seeing children as sexual beings and associated perceived behavioral possibilities of offending behaviors. First proposed by Ward and Keenan (1999), seeing children as sexual beings is a commonly identified implicit theory and cognitive distortion identified amongst those who have sexually offended against children and particularly in those who have committed CSEM offenses (Bartels & Merdian, 2016; Merdian et al., 2014; Soldino et al., 2020; Ward & Keenan, 1999). Research also suggests that the proliferation and ease of access to CSEM results in normalising such offense-supportive cognitions and reduces inhibitions to offend (Beech et al., 2008).

Consistent with this notion, our findings identified the role of CSEM as an educative tool to teach viewers the types of offending behaviors in which they can engage and how to offend with minimal risk of detection by authorities. This is consistent with previous research findings that CSEM can act as a guide for the production of CSEM, where viewers "copied" what they saw (Quayle & Taylor, 2002). Our findings are also consistent with research finding that individuals who consume violent adult pornography may internalize distorted beliefs about sexual experiences and may lead to a sense of legitimization of violence (de Roos & Ferrando, 2025).

CSEM use may also function as a risk factor for escalation crossover by inducing a heightened sexual state in individuals, lowering inhibitions, and potentially increasing the likelihood of committing a contact offense. Previous findings in Ahmad et al. (in press) also demonstrate a progressing and escalating pattern of pornography use, where users typically begin with consumption of legal adult pornography and progress to CSEM following desensitization and diminished sexual satisfaction leading to a pursuit for novelty and extreme material. This addictive cycle of sexual stimulation and novelty seeking can create an environment of increased risk, particularly when situational factors such as opportunity are present.

On the other hand, some respondents described their engagement with CSEM as an alternative outlet or substitute to help them manage their urges and prevent contact offending. This is consistent with research demonstrating that for some, CSEM is used as a substitute to contact offending, whereas for others, it can be both the blueprint and stimulus for a contact offending (Quayle & Taylor, 2002). It is difficult to ascertain, however, whether CSEM functioned more commonly as a driver or inhibitor of escalation crossover. Both functions were observed in the data for this study, where CSEM appeared to operate differently for different respondents. This duality may speak to the heterogeneity of CSEM users and highlights the need for further research in breaking down the paradoxical role that CSEM may play in offending trajectories.

Another novel finding highlighted by this study is the diversity of self-identities within the online self-identified pedophile community and their associated moral frameworks. Previously Ahmad et al. (in press) identified these types of self-constructed identities within the forum, and the current study expands on these previous findings by understanding their spread and distribution. Our findings provide important information into the heterogenous profiles and pseudo-moral frameworks associated with pedophilic identities.

A large proportion of respondents of the third poll, almost 80 percent, identified as procontact. Out of this group, majority were actively engaging in sexually offending children, appearing to be operating under a self-constructed pseudo-moral framework of offending only if they perceived the child to consent. This finding reveals important insights into the cognitive distortions present within this cohort, specifically seeing children as sexual beings with the understanding, agency, and capacity of consenting to sexual activities (Ward & Hudson, 2000). Thirty percent of pro-contact pedophiles said they were not actively engaging in sexually offending behaviors; however, this was due to external inhibitors such as fear of societal repercussions or the lack of practical opportunities to offend. These reasons being cited suggest absence of internal inhibitors and describe a restraint based on circumstance of self-preservation. External inhibition is less stable than internalized moral reasoning (Beech et al., 2008) suggesting that if such barriers were not present, these individuals may be at heightened risk of escalation crossover. In contrast, 30 percent of all respondents who completed the third poll identified as conservative, out of which most were not active due to the inherent harm caused to the child, and others were not active due to wishing to avoid discovery trauma (i.e., the belief that societal reactions to the sexual relationship upon discovery cause harm to the child rather than the sexual relationship itself). The former group appeared to reflect an internalized moral reasoning based on general societal beliefs, indicating a stable inhibition against acting on their pedophilic attractions; whereas the latter group reflected an externally anchored belief of the potential harm to the child only due to the stigma associated with discovery of the abuse (as opposed to the abuse itself). Such a view appears linked with the cognitive distortions of seeing children as sexual beings as well as denial of injury (Ahmad et al., in press).

The diversity of these self-identified pedophiles, from pro-contact to conservative, reveals various and heterogenous pathways suggesting that risk and inhibiting factors may be influenced by various underlying self-constructed pseudo-moral frameworks. These findings may potentially inform the refinement of risk tools and interventions, as those with procontact views can represent a higher risk group where cognitive distortions and the absence of external inhibitors provide indicative evidence for facilitating escalation crossover.

Implications and For Future Research and Indicative Findings for Practice

Before considering the implications of our findings, it should be noted that any implication must be interpreted in light of our study's methodological limitations which have been detailed further below. Nonetheless, the findings provide indicative and preliminary insights into how a subset of forum respondents described the interplay between various factors in their accounts of crossing the line. These observations identify potentially important questions for future research and offer points for consideration in practice, particularly where practitioners or investigators encounter comparable self-presentational narratives, offense-supportive beliefs, and situational accounts.

Our findings provide insights into the relative rates of engagement in a diverse range of online behaviors relating to pedophilia. Individuals may be engaging in multiple activities simultaneously, making it difficult to understand pathways and trajectories of escalation crossover. These findings may indicate that risk assessment tools cannot rely on simplistic or categorical classifications and there is a need to consider a complex, multi-tiered approach to understand the diverse and rapidly evolving nature of online behaviors. Further, the increasing popularity of fictional materials such as shotacon, lolicon, written CSEM, and AI generated CSEM raises important questions about whether such outlets are inhibitory in nature or have the potential to contribute to the desensitization and normalization of offensesupportive cognitions.

It is important to note that CSEM use was identified as having a complex relationship with escalation crossover wherein it appeared to be paradoxically both a potential driving and inhibiting factor. This complexity and multifaceted engagement with CSEM requires further research to understand what differentiates individuals using it as an inhibiting factor versus those whose use may drive their likelihood of progressing to escalation crossover.

Nevertheless, this indicates the need for interventions for CSEM-only users to disrupt the escalation crossover pathway. Interventions may benefit from challenging the offensesupportive cognitions and implicit theories that appear to be normalized and internalized by CSEM consumption. Opportunity was identified as another driver, especially when combined with other factors such as disinhibition. This suggests that dynamic situational factors are critical to escalation crossover, particularly for cohorts with existing motivational and facilitating factors and lacking in psychological barriers to offending such as self-control.

The exploration of diversity of self-identities highlighted that motivations, rationales, and pseudo-moral frameworks may also be important to understanding both offending and restraint behaviors, including the potential for escalation crossover. Importantly, pedophile self-identities, particularly pro-contact identities indicating lack of internalized reasons for restraint present a fundamentally different risk profile to those with conservative identities, with the former having potentially higher and more complex levels of offense-supportive cognitions. This may be specifically important to be aware of in the case of those who are not actively offending due to external inhibitors and may be at higher risk of situational or opportunistic offending. These findings may also indicate that intervention programs could benefit from incorporating such constructed identity profiles to address their associated pseudo-moral boundaries and develop alternative pro-social frameworks.

Limitations

The findings of this study must be weighed against its inherent limitations. This study presents novel insights into the diversity of online behaviors and the nature of escalation crossover with its strength being its uncensored, unfiltered data from a hidden population that would otherwise be inaccessible for research; however, the data are preliminary and derived from a small, voluntary and anonymous sample within a single darknet forum. As respondents may have differed across the separate polls, and because accounts could not be independently verified, the findings cannot draw causations or support definitive conclusions about risk assessment or intervention. They should therefore be read cautiously with regard implications within clinical, investigative, or prevention practice. The representativeness of our findings are inherently limited noting our reliance on a small sample of self-reported data that may be biased or otherwise not accurately reflect the true beliefs and experiences of respondents. It remains unclear how generalizable the findings are to the broader population of individuals with pedophilic attractions, or even the full membership of the forum itself. It should be noted that participation in the polls was entirely voluntary and we received small sample sizes that limit the generalizability of our results. Further, the forum is described as a platform to discuss and foster "loving, caring, consensual relationships" with children, including sexual relationships. This may have led to a disproportionate number of pro-contact forum respondents that may not be reflective of other online or offline communities. Another limitation is that anonymous forum responses may represent self-presentation rather than accurate real-world behavior. The forum had an established social hierarchy which could have encouraged respondents to exaggerate or minimize their experiences. Although anonymity may reduce barriers to disclosure, it does not establish truthfulness. The study therefore cannot determine whether claims of contact offending, "crossing the line," nonoffending restraint, or particular ideological positions were accurate, complete, or consistently held.

It should also be noted that the construction of the polls with fixed choice responses may have influenced the responses received. This was somewhat mitigated by the option to provide free-text responses for elaboration and to capture nuance; however, the fixed-choice responses, particularly the single-select polls, may have constrained respondents to choose an option that may not have captured the full complexity of their experiences.

We utilized a deceptive approach to engage with the forum. This was ethically justified to access this hidden and stigmatized community of self-identified pedophiles and our use of deception allowed us to collect valuable and novel insights; however, this approach presents inherent limitations to the study. To maintain our credibility as a fellow selfidentified pedophile, we had to ensure that the framing of our questions and polls were consistent with the forum's vernacular and framed as pursuits of curiosity of others' experiences or requests of advice. We had to exercise caution in maintaining this identity, which limited our ability to ask direct or probing questions to avoid suspicion as researchers, potentially affecting the specificity of our findings.

Conclusion

This study engaged with an online self-identified pedophile community on the darknet to develop an understanding of i) the diversity of online offending behaviors, ii) the selfreported rates and drivers of escalation crossover, and iii) anti-offending or pro-offending ideologies associated with pedophile self-identification. Findings revealed a significant diversity of online behaviors, the most commonly engaged activities being viewing softcore CSEM, reading fictional CSEM, viewing non-nude content, viewing hardcore CSEM and collecting CSEM, and viewing shotacon or lolicon. A significant proportion of respondents, almost half, said they had crossed the line, with the main reported drivers of escalation crossover identified being opportunity (in combination with impulsivity and CSEM consumption intoxication); CSEM (desensitization, a desire to produce), including the offense-supportive cognitions normalized and internalized through its engagement; and indulgence in fantasies. Our analysis of self-identified ideologies demonstrates that majority of respondents identified as being pro-contact pedophiles, meaning they were supportive of having sexual relations with children, and a smaller minority being conservative which implied they were generally not supportive of having sexual relations with children.

Associated pseudo-moral frameworks identified with the identities are discussed. Together, our findings indicate the need for nuanced and individualized approaches to risk assessment and intervention to consider the self-constructed pseudo-moral frameworks and unique profiles that may disrupt pathways to escalation crossover.
