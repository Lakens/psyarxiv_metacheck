What we found

We found 9 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Near-death experiences (NDEs) are most often reported in association with physiologically life-threatening events such as cardiac arrest or coma, but phenomenologically similar reports, near-death-like (NDE-like) experiences, also arise in the absence of a life threat, for example during syncope, deep meditation, hypnosis, and psychedelic states. This synthesis reviews the evidence on whether the phenomenology, psychological correlates, and aftereffects of NDEs differ from those of NDE-like experiences. Comparing the largest retrospective survey, a mixed-methods online study, and a within-person psychedelic comparison, consistently indicate broad overlap in core features across contexts. At the same time, encounters with deceased persons or spiritual entities were reported in 34.7% NDE accounts and none of 25 NDE-like accounts in one exploratory study, and a singlecase comparison found life review, encounters with the deceased, and a threshold or point of no return present only in the NDE. However, no study was designed to test equivalence, samples were largely self-selected and retrospective, and formal measurement-invariance testing of the instruments in use across etiologies is still lacking. These constraints caution against concluding phenomenological identity. The implications of this evidence for neurophysiological, psychological, evolutionary, and metaphysical interpretations are analyzed, and priorities for prospective, preregistered, adequately powered research are outlined.

1. Introduction

Near-death experiences (NDEs) are profound episodes of what has been termed disconnected consciousness: vivid, internally generated experience occurring, from an outside observer's perspective, in circumstances of severely reduced responsiveness, most typically during cardiac arrest, pathological coma, or other conditions involving an actual or potential physical threat to life (Martial et al., 2025). Prototypical features include an impression of leaving the body, movement through a tunnel toward a light, encounter with other beings or deceased relatives, a panoramic review of one's life, intense positive emotion, and a perceived boundary or point of no return. Reports that are phenomenologically similar in most or all of these respects also occur in contexts that are plainly not life-threatening, during fainting (syncope), sleep-related states, deep meditation, hypnosis, and after administration of psychoactive drugs, principally serotonergic psychedelics but also, in the older pharmacological literature, the N-methyl-D-aspartate antagonist ketamine (Jansen, 1997; Rogo, 1984). Following the convention of the literature reviewed here, I refer to the former as NDEs (or, where the context requires, clinically verified NDEs) and the latter as NDE-like experiences.

The coexistence of these two classes of reports poses the central question of this review: does the phenomenology of an NDE depend on the proximity of its trigger to actual physiological death, or can experiences of the same kind arise when life was never in danger? The question matters for theory. If NDE features are related to their etiology, explanations may be trigger-specific, for instance in terms of cerebral hypoxia or the neurophysiology of the dying brain. If NDE features are not related to their etiology, then either very different triggers converge on shared mechanisms, or the source of the experience is not tied to any particular trigger at all. The comparison is also practically consequential, because NDE-like reports are common in clinical and non-clinical populations, and instruments developed for NDEs are routinely used to classify them.

Three terminological cautions are implicated in what follows. First, the boundary between NDE and NDE-like experience is definitional rather than natural. A report is classified as an NDE-like experience by the absence of a documented life threat, and in most studies that absence is established by self-report rather than medical records. When the primary studies are evaluated below, I accordingly distinguish self-identified life threats, which the experiencer reports but which are not documented in medical records, from medically documented crises; wherever the distinction matters to a study's interpretation, it is stated. Second, the taxonomy adopted here is defined by objective threat rather than by the experiencer's subjective appraisal. An individual who was not physiologically close to death but believed at the time that they were dying, as can happen in uncomplicated syncope, a panic attack, or minor trauma, is counted here as an NDE-like experiencer, even though some models deliberately encompass situations that are actual, potential, or merely perceived threats, as NEPTUNE does (Martial et al., 2025), and even though subjective nearness to death may itself shape the phenomenology: some classic reports of NDE features were in fact obtained from patients who were not, medically speaking, near death (Owens et al., 1990). Whether perceived-threat experiences form a third, intermediate category rather than a subclass of NDE-like experiences is an open empirical question, taken up in Sections 8.2 and 8.6. Third, the phrase "clinically verified" is reserved here for cases in which a physiological crisis (typically coma or cardiac arrest) is medically documented, as in the coma-survivor samples of Charland-Verville et al. (2014); it does not imply verification of the experience's content. Throughout this article, "classical NDE" refers to experiences reported after a self-identified or documented lifethreatening event, and NDE-like to those reported without one, mirroring the usage of the primary studies.

This paper is a narrative synthesis, not a systematic review, and its literature base was assembled as follows. The primary comparative studies were identified through searches of Europe PMC (which indexes MEDLINE/PubMed content) combining neardeath experience with terms including near-death-like, psychedelic, DMT, ketamine, meditation, hypnosis, syncope, personality, and fear of death, with no date restriction; through forward and backward citation tracing from the comparisons identified; and through the reference lists of recent integrative reviews (Martial et al., 2025). Studies were treated as primary comparisons if they (a) directly compared individuals reporting NDEs with individuals reporting NDE-like experiences, (b) compared an NDE with another profound altered state within the same person, or (c) elicited NDE-like phenomenology under controlled conditions. Single-case reports were excluded, with the single exception of the unique within-person comparison of Michael et al. (2023), and purely theoretical or clinical-management literature was used for context only. These criteria admitted the direct comparisons and controlled elicitations reviewed in Section 3 regardless of publication date. Among pharmacological triggers, the studies meeting these criteria were, in practice, serotonergic psychedelics: ketamine has been proposed as a pharmacological model of NDEs since the 1980s (Jansen, 1997; Rogo, 1984), but the searches identified no controlled comparison of ketamine-induced states with actual NDErs, so ketamine figures in this review as a candidate paradigm for future research (Section 8.6) rather than as a reviewed comparison.

The remainder of the article proceeds as follows. Section 2 sets out the definitional and measurement foundations; Section 3 reviews the phenomenological comparisons across etiologies; Sections 4 through 6 examine candidate discriminating features, psychological correlates, and aftereffects; Section 7 develops a methodological critique; Section 8 considers what the evidence implies, and does not imply, for theoretical interpretation, and closes with discriminating predictions and research designs; and Section 9 offers concluding reflections.

2. Definitional and Measurement Foundations

>Definitional and Measurement Foundations

2.1 A Contested Distinction

The concept of an NDE emerged from reports by resuscitated and comatose patients, and only later were instruments created to identify and grade the experiences. The resulting risk of circularity has been recognized within the field. On the basis of a case report of a near-death-like experience without any life-threatening condition, Facco and Agrillo (2012) 2018) pointed out that no instrument had been designed for the meditation-induced experiences they studied.

2.2 The Greyson NDE Scale

The most widely used instrument is the NDE Scale developed by Greyson (1983).

It comprises 16 items grouped into cognitive, affective, paranormal, and transcendental subscales, each scored 0 to 2 for a total range of 0 to 32, with a total score of 7 or above conventionally treated as the threshold for an NDE. Two features of its development deserve emphasis in the present context. The scale was constructed from retrospective accounts of self-identified experiencers, a construction sample of 67 participants reporting 73 NDEs (as summarized in Timmermann et al., 2018), whose proximity to death was heterogeneous and, in many cases, not medically documented. The Greyson scale was therefore not developed and validated specifically in cardiac-arrest populations, contrary to a common implicit assumption in this literature; it is a scale for grading the content of experiences that people already report as near-death, whatever their context. Its crosscontext validity, the question of whether a given score means the same thing in a cardiacarrest survivor, a meditator, and a psychedelic user, was not established at the outset and has not been systematically established since.

The psychometric picture is, however, more differentiated than a simple deficit. In a Rasch scaling validation, Lange, Greyson, and Houran (2004) showed that the NDE Scale satisfactorily fits a unidimensional rating-scale model with interval-level properties, with item semantics invariant across experiencers' gender, current age, age at the time of the NDE, and the intensity and elapsed time of the experience; the same authors extended this validation program in subsequent work (Lange et al., 2015), and the resulting hierarchy of increasingly rare features has proved stable over two decades (Pehlivanova et al., 2026). What this work did not do is compare etiologies. It establishes the scale's internal structure and its invariance across demographic and experiential variables, not its equivalence between, say, cardiac-arrest survivors and meditators, which is the crosspopulation question the present literature requires.

2.3 The NDE-C Scale and Recent Validations

The 20-item Near-Death Experience Content (NDE-C) scale, developed and psychometrically validated by Martial et al. (2020), was designed to improve content coverage and reliability relative to earlier instruments; it is scored 0 to 80 with a conventionally used threshold of 27. The NDE-C has since been translated and examined in other populations, including a Chinese translation and validation (Li et al., 2024) that was used in the study of Xue et al. (2026) reviewed below. Most relevant to the clinical aspect of this field, Stripp and Hvidt (2026) evaluated the psychometric properties of a Danish translation of the NDE-C in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest survivors identified through the Danish Cardiac Arrest Register. Of 2,262 invited survivors, 855 responded (37.8%), and 226 participants who reported memories from their cardiac arrest completed the scale. Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach's α = .92), and a confirmatory factor analysis supported the intended five-factor structure conceptually, but the global fit indices were mixed, with most falling below conventional thresholds for good fit (CFI = .858, TLI = .831, RMSEA = .093, SRMR = .062). The authors' own conclusion is that the Danish NDE-C shows strong internal consistency and a preliminary acceptable, although not good, factor structure, and that further psychometric validation is necessary. The NDE-C is thus a promising but still maturing instrument; neither it nor the Greyson scale has undergone the formal multi-group measurement-invariance testing across etiologies that their cross-context use presupposes (see Section 2.2).

2.4 Comparing Scores Across Etiologies

The comparison at the heart of this review inherits these measurement limits. When a cardiac-arrest survivor and a meditator both score above a threshold on the same scale, what has been demonstrated is a partial overlap in the features the scale samples, not phenomenological identity, and not even equivalence in the features sampled unless the scale functions equivalently in both groups (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016). Under the framework of Putnick and Bornstein (2016), comparing observed or latent group means across etiologies presupposes configural invariance (the same factor structure in each group), metric invariance (equal factor loadings), and scalar invariance (equal item intercepts or thresholds), so that a given item endorsement reflects the same level of the latent construct rather than etiology-specific differential item functioning; the Rasch program summarized in Section 2.2 established the first of these requirements in a strong form within single populations, but none of the comparisons reviewed below has tested them across etiological groups. This distinction between threshold-based classification, feature overlap, and demonstrated equivalence recurs throughout the evidence reviewed next.

3. Phenomenological Comparisons Across Etiologies

>Phenomenological Comparisons Across Etiologies

3.1 Clinically Documented Crises Versus Non-Life-Threatening Events

Charland-Verville et al. (2014), in their retrospective survey of 190 self-reported experiencers who scored above the conventional Greyson threshold, found that 140 (73.7%) had undergone a pathological coma of documented anoxic, traumatic, or other etiology, and 50 (26.3%) had lived through a non-life-threatening event. Neither the intensity of the experience (the Greyson total score) nor the frequency of its content features differed between the "real NDE" and "NDE-like" groups. Within the "real NDE" group the content did not vary with the cause of coma. Peacefulness was the most frequently reported feature in all groups (89-93%), and only 2 of 190 participants (about 1%) described a distressing experience. The authors' conclusion was that "it appears that 'real NDEs' after coma of different etiologies are similar to 'NDE-like' experiences occurring after non-life threatening events" (p. 9).

Two further results of the same study qualify this conclusion and are often overlooked. First, the recruitment was retrospective and self-selected, largely through organizations and media devoted to near-death experiences, and the authors explicitly caution that such a sample may not represent the experiencer population, noting in addition the ill-defined nature of the non-life-threatening conditions. Second, when the authors compared their retrospectively recruited anoxic-coma cases with historical data from prospectively studied post-anoxic patients, the retrospectively recruited experiencers reported different content. Retrospective recruitment, in other words, may itself shape what is reported, a point of first importance for a literature built on retrospective surveys.

Memory characteristics tell a convergent story. Cassol et al. (2019) screened 71 experiencers with the Greyson scale, 48 of whom had lived a real NDE in a life-threatening context and 23 of whom reported an NDE-like experience without a life threat. The two subgroups did not differ in the proportion who recalled their experience among their two main self-defining memories (63% and 48%, respectively, a nonsignificant difference), and among the 41 participants who did so, the NDE or NDE-like memory was rated as more central to identity than their other self-defining memories, with the richness of the experience (its Greyson score) associated with that centrality. The authors concluded that the self-defining status of the memory appears to be tied to its phenomenological content rather than to the circumstances in which it arose. Related work on the same question found that the memories of NDEs reported by coma survivors carry more phenomenological characteristics than memories of imagined events, and indeed more than memories of real events, indicating that they are recalled as genuine perceptual memories rather than as fantasies (Thonnard et al., 2013). The narrative structure of NDE accounts themselves, in turn, has been mapped thematically in cardiac-arrest survivors, yielding a sequence of recurring time-bounded features rather than an undifferentiated report (Cassol et al., 2018).

The most recent direct comparison comes from a Chinese online sample (Xue et al., 2026), an exploratory mixed-methods study in which 63 participants entered the quantitative analysis, 23 reporting an NDE, 25 an NDE-like experience, and 15 reporting experiences below the threshold of the Chinese NDE-C scale, and 48 provided narratives for thematic analysis. Narratives from the two groups shared recurrent features: out-ofbody experiences, bright light, bodily sensations, emotional changes, and altered perceptual content. At the same time, NDE narratives showed descriptively higher frequencies of peaceful emotions, personal insights, and panoramic life review, and the single most pronounced group difference concerned encounters: deceased loved ones or spiritual entities appeared in 8 of 23 NDE narratives and in none of the 25 NDE-like narratives, the one difference that remained significant after false-discovery-rate correction in the authors' exploratory tests. Culturally specific Chinese imagery (references to Guimen Pass, Black and White Impermanence, karmic judgment, and related motifs) was uncommon in both groups (4 of 23 and 1 of 25), a difference that likewise did not survive correction. The authors stress that their classification rested entirely on self-report without clinical verification, that the groups were partly defined by their NDE-C responses, and that their between-group tests should be read descriptively rather than as confirmatory. The study therefore supports broad overlap while providing the strongest available, though still exploratory, group-level signal of a content difference, precisely in one of the features discussed in Section 4. et al. (2018) administered intravenous N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) to 13 healthy volunteers in a fixed-order, placebo-controlled, single-blind design, with the Greyson scale as the primary outcome. All 13 participants scored above the NDE threshold after DMT, and the total score was strongly elevated relative to placebo (d = 3.09); 15 of the 16 items were rated higher under DMT, 10 of them significantly so after correction for multiple testing. In a separate comparison with a gender-and age-matched sample of 13 individuals who had reported an actual NDE after a life-threatening episode, the total scores of the two groups were, in the authors' words, comparable (t(12) = 1.85, p = .089, d = 0.49). One participant reached the threshold score of 7 in the placebo condition itself. The comparison is frequently cited as showing that the DMT state and the NDE are statistically indistinguishable; however, a nonsignificant difference between groups of 13 is not evidence of equivalence, no equivalence test was performed, and the design had little power to detect a moderate difference (see Section 7.5). At the item level, the only feature that differed nominally between the two groups is directly relevant here: the threshold item, "Did you come to a border or point of no return?", was scored higher by the actual NDE group than in the DMT condition (t(12) = 2.74, p = .018 uncorrected, d = 1.07), a large effect that did not survive correction for the 16 items tested. What the study does establish is that a psychedelic can reliably elicit experiences that clear the conventional NDE threshold.

3.2 Pharmacological Analogues

Timmermann

A unique within-person case analysis was reported by Michael et al. (2023), who interviewed an individual documented as having had a profound NDE during a coma caused by bacterial meningoencephalitis and, years later, an experience with 5-methoxy-DMT. Thematic comparison showed substantial overlap, especially in the major mystical domains such as ego dissolution and the transcendence of time and space (the NDE also shared many characteristics with psychedelic experiences generally, including entering other worlds, encountering entities, synesthesia, and perinatal regression). Three themes, however, emerged only in the NDE: life review, encountering the deceased, and the threshold or point of no return; the authors note that the NDE included other beings or religious and deceased spirits whereas the 5-methoxy-DMT experience did not, and describe these divergences as potentially the most robust differences between the two states. It is worth adding that the participant himself judged the two experiences not similar enough to attribute the NDE to endogenous psychedelic activity. The authors propose, speculatively and with explicit caveats, that different upstream causes (5-HT2A receptor agonism in the psychedelic case, neocortical damage from meningoencephalitis in the other) could produce similar downstream top-down cortical disinhibition. A single case cannot establish how generally these observations hold, but as a within-person comparison it controls for the reporting style of the experiencer in a way no between-group design can.

The within-person logic was extended to a sample by Martial et al. (2024a), who surveyed 31 adults reporting both an NDE (NDE-C score at or above 27) and a psychedelic experience with lysergic acid diethylamide, psilocybin, ayahuasca, DMT, or mescaline, analyzing the two experiences of each participant with frequentist and Bayesian methods.

They found areas of overlap between the experiences in phenomenology, attribution of reality, psychological insight, and enduring effects, together with differentiating features: most notably, the feeling of dying or being dead and disembodiment were reported more often for NDEs, whereas unusual visual and sensory imagery was more pronounced in psychedelic experiences. Within-person designs of this kind are among the most informative available, because each participant serves as their own control for narrative style and belief.

3.3 Meditation-and Hypnosis-Induced Experiences

Van Gordon et al. (2018) followed 12 advanced Buddhist meditators (two-thirds of them monastics, with an average of roughly 28 years of meditation practice) over three years, comparing a death-related meditation reported to induce NDEs (MI-NDE) against two control practices in the same practitioners. Admission to the MI-NDE condition required a score of 7 or above on the Greyson scale for the most recent such experience, so above-threshold scores were partly guaranteed by the sampling. MI-NDE sessions nonetheless scored significantly higher than both control conditions. Strikingly, the mean Greyson scores of both control conditions themselves exceeded the NDE threshold (standard meditation: M = 7.17, SD = 1.34; standard meditation with death contemplation: M = 8.0, SD = 1.28); ordinary meditative states, in other words, averaged above the conventional cutoff. The embedded qualitative analysis found that participants were consciously aware of experiencing an NDE, retained volitional control over its content and duration, and reported a rich array of non-worldly encounters, alongside features the authors describe as novel relative to ordinary NDE reports. The authors themselves caution that no instrument was designed for meditation-induced experiences and that the NDE scale may lack specificity in this population.

Hypnosis provides a further analogue. In an exploratory study, Machado Ferreira, Farinha, and Simões (2022) examined experiences elicited under hypnotic induction and reported that among the 17 participants whose totals met the criterion of 7 or above on the Greyson scale, the resulting profiles were similar to those of classical near-death experiencers in both content and intensity.

Like the meditation evidence, this finding indicates that a conventional NDEinstrument threshold can be crossed in a state that is neither dangerous nor, in the ordinary sense, near to death; the study's exploratory character and its exclusively scale-based comparison should, however, be kept in view.

Their present evidentiary weight ranges from one exploratory group comparison to one case study plus one uncorrected item-level difference, and they should be treated as targets for prospective research (Section 8.6) rather than as established differentiators.

5. Psychological Correlates

The sharpest quantified difference between the populations concerns not the content of the experiences but the psychology of those who report them. Martial et al.

(2018) compared five groups recruited and screened with the Greyson scale: experiencers whose NDE occurred in a life-threatening context (n = 51), experiencers whose NDE-like experience occurred without one (n = 57), non-experiencers without life threat (n = 20), people who had come close to death without recalling an NDE (n = 50), and healthy controls (n = 50). On the Creative Experiences Questionnaire, a measure of fantasy proneness, the NDE-like group scored significantly higher than the classical NDE group (d = 0.56), than the non-experiencers without life threat (d = 0.83), and than the healthy controls (d = 1.20). Fantasy proneness also correlated with Greyson total scores across the sample as a whole (Spearman ρ = .32).

Two details of these results matter for their interpretation. First, the comparison between the classical NDE group and the people who had faced death without an NDE, a comparison of substantial theoretical interest, was not significant only after Bonferroni correction; uncorrected, the difference had a nominal p of .019 with a medium effect size (d = 0.47), so the finding of no difference should be held loosely. Second, the classical NDE group did not differ from healthy controls in fantasy proneness. The overall pattern, a trait elevation specific to the NDE-like reporters, absent in classical NDE reporters, suggests that fantasy proneness may be involved in who reports NDE-like experiences, or in how such experiences are elaborated and narrated, rather than in near-death phenomenology as such. The authors are explicit that the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference in either direction, that medical documentation of the life-threatening events was lacking, and that fantasy proneness and NDE intensity may partly share item content.

A subsequent multivariate study from the same group complicates this picture.

6. Aftereffects

The largest comparison of enduring effects is the retrospective survey of Sweeney et al. (2022), with 3,192 respondents: 933 who reported a non-drug near-death or other non-ordinary experience, and 2,259 who reported an experience occasioned by a psychedelic (lysergic acid diethylamide, psilocybin, ayahuasca, or DMT). Large majorities of both groups attributed a decrease in fear of death to the experience (88% of the non-drug group and 89% of the psychedelic group), and members of both groups rated the experience as highly personally meaningful and spiritually significant; indeed, participants in the non-drug group were more likely to name it the single most meaningful experience of their lives. Differences between the groups were modest and specific: psychedelic experiencers scored higher on the Greyson scale total (and on the cognitive, affective, and paranormal subscales, whereas on the transcendental subscale the non-drug group scored higher than the psilocybin and LSD groups but lower than the DMT and ayahuasca groups) and were more likely to meet criteria for a complete mystical experience on the Mystical Experiences Questionnaire (55% versus 47%), whereas the non-drug group showed the larger decrease in death avoidance (mean change -2.01 versus -1.43 on the relevant Death Attitude Profile subscale). The shared outcome, a marked and enduring reduction in death fear, accords with a longer survey tradition documenting lasting changes in beliefs, attitudes, and behavior after NDEs (Groth-Marnat & Summers, 1998) and with the finding that NDE memories are highly central to identity (Cassol et al., 2019). These are retrospective self-reports of change, gathered from self-selected online samples and rated before-and-after in a single sitting, so they describe experienced transformation rather than measured trajectories; but the convergence across drug and non-drug groups, and with the smaller prospective literature, is nonetheless striking.

7. Methodological Critique

>Methodological Critique

7.1 Definitional Validity and Circular Classification

The NDE/NDE-like distinction is contested at its definitional root. The label NDE predates its measurement, and the instruments that now operationalize it were built to grade experiences that people already called near-death. As Facco and Agrillo (2012) observed, other transcendent experiences may rate highly on those same instruments. The circularity is not merely abstract. In several of the studies reviewed above, the groups compared were partly defined by the very scores that were then compared. Xue et al.

(2026) classified their groups partly by NDE-C responses and accordingly interpreted between-group scale differences descriptively rather than as independent evidence, and Van Gordon et al. ( 2018) admitted meditators to their NDE condition only if they had already scored above the Greyson threshold. In such designs, threshold comparisons are partly built into the sampling, and content comparisons inherit the scale's own definition of the phenomenon.

7.2 Instrument Development and Population Validity

A premise frequently taken for granted in this literature, that the standard instruments were developed and validated in cardiac-arrest populations and are therefore being extrapolated when applied elsewhere, does not survive scrutiny. The Greyson scale was constructed from retrospective, self-identified accounts of heterogeneous proximity to death (Greyson, 1983), and the NDE-C, although psychometrically stronger, was likewise developed outside any cardiac-arrest-specific sampling frame (Martial et al., 2020). The first psychometric evaluation of an NDE instrument in a medically well-defined cardiacarrest cohort, the Danish NDE-C study of Stripp and Hvidt (2026), is recent, was achieved in translation, and returned a mixed factor structure whose authors call for further validation. In short, the field's measurement was not built for, and has not been verified in, the clinical anchor population against which NDE-like experiences are being compared.

What cross-etiology comparisons currently possess is two instruments whose scores have never been shown to function equivalently across the populations being compared: the Rasch validation program summarized in Section 2.2 established strong internal structure and demographic invariance for the Greyson scale (Lange et al., 2004; Pehlivanova et al., 2026), but no multi-group test of configural, metric, or scalar invariance across etiologies exists for either instrument.

7.5 Inference Standards: Absence of Evidence, No Difference, and Equivalence

The most consequential inferential habit in this literature is the reading of nonsignificant group differences as evidence of similarity. Absence of a significant difference is not evidence of no difference, and evidence of no difference is not evidence of equivalence. The first is related to statistical power, the second requires an interval estimate around a null, and only the third, the demonstration that effects fall within prespecified equivalence bounds, can support a claim that two states are comparable in a measured respect (Lakens, 2017). No classical-comparison study in this literature performed an equivalence test; the earliest Bayesian treatment of overlap appears in the within-person study of Martial et al. (2024a). The oft-quoted statistical indistinguishability of DMT-induced and actual NDE profiles (Timmermann et al., 2018) rests on a p of .089 with 13 participants per group, an uninformative result for the equivalence question. Until comparisons are designed with equivalence bounds and the power to bind them, the defensible summary of the phenomenological literature is that no adequately powered study has found a difference in global scores, not that the states have been shown to be equivalent.

7.6 What the Current Evidence Supports

With these standards applied, three conclusions about the substantive questions can be graded. On psychological predispositions, the evidence is tentative and internally complex: two cross-sectional studies from the same group link different combinations of traits and beliefs to the two kinds of reporting, with fantasy proneness tied to NDE-like reporting in univariate analysis (Martial et al., 2018) but to classical NDE recall, alongside Openness, in multivariate analysis, and with spiritual beliefs carrying the NDE-like association (Bicego et al., 2023); all of it is retrospective, without medical verification or causal direction, and includes one nominally significant comparison concealed by correction. On aftereffects, the evidence is convergent but retrospective: reduced fear of death and high felt meaningfulness follow both NDE-like and drug-induced experiences (Sweeney et al., 2022). On the etiological independence of phenomenology, the claim that content does not depend on the trigger, the evidence is insufficient as a strong claim. What has been shown is a partial overlap under heterogeneous instruments without invariance testing, with at least one exploratory group difference (encounters with the deceased), one uncorrected item-level difference (the threshold), and one within-person case (life review, deceased, threshold) pointing the other way. The honest position is that etiological independence is an open empirical question, not an established finding.

8. Theoretical Interpretations

>Theoretical Interpretations

8.1 Empirical Constraints on Any Account

A theory of NDEs that also claims to account for NDE-like experiences should be consistent with at least the following observations, each of which has the evidential limits described above.

 A core set of features, with peacefulness most frequent, recurs across contexts, and total scale scores are comparable between NDEs and spontaneous NDElike experiences in self-selected samples (Charland-Verville et al., 2014; Martial et al., 2020).

 Overlap is partial and feature-dependent. Border and gateway features separate NDEs and spontaneous NDE-like experiences from induced states; deceased encounters and life review show inconsistent group differences (Table 2).

 Scale-defined NDE-like experiences can be induced by drugs, scripted hypnosis, and practiced meditation, but the induced profiles differ in specific items and in retained control and awareness (Timmermann et al., 2018; Van Gordon et al., 2018; Ferreira et al., 2022).

 Trait variables such as fantasy proneness, absorption, dissociation, and REMintrusion propensity are associated with reporting, with a stronger signal for NDE-like experiences in one study (Martial et al., 2018a); causality is unresolved.

 Culturally specific imagery occurs in a minority of narratives, alongside recurrent core features (Xue et al., 2026).

 Experiences are recalled as vivid and self-defining, and the retrospective or prospective mode of assessment changes feature frequencies (Cassol et al., 2019a; Charland-Verville et al., 2014).

 Distressing experiences are rare in Greyson-scale samples (2 of 190 in Charland-Verville et al., 2014), but the scale contains no item on negative emotion, and about 14% of accounts have been described as distressing in other analyses (Cassol et al., 2019b , as cited in Martial et al., 2020).

8.2 Proximate Neurophysiological Mechanisms

The most developed integrative proposal is the model of Martial et al. (2025), the Neurophysiological Evolutionary Psychological Theory Understanding Near-Death Experience (NEPTUNE), which frames the NDE as a cascade of concomitant psychological and neurophysiological processes within an evolutionary framework. At the neurophysiological level, the model holds that core features can result from impaired cerebral blood flow causing systemic hypotension, hypoxia, and hypercapnia resulting in acidosis, and from increased neuronal excitability causing dysregulation of key neurotransmitter systems (Martial et al., 2025, p. 298). It is essential to read this model correctly in the present context. NEPTUNE is explicitly pitched at near-death experiences in the broad sense of experiences arising in situations that involve an actual or potential physical threat, or that are perceived as such. It is therefore not an arrest-specific account that cross-trigger similarity would refute; it is precisely a convergent account, one in which multiple physiological routes funnel into shared downstream processes. For the taxonomy of Section 1, this also means that NEPTUNE, as a model of actual, potential, and perceived threats, spans the very boundary this review draws between NDE and NDE-like experiences, and it predicts that perceived-threat experiences should share the phenomenology of actual-threat ones, a testable position. Greyson and Pehlivanova (2025), in a peer-reviewed commentary, argue that NEPTUNE and neurophysiological accounts more broadly leave important facets of NDEs unexplained; their critique identifies where the model is incomplete rather than where cross-trigger data contradict it.

Several more specific mechanisms populate the field. The REM-intrusion hypothesis proposes that NDEs arise when rapid-eye-movement sleep physiology intrudes into wakefulness through an unstable arousal system (Nelson et al., 2006). In a crowdsourced study of 1,034 respondents, self-reported REM intrusion was indeed more common among those reporting NDEs (47%) than among sub-threshold experiencers (26%) or non-experiencers (14%), a correlational but substantial difference (Kondziella et al., 2019). Lesion and stimulation evidence locates the out-of-body component in the temporoparietal junction (Blanke et al., 2004). Psychedelic findings demonstrate pharmacological elicitation of threshold-clearing experiences (Timmermann et al., 2018), and the within-person case of Michael et al. (2023) offers a speculative sketch of convergence, with top-down cortical disinhibition reached by different upstream causes; that paper also discusses predictive-processing frameworks, such as the relaxed-beliefs account of psychedelic action, as a common vocabulary for such convergence.

The speculative hypothesis that endogenous DMT is released in quantity at death remains unconfirmed.

What does the cross-trigger phenomenological overlap imply for these models? It constrains, rather than refutes, neurophysiological explanation. It rules out explanations that require a single life-threatening trigger, cerebral hypoxia alone, or cardiac arrest alone, as sufficient causes of the phenomenology, because the phenomenology demonstrably also occurs without them. But it is exactly what convergent models anticipate: if disparate triggers share downstream targets, a disinhibited cortex, an unstable arousal boundary, perturbed self-processing at the temporoparietal junction, then phenomenological similarity across triggers is a prediction, not an anomaly, of the neurophysiological family of accounts.

8.3 Psychological Construction and Function

Psychological accounts explain the same constraints differently. Expectancy and cultural-schema approaches predict that content should be assembled from available cultural materials and should vary with them; the finding that culturally specific imagery is uncommon yet interpretively salient in Chinese narratives (Xue et al., 2026) is consistent with a construction in which culture shapes the reading of the experience more than its raw ingredients. Memory-reconstruction approaches emphasize that retrospective reports are shaped at recall, and are supported by the content differences between retrospectively and prospectively recruited experiencers (Charland-Verville et al., 2014). Depersonalizationtype responses to threat, derealization, altered time sense, detachment, have been documented since the classic psychiatric literature on life-threatening danger (Noyes & Kletti, 1977) and plausibly supply early scaffolding for the experience. Trait accounts are sharpened, and complicated, by the personality and belief correlates of Martial et al. (2018) and Bicego et al. (2023). If NDE-like recall is tied to spiritual beliefs, and classical NDE recall to imaginative and openness traits, then some reports may partly reflect a general imaginative or believing style, or a reporting culture around extraordinary experiences, rather than the circumstances of the experiences themselves. Finally, King (2025) proposes a self-determination account: the prototypical features of the NDE, the encounter with loving presences, the sense of being needed, the choice to return, can be read as satisfying the basic psychological needs of relatedness, competence, and autonomy, which would explain the experiences' enduring motivational aftereffects. This is a functional reading that generates testable content predictions (for example, that encounter narratives should cluster around acceptance, belonging, and choice) and is best treated as a productive hypothesis rather than an established explanation. et al. (2021) conducted a preregistered systematic investigation of the hypothesis that thanatosis, the passive, death-feigning defense behavior widespread across animal taxa, is the evolutionary foundation of NDEs, arguing that the two overlap in triggers, phenomenology, and effects, and that human language transformed an ancient threat response into the rich perceptions now reported, extending them beyond predatory contexts. The proposal is noteworthy for its method and its cross-species evidence base, but it is explicitly a line-of-evidence argument for a hypothesis, not a demonstration, and it is not established that a behavior preserved for predator escape explains experiences arising in meditation, hypnosis, or syncope. NEPTUNE likewise embeds its mechanisms in an evolutionary framework (Martial et al., 2025). Evolutionary accounts of this kind are compatible with the convergence logic above and inherit the same burden: to explain not only threat-triggered cases but the benign-trigger ones, and to generate discriminating predictions (for example, about the contexts and populations in which NDE-like phenomenology should appear) rather than retrospective fit.

8.5 Metaphysical Interpretations

Some authors read the evidence of cardiac-arrest NDEs as indicating that consciousness can persist and function when the brain, by available measures, cannot, and hence that NDEs mark the separability of mind from brain (e.g., Angeli-Faez et al., 2025); others argue that nothing about NDEs is beyond the reach of established neuroscience (e.g., Mobbs & Watt, 2011). The evidence reviewed here does not adjudicate between these positions, and it is important to be clear about why. Cross-trigger phenomenological similarity is equally consistent with shared brain mechanisms reached by different routes and with a non-physicalist account on which the brain is one route among several by which the same kind of experience becomes accessible; the data underdetermine the ontology.

What can be said is methodological: a metaphysical interpretation acquires scientific standing exactly when it generates independently testable predictions, most obviously, prospective tests of veridical perception under physiologically documented conditions, with preregistered targets and blinded coding. No completed test with verified target hits is part of the present evidence base. The two prospective studies that embedded such tests during resuscitation, AWARE I and AWARE II, yielded no verified identification of the hidden visual targets; in AWARE II, no participant identified the visual image and only one of 28 interviewed survivors identified the auditory stimulus, with low survival and the rarity of reportable experiences limiting the tests (Parnia et al., 2014 (Parnia et al., , 2023)). Survival or transmission interpretations should therefore be treated in a scientific context as philosophical possibilities held to that standard, not as findings.

9. Final Reflections

The evidence reviewed supports a limited but substantive conclusion: in selfselected, retrospective samples, people who report spontaneous NDE-like experiences describe content and overall intensity that closely resemble those of people who report NDEs after life threat, and scale-defined NDE-like experiences can be induced. It does not support the stronger conclusion that the two kinds of experience are phenomenologically identical, because nonsignificant differences were not tested for equivalence, group classification was unverified, samples were small and partly non-independent, and the instruments were not shown to be invariant across contexts. Scientific conclusions and philosophical implications should be kept distinct. The reviewed evidence constrains theories: any account must explain overlap, candidate discriminators, trait-and-belief differences, memory quality, and aftereffects at once, and purely trigger-specific neurophysiological stories are, on current evidence, insufficient. But the same evidence does not select between a completed neurophysiological account, a psychological-construction account, and a metaphysical account of consciousness; that selection awaits discriminating, preregistered tests of the kind sketched above, and it may not be fully resolvable by phenomenological comparison at all.

The priorities follow directly: prospective recruitment through medical registers with documented crises and matched comparison groups; preregistration of hypotheses, feature definitions, and analysis plans (Nosek et al., 2018); cross-cultural sampling with culturally validated instruments; psychometric revalidation of the existing scales and formal measurement-invariance testing across etiologies; blinded coding of narratives; adequately powered comparisons with explicit equivalence tests; and prospective longitudinal measurement of aftereffects.

If the three candidate features survive that program, the NDE/NDE-like distinction acquires empirical content; if they do not, the distinction will have been shown to be, in phenomenological terms, an artifact of our categories rather than a fact about the experiences. Either result would be a genuine advance over the present state, in which the field's central comparison remains, for the most part, untested at the standard it now knows how to meet.
