Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Full text

Abstract

Over ten years ago, we published the first data collected with Virtual Silcton, a desktop virtual environment that enabled objective measurement of navigation behavior in the lab.

Virtual Silcton has since been used in dozens of studies by multiple lab groups, across a wide range of participants. While measuring navigation behavior with virtual environments has never been easier, what have we been measuring, exactly? In this focused review, we outline the history and context of navigation research that led to the development of Virtual Silcton, then propose a framework for Virtual Environment Paradigms -the environment, learning procedure, and tasks which are often grouped into one package -of which Virtual Silcton is a prominent example. In addition to reviewing the ways that Virtual Silcton has been used and key findings from that paradigm, our aim is helping researchers determine what type of assessment best suits their purpose. We conclude by discussing challenges to measuring navigation behavior virtually in the future, as well as discovering opportunities for translation.

Introduction

Spatial navigation is a fundamental human activity that enables people to safely and efficiently move and engage with their environment. Given its importance in daily life, it is critical to understand spatial navigation and the underlying cognitive mechanisms that support it, whether they function properly or not. Understanding spatial navigation can help improve navigation support (e.g., signs or maps), guide the development of targeted training interventions, and provide insights into cognitive health (e.g., the early detection of cognitive impairment). Spatial navigation, which we define as first-person movement (or simulated movement) with the goal of reaching a destination beyond what can be immediately perceived, varies widely within and across people and environments. Some people excel at navigating, while others frequently become disoriented or lost. Likewise, some environments offer rich features that support orientation, while others with complex layouts, few distinctive features, or weak and inconsistent signage make navigation considerably more difficult (e.g., a dense jungle, or a congested, irregular city). Understanding the cognitive mechanisms responsible for why some people frequently get lost and why many people get lost in particular places is therefore both an important aim and a significant challenge.

With such distinct sources of variability, developing a way to control the navigation experience as closely as possible is critical, especially in studying individual differences. But unlike tasks designed for working memory or language, tasks situated in environments large enough to study spatial navigation are not portable, making replication across geographic locations impossible. Moreover, the same navigation task can be solved differently by different people, at different times, or in different environments. Even when the same environment is used, familiarity remains difficult to control because individuals often differ in their prior experience with real-world environments.

Over the last 30 years, these problems have been somewhat, though not entirely, overcome by the use of virtual reality, which can simulate large-scale environments on desktop computers or other more immersive presentation devices (e.g., Cave automatic virtual environment (CAVE) systems, Meta Quest, HTC Vive, Oculus). In that time, virtual reality has been increasingly used to measure spatial navigation behavior reliably across people and across environments. But with wider adoption of virtual reality came more variable ways to measure spatial navigation. Over ten years ago, we developed one prominent desktop-based virtual reality tool to measure navigation behavior: Virtual SILCTON (Spatial Intelligence and Learning Center Test of Navigation, hereafter: Virtual Silcton). We continue to maintain Virtual Silcton as a resource for the community, but it sits within a much richer landscape of virtual reality research than it did when it was developed. In the current manuscript, our primary goal is to contextualize the varying usages of virtual reality to the measure of spatial navigation.

We introduce the term Virtual Environment Paradigm to refer to the specific combination of a research protocol with a virtual environment. The environment is the set of sensory stimuli used to create a simulacrum of reality, whereas the research protocol specifies the learning procedure (i.e., how the environment is learned) and the tasks (i.e., how the participant's knowledge is tested). The term Virtual Environment Paradigm is useful because it distinguishes a virtual environment, which can be learned in any number of ways, from a research protocol, which can occur in any environment, while acknowledging that particular research protocols tend to occur in the same environment. Historically, because developing virtual environments is challenging and labour-intensive, Virtual Environment Paradigms have become popular because, as the virtual environment was shared, it tended to be shared along with the research protocol (including the scripts to program the tasks, participant instructions, and analysis code).

Critically, without such an organizing framework, the field has produced research tools whose implementations vary enough to make spatial behavior difficult to compare and replicate across studies. The virtual Morris Water Maze illustrates the challenge of establishing these connections retrospectively. Thornberry and colleagues (2026) documented substantial variation across implementations in the environment, learning procedures and task, despite their common identification as versions of the same paradigm. As a result, what appears to be a single Virtual Environment Paradigm has effectively become a family of related but non-equivalent assays of spatial behavior. Their effort to establish standardized procedures and reporting practices illustrates both the value of standardization and the difficulty of imposing it after a paradigm has already spread across laboratories.

The proliferation of Virtual Environment Paradigms has been a boon to spatial navigation research, particularly for individual differences experiments that require large sample sizes. But much of what is known about spatial navigation behavior arises from a time before these tools existed. Before describing our framework, we first look at the way that spatial navigation behavior has been measured historically and what Virtual Environment Paradigms looked like prior to Virtual Silcton.

A history of individual differences research in spatial navigation ability

The first evidence, beyond anecdotal reports, that spatial navigation ability varied widely came from studies asking people to draw sketch maps of environments they were already familiar with. In a seminal study building directly on Lynch (1996) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?6hfu3B Appleyard (1970) found striking differences in accuracy and in how participants structured the same space, distinguishing between sequential maps dominated by roads and connections and spatial maps organised around landmarks and districts. Importantly, he showed that these differences were associated with factors such as education, familiarity, occupation, sex, and mode of travel. Similarly, Murray and Spencer (1979) showed that individual differences in mapping were associated with factors ranging from geographical mobility and mental imagery to basic graphic ability, already foreshadowing the challenges in separating navigation ability from the methods used to measure it. Alongside these empirical findings, developmental accounts of spatial representation were being formalised by Hart and Moore (1973) and, more influentially, by Siegel and White (1975) who proposed that individuals acquire environmental information by progressing through qualitatively different stages of landmark, route and survey/configurational knowledge.

Building on this foundation, researchers in the 1980s and 1990s tested how spatial navigation was acquired in different environments using increasingly controlled and varied methods. Studies recruiting both children and adults ranged from those examining spatial learning through direct experience in real buildings and unfamiliar neighborhood environments to those using more controlled presentation of routes using maps, sequential photographs, slides or video (Gale et al., 1990; Gärling et al., 1981; Golledge et al., 1993; Hirtle & Hudson, 1991; Moar & Carleton, 1982; Thorndyke & Hayes-Roth, 1982). Across these studies, spatial knowledge acquisition was assessed through a combination of methods, including landmark recognition, pointing judgments, distance estimates, route sequencing or reproduction, and sketch maps. During this period, a particularly important line of work examined the capacity of individuals to integrate knowledge acquired through individual routes into a broader representation of an unfamiliar environment (Gale et al., 1990; Golledge et al., 1993).

During this period, researchers also began probing more directly into the cognitive basis behind individual differences. For example, Thorndyke and Stasz (1980) found that differences in good and poor map learners could be explained by the manner in which they directed attention, encoded and monitored their own learning. Pearson and Ialongo (1986) addressed this relationship more directly by showing that performance on psychometric measures (e.g., embedded figures test and mental rotation), although moderately related, tended to load on a separate factor from landmark and route knowledge. In a large study (n = 1148), Bryant (1991) further showed that this relationship may also depend on how spatial knowledge is acquired.

Using the embedded figure test, they showed that visual-spatial ability predicted spatial orientation after learning the environment from a filmed version of the route, but not after a direct auto tour. Following this line of investigation, Allen and colleagues (1996) provided additional evidence that the relationship between general spatial ability and the acquisition of environmental knowledge depends on more specific processes, such as spatial sequential memory and perspective taking.

In parallel with these studies, researchers were also investigating whether people had insights into their own navigational abilities (Bryant, 1982; Kozlowski & Bryant, 1977). Kozlowski and Bryant (1977) found that after repeated exposure to a novel maze (labyrinthine tunnels beneath Wesleyan University dormitories), participants reporting a good sense of direction became increasingly accurate at pointing judgments, whereas those reporting a poor sense of direction showed no improvement. Interestingly, while their questionnaire also included questions about environmental exploration, giving and following instructions, and route memory, sense of direction measured through a single question (i.e., "How good is your sense of direction?") was found to be highly reliable and predictive of pointing accuracy to location in a familiar environment. Sholl (1988) connected this work back to the psychometric literature, finding that individuals with a poor self-reported sense of direction can perform just as well as those with a good sense of direction on conventional spatial tasks (e.g., mental rotation, paper folding), but perform worse in tasks that require them to mentally update their orientation relative to the environment. In a landmark publication, Hegarty and colleagues (2002) synthesised this work into the Santa Barbara Sense of Direction Scale (SBSOD). Across a series of validation studies, they showed that a 15-item scale was highly reliable and was most strongly related to tasks requiring spatial updating and orientation (particularly pointing to unseen landmarks and knowledge acquired through direct navigation), but only weakly related to conventional psychometric measures such as embedded figures and mental rotation. Although not an objective measure of navigation behaviour, the field now had a robust and reliable measure of selfreported navigation ability that could be systematically compared with objective behaviour measures.

Meanwhile, advances in computer graphics were changing how spatial navigation could be studied. By the mid-1990s, virtual environments with varying degrees of interaction and realism were becoming increasingly popular. For the study of individual differences, this meant that researchers could expose participants to a growing number of more realistic environments while systematically controlling the information available to them. Early studies used these environments to revisit questions about the role of landmarks on route learning. For example, Tlauka and Wilson (1994) found that landmarks can facilitate route learning but only when competing verbal strategies were disrupted. Peruch and colleagues (1995) showed that participants were more accurate when shortcutting to specified targets following active rather than passive exploration, while May and colleagues (1995) found that increased misalignment between a learned map and the environment progressively impaired speed and accuracy. Other studies investigated whether spatial knowledge acquired virtually transferred to the corresponding real environment and found that rehearsal of a complex route could transfer to real-world performance, although less effectively than direct rehearsal and with mixed evidence regarding the transfer of survey/configurational knowledge (Bailey & Witmer, 1994; Wilson et al., 1997; Witmer et al., 1996).

With virtual environments providing tighter control over participants' experiences, researchers could also begin to separate the environment's effects from differences among navigators. For example, Darken and Sibert (1996) showed that available navigation aids influenced the strategies participants used to search large virtual environments. Ruddle and colleagues (1997) found large individual differences in how quickly individuals learned the same virtual building (despite everyone improving with training), and this variation was associated with their knowledge of the direction and distance between locations. Similarly, Gillner and Mallot (1998) reported large differences in search errors and distance estimates, with better navigators capable of producing more accurate sketch maps and showing greater transfer across routes . Importantly, some variation in performance could be explained by the mode of interaction itself, with spatial updating becoming more accurate when participants physically walked through the virtual environment than when rotation and translation were visually provided (Chance et al., 1998).

By the turn of the century, the question of whether virtual environments could be used to study the acquisition of environmental knowledge was supplanted with the question of why individuals differed so markedly in their navigation abilities. This period witnessed a renewed attention to familiar sources of variation, including age, sex, cognitive abilities, and navigation strategy (Astur et al., 2004; Cutmore et al., 2000; Kraemer et al., 2017; Moffat et al., 2001; Moffat & Resnick, 2002; Sandstrom et al., 1998) in addition to affective and personality factors (Pazzaglia et al., 2018; Sas et al., 2004; Walkowiak et al., 2015). At the same time, it became increasingly evident that performance in these virtual environments reflected the interaction between these individual factors and the actual technology. Proficiency with the virtual interface and general computer and gaming experience (Richardson et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?o4D3ep 2011; Wallerhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?QHmVG8 2000; Waller et al., 2001) , as well as presence and simulator sickness (Lampton et al., 2000; Suma et al., 2010) , were all shown to affect spatial learning. In time, virtual navigation also became compatible with PET, fMRI, and EEG, allowing differences in navigation accuracy, strategy, and sex, among other variables, to be related to differences in neural activity (Grön et al., 2000; Hartley et al., 2003; Iaria et al., 2003; Kober & Neuper, 2011; Maguire et al., 1998). Together, this work made clear that individual differences observed in virtual navigation reflected not only the navigator but also the particular environment, cues, and interface through which navigation was measured.

Emerging from these decades of research, culminating in widespread adoption of virtual environments for studying spatial navigation behavior, was a proliferation of Virtual Environment Paradigms that rarely extended beyond a single research group or even a single experiment. Researchers typically built environments for a specific study, used them once, then abandoned them. In the next phase of research on spatial navigation, researchers began merging and sharing virtual environments with the procedures used to learn them and to test participants' knowledge. As the uptake of virtual environments increased, the difficulty and time required to develop them became even more costly. Within that context, rather than designing their own tools, researchers could adopt existing tools that combine a particular virtual environment with the protocol (e.g., code) used to administer it. The story of the development and subsequent uptake of Virtual Silcton is, in part, due to the historical context in which virtual environments were difficult and time-intensive to build, thereby locking a particular environmental context to an experimental paradigm.

A History of Virtual Silcton

The origins of Virtual Silcton can be traced to studies by Ishikawa and Montello (2006) and Schinazi and colleagues (2013) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dCBLWj which examined how individuals acquire spatial knowledge through repeated experience in unfamiliar environments modelled after experiments using the Route Integration Paradigm by Hanley and Levine (1983). After Hanley and Levine (1993) showed that learning to connect simple routes was more effortful than learning them on their own, Golledge and colleagues (1993) showed that even relatively strong knowledge of individual routes did not necessarily translate into an integrated representation of the wider environment, though their study exposed participants to routes via a slide projector. Ishikawa and Montello (2006) examined acquisition of spatial knowledge in the route integration paradigm over time by driving participants along routes to learn landmarks along two separate routes over multiple weeks and later asked them to estimate distances and directions and to draw sketch maps. Critically, the experiment was designed to assess individual differences in how people move beyond learning routes in isolation to integrate them into a coherent, flexible representation of the environment. While these studies showed substantial individual differences in the acquisition of spatial knowledge, some participants integrated the two routes after minimal exposure. These findings challenged the dominant view Siegel and White (1975) that spatial learning proceeds through qualitatively distinct stages, progressing strictly from landmark to route to survey knowledge. Schinazi and colleagues (2013) built directly on this paradigm, but with two important differences. First, rather than being passively driven or shown slides, participants travelled on foot while following an experimenter by walking the grounds of an unfamiliar university campus (Temple's Ambler campus, though it has since undergone major renovations). Disorienting subjects involved laboriously porting them around on a wheelchair before starting the next route.

Beyond locomotion, which provides proprioceptive and vestibular information known to support spatial updating, moving through the environment allowed participants to remain engaged with the task and to allocate their attention more consistently to the task (c.f., (Chrastil & Warren, 2013). Second, the researchers introduced a neuroscientific dimension by showing that the ability to rapidly form an accurate representation of a novel environment, as assessed by direction judgments between landmarks along the routes, was associated with larger hippocampal volume.

However, the very features that made studies by Ishikawa and Montello (2006) and Schinazi and colleagues (2013) powerful also imposed substantial constraints. While these realworld studies enhanced ecological precision, they sacrificed experimental control because exposure could not always be matched, environmental conditions varied, and precise behavioural measurements (e.g., compass pointing) were difficult to obtain. Real-world studies are also timeintensive, often requiring repeated sessions over weeks, which makes them prone to attrition and difficult to standardise across participants. Notably, scaling real-world paradigms across laboratories or populations is impractical, if not impossible.

Virtual Silcton emerged in response to these challenges at a time when there was a lack of accessible, ecologically grounded, realistic virtual environments for assessing individual differences in navigation ability. By recreating the structure of the real-world task within a controlled virtual environment, Virtual Silcton sought to preserve the theoretical core of the route integration paradigm while gaining more precise control over exposure and behavioural data logging. In doing so, Virtual Silcton enabled reproducibility across sites and the testing of larger, more diverse samples. Of course, this transition came at a cost, as the shift to desktop virtual reality altered the sensory and embodied cues available during learning, forcing participants to rely on vection rather than proprioception.

Virtual Silcton also filled a critical gap in spatial navigation measurement. Previously, the only way to reproducibly measure spatial navigation behavior was to use self-report methods.

Other navigation measures were either custom-built and thus not replicating previous work, or were based on real-world environments. Thus, alongside technical assistance from Drew Dara-Abrams and and support of Nora Newcombe and the Spatial Intelligence and Learning Center, we designed a Virtual Environment Paradigm with the intent to enable easy reproducibility and accessibility. Virtual Silcton was developed to be embedded in a web platform that allowed for lab-specific partitions and study-specific data collection. Rather than relying on informal sharing or downloadable files, Virtual Silcton allowed researchers to use precisely the same environment, paradigm, scripts, data collection procedures, and eventually data analysis tools.

For the first time, it was possible for researchers whose primary focus was not spatial navigation to administer an objective measure of spatial navigation behavior.

Preserving the route integration paradigm at its core, the website version of Virtual

Silcton is most typically administered using the same standard procedure, though some variation has also occurred. The standard procedure is as follows: two main routes are learned (order randomized across participants) by traversing a path marked by red arrows. Each route contains four target buildings, signaled with blue gems hovering along the route and named with signs alongside the path. Participants are instructed to learn the names and locations of each of the eight buildings with respect to all other buildings and told that they will be asked to complete a series of tasks afterwards. Participants may travel at their own pace to go from the beginning to the end and back to the beginning of each route. They are restricted to traveling along the marked routes; should they stray, their progress is impeded by invisible walls. After learning the two main routes, participants learn two connecting routes (also in a randomized order) to understand how the main routes are positioned relative to each other.

In Virtual Silcton, participants typically complete two tasks. In an onsite pointing task, participants are placed next to each building they learned and must point an onscreen crosshair at each of the other seven buildings. Proceeding in route order, participants are then teleported to the next building, where they have to point to all the buildings again and so on for all eight buildings. We measure pointing error -how accurately participants could point to each building.

Trials in the pointing task can be divided into "within-route" and "between-route" trials. These labels indicate the relation between the building the participant is standing next to and the building they must point to: whether those two buildings are within the same main route or between the two main routes. In a model-building task, participants must place overhead views of each of the eight buildings around a rectangle to indicate their positions in the actual environment. We measure the accuracy of building configurations using bidimensional regression (Friedman & Kohler, 2003; Tobler, 1994).

Over the past 12 years, Virtual Silcton has been used in 31 peer-reviewed publications and 11 undergraduate, master's, and doctoral theses, with many additional studies citing the paradigm or reviewing its findings. Perhaps the most consistent finding across Virtual Silcton studies is that learning individual routes and integrating them into a flexible spatial representation are dissociable, with the latter being the more demanding and perhaps spatial-specific component of navigation ability. This work has established the influential distinction between integrators, non-integrators and imprecise navigators, showing that some individuals can form accurate global representations, whereas others remain limited to local route knowledge or perform poorly overall (Weisberg et al., 2014; Weisberg & Newcombe, 2016). These differences, however, cannot be simply explained by a single factor. Better performance in Virtual Silcton is consistently associated with spatial or visuospatial working memory (Blacker et al., 2017; Keller & Sutton, 2022; Weisberg & Newcombe, 2016 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zDmbX8 Bushinski & Redick (2025)). Perspective taking ability has also emerged as an important predictor of Virtual Silcton performance in both children and adults, often more strongly than mental rotation (Cocquyt et al., 2022; Keller & Sutton, 2022; Lader et al., 2025; Nazareth et al., 2018; Ruginski et al., 2019; Weisberg et al., 2014; Youngson et al., 2019) Curiously, providing additional spatial information during learning does not reliably improve navigation performance in Virtual Silcton. Pre-exposure to landmarks along the routes did not improve performance (Youngson et al.https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?03yzqX 2019) , and neither self-generated maps, externally-provided accurate maps, nor verbal descriptions improved pointing performance, although maps did support better map reconstruction (Jaeger et al., 2023). Similarly, providing participants with global directional cues (e.g., a compass or a salient mountain range) in immersive virtual reality does not improve pointing or map building performance in Virtual Silcton, despite participants successfully encoding the cue direction (Yüksel et al., 2025).

In contrasthttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?EUiiJY studies examining the characteristics of the environment itself have shown that environmental structure, quantified using space syntax (Hillier & Hanson, 1984) , plays an important role in spatial learning. Using the standard Virtual Silcton paradigm, Pagkratidou and colleagues (2020) found that participants were more accurate when pointing to locations with higher integration, connectivity and visual accessibility scores. Building on this work, https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?m7f8en asked participants to freely explore Virtual Silcton and found that those who spent more time exploring regions with high connectivity scores performed better in the pointing and mapping tasks. Simply exploring a larger proportion of the environment was not correlated with better performance.

Research using Virtual Silcton has yielded important insights into factors that shape navigation ability across the lifespan. In studies with cross-sectional and longitudinal data, accuracy on the pointing task was found to improve throughout childhood, with within-route knowledge developing ahead of route integration and navigation abilities reaching adult levels by 12 years of age (Brucato et al., 2023; Nazareth et al., 2018). Interestingly, neither perspective taking, mental rotation, nor parental spatial ability predicted developmental gains over time (Brucato et al., 2023). Findings on sex differences have proven more nuanced and tend to support the view that observed differences are influenced more by task characteristics and participant cohort than by intrinsic differences in navigation ability. Though many Virtual Silcton studies report sex differences, only two have examined them directly (Cocquyt et al., 2022; Youngson et al., 2019). These studies showed that while men outperform women on between-route pointing and map-building tasks, these differences do not translate neatly to realworld navigation, where women can perform just as well as men or better.

Virtual Silcton has been used to answer more applied questions, such as how navigation technologies relate to spatial learning. Greater GPS dependence was associated with poorer environmental learning, and this association could not be explained by reduced mental rotation and perspective-taking abilities (Ruginski et al., 2019). More recently, Topete and colleagues (2024) showed that this finding likely reflects broader GPS dependence (rather than reliance on any specific GPS function) and a lower tendency toward exploration. Notably, Teimouri and Richter (2025) demonstrated that enriching standard turn-by-turn instruction, typical of GPS guidance, with route-defining information (e.g., landmarks, prominent streets, directional cues) improves spatial knowledge acquisition, as evidenced by more accurate guided navigation and sketch maps.

Virtual Silcton has also contributed to a central debate in neuroscience -whether individual differences in navigation ability correlate with brain structure, particularly hippocampal volume. While the real-world precursor to Virtual Silcton (Schinazi et al., 2013) linked rapid cognitive map formation to larger hippocampal volume, studies using Virtual Silcton have generally failed to find a robust relationship between performance on spatial tasks and hippocampal structure (Sahoo et al., 2026; Weisberg et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2025).

Critically, a well-powered, pre-registered study by Weisberg and colleagues (2019) found no association between hippocampal volume and performance on the pointing and model-building tasks in Virtual Silcton even after controlling for age, gender and spatial ability. A follow-up study with the same dataset using radiomics and deep learning models of brain structure also failed to find evidence that more complex structural differences could predict performance (Sahoo et al., 2026 https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TnKSaz. An earlier exploratory reanalysis of this data, however, showed that the null effect may have been obscured by substantial heterogeneity among participants (He & Brown, 2019): significant, albeit small, hippocampal-performance correlations emerged among individuals with higher self-reported sense of direction and route-integration abilities. More recently, Zheng and colleagues (2025) found that intensive navigation training can improve navigation performance in Virtual SILCton, but training fails to produce any measurable changes in hippocampal or whole-brain structure. Instead, training is associated with changes in taskrelated information connectivity between cortical regions linked to cognitive control.

Despite the many experiments that have used Virtual Silcton, it is only one Virtual Environment Paradigm within a larger ecosystem of tools to study virtual navigation. But which tool is right for which research purpose? And how can tools be categorized and described such that findings with one tool can be compared or contrasted with findings from another tool? In the next section, we propose and describe a Virtual Environment Paradigm Framework -a series of dimensions along which navigation measurement tools can be categorized.

Virtual Environment Paradigms

Virtual Silcton is an example of a Virtual Environment Paradigm, which separates two aspects of virtual reality spatial navigation research: the virtual environment (i.e., the replica of Virtual Ambler's campus) and the research protocol (i.e., the route integration learning procedure and the pointing and model-building tasks). More generally, the virtual environment is the context in which the experiment takes place -the cues, buildings, design, and simulated physical nature of the space. The research protocol consists of two components: the learning procedure, or how the participants learn the space, and the tasks, or what they are tested on later.

Virtual Environment Paradigms commonly bundle environments with learning procedures and tasks, though in theory these are separable components in navigation research.

Although such research is rare, several recent studies have directly examined how the environment and task characteristics influence behavior. Lader and colleagues (2025) compared performance between Virtual Silcton, Squaretown (a novel virtual environment), and a realworld indoor environment (a psychology building), using similar tasks across each environment.

They found that performance was largely correlated across tasks, but that an environment-based model provided the best fit to the data. In a similar study, https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VdbIIW used the same set of tasks (pointing, map-making, and wayfinding) for Virtual Silcton as well as two other virtual environments (Dual Solution Paradigm and Sea Hero Quest). They found that a one-factor model explained significant variance, suggesting that navigation ability generally drove performance across all environments and tasks. Although the environment did contribute additional explanatory power, the task did not. Critically, these experiments varied the taskswhat participants were asked to do after learning -and the environments -the context in which learning the spatial information took place. They did not systematically alter the learning procedure -how the environment was learned. More generally, such direct comparisons across components are rare. Comparing across Virtual Environment Paradigms therefore, necessitates a systematic way of describing the components that comprise a navigation experiment.

The Virtual Environment Paradigm Framework

We propose the following framework to position Virtual Environment Paradigms along a set of dimensions. We hope this framework can serve as a guide to the community, situating existing tools and allowing newly built ones to specify where in the ecology of Virtual Environment Paradigms they might sit. Although independent in theory, the framework's dimensions are likely to interact, and existing tools cluster around certain combinations. For instance, the virtual Morris Water Maze is typically administered in a relatively sparse, vistascale circular arena, though the paradigm could just as easily be completed in a larger, noncircular arena with more distal and local cues available. Our framework also allows us to identify gaps in the landscape as well -Virtual Environment Paradigms that do not yet exist, but perhaps should.

Purpose

One property sits outside of the main three dimensions -the reason why spatial navigation is being measured at all. Many Virtual Environment Paradigms emphasize measurement for basic science purposes, while others provide insights into more realistic behaviours. We find it useful to distinguish between functional and procedural forms of spatial navigation measurement, each with its own distinct purpose. Functional spatial navigation measurement emphasizes whether or not the navigator reaches their goal, regardless of the cues, representations, and strategies used. A strong functional navigator is one who can return home regardless of available cues, whether they associate turns with key nearby landmarks, infer a direction based on distal landmarks (e.g., a tall building), or even use directions from a GPS. In contrast, measuring spatial navigation procedurally will emphasize the exact tactics used to achieve the behavior. For example, in a perfectly dark, open environment, the only way to find a starting location after traversing a path is to use idiothetic cues to path integrate. In a functional navigation study, individual variability consists of both choosing an appropriate strategy and using it effectively. In a procedural navigation study, only proficiency with the strategy enabled by the environment and task can be used to achieve the goal. These distinct purposes make the design and use of Virtual Environment Paradigms quite different. While functional measurement seeks rich, ecological environments to emulate real-world wayfinding, procedural measurement seeks carefully controlled, delineated environments. Understanding the purpose for which an environment is being used is the first step toward reasoning about how to construct the environment, learning procedure, and task.

Properties of the Environment

The environment is the context in which the experiment takes place. An environment can be real, virtual, augmented, or imagined. In Virtual Environment Paradigms, the environment typically consists of visual cues that are distinct from the mode of interaction (i.e., display format and movement control) and the learning procedures/tasks. Scale. Montello (1993) defined navigation-relevant scales across three types. Vista scale environments mean that most everything can be apprehended without movement. There might be occlusion within the environment, but a vista scale environment does not require integration across multiple viewpoints. Examples of environments at vista scale include the Morris Water Maze, and the Y-maze. Environmental scale environments are large enough that distinct locations must be occupied to view or learn the entire space, but not so large as to be personally navigable over reasonable time scales. Virtual Silcton, Virtual Tubingen, and most levels in Sea Hero Quest (Coutrot et al., 2020) are on an environmental scale. Geographical space is larger than what can be reasonably traversed by an individual. Typically, virtual environments are not large enough to cover geographical space, though exceptions might include open-world video games like Zelda or Skyrim (but even these use fast travel to teleport between locations that are too far to travel).

Complexity.

Unlike scale, complexity eludes easy definitions. Measures such as entropy (Coutrot et al., 2022; Juliani et al., 2016; Slone et al., 2016; Yesiltepe et al., 2023) or visual complexity (Baltaretu et al., 2015) capture some aspects of this dimension. This dimension is designed to capture how intuitive or schematized a virtual environment may be. For instance, a simple circular arena (e.g., Morris Water Maze) and a regular grid (e.g., Square Town) would be low in complexity. An organic city or open forest would be high in complexity. Simple environments can be easily and quickly understood either through sensory means or by relating them to a known schema (e.g., a regular grid pattern would have low network entropy and make it easy to predict how paths will relate to one another). In contrast, complex environments require experience to learn and cannot be quickly mapped onto existing or easily formed schemas to circumvent the experience. Cue Richness. Related to complexity, cue richness is a dimension to describe how much information an environment contains. A cue, loosely defined, is any part of an environment that may be used to learn or remember spatial information. A cue-rich environment might have local and distal cues available of different types that provide different sorts of information. A sun can provide directional information, while abundant, unique buildings can provide location or direction information. Gradients, like terrain slope or color, can provide direction or location information. Landmark labels (e.g., building names), street signs, or other contextual cues can also be used to remember spatial information.

Familiarity.

A virtual environment might replicate a known real environment (like Virtual Silcton), or fabricate an entirely new or unknown place. A virtual environment may also be simple enough that anyone familiar with a room or a simple grid could, in theory, know the space. Similarly, buildings or landmarks could be familiar or novel.

Properties of the Learning Procedure

The learning procedure is the way that a participant comes to know the environment and elements within it. While the virtual environment properties constrain the learning procedure, each can be independently modified. For instance, a route integration paradigm is not possible in vista-scale space if the entire environment can be viewed from a single vantage point. The learning procedure describes how participants learn and the virtual environment for the tasks that follow, although these often blend together, with many studies measuring performance during the learning phase. Similarly, participants could gain knowledge about an environment by completing the tasks. We acknowledge this overlap while defining the properties of each section as a testament to the fact that most Virtual Environment Paradigms typically separate the learning and task phases.

Learning Structure. The learning structure refers to how learning proceeds in the virtual environment. Broadly, learning structure can be constrained or open. Constrained learning limits the participant to specific routes, paths, sections, or even components of the environment. A constrained learning structure might involve predetermined routes, paths, or sections. In contrast, the learning structure could be more open-ended. Using the Dual Solution Paradigm (Marchette et al., 2011) as an example, the typical canonical learning structure constrains exposure to the environment along a single, predetermined route. Howeverhttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SDiJ6d in the Maze-Learning Task (Chrastil et al., 2015) , despite a similar maze-like environment, participants can explore the environment freely with no fixed routes for participants to follow. In that structure, participants are free to roam about the environment, looking for landmarks or simply trying to learn the space.

Scaffolding.

To what extent is learning the environment supported in a staged or scaffolded way? In scaffolded learning, participants might learn the names and images of objects or landmarks before learning their locations around the environment. Or they may be required to master a route-following task prior to learning direct paths between specific landmarks.

Scaffolding could also occur through graded increases in difficulty (partially a property of tasks as well) or through the use of instructions, examples, and checkpoints. Providing experience with controllers or joysticks prior to learning an environment would be another example of scaffolding.

Guided / Unguided.

Learning the environment can also be guided or unguided. Though similar to learning structure, this property refers to whether the participant receives explicit information about how to proceed through the environment. An open structure could provide guides in the form of beacons indicating key landmarks. In a guided learning procedure, the participant is led through the environment via cues (e.g., a map in Sea Hero Quest). In an unguided learning procedure, the participant must learn the environment for themselves.

Learning can be unguided, but still constrained. For instance, in Virtual Arida, we used a routefinding paradigm in which participants must locate one landmark in a large city. Once found, they must navigate back to their starting location and do so repeatedly until they have found the optimally-short route. In this case, learning is constrained to a particular task, but the participant is not guided on how to learn the environment.

Tools Available / Unavailable. As in the real world, many Virtual Environment

Paradigms offer tools the participant cues to apprehend more or gain additional knowledge about the environment. During learning, a map, compass, or verbal directions might be available.

Alternative sensory information (e.g., sounds) could be provided, or, capitalizing on the ability of virtual environments to allow for things that would be impossible in the real world, participants might be able to see through walls or change their perspectives.

Properties of Tasks

After learning, participants' knowledge of the environment is tested through various tasks. The same paradigm could use several different tasks, each of which may be distinct on the properties enumerated below. Generally, a task is defined as an explicit measure of the participant's knowledge of some aspect of the environment. Typically tasks might involve naming or identifying landmarks, recreating routes, wayfinding to goals, indicating directions or distances between locations, or making or assembling maps.

Perspective. The visual or imagined viewpoint from which spatial knowledge must be accessed. A task may require a navigator/ground-level perspective (e.g., route recreation, scene recognition), a bird's-eye perspective (e.g., map drawing, placing landmarks on a map), or require perspective transformation between viewpoints (e.g., learning from ground level but reconstructing the environment from above). Tasks can also vary in whether the tested perspective is the same as learning or novel.

Reference Frame. The coordinate system in which the response is represented.

Responses may be egocentric, defined relative to the navigator's current or imagined position and heading (e.g., "Which way is the library from here?"), or allocentric, defined by relationships among environmental features independent of the navigator's current orientation (e.g., "Where is the library relative to the church?"). Some tasks require translation between reference frames.

Landmark/Route/Survey Knowledge.

A property we derive from Montello (1993) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?NUy2t2 Siegel and White (1975) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?NUy2t2 in which tasks may differ in the type of environmental knowledge they primarily probe. Landmark knowledge concerns the identity or recognition of locations or features within an environment. Route knowledge concerns sequential or action-based relationships among locations, such as knowing which turn follows a particular landmark or reproducing a previously traveled path. Survey knowledge concerns configurational relationships among locations that are not tied to a particular traveled route, such as estimating straight-line direction or distance between landmarks or reconstructing the environment on a map. These categories need not be mutually exclusive: a single task may draw on multiple forms of knowledge.

Static or Dynamic.

Tasks also vary in the amount of movement or spatial updating required during retrieval. At the relatively static end, participants may respond from a fixed viewpoint. For example, they may estimate a numerical distance, recognize a landmark, or view an intersection and select the appropriate turn. More dynamic tasks require rotation, translation, or continuous updating of position or orientation. For example, physically rotating to point toward an unseen landmark is more dynamic than indicating whether that landmark lies to the left or right of a specified heading, while recreating a route through an environment requires continuous translation and rotation. Static-dynamic distinctions overlap with, but are not equivalent to, distinctions between active and passive navigation, which can additionally involve sensory-motor information, decision making, attention, and control over exploration (Chrastil & Warren, 2012).

Secondary Properties: The Interaction

Realism. Virtual environments vary in the extent to which they resemble environments that could be encountered in the real world. This encompasses several partially separable properties. Visual fidelity concerns how realistically the environment is rendered, whereas ecological plausibility concerns whether its layout, landmarks, and other features resemble those of naturally occurring environments. For example, Virtual Silcton recreates a real-world university campus (although the buildings used in Virtual Silcton were not replicas of their realworld counterparts), whereas other paradigms (e.g., the Dual Solution Paradigm, or circular arenas used in Morris Water Maze tasks) use simplified but still plausible environments such as barren arenas or hedge mazes. Greater realism may increase ecological validity, but often at the cost of experimental control because realistic environments contain numerous correlated cues to location and orientation. Finally, visual realism does not require geometric or physical plausibility: virtual environments can appear entirely naturalistic while containing teleportation, wormholes, impossible spatial relationships, or other violations of Euclidean geometry (e.g., Warren et al. 2017).

Immersion and Presence.

Virtual navigation paradigms vary in the degree of sensory and motor immersion afforded by the display and interaction system. A desktop environment viewed on a monitor provides relatively limited immersion, whereas head-mounted displays with wide fields of view, stereoscopic vision, head tracking, and physical movement provide substantially more. Immersion is a property of the technological system, whereas presence-the subjective experience of being located within the virtual environment-is a participant-level experience that can be measured experimentally (Bowman & McMahan, 2007). Thus, more immersive systems may promote greater presence, although the two constructs are not interchangeable.

Locomotion / Movement Mode. The manner in which participants generate movement through a virtual environment varies widely. Desktop virtual reality commonly uses keyboards, mice, joysticks, or game controllers. Immersive virtual reality may combine hand-held controllers with physical rotation, room-scale walking, redirected walking, omnidirectional treadmills, bicycles, or other interfaces. These methods differ not only in their means of control but also in the body-based information they provide: physical movement can introduce vestibular, proprioceptive, motor, and efference-copy signals that are absent or greatly reduced during joystick-or keyboard-controlled locomotion. More unusual interfaces, including gazebased or brain-computer control, are also possible but uncommon (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?uIYMYF Hu et al., 2022).

Secondary Properties: Ease of Deployment

Virtual navigation paradigms vary substantially in the resources required to reproduce, modify, and administer them. At one extreme, a paradigm may require specialized head-mounted displays, tracking equipment, dedicated physical space, proprietary software, or custom-built hardware. Software may be available only as a compiled executable, limiting modification or adaptation. At the other extreme, browser-based paradigms can be administered on conventional computers and distributed remotely with little specialized equipment. Ease of deployment therefore reflects several factors, including hardware requirements, software availability and editability, platform compatibility, technical expertise, physical testing space, and suitability for remote administration.

Virtual Silcton

Virtual Silcton provides a useful example of how these dimensions combine within a single Virtual Environment Paradigm. Originally developed as a virtual analogue of a real-world route-integration paradigm (Schinazi et al., 2013; Weisberg et al., 2014), Virtual Silcton reproduces the spatial configuration of a real college campus and was designed to measure individual differences in learning an environmental-scale space. As a measurement paradigm, it sits somewhere between the functional and procedural ends of our framework. Participants are free to use whatever visual cues or spatial representations are useful for learning the environment, consistent with a functional assessment of navigation ability. At the same time, the canonical learning procedure is carefully structured to create a particular learning problem: participants first learn two spatially separated routes and subsequently experience routes connecting them, enabling comparison of knowledge for locations experienced along the same route with knowledge that must be integrated across routes.

On the environmental dimensions, Virtual Silcton is environmental in scale: its layout cannot be apprehended from a single location and must be learned across successive viewpoints.

It is moderately complex and relatively cue-rich, containing a network of roads, distinct buildings, vegetation, and other local and distal features rather than a small number of experimentally isolated landmarks. It is also visually and ecologically realistic, having been modeled on Temple University's Ambler campus, although the environment is unfamiliar to the participants who complete the task. These properties distinguish it from more schematic paradigms such as virtual Morris Water Mazes or regular grid environments. They also illustrate tradeoffs within the framework. Virtual Silcton provides numerous naturalistic sources of spatial information, but those cues are correlated in ways that make it difficult to determine precisely which information a participant used to learn a location or infer a direction.

The canonical learning procedure is highly constrained, scaffolded, and guided.

Participants traverse two predetermined main routes, learning four named target buildings along each, before following connecting routes linking the previously learned areas. Arrows specify where participants should travel, and target locations are explicitly marked and named. Thus, participants do not determine where to explore, even though they retain substantial freedom in what environmental information they attend to and how they represent it. Importantly, these properties belong to the learning procedure rather than to Virtual Silcton as an environment. The same environment has been used with free exploration, in which the route constraints and guidance are removed (e.g., Brunec et al., 2023). This distinction illustrates one advantage of defining Virtual Environment Paradigms as combinations of separable properties: identical environmental geometry can support quite different forms of spatial learning.

The standard tasks following learning further illustrate how multiple task properties can coexist within a single paradigm. In the onsite pointing task, participants are placed at each building and indicate the direction of the other buildings. The task, therefore, uses a navigatorlevel perspective and a directional, egocentrically expressed response, while nevertheless probing knowledge of configurational relationships among locations. It is also moderately dynamic: participants rotate their viewpoint to produce a pointing response, but do not translate through the environment. In contrast, the model-building task asks participants to arrange overhead images of the target buildings to recreate their spatial configuration. It uses a bird's-eye perspective, an allocentric response format, and primarily configurational knowledge, while being almost entirely static. The fact that these two tasks follow precisely the same learning experience is important: performance on Virtual Silcton is not a unitary measurement, because different retrieval tasks place substantially different demands on the knowledge acquired during navigation.

Finally, the canonical version of Virtual Silcton couples this environment and procedure with a relatively low-immersion interaction and deployment format. Participants typically navigate on a desktop computer using keyboard and mouse controls, receiving visual optic-flow information without the vestibular and proprioceptive signals associated with physical translation through space. The paradigm can be administered on standard computing hardware and has been distributed via web and standalone interfaces, making it comparatively easy to deploy compared with immersive systems. These interaction properties, however, are not intrinsic to the environment or learning procedure. Virtual Silcton has also been adapted for immersive headmounted displays and movement interfaces, including an omnidirectional treadmill, while retaining much of the same environment and learning structure. Such variants shift the paradigm substantially along the immersion, locomotion, body-based cue, and ease-of-deployment dimensions without necessarily altering the spatial problem under study.

Virtual Silcton, therefore, illustrates the central premise of the Virtual Environment Paradigm framework: commonly named paradigms are better understood as bundles of partially separable design choices, any of which can be manipulated to ask whether an empirical finding generalizes beyond one particular implementation. SPACE SPACE provides a contrasting case to Virtual Silcton. Whereas Virtual Silcton was primarily developed to investigate individual differences in the acquisition and integration of spatial knowledge in an unfamiliar environment, SPACE was designed as an iPad-based serious game for detecting spatial and navigational deficits that may indicate cognitive impairment (Schinazi et al., 2026). SPACE includes a visuo-motor training phase and five spatial tasks: path integration, pointing, mapping, associative memory, and perspective taking (Colombo et al., 2024). Subsequent work has shown that performance on these tasks relates to general cognitive status (Colombohttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8b8REY Mintahttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?bsAV5u Thrash, et al., 2026) , clinically rated severity of cognitive impairment (Colombo, Minta, Taylor, et al., 2026) , and hippocampal volume (Minta et al., 2026). Rather than asking whether participants can learn an environment using the information available in it, SPACE deliberately restricts this information and utilises different tasks to capture specific components of navigation ability. As such, SPACE lies further toward the procedural end of the framework than Virtual Silcton.

On the environmental dimension, SPACE and Virtual Silcton are both situated at the environmental scale, but differ substantially in complexity and cue richness. In SPACE, participants are required to keep track of changes in their position and orientation as they learn the location of different landmarks (between 2 and 6). The tasks in SPACE take place on a fictional planet that is visually detailed yet deliberately sparse, containing only background mountains, a rocket, and landmarks. During the path integration task (which doubles as the learning phase), participants encounter only two landmarks per trial that become visible as they approach and disappear as they move away. Importantly, participants cannot see a landmark The learning procedure in SPACE is also tightly staged and scaffolded. Prior to completing the five spatial tasks, participants are trained to use the control interface to rotate, translate, and combine these movements to navigate the virtual environment. However, guidance is asymmetric. On each trial of the path integration task, participants are guided by a robot to two landmarks on opposite sides of a triangle and are asked to return unguided to the rocket (home), which remains invisible until participants indicate their arrival. During the guided forays to the landmarks, participants receive real-time instructions from the robot (e.g., if they deviate from the path). Participants can also use an on-screen widget that indicates the magnitude of their rotations. The landmarks encountered during the path integration trials provide the spatial information that is later tested through the pointing, mapping and associative memory tasks.

Unlike Virtual Silcton, in which route learning and assessment are separated, learning and assessment are deliberately interwoven during the path integration task.

The spatial tasks were specifically designed to tap on different aspects of navigation and acquired spatial knowledge. Path integration is a highly dynamic first-person task that requires the continuous updating of position and orientation during movement. Similar to Virtual Silcton, the pointing task is moderately dynamic and uses a first-person perspective, in which participants are placed in front of a landmark and asked to rotate to point (send the robot) toward another landmark. As such, the task uses an egocentrically expressed directional response while probing configurational relationships among locations. The mapping task is also analogous to that of Virtual Silcton in that it requires a largely static, bird's-eye view reconstruction of the landmark configuration, achieved by dragging and dropping icons onto a canvas, with a centre icon indicating the rocket's position. The perspective taking requires transformation between allocentric information and an imagined egocentric perspective. The task follows the same semistatic logic as the mapping task and is an adaptation of the Perspective Taking Spatial Orientation Test (Hegarty & Waller, 2004; Kozhevnikov & Hegarty, 2001). Participants are provided with the correct bird's-eye map and are asked to imagine standing at one landmark, while facing another landmark, before dragging and dropping an icon to indicate the direction of a third landmark. SPACE, therefore, does not provide a single measure of spatial or navigation ability, but deliberately samples a broader range of task demands within the same paradigm.

In SPACE, this controlled task battery is coupled to a low-immersion but deliberately configurable interaction and deployment format. SPACE was developed to run on iPads (iOS 16.6.1 or later), with translation and rotation generated by taps and swipes on the touchscreen. SPACE can also be deployed on desktop computers, allowing the same virtual environment and task structure to be administered using conventional computer controls. Similar to Virtual Silcton, movement is visually simulated through optic flow information without corresponding vestibular or proprioceptive signals. The main distinguishing feature of SPACE is its configurability. While most of the work to date has used the standard configuration on iPad, SPACE was explicitly built so that researchers can vary the control interface (i.e., tap & swipe, virtual joystick, anchor), player and robot speed, tasks included in the assessment, task order, task repetitions, as well as the number of landmarks and their positions. Landmark configurations can be saved as JSON files, allowing for an identical version to be deployed across laboratories and clinics.

The Future of Virtual Environment Paradigms

Virtual Silcton and other Virtual Environment Paradigms serve a critical need in spatial navigation research by establishing common tools that examine spatial behavior in consistent and replicable ways. Yet, due in part to the difficulty in creating and sharing these tools, the variety of properties we reviewed here is not systematically or comprehensively sampled. By providing a framework that situates Virtual Environment Paradigms within the broader context of spatial research, we hope to enable the field to address gaps in our ability to test spatial knowledge in a variety of ways. Virtual Silcton serves a key niche and will continue to do so, but to achieve more precise measurement of navigation processes or more generalizable and transferable As more Virtual Environment Paradigms are developed, researchers also risk losing the ability to easily replicate each other's work, while connecting the network of paradigms and tracing how they evolved or branched off from one another will become increasingly difficult.

As discussed, the Morris Water Maze shows how one seemingly simple Virtual Environment Paradigm has spiralled into each version becoming its own slightly different assay of spatial behavior (Thornberry et al., 2026). Thus, tools that enable more effective linking of environment typologies can promote a more interconnected spatial navigation literature.

Translation to real-world navigation

Whether and how spatial navigation behavior measured in virtual environments in the lab translates to real-world navigation is a critical gap that future research needs to address. While some studies can be conducted identically in real and virtual environments (e.g., path integration studies, Virtual Silcton, the water maze, etc.), others can not. This gap is especially critical for functional Virtual Environment Paradigms, where the goal is to discover deficits in achieving spatial goals, rather than to measure specific processes, which could be compensated for or avoided in real life scenarios. Our Virtual Environment Paradigm framework can contribute to these translation efforts by helping determine which paradigms should be translated to real-world experiments, in particular, which features of the environment, learning protocol, and task should be retained to ensure a match. Conversely, by focusing on tackling a real-world problem, the Virtual Environment Paradigm framework can guide the selection of properties that best match the environment, learning procedure, and task that comprise that behavior. For example, if the goal is to improve driving behavior in difficult-to-navigate cities, a large-scale, feature-rich, complex environment with a learning procedure that is unguided but features tools like a simulated GPS during a wayfinding task would be most appropriate. Removing some of these features to isolate mechanisms of difficult driving can also be guided by the framework (e.g., is driving as difficult without GPS, or in a simpler city, or starting with guided learning).
