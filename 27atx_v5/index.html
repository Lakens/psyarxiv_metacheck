Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

How the brain processes facial expressions and whether this process requires focal attention have been central topics of neurophysiological research on face processing. In a seminal study, using scalp-recorded event-related potentials (ERPs), Eimer, Holmes, and McGlone (2003) found that emotional and neutral faces were differently processed in the brain as early as 160 ms after stimulus onset. Importantly, these ERP differences disappeared when attention was directed away from the face stimuli, suggesting the necessity of focal attention in facial expression processing. These results have greatly motivated further research in the field. Yet, subsequent research did not always agree with these findings, and the study was never directly replicated. Hence, this study has been selected by #EEGManyLabs, a global and multi-laboratory replication project, for a high-powered direct replication across multiple laboratories (N = 41 in each laboratory). We hypothesize the replication of the original positive findings, including the ERP effects differentiating emotional and neutral faces and their dependence on focal attention. To address the debate on facial expression processing and the role of N170, we will also take this opportunity to reassess key negative findings of the original study, namely no difference between emotional and neutral facial expressions in N170 and no ERP differences among the six facial expressions. Replication or revision of these findings will confirm the necessity of focal attention in emotional face processing and the postulated biomarkers of basic emotion processing. This study will provide a reproducible benchmark on face processing.

Introduction

The ability to process facial expressions rapidly and accurately is central to human social communication (Anderson & Phelps, 2001; Fox et al., 2000; Öhman et al., 2001). Faces convey critical information about another's emotional state and intentions, and the way the brain responds to these visual cues has been a topic of extensive research over the past few decades (Ekman, Friesen, & Ellsworth, 1972; Niedenthal & Ric, 2017). Furthermore, research has also been trying to determine whether focal attention is needed for facial expression processing, which represents the level of automaticity in perceiving emotion in faces (Pessoa et al., 2002; Vuilleumier et al., 2001).

A seminal study in this field by Eimer, Holmes, and McGlone (2003) demonstrated that several scalp-recorded event-related potential (ERP) activities (as early as 160 ms after stimulus onset) distinguished between laterally presented neutral and emotional faces when visual attention was allocated to faces. More importantly, they also showed that the effect disappeared when attention was diverted by a demanding perceptual task. The findings suggested the necessity of focal attention in processing of facial expressions (e.g., Pessoa et al., 2002) and challenged the notion that emotional face processing occurs pre-attentively (e.g., Vuilleumier et al., 2001). The work by Eimer et al. (2003) was among the first to demonstrate that attention modulates the neural processing of emotional faces (i.e., the attention-gating account of emotional face processing), and it has shaped much of the subsequent empirical and theoretical landscape. After 23 years of its publication, Eimer et al. (2003) still remained a classic study and has consistently been cited throughout the years. As of February 2026, this article had been cited over 630 times.

However, the findings of the Eimer et al. (2003) study have been often debated upon. While it is widely accepted that emotional faces elicit enhanced ERP responses in comparison to neutral faces as found in the original study (e.g., Leppänen et al., 2007; Luo et al., 2010; Smith, 2012), whether these effects require focal attention remains a topic of debate. More crucially, the absence of any difference in the N170 component (a biomarker of early structural encoding of face stimuli; e.g., Bentin et al., 1996), both between emotional and neutral faces and among the six emotions, have been questioned in later research. Despite research advancements, due to its classic status in the face processing field, Eimer et al. (2003) is often cited to introduce the research background of emotional face processing (e.g., Fox et al., 2007; Sperl et al., 2020) and sometimes cited to support new findings without any direct investigation of the findings from Eimer et al. (2003) (e.g., Balconi & Canavesio, 2016; Gantiva et al., 2019). Therefore, a direct replication is deemed necessary yet still missing to date. As such, the present study was selected for preregistered direct replication by a community of EEG experts of the #EEGManyLabs, which is a global and large-scale multi-laboratory replication programme undertaking direct replications of highly influential studies with predefined sample sizes and high statistical power (90% power for 50% of the original effect sizes) (Pavlov et al., 2021).

The original study by Eimer et al. (2003) employed a within-subject design in which 14 participants completed two tasks (emotion and line tasks). In the emotion task, participants were asked to discriminate between neutral faces and emotional faces (laterally presented to the left and right of the fixation point) showing one of the six basic emotions (disgusted, fearful, happy, sad, surprised, angry) in each block. In the line task, participants were asked to judge the length of line segments closer to the central fixation while viewing the same face displays. This perceptual task demanded visuospatial attention away from the face stimuli. Their results showed ERP differences between the neutral and emotional face conditions (termed "valence effect" in the original publication; we changed this term to "emotion effect" in the current study given that this effect could also relate to arousal; Russell,1980), and more importantly, the modulation of the emotion effect by the involvement of focal attention. With focal attention (emotion task), significant ERP differences were found between emotional and neutral faces (not differentiating among the six emotions) at four time windows over frontal, central, temporal, and occipital scalp regions (with the earliest window being 160-215 ms in the frontal area). However, no such difference was found when focal attention was directed away from faces. These results contradicted the basic emotion theories, which suggest that the basic emotions are rapidly detected by automatic and distinct neural mechanisms operating independently of the involvement of voluntary attention (Ekman, 1992; Ortony & Turner, 1990).

Furthermore, there were two noteworthy non-significant results in Eimer et al. (2003) -there was no emotion effect in the N170 component, and no difference among the six basic emotions either. The absence of the emotion effect in N170 agreed with the Bruce and Young model in that structural encoding of faces (represented by the N170 component; Bentin et al., 1996; Bentin & Deouell, 2000) and the processing of emotional facial expressions are separate processes in the brain (Bruce & Young, 1986; Eimer, 2011). According to the Bruce and Young model (1986), the first stage of face processing is structural encoding, which derives a description of the face structure. The encoded face structure will then be fed into parallel and independent routes of identity processing (for familiar faces) and non-identity processing (for unfamiliar faces). A face's emotional state is analysed in the non-identity route after structural encoding (see also Lander & Butcher, 2015; Young, 2018). The absence of any difference among the emotions suggests that facial expression is processed in the same manner in neocortex and with the same time course.

In short, the findings of Eimer et al. (2003) can be summarized in four aspects. The first is the presence of emotion effects (enhanced processing of emotional faces relative to neutral faces) in several ERP activities with focal attention allocated to faces. The second is the absence of these emotion effects with focal attention directed away from faces. The third is the absence of any emotion effect in N170. And the last aspect is the absence of any difference among the six emotions. The first aspect has been consistently supported by later research, which showed the emotion effect over several comparable time periods and scalps regions (e.g., Bar-Haim et al., 2005; Leppänen et al., 2007; Luo et al., 2010). However, the other three aspects have been heavily debated in the literature.

The debate regarding the second aspect (i.e., absence of emotion effects without focal attention) has been focusing on the level of automaticity of emotional face processing. Research carried out around the time of Eimer et al. (2003) agreed with the original study. For instance, Holmes, Vuilleumier, and Eimer (2003) showed participants arrays containing two faces and two houses, with faces displaying either a fearful or neutral expression. Spatial attention was directed to the faces or houses. Results showed stronger frontal positivity (approximately 100 ms post-stimulus) for fearful faces relative to neutral faces when attention was directed to faces. Just as Eimer et al. (2003) showed, this difference was absent when attention was paid to the houses. Additionally, the result was also confirmed by Holmes, Winston, and Eimer (2005), who demonstrated the same presence and absence of emotion effect respectively with and without focal attention on faces. Conversely, the emotion effect was totally absent in spatially filtered face stimuli. As such, around that time, it was concluded that emotional face processing is not fully automatic and is modulated by attentional manipulations (e.g., Palermo & Rhodes, 2007; Pessoa, 2005).

However, it soon turned out that the automaticity level of emotional face processing may depend on the location of face stimulus presentation. The evidence supporting Eimer et al. (2003) all came from studies presenting face stimuli laterally (to the left and right of the fixation). In contrast, the literature started to show that early emotion-related processing appears to proceed automatically when faces are presented at the location of the fixation point. Holmes, Kiss, and Eimer (2006) presented face stimuli at the fixation, and found that the initial stage of emotional face processing (160-220 ms) occurred even when spatial attention was directed to a line task (for a review, see Eimer & Holmes, 2007). Similarly, Schindler et al. (2020)also found that the emotion modulation effect in N170 (which is at the same time stage of Eimer et al.'s early frontal activity, but occurs in the occipital area instead) was task-independent, highlighting the automaticity of early facial expression perception (specifically at the time of structural encoding of faces). While centrally presented faces can elicit early, pre-attentive responses, later sustained processing, including early posterior negativity (EPN, which represents an early attention mechanism enhancing brain responses to emotional information; Schindler et al., 2019; Schupp et al., 2006) and late positive potential (LPP, which indexes emotional stimulus evaluation and attentional control; Schindler et al., 2019; Schupp et al., 2006), may still require focused attention. Interestingly, even when participants did not have awareness of facial expressions (subliminal presentation), an early difference between neutral and fearful faces was still found in the occipitotemporal area (Pegna et al., 2008).

A critical counterargument to the notion of fully automatic processing of emotional face stimuli (centrally presented) is that some residual attention may still be available for the processing, if the non-face tasks used in the studies above were not difficult enough to totally deplete the attentional resources (e.g., Pessoa et al., 2002). This counterargument is especially noteworthy given that object identification is typically associated with foveal processing, which differs from and probably runs in parallel to peripheral processing (Ludwig et al., 2014). To test this counterargument, Schindler et al. (2021) presented stimuli at fixation and manipulated the perceptual load of the non-face task to assess the amount of residual attention needed for the emotional face processing, as high perceptual load tasks can effectively exhaust attentional resources available for other processes (Lavie, 2005). They found that the emotion effects in early ERP activities (P1 and N170) were not modulated by load, whereas the later effect in EPN was dependent on attention. Together, these findings suggest that the earliest stages of facial expression analysis operate without capacity limits, whereas subsequent processing remains attention bounded. The combined evidence (presentation location and perceptual load manipulation) suggests that the level of automaticity decreases for early processing of emotional faces with lateral stimulus presentation in comparison to presentation at fixation. This central-peripheral dissociation is especially noteworthy because Eimer et al. (2003) placed their faces in the parafovea (2.2° from fixation centre-to-centre, 3.4° to the far edges) -an eccentricity that retains considerably higher acuity than the periphery which is beyond 5° from fixation (Larson & Loschky, 2009; Rayner, 1998) yet is already subject to attentional gating. Eimer et al.'s (2003) results thus mark a boundary between foveal and peripheral emotion processing.

Taken into consideration of all the empirical evidence discussed above (i.e., lateral vs. foveal image presentation, attention modulation by non-face tasks, and perceptual load manipulation), several theoretical accounts were developed to characterize the graded effects of attention on emotional face processing. Pessoa (2008) and Pessoa and Adolphs (2010) proposed multiple levels of automaticity: subcortical routes can trigger rapid affective responses even when attention is limited, whereas ERP components that originate from cortical structures (such as P1 and N170) are modulated by available attentional resources. Luo et al. (2010) further suggested that coarse detection of some negative emotions (especially fear) can occur automatically in early neural responses (e.g., P1), while precise categorization requires attentional investment. A review by Schindler and Bublatzky (2020) concurs: early responses are typically attention-independent, whereas sustained engagement with emotional content, particularly for complex or subtle expressions, depends on focal attention. This body of work therefore converges on a dual-process view: rapid, automatic detection of emotional salience is followed by attention-dependent elaboration.

Given the pivotal role of Eimer et al.'s (2003) finding of the absence of the emotion effect without focal attention in the above-mentioned theoretical advancements, it is crucial to replicate the result by a high standard of contemporary ERP research. Specifically, the original study lacked statistical power with a sample size of 14. In the original study (regarding the first two aspects of findings, i.e., the emotion effects and their attentional modulation), if the emotion effect was still present without focal attention but too weak to be detected with the limited statistical power, a high-powered replication will provide additional information indispensable to the field. Confirming (or refuting) the attentional modulation originally reported would provide a more secure foundation for debates about the automaticity of emotional face processing. Accordingly, the current study reproduces the original experimental protocol to reassess with enhanced statistical precision the role of spatial attention in the early and late cortical dynamics of facial-expression perception. Related to the first two aspects of Eimer et al.'s (2003) findings, there are two hypotheses: Hypothesis 1 (H1): In the emotion discrimination task (attention directed to faces), emotional faces will elicit enhanced ERPs relative to neutral faces, including: H2: In the line discrimination task (attention directed away from faces), no emotion effect as in H1 will be found in any of these five ERP activities (correspondingly H2.1 to H2.5).

The third aspect of Eimer et al. (2003) concluded that the N170 component was unaffected by emotional content in either task (i.e., no matter whether attention was allocated towards or away from the faces). N170 is widely recognized as an index of the structural encoding of faces (Bentin et al, 1996; Bentin & Deouell, 2000; Rossion & Jacques, 2011). It has a bilateral occipitotemporal distribution with neural sources in the face-selective fusiform face area (FFA) of the brain (Gao et al., 2019; Kanwisher & Moscovitch, 2000). N170 shows enhanced amplitudes responding to face and face-like (e.g., schematic faces and Mooney faces) stimuli relative to non-face objects (Bentin et al., 1996; Eimer et al., 2011; Itier & Taylor, 2004). Compared with upright faces, inverted faces result in an enlarged and delayed N170, demonstrating N170's sensitivity to configural (structural) information in faces (Rossion & Gauthier, 2002; Rossion et al., 2000). Therefore, by showing the absence of any emotion effect in N170, Eimer et al. (2003) supported the hypothesis that emotion processing occurs independently from structural encoding -a conclusion consistent with several studies reporting similar N170 amplitudes across facial expressions (Fisher et al., 2015; Hermann et al., 2002; Kiss & Eimer, 2008; Sprengelmeyer & Jentzsch, 2006). Many other studies, however, have shown that N170 does distinguish emotional from neutral faces and can even differentiate between specific emotions (Batty & Taylor, 2003; Blau et al., 2007; Carlson & Reinke, 2010; Righart & De Gelder, 2006; Smith, 2012). Fear and anger, in particular, often elicited larger N170 amplitudes than neutral faces (Batty & Taylor, 2003; Calvo et al., 2014; Faja et al., 2016; Leppänen et al., 2007 Leppänen et al., , 2008;; Pegna et al., 2008; Rossignol et al., 2005; Rellecke et al., 2012 Rellecke et al., , 2013)), a pattern confirmed by meta-analysis (Hinojosa et al., 2015). Some studies have even found stronger N170 responses to happy expressions (Luo et al., 2010; Righart & De Gelder, 2008). Collectively, these findings indicate that N170 can be sensitive to emotional information (for reviews, see Hinojosa et al., 2015; Schindler & Bublatzky, 2020).

In fact, despite the negative findings about N170 in Eimer et al. (2003), their data may have shown some N170-related ERP activities. Specifically, the frontal positivity between 160 and 215 ms may correspond to the vertex positive potential (VPP), an ERP component sharing a neural generator with N170 and likewise indexing early face perception (Joyce & Rossion, 2005). The presence of an emotion effect and its attentional modulation in this window raises the possibility that N170 could exhibit similar effects when statistical power is sufficient. Therefore, it is desirable to replicate the negative result in N170 of the Eimer et al. (2003) study (i.e., no emotion effect). Furthermore, to go beyond only showing consistency with the original finding (if we are to obtain a non-significant result), we will also run Bayesian analysis to obtain evidence for equivalence between the emotional and neutral faces as a firm replication of the original result.

Another potential factor contributing to the absence of emotion effect in N170 is the averaged-earlobes reference used by Eimer et al. (2003). This reference has been shown to reduce the amplitude of N170 and enhance the amplitude of VPP (Joyce & Rossion, 2005). As such, it is possible that N170 may be able to show the emotion effect with a reference more suitable for N170. In the literature, the common average reference is usually preferred for a strong N170 (e.g., Joyce & Rossion, 2005). However, in the current multi-lab research, we cannot guarantee having the same electrode layout across replicating labs, rendering the common average reference undesirable because of incomparable references across labs (see Dien, 1998). A more favourable choice is the reference electrode standardization technique (REST), which mathematically calculates a reference point at infinity where the electrical potential is zero (Yao, 2001). Working optimally from 16 to 128 electrode positions (higher electrode density leading to better REST accuracy; Liu et al., 2015), the REST referencing produces a reliable and strong N170 component similar to that with the common average reference (Wang et al., 2019), and importantly, does not vary with the electrode layout. Therefore, in addition to the replication pipeline with a reference same as or similar to that used in Eimer et al. (2003), we will run another pre-processing pipeline employing the REST reference for the analysis of N170 and the early frontal positivity (potentially similar to VPP).

Following the above discussion, in the present replication, we will reassess whether N170 responds differently to emotional and neutral faces. The original findings of Eimer et al. (2003) lead to the third hypothesis:

H3: N170 will show no emotion effect (i.e., no differences between emotional and neutral faces), regardless of the attentional manipulation. This will be complemented by evidence for equivalence from Bayesian analysis.

The final aspect of Eimer et al.'s (2003) findings is the absence of any difference in the ERPs among the six basic emotions. This finding suggested that all emotions are processed in essentially the same way. Subsequent research, however, has shown that different emotions can be processed differently. Negatively valenced and potentially threatening expressions (e.g., fear and anger) tend to be more salient and are processed more rapidly and automatically than other emotions, a pattern known as the negativity bias (Ito et al., 1998; Smith et al., 2003). Luo et al. (2010) confirmed this bias, demonstrating that negative expressions, particularly fear, elicit an enhanced P1 component even when attention is not focused on the faces. Threatening faces likewise produce attentionindependent enhancements of the EPN relative to neutral or friendly faces (Aguado et al., 2019; Rellecke et al., 2011; Schupp et al., 2004 Schupp et al., , 2006;; Weidner et al., 2022). Some of these studies employed emotion categorization tasks similar to that in the Eimer et al. (2003) study (Ito et al., 1998; Smith et al., 2003). Meanwhile, others either demanded more than categorizing emotional and neutral faces and requested participants to identify facial expressions (Aguado et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2010; Weidner et al., 2022), or reduced the attentional engagement by studying facial expression processing in passive viewing conditions (Rellecke et al., 2011; Schupp et al., 2004). Therefore, it is unlikely that the differences in the findings could be explained by task differences. These observations motivated us to explore whether ERPs would differ across the six emotions in the current replication. Following the results of Eimer et al. (2003), we will test the final hypothesis:

H4: ERPs do not differ across the six basic emotions in any of the measurements stated in H1 or N170, regardless of the attentional manipulation. This will be complemented by evidence for equivalence from Bayesian analysis.

To test these hypotheses, EEG data will be acquired in each replicating lab using the same stimuli and experimental procedure as those in the original study, along with the recording parameters staying as close as possible. Individual datasets from the replicating labs will be separately analysed by the leading lab using the same analysis pipelines, then put into internal random-effects meta-analyses for pooled estimates with higher statistical power.

Power analysis and sample size

The original study's key results were several significant Task × Emotion interactions ("Task × Valence interactions" in the original publication's wording) that demonstrated enhanced ERP responses to emotional faces relative to neutral faces when focal attention was allocated to the faces (emotion task), but not when attention was directed towards line segments (line task) (Eimer et al., 2003; sample size N = 14). This attention-dependent emotion effect was observed in five ERP activities over four temporal phases, including an early frontal positivity (160-215 ms, F(1,13) = 5.2, p < .05), a frontocentral positivity and an occipitotemporal negativity (both at 220-315 ms, Fs > 7.2, ps < .02), and positivities over frontal, central and parietal regions (320-495 and 500-700 ms, Fs > 10.0, ps < .01).

Following the #EEGManyLabs recommendations (Pavlov et al., 2021) and recognizing that ERP studies often overestimate effect sizes due to small samples (Clayson et al., 2019), we calculated the required sample size of the replication. The calculation was based on the weakest original significant Task x Emotion interaction found in 160-215 ms (F = 5.2, corresponding to η² p = 0.2857). We targeted 90% power to detect an effect half of the original effect (i.e., η² p = 0.1429) at an alpha level of .02. With these settings, an a priori power analysis using MorePower 6.0.4 (Campbell & Thompson, 2012) indicated a sample size of 82.

However, this sample size puts up a high demand on each lab's data collection work and is potentially impractical. Therefore, we have decided to reduce the required sample size from each replicating lab to 41 (half of the initial estimation; this reduced sample size is not related to the number or replicating labs). Following the meta-analytic theory (e.g., Borenstein et al., 2021), with a fixed total sample size, distributing participants across sites and then meta-analysing their effect sizes yields the same point estimate and nearly identical precision, provided the between-site heterogeneity is accounted for (see also Paul et al., 2025). As such, reducing the per-lab sample from 82 to 41 will not compromise the meta-analytic power of the study as long as the overall sample is large enough. Each replicating lab will collect behavioural and EEG data from 41 participants. If any participants are to be excluded from the analysis, more participants will be tested to replace the excluded participants to obtain usable data from 41 participants in each replicating lab.

Sensitivity power analysis

Following the decision to reduce the sample size per replicating lab from 82 to 41, we did a sensitivity power analysis using MorePower 6.0.4 to assess the minimum detectable effect size (MDES) that each replicating lab can achieve for the Task x Emotion interactions. With 90% power and an alpha level of .02, the analysis showed an MDES of η² p = 0.2587. This effect size is smaller than that of the weakest significant Task x Emotion interaction in the original study (frontal positivity at 160-215 ms, η² p = 0.2857). Expressed as power, each replicating lab will have 94.3% power to detect an effect of the magnitude reported for the weakest original interaction, and 98.9% and 99.9% power for the effects reported in the 220-315 ms and the later time windows. By comparison, the original study, with N = 14 and an alpha level of .05, could only reliably detect effects of η² p = 0.468 or larger at 90% power. Therefore, even with the halved sample size, each replicating lab is about twice as sensitive as the study being replicated, and at a more stringent alpha level. Furthermore, the metaanalysis will be highly powered. The replication study has eight participating labs so far, giving a total sample size of 328. With this pooled sample size, at 90% power and alpha level of .02, we will be able to detect the effect as small as η² p = 0.0386 (a small effect size). Eimer et al. (2003) did not find any difference in the N170 component, neither did they observe any differences across the six basic emotions. The current replication plans to further explore these patterns by including negative hypotheses (H3 and H4) based on the original study. To check whether the replication study will have enough power to statistically assess weak effects and be able to avoid mistakenly drawing negative conclusions, we ran further sensitivity power analyses with the same power and alpha level settings.

For the emotion effect in N170 and its interaction with task (H3), same as the frontal positivity above, the MDES is η² p = 0.0386 (a small effect size). For the facial expression effect and its interaction with the other factors (H4), the MDES is η² p = 0.0120 (a small effect size). Therefore, the planned analysis is capable of reliably detecting small effect sizes and will only show null findings if the effects are below these levels.

Variance across replicating labs

At the time of the Registered Report Stage 1 manuscript, apart from the leading lab in the UK, seven labs from seven countries (Australia, China, France, Spain, Türkiye, UK, and USA) have joined the project, equipped with EEG systems from four EEG equipment manufacturers (ANT Neuro, BioSemi, BrainProducts, and Neuroscan). As such, various aspects of EEG data acquisition will differ, including but not limited to EEG system make and model, electrode design (e.g., active vs. passive), electrode layout, electric conducting medium (conductive gel, saline, or direct electrode contact without medium), baseline electric noise level, recording software, trigger port (e.g., parallel port, USB port, lab streaming layer), trigger latency accuracy, laboratory layout (related to participant experience), working language, researcher ethnicity, participant ethnicity, and working habit/style of researchers. To minimize unnecessary variations, the data acquisition procedure is pre-determined and to be followed precisely by each replicating lab. Before a replicating lab starts collecting data, we will also ensure the timing accuracy of event triggers. If necessary, we will use the open-source stimulus-latency instrument developed by #EEGManyLabs (ElectroTime; Van den Bosch et al., 2026; see also https://electrotime.org/) to confirm the trigger latency. The data collected will be sent to the leading lab for data analysis with the same pre-processing and statistical pipelines. Necessary EEG preprocessing variations/adjustments (e.g., re-referencing) have been planned and clearly stated.

These methodological differences will contribute to the variance in the results. We will evaluate whether these differences influence replicability with the internal random-effects meta-analysis, which allows cross-dataset heterogeneity to be assessed. We will examine the heterogeneity estimates to determine whether the variability in effect sizes across replicating labs will exceed the expected level of measurement error (see Klein et al., 2018). Given that we have made our best effort to stay verbatim to the method of Eimer et al. (2003) and be as consistent as possible across the replicating labs, a heterogeneity level higher than measurement error will likely reflect differences in samples or pre-processing adjustments.

Procedure

The experiment was programmed in PsychoPy v2023.1.2 (Peirce et al., 2019), following the information provided in Eimer et al. (2003) and further information provided by the corresponding author (Prof. Martin Eimer) of the original study. The full program, along with a README file, is openly available on the project's OSF repository (https://osf.io/4n2t8/).

Because the replication will be conducted across replicating labs, detailed information about each lab's monitor (model, refresh rate, resolution), viewing distance, and face image scaling factor is summarized in Table 1. All replicating labs will make necessary changes to the program, including translating or rewording of the instructions for their participants, changing the method of manual responses, changing the trigger port address, changing the trigger values for block start and end, and slight tweaking for their own PsychoPy version. As stated above, each lab will try to stay with a 100% scaling factor if the hardware and lab setup allow. All replicating labs' experiment programs will also be available on the repository.

The experiment has 24 blocks, each of which has 80 trials. Participants will complete two discrimination tasks (emotion and line tasks), each comprising 12 consecutive blocks, with the task order counterbalanced across participants. Throughout the experiment, participants will be instructed to keep their eye gaze on the fixation cross and to minimize eye blinks and eye movements. In each block, no matter what the task is, neutral face pairs randomly appear on half of the trials, with emotional face pairs randomly appearing on the other half. Each of the six facial expressions (angry, disgusted, fearful, happy, sad, surprised; i.e. six block types) is presented on the emotional face trials for two blocks, with the order of block types randomized across participants. The length of each line segment in the display varies independently (short and long lines are equally likely to appear on either side of the display).

Each trial presents the stimulus display for 300 ms, followed by a 2,000-ms interval before the next trial starts (Figure 1). In the emotion task, participants will be asked to discriminate between emotional and neutral face pairs and ignore the line segments, and to respond as quickly and accurately as possible with left-or right-hand keypresses ('z' or 'm') for neutral or emotional faces. In the line task, participants will determine whether the line segments are identical or different in length while ignoring the faces, and to respond quickly and accurately by pressing 'z' or 'm'. The response-hand mapping is counterbalanced between tasks and across participants. Manual responses made within 2,000 ms from the stimulus onset will be accepted. The trial sequence will run regardless of the presence or timing of manual responses. After each block, feedback on participant performance (mean accuracy and RT) will be provided on the screen.

Methodologically, randomizing inter-trial intervals (i.e., interval jitter) is widely adopted in ERP research to remove electric activities evoked by preceding trials from the ERP responses of the current trial (Luck, 2014). Eimer et al. (2003) did not employ this and instead used a single and long interval duration (2,000 ms). In the original study, the ERPs during the pre-stimulus baseline period were sufficiently flat, suggesting that the absence of interval jitter was not a technical concern. Furthermore, despite the use of a fixed interval between trials, anticipatory manual responses are not to be expected because of the use of forced-choice responses instead of simple reactions (hence the ERP results are not expected to be influenced). Additionally, another potential issue is that participants may develop temporal expectations regarding the onsets of forthcoming stimuli, and as a result lead to some preparatory brain activities (e.g., Pütz et al., 2025). Because the current study is a direct replication of the Eimer et al. (2003) study, despite this possibility, we will apply a verbatim adoption of the original trial sequence to avoid any potential difficulties of interpreting results if we obtain any findings different from those of the original study.

Before running the experimental blocks, each participant will go through a practice block for each task. Participants will stop practising and proceed to the experimental blocks if they have reached 80% accuracy in a practice block in each task, as in the original study (M. Eimer, personal communication, 31 July 2025), with the accuracy calculated as the ratio of the number of correctly responded trials to the number of total trials (i.e., trials with no responses are treated as incorrect trials). If a participant does not reach the 80% accuracy level after one practice block, up to two more practice blocks will be carried out. If a participant's mean accuracy in a practice block remains below 80% after three practice blocks for any task, this participant will not proceed to the experimental blocks.

EEG data pre-processing and ERP measurement

All EEG data will be imported into EEGLAB (Delorme & Makeig, 2004) using data importation plugins and processed using two pipelines (Table 2), i.e., a direct replication pipeline that follows the original study as closely as possible, and an advanced pipeline following more recent standards. The pre-processing codes for both pipelines are available online (https://osf.io/4n2t8/). These codes have been tested with pilot data collected from several replicating labs for quality assurance and the choice of pre-processing parameters.

Replication pipeline

The data will be re-referenced to the average voltage of earlobes (or averaged mastoids, or averaged TP9/TP10, or averaged T9/T10 if EEG data from earlobes are unavailable) before being down-sampled to 200 Hz (the same as in the original study). A bipolar HEOG channel will be generated if HEOG data were acquired as separate monopolar channels. A digital bandpass filter of 0.1-40 Hz will then be applied. The filtered data will be segmented into epochs ranging from 100 ms before to 700 ms after stimulus onset, then baseline corrected for the 100-ms pre-onset period. Afterwards, artefact rejection will be carried out to remove epochs contaminated by horizontal eye movements (exceeding ±30 µV at the HEOG channel), eye blinks (exceeding ±60 µV at Fpz or Fp1 if Fpz is unavailable), and other artefacts (exceeding ±80 µV at any other key electrodes, including F3, Fz, F4, C3, Cz, C4, P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8, PO7, and PO8). If more than 50% of epochs in any of the two tasks from a participant are to be removed by this step, this particular participant will be removed from any further analysis. We will apply the 50% threshold to each task separately, because the block design of the two tasks may lead to poor quality data in one task only, disproportionately affecting results from one task. Finally, accepted epochs carrying correct responses will be averaged to create ERP waveforms for each condition (i.e., each of the 24 task, block type, and emotion combinations).

For ERP quantification, we will use the same channels and time windows reported in Eimer et al. (2003) for all the ERP activities. Specifically, N170 will be quantified as mean amplitude at P7 and P8 (labelled T5 and T6 in the original study) over the time window of 160-200 ms. Five successive time windows will then be used to quantify mean ERP amplitudes over five scalp regions separately, namely frontal (F3, Fz, F4), central (C3, Cz, C4), parietal (P3, Pz, P4), temporal (P7, P8), and lateral occipital (PO7 and PO8; labelled OL and OR in the original study) channels. These time windows are 120-155, 160-215, 220-315, 320-495, and 500-800 ms, which cover the periods showing emotional expression differences in studies predating the original study (Eimer & Holmes, 2002; Holmes et al., 2003).

Advanced pipeline

The data will be re-referenced and digitally filtered as above, but not be down-sampled. Afterwards, we will apply the ICA method (Onton et al., 2006). For this purpose, we will create a copy of the dataset and highpass filter it at 2 Hz (Klug & Gramann, 2021). Then, any data with no event triggers for longer than 5,000 ms will be removed. Independent component (IC) weights will then be computed with the Adaptive Mixture ICA (AMICA) method (Palmer et al., 2012), and be transferred to the original data. Another data copy will be created with the 2-Hz highpass filter for IC classification, which will employ ICLabel (Pion-Tonachini et al., 2019) to automatically identify IC sources. ICs identified as eye, muscle, heart, line noise, and channel noise with more than 80% probability will be flagged for rejection. These flagged ICs will be removed from the original dataset.

After the ICA procedure, the dataset will be segmented into 800-ms epochs (-100 to 700 ms relative to stimulus onset), baseline corrected, and artefact rejected. For artefact rejection, in addition to the three criteria above (eye movements, blinks, strong artefacts), trials showing prolonged weak activity (absolute voltage below 0.5 µV for more than 200 ms) at any of the key electrode locations (F3, Fz, F4, C3, Cz, C4, P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8, PO7, PO8) will also be rejected. As in the replication pipeline, a 50% epoch rejection threshold for each task will be used for participant removal. Average ERP waveforms will then be created based on epochs with correct behavioural responses. ERP waveforms will be quantified at the same selection of channels as in Eimer et al. (2003) except for the N170 component. In the original study, N170 was quantified at P7/P8. In the research of face processing, both electrode pairs P7/P8 and PO7/PO8 are usually involved with N170 amplitude measurement (for summaries, see Morgan et al., 2008; Rossion & Jacques, 2008). Despite that N170 is often illustrated at PO7/PO8 in the literature (e.g., Itier et al., 2007; Rossion & Jacques, 2011), interestingly, when being measured on its own (i.e., not measured as peak-to-peak difference from P1; e.g., Tanaka, 2018), N170 typically shows maximal amplitudes at P7/P8 instead of PO7/PO8 (e.g., Eimer, 2011; Gao et al., 2019; Itier et al., 2007; Itier & Taylor, 2004; Kuefner et al., 2010; Prieto et al., 2011). Sometimes, very strong (or even maximal) N170 amplitudes were also observed at PO9/PO10 (e.g., Gao et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2015; Rossion & Jacques, 2008). In publications more recent than Eimer et al. (2003), the most common practice has been combining P7/P8 and PO7/PO8 for N170 amplitude measurement (e.g., Kuefner et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2016; Morgan et al., 2008; Rossion & Jacques, 2008; Tanaka & Jiang, 2024). In the current replication, both electrode pairs P7/P8 and PO7/PO8 are required for data collection therefore available for data analysis. Therefore, for better result stability, in the advanced pipeline, we will measure the average N170 amplitudes over these four locations (P7/P8 and PO7/PO8). For the other five ERP activities, the channels for ERP quantification will remain the same as in Eimer et al. (2003), i.e., F3/Fz/F4, C3/Cz/C4, P3/Pz/P4, P7/P8, and PO7/PO8.

To determine the analysis windows reliably and objectively, the collapsed localizer method (Luck & Gaspelin, 2017) will be implemented separately for each replicating lab's dataset. From each lab's dataset, a localizer waveform will be generated for each electrode cluster (e.g., F3/Fz/F4) by averaging over these electrodes in the group, and across all conditions and participants. Then, for each localizer waveform, the local peak amplitude (the strongest voltage) will be determined within the corresponding analysis window in the original study. For instance, the N170 peak amplitude will be measured between 160 and 200 ms in the localizer waveform averaged over P7/P8/PO7/PO8 across all conditions and participants. Following this protocol, the other five peak-searching windows will be 120-155, 160-215, 220-315, 320-495, and 500-800 ms (identical to the analysis windows in Eimer et al., 2003), each of which will be applied to the five electrode clusters separately (F3/Fz/F4, C3/Cz/C4, P3/Pz/P4, P7/P8/PO7/PO8, and PO7/PO8).

For each ERP activity, once the peak amplitude has been determined in the collapsed localizer waveform from each replicating lab, the boundaries of the meanamplitude-measurement time window will be set at 50% of the peak amplitude (scanning from the peak to the left and right of the waveform). Our approach is a variation of the fractional peak latency procedure used for ERP latency quantification. The fractional peak latency procedure takes the time point where an ERP waveform reaches a certain fraction (e.g., 50%) of the peak amplitude of a component as the onset of that ERP component (Kiesel et al., 2008). This method has been widely and successfully employed in the literature (e.g., Grubert & Eimer, 2023; Kompatsiari et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2025). Our approach adapted this method to locate the two 50%-peak-amplitude points (respectively in the ascending and descending flanks of an ERP component) to determine the mean amplitude measurement window.

REST pipeline

Given that the averaged-mastoid reference may not be ideal for the amplitude measurement of N170 and early frontal positivity (potentially related to the VPP component), we will run an additional pre-processing pipeline employing a better re-referencing process, namely the REST method (Yao, 2001; for the MATLAB plugin, see Dong et al., 2017) to replace the averaged-mastoid re-referencing. All other aspects will remain exactly the same as those of the advanced pipeline. Following this pipeline, only the mean amplitudes of N170 and early frontal positivity will be analysed.

Planned statistical analyses

We will first check the overall accuracy of all participants. Following the criterion used in the practice blocks, any participants showing an accuracy level lower than 80% (the ratio of the number of correctly responded trials to the number of total trials, with trials without responses being treated as incorrect trials) in any of the two tasks will be excluded from further analysis.

Given the within-subjects design, the behavioural and ERP data will be statistically assessed with repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs; with Holm correction applied to all post hoc comparisons) and paired t-tests. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction will be applied to the ANOVAs whenever the sphericity assumption is violated. Outputs with p values smaller than .02 will be accepted as rejection of the null hypotheses. All analyses will be complemented by Bayesian counterparts, namely Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVAs and Bayesian paired t-tests, using the default Cauchy prior distribution, a heavytailed probability distribution which is often used as the default prior distribution for Bayesian analyses as it is suggested to be the simplest option satisfying consistency requirements for hypothesis testing (Jeffreys, 1961; Wetzels et al., 2012). For the Bayesian t-tests, the prior on the standardized effect size δ under the alternative hypothesis is a Cauchy distribution centred on zero with the scale parameter r, that is, δ ~ Cauchy(0,r); under the null hypothesis, δ = 0. The scale parameter (known as prior width in JASP; Love et al., 2019) is set to r = 0.707 for all fixed effects, except otherwise specified for H3 (the emotion effect in N170). For the Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVAs, we follow the default model structure of Rouder et al. (2012) as implemented in JASP (Love et al., 2019). The intercept is given a uniform prior, the residual variance a Jeffreys prior p(σ 2 ) ∝ 1/σ 2 , and the standardized fixed effects are assigned the default ANOVA prior that can be expressed via a g-prior with g ~ Inverse-Gamma(1/2, r 2 /2). This is equivalent to placing a multivariate Cauchy prior with scale r on the standardised fixed effects. The participant random-effect prior width is set to 1, as in the JASP default, and random slopes were included for all within-subject effects except the highest-order interaction. The Bayesian factors will be quantified by comparing across matched models. We will descriptively interpret the Bayesian factor results using the categories summarized by Lee and Wagenmakers (2014). For the BF10 value evidencing the alternative hypothesis (the opposite of the null hypothesis), 1 means no evidence in favour of the alternative hypothesis; 1 to 3 is anecdotal evidence; 3 to 10 is moderate evidence; 10 to 30 is strong evidence; 30 to 100 is very strong evidence; above 100 is extreme evidence. The reciprocal of BF10 (i.e., BF01) will suggest the corresponding evidence in favour of the null hypothesis.

If the frequentist and Bayesian analysis results diverge, we will favour the frequentist interpretation first as it is a direct replication of the original study. The Bayesian results will complement the frequentist interpretation.

Behavioural analysis

Differences in reaction time (RT) and accuracy between the emotion and line tasks will be first assessed using two paired t-tests. For each task, the RT and accuracy data will then be analysed using ANOVAs and Bayesian ANOVAs as stated above. In the emotion task, a two-way ANOVA will be conducted with within-subject factors Emotion (emotional vs. neutral) and Block Type (the six facial expressions). For the line task, an additional factor (Line Type with two levels identical lines vs. different lines) will be included, resulting in a three-way ANOVA.

ERP analysis

For each ERP activity resulting from each of the two pre-processing pipelines, the analysis of mean ERP amplitudes will start with a three-way ANOVA. The three factors are Task (emotion vs. line), Emotion (emotional vs. neutral), and Block Type (the six basic facial expressions). Following any significant main effects of Block Type in these ANOVAs, post hoc tests using Holm corrections will be used. Consistent with the original study, planned two-way ANOVAs will be performed for each task (irrespective of the Task x Emotion interaction outcome). Corresponding Bayesian ANOVAs will also be implemented using a Cauchy prior width of 0.707 except for H3. For H1 and H2 (predictions of positive findings), frequentist analysis outputs with p < .02 in the same direction as in the original study will be accepted as successful replications. BF10 ≥ 3 in the same direction as in the original study and BF10 ≤ 0.333 will be accepted as evidence for the alternative and null hypothesis, respectively. For H3 and H4 (predictions of negative findings), results showing p < .02 or BF10 ≥ 3 in either direction and BF10 ≤ 0.333 will be accepted as evidence for the alternative and null hypothesis, respectively.

Results confirming H1 and H2 should show a significant Task x Emotion interaction in the three-way ANOVA. Planned two-way ANOVAs should demonstrate significantly higher amplitudes for the emotional than neutral faces in the emotion task (Emotion main effect) in the five ERP activities, including frontal positivity 160-215 ms (H1.1), frontocentral positivity 220-315 ms (H1.2), occipitotemporal negativity 220-315 ms (H1.3), frontal-central-parietal positivity 320-495 ms (H1.4), and frontal-central-parietal positivity 500-700 ms (H1.5). The corresponding Emotion main effects are expected to turn out in favour of the null hypothesis in the line task (H2.1 to H2.5).

For H3, no significant Task x Emotion interaction is expected in the frequentist threeway ANOVA. The following two-way ANOVAs should also show no main effect of Emotion in either the emotion or line task. As in the original study, frequentist paired t-tests will be performed to compare the emotional and neutral faces in the emotion task, separately for the six basic emotions (i.e., the six Block Type levels). No significant differences are expected to be found in these t-tests.

In the Bayesian ANOVAs for H3, we will follow the same protocol (three-way ANOVA followed by two-way ANOVAs for the two tasks separately) and take evidence for the null hypothesis as confirmation of H3. One important change to make is that we will set several Cauchy prior widths for the emotion effect following the study of Hinojosa et al. (2015). Their meta-analysis found a significantly stronger N170 in response to emotional faces than to neutral faces (with all emotions combined), displaying a mean Cohen's d of 0.3325. Furthermore, they also found different emotion effect sizes across five emotions in their meta-analysis. Three of the emotion effects were significant, namely angry vs. neutral (Cohen's d = 0.2062), fearful vs. neutral (0.4219), and happy vs. neutral (0.2552); and two were non-significant, namely disgusted vs. neutral (0.4217, but with a large confidence interval that included zero) and sad vs. neutral (0.0769). Taking these results together, we observed that the plausible emotion effect in N170 is likely to be relatively small (in a range between 0.2062 and 0.4219), and that the emotion effect size is uncertain depending on the specific emotion -these probably were the reasons why Eimer et al. (2003) did not show any significant emotion effect in N170. These two observations suggest that the N170 data probably will not be highly informative, so that the different prior widths can reasonably lead to different Bayes factors (Depaoli et al., 2020; Vanpaemel, 2010).

Therefore, in the Bayesian ANOVAs for H3, we will specify three Cauchy prior widths for the Emotion factor to replace the default prior width (r = 0.707), corresponding to the reported values from Hinojosa et al. (2015) (the prior widths for all the other fixed effects will be kept at the default value of 0.707). The three prior widths are r = 0.3325, 0.2062, and 0.4219, at which the prior median absolute effect size (i.e., the value for which P(|δ| < r) = .50) equals respectively the overall, the smallest, and the largest emotion effect reported in the meta-analysis of Hinojosa et al. (2015). This protocol reflects our expectation that the plausible effect sizes are more concentrated near zero than the default Cauchy prior width of 0.707. The default scale parameter (prior width) of r = 0.707 assigns 65.7% of the prior probability to absolute effects larger than d = 0.4219, the largest emotion effect reported by Hinojosa et al. (2015), whereas r = 0.3325, 0.2062 and 0.4219 assign 42.5%, 28.9% and 50.0% respectively. For consistency, the follow-up two-way ANOVAs (for the two tasks separately) will use the same prior as in the starting three-way ANOVA, despite that Hinojosa et al. (2015) reported significantly larger effect size in the non-direct task (attention directed away from faces, Cohen's d = 0.3574) than in the direct task (attention directed to faces, d = 0.2025). Given that these two d values are within or very close to the range of our chosen prior widths (0.2062, 0.3325, 0.4219), we do not expect to encounter any potential issue in our protocol. Afterwards, in the emotion task, as in the plan for the frequentist analysis, we will follow up each Bayesian ANOVA with six Bayesian paired t-tests for the six emotions, all using the same prior setting as in the preceding ANOVA.

With these three specified Cauchy prior widths (nothing else will be changed in the Bayesian ANOVAs), we will run Bayesian sensitivity analysis (not to be confused with the sensitivity power analysis) to check the robustness of the Bayesian ANOVA results for N170. If the Bayesian evidence classification (e.g., anecdotal, moderate, strong) does not change across priors, we will conclude the Bayesian results to be robust. Alternatively, we will report the results as prior-sensitive. This approach will help us confidently conclude whether there is an emotion effect in N170 and the plausible effect size.

H4 is relevant to all the analyses planned above. The absence of the Block Type difference (i.e., no differences among the six emotions) in all the frequentist and Bayesian ANOVAs described above can be considered support for H4. However, it is important to note that, in the design of the original study, each block contains both emotional and neutral faces with equal probabilities. The inclusion of the neutral face data (50% of the dataset) in the analysis will reduce the power to detect facial expression differences. Hence, to directly test H4, instead of relying on the exact replication analyses mentioned above, we will run dedicated ANOVAs (both frequentists and Bayesian) with only the emotional face data to directly compare the six emotions. This will remove the Emotion (emotional vs. neutral) factor and turn the analysis into a two-way ANOVA of Task x Emotion Type. The factor Emotion Type has six levels corresponding to the six emotion types (we will not use the name "Block Type" anymore given that no full data from any block will be used).

Furthermore, it is difficult to directly run a meta-analysis on the effect of Emotion, which has six levels and does not have direction information. Therefore, we will adopt a different approach for the assessment of H4. For each of the six ERP activities (the five listed in H1 plus N170), we will run the frequentist Task x Emotion Type ANOVA (still only using the emotional face data) on the whole sample across all replicating labs, then follow up with a planned one-way ANOVA (only frequentist) for each task. This is to check the possibility of any evidence supporting the cross-emotion differences in ERPs. Any significant (p < .02) one-way ANOVAs will be accepted as indications of potential ERP differences among the emotions. We will then analyse individual replicating labs' data (emotional trials only) with paired t-tests (both frequentist and Bayesian) contrasting all emotion pair combinations. The Bayesian prior width will be set to 0.707.

Meta-analysis

To evaluate the replication success of the original study, meta-analyses of ERP data will be carried out following the #EEGManyLabs protocol (Pavlov et al., 2021). Specifically, we will run meta-analyses for the ERP data directly corresponding to the hypotheses, and will not run meta-analyses for behaviour data or any other ERP data. Table 3 summarizes the individual analysis results that will be entered into the meta-analyses. The frequentist analysis results will be entered into frequentist meta-analyses, whereas the Bayesian analysis results will be put into Bayesian meta-analyses. In cases where the frequentist and Bayesian analyses diverge, we will prioritize the frequentist interpretation complemented by the Bayesian results as evidence for or against the effect.

From the frequentist analysis results in each replicating lab's dataset, effect size (Cohen's d) and its standard error (SE) will be calculated for each hypothesis and put into a random-effects meta-analysis using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method, with the random effect being the replicating labs. This approach will address the heterogeneity in the datasets (due to differences in EEG systems, research practice, ethnicity/race, cultural influence, language being used, and samples). For each positive hypothesis, the replication will be accepted as successful if the meta-analytic estimate across all replicating labs is significant (p < .02) and in the hypothesized effect direction. For each negative hypothesis, we define successful replication as a non-significant meta-analytic estimate (p > .02). The values to be reported include point estimate, distribution of the weighted effect sizes, 98% confidence intervals, and heterogeneity (τ² ).

For the Bayesian meta-analyses of replication results, we will extract standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d) and their associated uncertainty (credible intervals) from each individual dataset. These will be entered into a Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis to estimate the pooled effect size and between-study heterogeneity. For each meta-analysis, we will specify a normal prior centered at 0 with SD = 0.5 on the overall effect and a weakly informative prior on the heterogeneity parameter (half-Cauchy with a scale of 0.5). Replication success for positive findings will be evaluated by examining whether the pooled posterior distribution supports an effect in the predicted direction and whether the Bayes Factor in favor of the alternative hypothesis (BF₁₀) exceeds 3, which is consistent with moderate-to-strong evidence. Replication success for negative findings (null hypotheses) will be demonstrated by BF10 ≤ 0.333. This approach will allow us to quantify the cumulative evidence across replication datasets while accounting for uncertainty. We will report the pooled effect (µ), posterior distribution, 98% confidence intervals, and heterogeneity (τ² ).

Exploratory LMM analysis

One cross-lab difference of significant theoretical importance is participant ethnicity. Face processing typically demonstrates own-race bias, a perceptual and social bias leading to better processing of faces from the ethnic background of a participant (Anzures et al., 2013; Rossion & Michel, 2011). In the current study, the face stimuli are only showing faces of white people, whereas participants will have varied ethnic backgrounds from multiple laboratories across the globe. This is likely to have an impact on the findings. The heterogeneity estimates of the meta-analyses will inform us on the existence of such an impact. To further address this impact and to make better use of the trial-level data, we will record ethnicity information from each participant, and (if statistically feasible) fit trial-level linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) as exploratory analysis alongside the planned metaanalyses. Another important cross-lab difference is language, which may also modulate face processing in the current study (e.g., Liu et al., 2023). However, we will not be able to obtain enough samples to address the language effects, especially given that it will be highly correlated (confounded) with replicating labs and ethnicity. Therefore, we will not assess the influence of language.

Therefore, the exploratory LMM analysis will include the experimental factors (Task, Emotion, Block Type), ethnicity, and their interaction(s) as fixed effects; random intercepts for labs and participants nested within labs; and random slopes of the experimental factors by lab and participant. If the model fails to converge or is singular, we will remove randomeffect correlations and then random slopes as necessary, retaining random intercepts where possible. Any deviations will be reported as exploratory. In the emotion task, participants will be asked to judge whether the faces are emotional or neutral. In the line task, participants will judge whether the line segments have the same length. Manual responses are to be made as quickly and accurately as possible within a 2,000-ms window.
