Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

People may use different processes to judge their current versus expected well-being. Using Wave 1 of the Global Flourishing Study (N = 98,412; 22 countries), we examined how predictors spanning past experiences, current circumstances, and identity and personality were associated with current and expected (five-year) well-being. We used stochastic search variable selection, a Bayesian method, to identify predictors reliably associated with each outcome, and focused interpretation on those with at least small effects. Participants generally expected their future well-being to exceed their current well-being. When each judgment was adjusted for the other, age and hope for the future showed opposite patterns: older participants reported lower, and more hopeful participants higher, expected well-being when controlling for current well-being.

Much of the explained variance was shared across domains, and past experiences contributed little unique variance once current circumstances and identity and personality were accounted for; this suggests that the past may matter for well-being judgments largely through its links to people's present lives. Our findings demonstrate that current and expected well-being draw on largely overlapping information that is weighted differently depending on whether people evaluate their current or expected lives.

Expected wellbeing may therefore draw on much of the same information as current well-being, weighted differently, with greater reliance on factors such as general optimism, beliefs about one's prospects, and the amount of time one expects to have ahead (Busseri et al., 2009; Carstensen, 2006; Lang et al., 2013).

Individuals often expect their future well-being to surpass their current well-being.

People generally perceive their lives as improving from the past to the present and expect continued gains into the future (Röcke & Lachman, 2008). Similarly, individuals anticipate experiencing more positive than negative future events (Newby-Clark & Ross, 2003). This tendency may reflect a broader optimism bias, whereby people systematically judge their future outcomes more favorably than their current circumstances (Weinstein, 1980; Sharot, 2011).

Longitudinal evidence shows that individuals report higher expected well-being than current well-being, despite the relative stability of current well-being over time (Frijters et al., 2009).

The magnitude and direction of expected well-being judgments vary with age. Younger and middle-aged adults generally expect improvement in their well-being, whereas older adults tend to expect stability or decline across several well-being domains (Ryff, 1991; Lang et al., 2013; Hong et al., 2019).

Expectations about the future are linked to later health and well-being: anticipated positive personal events have been shown to explain variance in depression beyond current wellbeing (Edmondson et al., 2014) and, among adolescents, positive expectations of the future are associated with predicted healthier behaviors in young adulthood (McDade et al., 2011).

Research on expected well-being specifically, however, has mostly examined it in relation to current well-being. For example, this work has shown that, at the national level, the gap between expected and current well-being is larger in countries with less favorable societal functioning (Busseri, 2022). Less is known about which factors are associated with expected well-being itself, and whether they match those associated with current well-being. This question matters for two reasons. First, the meaning of the gap between current and expected well-being depends partly on what expected well-being reflects; if expected well-being draws on information that current well-being does not, the gap carries that information as well. Second, expected wellbeing is often combined with current well-being in measures of population life evaluation wellbeing (e.g., Gallup, 2022), yet it is unclear what it contributes beyond current well-being. As such, evidence on where the correlates of the two judgments converge or diverge would help clarify whether expected well-being largely restates current well-being or whether it captures aspects of people's lives that current well-being judgments miss.

Predictors of Well-Being Judgments

To organize the broader range of variables that have been linked to well-being, we classified predictors into three conceptually distinct domains: past experiences, current circumstances, and identity and personality. We included variables related to early life experiences or historical factors in the past experiences domain, including health growing up and childhood abuse. We included variables related to present-day life conditions in the current circumstances domain, including age and physical health. We included variables related to personality traits, psychological factors, and beliefs in the identity/personality domain, including extraversion and purpose in life. Although variables within each of these domains are known to predict current well-being, the same cannot be said for expected well-being.

Past Experiences

Past experiences provide individuals with autobiographical information that may serve as one information source for current and expected well-being. Major historical events, like the COVID-19 pandemic, and childhood experiences, including childhood health, religious upbringing, parental relationships, adverse experiences, and living conditions, are associated with current subjective well-being in adulthood (Pitzer & Fingerman, 2010; Gassman-Pines et al., 2020; Bradshaw et al., 2007; Rohner & Veneziano, 2001; Amato, 1994; Wnuk 2021; Bamford et al. 2023; Rothwell & Davoodi, 2024; Chen & VanderWeele 2018; Gohm et al., 1998; Waters et al., 2008; Sobolewski & Amato, 2005). Past positive events are associated with more positive expectations of one's future well-being, whereas past negative events are associated with more negative expectations (Sailer et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2014).

Current Circumstances

Individuals with better physical health, education, financial security, stronger social connections, stable employment, and community belonging generally report higher current wellbeing, whereas health problems and other life stressors are associated with lower current wellbeing (Haim-Litevsky, 2023; Diener & Chan, 2011; Hernandez et al., 2018; Bonanomi & Rosina, 2022; Minichino et al., 2013; Santini et al., 2020). Demographic characteristics such as gender have also been linked to well-being, although these relationships are often more complex and may reflect broader social and contextual influences (Matud et al., 2019; Pinquart & Sörensen, 2001). Whether these same circumstances contribute similarly to expected well-being judgments is less clear. However, recent evidence suggests that income and education may play a particularly important role in expected well-being judgments because they increase individuals' perceived capacity to shape future outcomes (Mandel et al., 2026).

Identity and personality

The identity and personality domain reflects relatively stable aspects of the individual, including demographic identities (e.g., gender, religious affiliation), personality traits, and enduring psychological dispositions. These characteristics may shape how individuals interpret their lives. Schimmack et al. (2002) proposed that well-being judgments are in part based on chronically accessible sources of information; that is, information that is consistently retrieved when people evaluate their lives (e.g., reliance on relationship status and academic performance).

Personality traits help determine which sources remain chronically accessible over time, thereby influencing which information is most readily retrieved and how that information is evaluated when forming well-being judgments (Schimmack et al., 2002). Thus, a person's identity and personality may shape how they interpret their life circumstances, which then influences their global evaluations of their life.

A broad range of stable characteristics are associated with current well-being. Positive psychological characteristics, including positive affect, religiosity, and personality traits such as conscientiousness, openness to experience, and emotional stability, are positively associated with current well-being judgments (Sophie et al., 2022; Alkozei et al., 2018; Anglim et al., 2020; Villani et al., 2019). In contrast, anxiety, depression, and loneliness show negative associations (Seifert, 2024; Cao et al., 2025). Because these characteristics influence how people interpret the present moment, they may also shape how individuals imagine and evaluate their future life. For example, individuals with more positive conceptions of aging tend to expect their future wellbeing to exceed their current well-being, and these conceptions partially explain the association between age and the current-expected well-being gap (Busseri, 2024). In Choma et al.'s (2014) study, individuals who reported higher expected well-being relative to their current well-being reported less hope, which they attributed to wishful thinking.

Although one might alternatively organize these factors according to past, present, and future, future-oriented constructs such as optimism and hope are better conceptualized as relatively stable individual characteristics that influence how people interpret and project their lives into the future. Accordingly, we include them within the identity and personality domain in this study.

The Present Study

Prior work has largely examined predictors of current or expected well-being within a single domain or in isolation. This leaves open the question of how distinct predictor domains, including past experiences, current circumstances, and identity and personality, jointly and differentially relate to each type of judgment across diverse cultural contexts. The present study used data from Wave 1 of the Global Flourishing Study (GFS; Johnson et al., 2024) to identify which well-being-related factors, organized across three domains (past circumstances, identity and personality, and current circumstances), most consistently predicted current versus expected well-being judgments.

Given the large number of candidate predictors within each domain, our preregistered hypotheses (OSF registration DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/VMTK9) were stated at the domain level. First, we hypothesized that positive past experiences would be positively associated with both current and expected well-being. Second, we hypothesized that positive aspects of identity and personality would be positively associated with current well-being. Third, we hypothesized that positive current circumstances would be positively associated with current well-being. We also preregistered two exploratory questions: whether past experiences would be associated with expected well-being when controlling for current well-being, and whether current circumstances would be associated with current well-being when controlling for expected well-being. We extended these exploratory analyses to all three domains and both outcomes. Together, these analyses identify where the correlates of current and expected well-being converge and diverge, as a step toward understanding the information people draw on when evaluating their present and future lives.

Method Data

This study used data from Wave 1 of the Global Flourishing Study (GFS). The GFS is a longitudinal study that examines factors contributing to human flourishing in a large, internationally diverse sample spanning 22 countries and territories, with approximately 200,000 participants enrolled at Wave 1 (Johnson et al., 2024; VanderWeele et al., 2025). Wave 1 data were made available to researchers following preregistration of the research design through the project's Open Science Framework (OSF). Additional details regarding study design, sampling procedures, survey administration, and data access are available through the Global Flourishing Study documentation and OSF repository (codebook: www.cos.io/gfs and on the OSF project page at https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/3JTZ8).

All eligible Wave 1 respondents in the finalized analysis dataset were included. Analyses were conducted using complete cases for the variables entering each model. The main analytic sample consisted of 98,412 participants (50,465 female [51.3%], 47,916 male [48.7%]; 31 participants did not report gender), with a mean age of 46.42 years (SD = 17.62, range = 18-99).

Missing data

Our preregistration specified listwise deletion without imputation. During analysis, we found that much of the missingness in several variables did not reflect genuine non-response: our cleaning script had coded substantive responses such as "Not relevant" and "Unsure" as missing, along with skips and true non-responses. For six variables (CRITICAL, LIFE_APPROACH, GOD_PUNISH, TELL_BELIEFS, COMFORT_REL, LOVED_BY_GOD), these responses made up approximately 30-44% of the sample. Combined with listwise deletion across the full 95-variable predictor set, this retained only 18% of respondents.

We addressed this by revising the variable set rather than introducing unpreregistered imputation. Excluding the six variables increased the analytic sample to 98,412 respondents (48.5%). Results were highly consistent across specifications: in the full SSVS models, every predictor selected under the preregistered specification was also selected under the revised specification except one (trait anxiousness, "anxious, easily upset," for current well-being); no predictor changed effect direction, and the same predictors met the magnitude threshold. The revised specification selected additional predictors, all with small effects (|Avg Nonzero β| < .10). Given the larger, more representative sample and the lack of meaningful difference in results, we report the 98,412-participant specification as our primary analysis; results for the preregistered specification (n = 36, 576) are reported in Supplementary Tables S8-S11.

A related issue affected nine variables on childhood and parental relationships (e.g., feeling loved by one's father growing up, mother's religious service attendance at age 12), where "does not apply" (plausibly reflecting the absence of a parental figure) was also coded as missing. We retained these variables in both specifications, so respondents for whom they did not apply were excluded by listwise deletion. We did not recode responses for two reasons: their missingness was substantially lower than for the six variables described above and representing them would require substantive decisions beyond the scope of this study. We note this as a limitation.

Outcome Variables

Current well-being was assessed using the Life Evaluation: Today item from the Global Flourishing Study. Participants were asked, "On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?" Responses ranged from 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life), with higher scores indicating greater current well-being. Expected well-being was assessed using the Life Evaluation: Five Years From Now item. Participants were asked, "Just your best guess, on which step do you think you will stand in the future, say about five years from now?" Responses ranged from 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life), with higher scores indicating greater expected well-being.

Predictor Variables

Predictor variables were organized into three preregistered domains: past experiences, current circumstances, and identity and personality (Table 1). Of the 95 candidate predictors, 89 were retained in the primary analytic models reported in the main text (see Missing Data): 14 belonging to the past experiences domain, 43 to the current circumstances domain, and 32 to the identity/personality domain. We did not include country-specific/conditional response variables.

Past experiences included variables that represent early life experiences or historical factors, such as adverse childhood experiences and health growing up. Current circumstances included variables related to present-day life conditions, such as socioeconomic status and health. Identity and personality included variables reflecting personal traits, psychological factors, and beliefs, such as belonging and extraversion. Analytical Strategy

Analyses followed a preregistered protocol (DOI: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/VMTK9). We used Search Variable Selection (SSVS; George & McCulloch, 1993) implemented in the SSVS R package (Bainter et al., 2026). SSVS samples across the space of possible predictor combinations and estimates each predictor's marginal inclusion probability (MIP), the proportion of sampled models that include it, along with posterior estimates of effect direction and magnitude (George & McCulloch, 1993; Bainter et al., 2020). We chose SSVS over the penalized regression approaches used elsewhere in this project (see deviations from the pre-registered analytic plan) because it provides posterior inclusion probabilities for individual predictors rather than only group-level selection (see Bainter et al., 2023, for a comparison with lasso-type methods).

For each predictor, we report three posterior summaries with 95% credible intervals. The MIP indicates how reliably a predictor was selected. The average beta is the mean coefficient across all posterior draws, including those in which the predictor was excluded, and serves as a shrinkage-adjusted estimate that combines selection uncertainty and effect size. The average nonzero beta is the mean coefficient across only the draws in which the predictor was included and estimates effect size conditional on inclusion.

Consistent with our preregistration, we report predictors with MIP > 0.50 and we do not compute Bayes factors or p-values. We also flag predictors with |average nonzero beta| > .10 to focus interpretations on the most substantial effects. This value corresponds to the conventional lower bound for a small effect in individual differences research, although these guidelines are not specific to SSVS (Gignac & Szodorai, 2016; Funder & Ozer, 2019). We applied this to standardized partial coefficients. This flag is a descriptive aid and was not preregistered or used as an inferential criterion; smaller associations may still be meaningful, and we report all predictors with MIP > .50 regardless of effect size in the supplementary materials.

We entered all predictors simultaneously into models for two outcomes:, current and expected well-being. Each outcome was modeled twice: unadjusted and residualized on the other well-being measure via ordinary least squares (OLS). The residualized models test whether predictors retained evidence of association beyond the variance the two outcomes share.

Together, these made four full models. We repeated this procedure within each preregistered predictor domain (past experiences, current circumstances, and identity and personality). The resulting 12 domain-specific models test whether results held when predictors were evaluated within their own domain rather than against the full set.

No observations were excluded based on univariate or multivariate outlier flags, except for impossible values identified through the study codebook. Primary analyses were unweighted because SSVS does not readily accommodate complex survey weights.

Post-selection descriptive tests

To quantify each preregistered domain's relative contribution, we nested linear models using the predictors selected (MIP >.50) in the full SSVS models, in which all predictors competed jointly for inclusion. Given that the number of predictors selected differed across domains and no entry order could be justified a priori, we used leave-one-domain-out comparisons instead of a standard hierarchical regression. For each outcome, we fit a full model containing all selected predictors and three reduced models, each omitting one domain. Each domain's unique contribution was the difference in R² between the full and corresponding reduced model (ΔR²). This approach credits each domain with the unique variance it explains and does not depend on an arbitrary entry order. We report R², ΔR², and nested-model F-tests (F, df, p) for each domain and outcome; as an indicator of whether each ΔR² estimate differed significantly from zero, we report its associated F-tests

Deviations from the pre-registered analytic plan

The preregistered analytic plan specified three variable selection approaches: grouppenalized regression, sparse group lasso, and Bayesian stochastic search variable selection (SSVS). The original goal was to triangulate findings across complementary statistical frameworks and to identify predictions that showed consistent evidence across methods. During implementation, however, we determined that the two penalized regression approaches were not sufficiently aligned with the study's primary inferential intent.

The study was designed to evaluate evidence for individual predictors of current and expected well-being and to compare predictor-specific patterns across outcomes. However, the penalized group models were primarily optimized for prediction, and their selections were extremely sensitive to the broad groupings of predictors, correlations among conceptually similar variables, and the choice of regularization parameters. Given the large sample size, non-zero coefficients did not provide useful evidence for specific predictor variables and, frankly, merely showed that variables in these domains were predictive of our outcomes.

We therefore report the SSVS analyses as the primary analytic approach. SSVS more directly addresses the predictor-specific questions of our study by providing posterior inclusion probabilities for individual variables as well as posterior estimates of direction and uncertainty.

The preregistered predictor domains, outcome specifications, covariate adjustments, and posterior inclusion probability threshold of .5 were retained.

Because the final analyses provided here relied on a single modeling framework, we did not apply the preregistered criteria for convergence or robustness across methods. Conclusions are instead based on the posterior evidence obtained from the SSVS models and should be interpreted as conditional on that framework. The decision to omit the penalized models reflected closer evaluation of the alignment between the methods and the research question, rather than whether the omitted analyses supported the study hypotheses. The development and revision of the analytic code, including the preregistered implementations of all three methods, are preserved in the project's timestamped GitHub commit history. Additionally, results from the group penalized regression analysis and the group lasso are presented in the supplementary materials.

Twelve preregistered variables were excluded from the analyses because they were categorical or country-specific and could not be entered as single continuous predictors: country of residence, household income, employment status, political identification, region (three variables), religious affiliation (three variables), and self-identification (two variables).

Household income was recorded in country-specific categories; perceived difficulty living on present income and household expenses were included as indicators of financial circumstances.

In an exploratory analysis that was not preregistered, we examined whether the key associations reflected differences between countries. We centered all variables within country and reestimated each full model using ordinary least squares regression, including the predictors selected in the corresponding SSVS model.

Discussion

Interpretation of Results

Our preregistered hypotheses were broad. We expected favorable past experiences, favorable aspects of identity and personality, and favorable current circumstances to be associated with higher current and expected well-being. We evaluated these hypotheses through the predictors selected in the SSVS models, and through each domain's unique contribution in the nested model comparisons. Overall, the hypotheses were supported, although the strength of that support differed considerably across domains.

Identity and Personality

We found support for the identity/personality hypothesis. The domain contributed unique variance to current well-being and expected well-being; predictors remained associated with each outcome after controlling for the other. Happiness and life satisfaction were the strongest and most consistently selected predictors of both outcomes across the full and residualized models. Additionally, hope for the future and expecting more good than bad were associated with expected well-being. Personality traits were frequently selected but had small coefficients.

The predictors that exceeded the magnitude threshold share content with the outcome itself.

Happiness and life satisfaction are established indicators of subjective well-being (Diener et al., 1984; Oishi et al., 2021), and hope and optimism concern expectations about one's future, which is what expected well-being asks respondents to judge. Their strong associations therefore partly reflect conceptual overlap with the outcome.

Current Circumstances

The hypothesis that favorable current circumstances would be associated with higher current well-being was also supported. Greater difficulty living on present income was associated with lower current well-being, and other current circumstances variables, including physical health, education, and worthwhileness, were selected with smaller coefficients. Our exploratory analyses showed that current circumstances were also associated with expected wellbeing. After controlling for current well-being, the current circumstances domain contributed nearly twice as much unique variance to expected well-being as identity/personality did. This suggests that expectations of more or less improvement in well-being than others with the same current well-being depend more on circumstances with clear implications for the future, such as age, health, and finances, than on past experiences and stable dispositions.

Domain contributions

Altogether, the nested model comparisons suggest that the three domains are better understood as overlapping sources of well-being. In both unadjusted models, most of the explained variance was shared across domains, and this pattern held when the analysis was restricted to predictors with MIP > .95. People's past experiences, current circumstances, and identity/personality appear to be intertwined, and a large part of what predicts current and expected well-being cannot be attributed to any single domain. The predictors most consistently exceeding the |β| > .10 threshold across models (happiness, life satisfaction, and, for expected well-being, hope for the future) are conceptually related evaluative constructs; their empirical overlap likely accounts for their consistent selection and comparatively large effect sizes.

Although a broad range of past, present, and identity-related factors showed reliable evidence of association with well-being, only a small subset, largely items that share content with the outcomes, show effects of a magnitude likely to be practically meaningful.

Individual Predictors

At the individual-predictor level, happiness and life satisfaction were associated with both outcomes in the unadjusted models and were the strongest correlates of current well-being.

Their associations with expected well-being fell below the magnitude threshold once current well-being was controlled for. In other words, happiness and life satisfaction were associated with expected well-being mainly through their association with current well-being.

Age exhibited opposite associations with the two residualized outcomes in both the full and domain-specific models. Among participants with the same current well-being, older participants reported lower expected well-being; among participants with the same expected well-being, older participants reported higher current well-being. Thus, compared with younger participants, older participants' ratings of their expected well-being were closer to, or lower than, the ratings of their current well-being.

In the residualized domain-specific models, we found that, among participants with the same current well-being, those who reported greater hope for the future reported higher expected well-being; among participants with the same expected well-being, they reported lower current well-being. That is, participants with greater hope for the future rated their expected well-being further above their current well-being. This finding appears to diverge from the aforementioned findings of Choma et al. (2014), in which participants with lower hope perceived greater expected well-being than current well-being. However, our measures differ (life evaluation vs.

life satisfaction; hope for the future as a single item vs. hope as a full scale), and they used latent growth curve analyses instead. Beyond life satisfaction and happiness, feelings about one's income, household income growing up, health growing up, and worthwhileness were the most consistently selected predictors for current well-being, though only in the domain-specific SSVS models. For expected well-being, expecting good things in life, gratitude, physical health, and worthwhileness were the most consistently selected predictors, though only in the domainspecific SSVS models.

Implications

The distinct predictor profiles identified for each outcome provide empirical support for treating current and expected well-being as related but distinct judgments. Expected well-being judgments carry independent associations with health, behavior, traits, and life outcomes beyond current well-being (Lang et al., 2013; Edmondson et al., 2014; McDade et al., 2011), and reflect a distinct forward-looking judgment process through which people construct imagined futures (Gilbert & Wilson, 2007).

Our findings regarding the relationship between age and current and expected well-being consistent with prior work, in that younger and middle-aged adults generally expect higher wellbeing in the future, whereas older adults tend to expected lower well-being (Ryff, 1991; Röcke & Lachman, 2008; Lang et al., 2013; Hong et al., 2019). Our findings extend this work in two ways. First, age remained among the strongest predictors of current and expected well-being when evaluated alongside 88 other predictors. Second, the pattern held when differences between countries were removed, which indicates that it is not specific to a few national contexts.

Socioemotional selectivity theory proposes that as perceived time remaining in life narrows, motivational priorities shift from future-oriented expansion toward present-focused emotional regulation and meaning (Carstensen, 2006; Carstensen, 2021). A shrinking time horizon may similarly recalibrate what a well-being judgment represents for an older versus a younger respondent.

Additionally, prior research demonstrates that people's conceptions of aging partially account for the relationship between age and expected well-being (Busseri, 2024).

Therefore, we posit that age's association with well-being judgments may in part reflect normative beliefs about aging and shifts in motivational orientation.

Current and expected well-being judgments may share a common evaluative process instead of reflecting fundamentally distinct cognitive operations. However, the divergence in specific predictors and their magnitude across outcomes suggests that the specific sources of information weighted within that process may differ depending on the temporal dimension of life evaluation (ie., now versus in five years). Experimental and process-level research would be needed to directly test this interpretation.

Limitations

These interpretations are constrained by several methodological limitations. First, the cross-sectional design reflects associations at a single time point and cannot establish whether identified predictors causally influence well-being judgments. Second, analyses were conducted on complete cases (N = 98,412 of 202, 897 surveyed); missing data patterns may limit the representativeness of findings within the broader GFS sample (see Missing Data). Relatedly, several country-or religion-specific items were excluded from analyses due to their conditional administration, which may limit the study's ability to capture culturally or religion-specific predictors of well-being. Third, primary analyses were conducted without sampling weights, since the regularization-based methods used here do not accommodate complex survey design features; this may limit generalizability at the population level. Fourth, both outcomes and most predictors rely on self-report, and shared method variance may inflate observed associations.

Fifth, while SSVS reliably identifies predictors that consistently appear across posterior samples, it identifies associations rather than mechanisms; the present study cannot speak to the cognitive or psychological process through which identified predictors influence well-being judgments.

Finally, our analyses pooled respondents from 22 countries, and the variable selection did not account for country of residence. Although the key associations were similar in within-country OLS models, those models used the predictors selected from the pooled sample, so we cannot rule out that selection itself would differ within countries. Future country-specific analyses will help clarify how consistent these patterns are across cultural contexts.

Despite these limitations, the present study demonstrates the utility of data-driven variable selection approaches for identifying reliable predictors of well-being outcomes across large, high-dimensional datasets. This approach may complement traditional theory-driven hypothesis testing in well-being research.
