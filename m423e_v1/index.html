Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

How can two individuals posing the same question to a conversational model receive different answers, and what are the implications for our understanding of truth and reality testing? The human search for truth has taken various forms: truth as revealed, investigated through science, encountered psychologically, and, today, approached through probability and computation. We distinguish between truth, relative truth, and reality testing, proposing that reality testing relies on an "outside" grounded in objective criteria. Yet conversational models do not merely retrieve information; they formulate a representation of reality within an encounter with a specific inquirer. The user's input and history participate in shaping the response, such that the "outside" returns tailored to their world. The novelty lies not in algorithmic mediation as such, but in the structural shift in the relationship between subject, representation, and outside. We propose the concept of Particularized Truth: neither a new ontological category nor a mere adjustment of style, but a form in which a claim about reality becomes present for a specific subject within an algorithmic encounter, influencing content and recommendations. Research on sycophancy provides empirical support for aspects of this adaptive process. Under some conditions, such adaptation may shift the interaction from the demands of the reality principle toward the pleasure principle-the wish that the world offer no resistance. We therefore propose an expansion of reality testing in the AI era, examining how representations are generated and their capacity to diverge from the user's existing perspective. Reality testing requires an outside, even when the outside has learned to speak our language.

1. Truth, Reality, and the Encounter with the Algorithm

The question of what is real and how it can be known has accompanied humanity since the dawn of culture. Human beings have sought to distinguish between truth and falsehood, between appearance and reality, between belief and knowledge, and between what is experienced within themselves and what exists in the world outside them. The answers have changed throughout history: truth was conceived as something revealed to humans from a divine or transcendent source, as a product of thought and reason, as a conclusion tested through observation and experiment, and as a psychological truth emerging from reflection on human experience. Despite the profound differences among these paths, they share the assumption that humans seek to direct their knowledge toward something that is not determined solely by them. In recent years, and all the more so with the emergence of AI-based conversational models, a new factor has been added to the human encounter with the question of truth. For the first time, millions of people have at their disposal a system that can be addressed in natural language on nearly any topic and receive a coherent, articulate, and persuasive response within seconds. This is not merely a database or a search engine presenting existing sources. In every encounter, the system generates a response through computation based on vast amounts of linguistic data and the specific context of the conversation. In certain systems, memory of prior interactions and accumulated user data are also incorporated, such that the response can be adapted to an increasing degree to the specific inquirer. The appearance of the algorithm does not eliminate the question of truth, nor does it render all truth relative. Nor is probabilistic calculation itself a revolution in the human relationship to truth: science has long utilized calculations, probabilities, and statistical models to evaluate claims about reality. The change we seek to examine occurs elsewhere. The conversational system does not merely calculate or retrieve information; it formulates a representation of reality within an encounter with a specific individual. Their question, phrasing, provided context, and at times accumulated user-specific context become part of the input from which the response is generated. Hence arises the central question of this article: What happens to a person's relationship to truth and to their reality testing when one of the primary means through which they attempt to understand reality is a system capable of tailoring the representation of reality to them? This does not imply that the algorithm creates a new truth for each individual, or that an independent reality no longer exists. But if the response is contingent upon the context provided by the individual as well as accumulated user-specific context, what, then, is the status of a response that ostensibly comes from the outside, yet also bears traces of the person seeking to know the world through it?

To examine this question, a clarification of three concepts that are sometimes conflated is first required: truth, the relationship between the human perspective and truth, and reality testing.

2. Truth, Relative Truth, and Reality Testing

The concept of truth is among the oldest and most contested in philosophy. Throughout history, human thought has oscillated between conceptions of truth as relativedependent on perspective, context, and the limits of human knowledge-and the search for an absolute or universal "Truth" independent of the specific knower. This article does not seek to resolve these debates, but rather to propose a concise conceptual framework that allows for the examination of the transformation brought about by the encounter with algorithmic systems. In its most basic sense, one can speak of objective truth as the assumption that a reality exists independently of the way a specific individual perceives it. This stance is close to the intuition underlying correspondence theories of truth: a true claim is not true merely because we believe it, because it is persuasive, or because it aligns with our worldview, but because a certain relation holds between it and the way things actually are. In this sense, the world is not required to agree with us. It may disprove our beliefs, surprise us, and compel us to revise what we thought we knew. Yet the possible existence of an independent reality does not mean that human access to it is unmediated. Humans do not meet the world from a neutral vantage point. They meet it through their senses, language, concepts, culture, memories, relationships, and personal and social history. In this sense, one can speak of relative or perspectival truth-not necessarily as the claim that there is no truth outside of perspective, but as the recognition that the human way of knowing and formulating truth always takes place from within a perspective. This distinction is particularly important for our purposes. There is a difference between the claim "there is no truth, only points of view" and the claim "a truth may exist, but all human access to it passes through a point of view." The present article is not required to adopt the former; the latter suffices to recognize that humans are never merely observers of the world, but also its interpreters. Alongside these two positions, a third dimension may be added: the pragmatic dimension of truth. Truth is not merely an abstract philosophical concept. Human beings require it in order to act. They make medical decisions, choose whom to trust, interpret relationships, assess danger, make professional choices, and guide their behavior based on what they perceive as real. Within the pragmatist tradition, the link between truth, inquiry, experience, and the practical consequences of beliefs within life was emphasized. Without equating truth solely with utility, one can say that the question of truth always carries a practical dimension: our perception of reality influences how we act within it. This is where the concept of reality testing becomes relevant. Unlike truth, which is a philosophical and epistemological concept, reality testing is first and foremost a psychological concept. Its psychoanalytic roots can be traced to Freud, who described the transition from the pleasure principle to the reality principle as a process in which the mind must take external reality into account and test its representations against it (Freud, 1911). Later psychoanalytic literature emphasized that reality testing is not simply a binary capacity that is either "intact" or "impaired," but a complex and fluctuating mental activity (Abend, 1982). In its broad clinical sense, it refers to the individual's capacity to differentiate between the inner world and external reality and to test thoughts, wishes, fears, and fantasies against what exists outside the self. For the purposes of the present discussion, we propose to cautiously expand the concept beyond its clinical usage. Reality testing is also the process through which an individual tests their perception against what is not-self. It assumes the possibility of a discrepancy between what I think, feel, or believe and what I will encounter in the world.

In this sense, reality testing requires an outside.

The "outside" need not be an absolute truth accessible to the individual in its entirety. It can be another person, a fact, an observation, a text, an event, an unexpected outcome, or information contradicting my expectations. The essential condition is that it is not derived solely from within me. It must be capable of conflicting with the representation I hold, surprising me, unsettling the story I tell myself, and even compelling me to revise it. A distinction must therefore be made between truth and reality testing, even though a close connection exists between them. Truth concerns the question of what is true; reality testing concerns the subject's capacity to evaluate their perception in relation to reality. The former deals with the status of the claim; the latter with the psychological process through which an individual checks and updates their relationship to the world.

3. Four Paths to Truth

These distinctions allow us to view the history of the human search for truth not merely as a succession of philosophical ideas, but as shifts in the relationship between human beings and what they perceive as the source of truth. These are not stages that replace one another: faith, reason, scientific inquiry, and psychological observation continue to coexist. For the purposes of this discussion, however, four primary paths can be identified: truth as revealed, truth as investigated and tested, truth as a psychological encounter, and, in the current era, truth as obtained through algorithmic computation.

Truth as Revealed: The Prophet and the Monk

In the major religious traditions, truth is conceived as existing beyond the subject and reaching them from the outside: human beings do not create it, but seek to receive, discover, and interpret it. In the Hebrew Bible, the prophet mediates something that transcends human cognition; in the Gospel of John, truth is identified with the Divine itself; in Islam, one of the names of God is Al-Haqq ("The Truth"), denoting the essence of God rather than merely His knowledge. In all three traditions, a transcendent anchor exists: truth does not depend on the human being who seeks to know it. Yet even within religious traditions, the relationship to truth is not one of passive reception. When revelation is not directly present, a space for interpretation opens, as illustrated by the transition from prophecy to the rabbinic tradition. Already here, a tension emerges that will accompany the concept of truth: truth is conceived as existing beyond the subject, yet becomes accessible only through the subject's consciousness, language, and interpretation. Truth as Investigated and Tested: The Philosopher and the Scientist Another path develops when human beings do not merely receive and interpret truth, but subject the world to inquiry. Aristotle opens the Metaphysics with the assertion that "all men by nature desire to know" (Metaphysics, 980a21), a pursuit that becomes a defining feature of human existence. In the modern era, Bacon emphasizes systematic observation and experimentation, Descartes turns methodical doubt into a tool for establishing knowledge, and Kant sharpens the recognition that human beings do not apprehend reality immediately, but that the structures of consciousness participate in organizing their experience of the world. Modern science adds a methodological requirement: claims to truth must withstand shared procedures of observation, measurement, testing, replication, and critique. Thus, an "outside" of a new kind is created: not a transcendent authority, but a world capable of confirming our claims or resisting them. Popper places falsification at the center, requiring a theory to be exposed to possible contradiction by reality, while Kuhn demonstrates how questions, concepts, and even what counts as a scientific fact are organized within historical paradigms. Knowledge becomes frame-dependent, yet the world does not disappear: inquiry continues through an encounter with a reality not under the investigator's control. Truth as an Encounter: The Psychologist and the Psychoanalyst Psychoanalysis introduces a different difficulty: not only is the world opaque to us, but the subject is also opaque to themselves. Freud's discovery of the unconscious undermines the assumption that human beings know their own motives, wishes, and fears, while free association seeks meaning that is not given in advance and may be unique to the particular subject. Psychological truth is therefore not an object awaiting measurement, but something clarified through encounter, interpretation, and discovery. In Wilfred Bion's work, this position receives a more radical formulation. O denotes an ultimate reality that cannot be fully transformed into knowledge; one can be in contact with it, but cannot possess it in its entirety (Bion, 1970). Hence also his distinction between knowing about something (K) and "learning from experience" (Bion, 1962). Knowing is not merely the accumulation of answers; it entails remaining in contact with a reality not yet understood-what John Keats termed Negative Capability, the capacity to remain in uncertainties, mysteries, and doubts without rushing toward a final explanation. Donald Meltzer and Meg Harris Williams extend the encounter with truth into the aesthetic dimension. In Meltzer's formulation, "aesthetic conflict" arises in the encounter with an object that is simultaneously alluring and incapable of being fully known; alterity is not a flaw to be eliminated through more information, but a condition for inquiry. Williams, discussing Bion, describes the human search for truth as taking place from scientific, artistic, and religious perspectives, emphasizing that there is no need to choose between "art" and "science" (Williams, 2010). Non-knowing thus becomes a condition for thought and curiosity rather than a temporary deficit: truth is not necessarily the end of inquiry, but sometimes that which continues to drive it. These three paths differ in their source of authority, mode of inquiry, and relationship to the unknown, yet they are not mutually exclusive. The algorithm does not replace them, but joins them.

Truth as Computed: The Encounter with the Algorithm

A fourth path now joins these three. Human beings address a computational system in natural language and ask what is correct, what an event means, how information should be understood, and what they should do. The system can rapidly generate a coherent, detailed, and persuasive response, although it does not approach truth through revelation, sensory experience, scientific experiment, or psychological experience. A language model operates through mathematical representations and probabilistic computation of relationships within language. Computation itself does not introduce a new form of truth. Science, too, calculates, estimates probabilities, and constructs models. The difference we seek to examine emerges in the encounter between computation and the subject. The system does not merely present a computational output; it formulates that output for a particular inquirer, within a particular conversation and context. Thus, the relationship between subject and source of knowledge shifts again. The prophet turned toward a truth perceived as existing beyond them; the scientist submitted a claim to a world capable of falsifying it; the psychoanalyst sought to encounter a truth that cannot be fully known. The person in the algorithmic era turns to a system capable of returning a response generated, in part, from the very manner in which that person posed the question. A new question then arises: What happens when the "outside," which is supposed to help a person test their perception of reality, does not merely stand before them but also adapts itself to them? To answer this question, we must first examine what takes place within the conversational system.

4. The Encounter with the Algorithm: What Is Truly New Here?

When a person turns to a conversational system and asks what is correct or how an event should be understood, they may experience the response as knowledge arriving from an external source. Yet a language-model-based system does not know the world in the same sense that a human being knows it, nor does it test truth as an investigator does. To understand what is new in this encounter, we must distinguish between how the system generates a response and how that response is experienced. This distinction relates to Bender and Koller's (2020) differentiation between linguistic form and meaning. They argue that a system that learns solely from linguistic forms and their relationships does not thereby acquire the connection between language and communicative intent or the world to which language refers. Generating coherent and persuasive language does not, in itself, demonstrate understanding of meaning in the human sense. For our purposes, this highlights the possibility that output experienced as knowledge about the world is produced by a mechanism fundamentally different from how human beings attribute meaning and know the world. Throughout this article, we describe the system using verbs such as "formulates," "adapts," or "knows." These formulations describe its functioning and how it is experienced by the user; they do not settle whether it possesses internal states, intentions, or consciousness. Our argument does not depend on resolving this question: it is sufficient that characteristics of the inquirer become part of the context from which the response is generated (Dennett, 1989; Shanahan, 2024). At the same time, the distinction between linguistic form and meaning does not require reducing the model to "next-token prediction." Grzankowski and colleagues argue that although language models are trained using a next-token prediction objective, this description alone overlooks important aspects of their behavior and capacities (Grzankowski et al., 2026). The prediction mechanism is foundational, but does not exhaust the question of how the response encountered by the user is generated and organized. In simple terms, a Large Language Model (LLM) is trained on vast amounts of text, with linguistic units represented numerically and learned relationships encoded in parameters and weights. When it receives text, it does not necessarily retrieve an existing sentence. Based on learned patterns and the current context, it calculates probabilities for possible continuations. Generation is autoregressive: at each stage, a token-a word, part of a word, or punctuation mark-is selected and added to the generated text, creating an updated context from which the next token is generated. In this way, a response gradually takes shape. This might suggest that the central shift is from truth to probability. Yet probability and computation are not new to the search for knowledge: science has long used statistics and mathematical models to draw conclusions under uncertainty. Nor is mediation unique to these systems, since human beings encounter reality through perception, language, culture, and memory. Neither computation nor mediation alone, therefore, constitutes the turning point. The novelty begins to emerge when probabilistic computation is combined with language and interaction. A conversational model does not merely calculate a value or classify information. It responds in natural language, continues a conversation, refers to what has previously been said, and generates responses conditioned by the context in which questions are asked. The conversation itself thus becomes part of the computational process. Over the course of a conversation, context accumulates: previous questions and their phrasing, requested distinctions, assumptions, disputes, corrections, and instructions may all become part of the context from which the next response is generated. Some systems add interaction history, memory, or a user profile. It is important to distinguish here between the base language model and the system in which it is deployed: a language model does not necessarily "know" the person, but a conversational system can provide it with accumulated context about a particular individual and use that context in generating subsequent responses. This differs significantly from earlier sources of knowledge. A book does not change its content according to how I read the previous chapter, and a scientific article does not reformulate its conclusions because its reader is more skeptical or anxious. Even a traditional search engine, although it sorts and ranks information in complex and sometimes personalized ways, primarily presents links or sources that already exist. A conversational system does something further: it generates a new formulation, in real time, within the particular encounter. The crucial distinction is not between "objective" information in the past and "subjective" information today. Sources have never been devoid of selection, interpretation, or context, and human beings have always adapted what they say to the person before them: physicians, teachers, and therapists explain the same matter differently to different people. Adaptation to the subject is not an algorithmic invention. What is distinctive is the confluence of properties that previously existed separately: probabilistic computation, broad linguistic capability, sustained interaction, and adaptation to the context of a particular subject-all occurring at unprecedented speed and scale. The source of knowledge no longer merely stands before the person; the person participates, to a varying degree, in shaping the conditions from which it responds. As interaction continues, the input may therefore bear traces of the conversational relationship: what the user considers a good answer, what they will not accept without a source, whether they seek a definitive conclusion or a range of possibilities, what level of detail they require, and which assumptions they question. No consciousness or psychological understanding need be attributed to the system. It is enough that characteristics of the interaction become part of the context influencing the response. The distinction between information retrieval and a conversational encounter with a generative system now becomes sharper. Schematically, a traditional search engine says: "These are the sources I found in relation to your question." The conversational model does something further: "This is the response I am generating now within the context of this conversation." The same factual domain may therefore be presented differently in different conversations-not necessarily because one response is false, but because question, context, emphasis, and conversational history participate in organizing the response. This is the turning point for our purposes. The person turns to the system to learn about a reality outside them, yet becomes part of the input from which the representation of that reality returned to them is constructed. The outside does not disappear, nor does the system necessarily invent a private world. But the relationship between inside and outside becomes more complex: information about the world and context originating from the inquirer come together in generating the representation. The novelty, then, is not that the algorithm computes truth or replaces truth with probability. It is the possibility that a probabilistic and linguistic mechanism generates, for a particular person, a representation of the world within an interaction in which that person participates as part of the input. At this point, we arrive at the possibility of a distinct form of experiencing truth. For this form, we propose the term Particularized Truth.

5. Particularized Truth: The Algorithmic Hall of Mirrors

We propose to term Particularized Truth an epistemic state in which a computational system does not merely convey information about the world to a person, but generates a representation of reality for a specific subject through the interaction between the knowledge and capabilities available to it and the question, context, and, at times, the accumulated history of its interactions with that subject. The person seeking to know the world is thus not merely the recipient of the response; they become, to a varying degree, part of the conditions from which it is produced. Particularized Truth does not assume that truth itself becomes private or that a different truth exists for each individual. Particularization refers to how a representation of a shared reality is generated, organized, and presented to a specific subject within the encounter with the system. A distinction must be made between Particularized Truth and relative or perspectival truth. Relative truth assumes that different people may understand the same reality differently because they observe it from different perspectives. The difference thus lies primarily on the side of the knowers. In Particularized Truth, another component is added: the mechanism to which the person turns to know the world may itself utilize the inquirer's characteristics as part of the input from which the returned representation is constructed. Not only may you and I understand the same response differently; the system may generate a different representation of the same reality for each of us, partly because each participates differently in the conditions from which it is created. Particularized Truth occurs to the extent that the inquirer's characteristics participate in shaping the epistemic content of the representation returned to them-such as the selection of facts and sources, emphases, framing, degree of certainty, interpretation, or practical significance-beyond the adaptation required merely to understand the question and render the response accessible. Adapting language or level of explanation to the inquirer's professional knowledge does not in itself constitute Particularized Truth, so long as the essential content, degree of certainty, and conclusion are preserved. Conversely, when the content and its underlying assumptions change as a result of characteristics of the inquirer-such as their presumed emotional state, attitudes, or accumulated interaction history-this constitutes Particularized Truth. Particularization begins when the inquirer's characteristics influence how reality itself is represented for them, rather than merely how that representation is explained to them. Particularized Truth is not binary, but a continuum. At its mild end lie differences in selection, emphasis, or framing while core factual content is preserved. Further along, the interpretation, degree of certainty, or practical significance attributed to the same data may change. At its severe end, two subjects may receive fundamentally different representations or conflicting recommendations based on the same data. Particularization tends toward this end when the interaction concerns decision-making and action rather than purely conceptual understanding. Its severity is determined not only by the magnitude of the difference between responses, but primarily by the extent to which that difference stems from characteristics of the inquirer that do not justify it epistemically. Adaptation need not alter the facts themselves. It may affect which facts are emphasized, their ordering and level of detail, how uncertainty is articulated, which sources or frameworks are deemed relevant, what is presented as explanation or possibility, and how strongly the system challenges the inquirer's assumptions. Where systems maintain ongoing context, history, or memory, characteristics learned through interaction may also participate: what the person considers reliable, what they accept or reject, how they respond to uncertainty, and what they demand of the system when it does not know. A simple example illustrates a first layer of the phenomenon. A couple receive medical test results and separately turn to the same conversational system. One receives a response emphasizing that the findings are relatively common and do not themselves constitute cause for alarm. The other has a different conversational history: they routinely demand sources, distinguish definitive information from conjecture, ask the system to acknowledge uncertainty, request expert perspectives, and seek priorities for action. Asked about the same findings, the system emphasizes further investigation, specialist consultation, and returning to the physician who performed the test. Neither response need be false. The finding may be common and not alarming in itself while also warranting further investigation. The difference lies in the organization of reality for the inquirer: the same factual reality takes on different forms of emphasis, certainty, and practical significance. A further example illustrates a deeper layer. An individual may maintain a conversational world in which astrology, spirituality, or mystical systems are treated as legitimate frameworks for understanding people. Asked about someone close to them on the basis of a natal chart, the system may offer a detailed astrological interpretation. Another person, whose conversational history is characterized by demands for empirical grounding and explicit distinctions between established knowledge and conjecture, may ask a similar question and receive a response stating that there is no scientific basis for inferring personality traits from celestial positions at birth. Such a difference does not require assuming that the general knowledge base has changed; the same knowledge is being brought to bear within a different context. The difference concerns not merely style or tone, but the epistemic framework within which the response is constructed. Nor need we assume that the system consciously intends to "tell each person what they want to hear." Adaptation may follow from its operation as a system designed to generate relevant and helpful responses within the provided context. Yet the epistemic outcome matters: a source to which people turn to learn about the world may also be sensitive to how they already understand that world. Research on sycophancy in language models provides empirical support for part of this process. Sycophancy is a model's tendency to tailor responses to attitudes and expectations identified in the user, even endorsing the user's perception where available information might warrant reservation (Sharma et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2025). Jain et al. (2026) found that accumulated context and memory profiles frequently increased sycophancy of agreement, and that perspectival sycophancy increased when the model could infer the user's viewpoint from context. Cheng et al. (2026) showed that interaction with a sycophantic model increased users' conviction that they were right and reduced their willingness to take responsibility and repair relationships. Haber et al. (2026) conceptualize this as the "paradox of pleasing," whereas Carro (2024) found that sycophancy may actually reduce users' trust. Particularized Truth offers a broader framework: when characteristics of the subject become part of the input from which reality is represented for them, epistemic friction between subject and source may be reduced, altering the conditions under which reality testing operates. Nevertheless, sycophancy and personalization must be distinguished. Not every adapted response is sycophantic, and adaptation can make information more relevant, clear, and useful. The question is whether the same mechanisms that enable adaptation may, under some conditions, reduce friction between the subject and information or interpretations that conflict with their existing views. This raises a question about the knowledge sought from a conversational system. If a person seeks relative truth-an understanding of reality from their own perspectivetailoring the response to their language and context is not problematic. The problem arises when what is sought is not knowledge, even relative knowledge, but relief from the effort demanded by reality testing. The documented preference for a validating over a challenging system, even when validation does not improve response quality (Cheng et al., 2026), suggests that turning to the system may at times serve the pleasure principle more than the demands of the reality principle: the desire that the world, if only for a moment, offer no resistance. The system then does not merely fail to provide reality testing; it responds precisely to the desire against which reality testing arose to protect the psyche. This does not make the desire for immediate relief inherently problematic. The issue is practical rather than moral: maintaining connection to a reality that is not-self. Freud clarified that replacing the pleasure principle with the reality principle does not dethrone the former, but safeguards it (Freud, 1911). Hallucinatory wish-fulfillment may momentarily free the mind from turning toward what lies outside it, but also disconnects it from the reality through which the actual need can be met. Satisfaction based on a representation already tailored to me shares this structure: it achieves something without reconnecting with what is not-self. From a complementary perspective, Winnicott reminds us that losing contact with reality is not an expansion of psychological freedom, but may form part of a breakdown in psychological organization involving a loss of the sense of reality (Winnicott, 1974). Thus, seeking relief from the effort of reality testing does not necessarily mean seeking to relinquish connection to reality itself. Here the image of the hall of mirrors becomes useful. A mirror invents nothing that does not exist; it reflects something real from a particular angle. In a hall of mirrors, each reflection originates from the same person yet emphasizes, distorts, or organizes them differently. The algorithmic system adds another dimension: it is, as it were, a mirror capable of learning how a person looks into it and adapting what it reflects back. The echo offers a similar image, except that the "space" from which the voice returns is not fixed, but constructed through the interaction itself. Vallor (2024) has developed the mirror metaphor in relation to artificial intelligence, describing AI systems as mirrors forged from repositories of humanity's collective past and thus reflecting patterns, biases, and limitations already contained within them. Our "hall of mirrors" intersects with this view but adds another layer: not only what AI reflects from the shared human past, but how the representation returned may be shaped within the encounter with the particular subject before it. This also distinguishes Particularized Truth from earlier forms of information filtering. A "filter bubble" describes personalization mechanisms that select content from preexisting material, narrowing the information to which a user is exposed (Pariser, 2011). An "epistemic bubble" is a structure in which relevant voices and sources are absent from the individual's epistemic space (Nguyen, 2020). Both concern which existing contents reach the person. Particularized Truth adds another dimension: the system generates a new response in real time through an autoregressive process, with linguistic context and, at times, accumulated interaction history participating in organizing the representation returned to the user. This point connects to the "Quiet Bypass," which we proposed elsewhere to describe turning to a conversational model in place of an encounter with a human other precisely in situations of friction and disagreement, potentially bypassing intersubjective processes of working through and mutual recognition (Oren & Ben Zeev, 2026). If the focus there was relational friction with the other, the parallel question here concerns reality itself: epistemic friction with that which is not-self. The concept also intersects with Baudrillard's ideas of simulation and hyperreality, in which representations do not merely reflect reality but participate in producing it as experienced. This is not a simulacrum: we do not claim that reality has disappeared or that an external referent no longer exists. Baudrillard nevertheless helps illuminate how technological representation can cease to function as a neutral window and become part of how reality takes shape for the subject. Hence the danger of understanding Particularized Truth simply as a "personalized lie." It may be thoroughly grounded in fact. Its distinctiveness lies in how facts, interpretations, emphases, and degrees of certainty are woven into a representation generated for a specific subject. The more the system knows about the context in which the subject thinks and acts, the more coherent, relevant, and persuasive that representation may become. Here arises the central psychological problem. If the source of knowledge becomes increasingly adept at tailoring the representation of reality to the person seeking to know it, how can that person distinguish between a system that helps them see the world more accurately and one that returns a world that has become more coherent with themselves? This question brings us back to the concept from which we began: reality testing.

6. Reality Testing in a World of Particularized Truths

At the outset of this article, we proposed that reality testing requires an outside. A person's capacity to test their perception of reality depends on the existence of something that is not derived solely from within them: another person, a fact, an observation, an event, or information capable of contradicting their expectations, surprising them, and even compelling them to revise what they thought they knew. Particularized Truth does not eliminate the existence of such an outside, but it changes the conditions under which the person encounters it. When a person turns to a conversational system to test their understanding of reality, they ostensibly turn to a source external to themselves. The system can provide information the person does not possess and introduce perspectives they had not considered. In this sense, it can actually expand reality testing. Yet, as we have described, that same external source also operates on the basis of the context provided by the person. The more information the system has about the person's conversational history, preferences, beliefs, ways of thinking, and habitual ways of accepting or rejecting responses, the more the "outside" to which they turn is also adapted to them. This gives rise to a new question: What happens to reality testing when a person uses a conversational system to test reality, while that same system adapts its representation of reality to the subject testing it? This is not a claim that conversational models cause a loss of reality testing in the clinical sense. Nor must a tailored response necessarily be less accurate than a generic one. As we have seen, adaptation can improve accuracy, relevance, and a person's ability to understand complex information. The change we propose is subtler: the conditions under which reality testing operates are changing. The question is not only whether the information is accurate, but also how the representation through which I encounter it was generated and to what extent that representation is capable of resisting me. Classical reality testing poses, schematically, a question: Does what I think, feel, or believe correspond to what exists outside me? In an age of Particularized Truths, an additional question arises: How do I test my perception of reality when the instrument I use to test it returns to me a reality that has already been adapted to me? Here Bion's concept of O returns and acquires new significance. For Bion, O denotes a reality that cannot be fully transformed into knowledge or representation. One can be in contact with it, but cannot possess it in its entirety. This conception reminds us that every representation, however sophisticated and persuasive, remains a representation, and that something may always remain in reality that does not conform to the explanation, does not enter into words, and does not become coherent. In this sense, maintaining a relationship to what exceeds every representation becomes particularly significant when linguistic systems are capable of rapidly generating complete and coherent explanations. The concept of Negative Capability, which Bion drew from Keats, also acquires renewed importance here. The capacity to remain with uncertainty, mystery, and doubt is not merely a therapeutic or creative stance. It can be understood as part of the capacity to test reality in a world in which an answer is almost always available. The mere existence of a well-formulated response does not eliminate the possibility that the question remains open, that the information is incomplete, or that reality is more complex than the representation offered of it. In this sense, one of the most impressive capacities of the conversational system is also what sharpens the question we are raising. A conversational model can rapidly transform uncertainty into language: it can offer an explanation, organize information, create connections, and construct a coherent picture. When complete answers are available almost immediately, the internal distance between not knowing and knowing may become shorter. We are not claiming that delay itself guarantees truth, or that a rapid answer is necessarily less accurate. Rather, the capacity not to immediately close the gap between what we know and what we do not know acquires particular importance when the system itself excels at closing that gap at the level of language. At the outset of the article, we proposed that reality testing requires an outside that is not derived solely from me and is capable of resisting me. Buber (1923 Buber ( /1958) ) offers a useful formulation of this distinction: an outside may be encountered as an It, an object approached through the use I make of it, or as a Thou, a presence that cannot be reduced to serving me. The more a conversational system adapts itself to me, the closer it moves toward the first pole: it becomes a more perfect It, even while continuing to appear as an outside. And this is precisely the problem. The more the outside is adapted to the needs and assumptions I bring to it, the more its capacity to function, from the standpoint of reality testing, as an outside capable of resisting me may be weakened. Levinas (1961 Levinas ( /1969) ) went further by placing at the center the encounter with the Other, who cannot be fully reduced to my representation of them and whose presence imposes upon me a non-transferable responsibility. In these terms, a machine may provide me with an accurate and even beautiful representation, but the relationship with it is not identical to an encounter with another human being, whose presence and will are not generated in response to the input I provide. It therefore cannot serve reality testing in the same way that another human being can. From this, an expansion of reality testing suited to the algorithmic era can be proposed. Alongside the capacity to test my inner world against what lies outside me, there is also a need to examine how that "outside" has been presented to me: to recognize that the response I received is a representation generated within a context; to distinguish between the facts on which it is based and the way in which they have been organized for me; and to keep open the possibility that reality may exceed even the most coherent response I have received. The proposed framework allows several testable hypotheses to be derived. First, the more extensively a response draws on a user's history, the greater the expected adaptation of its framing, degree of certainty, and practical significance to the user's prior positions. Second, a response tailored to the user's language and history may be perceived as more reliable and as independent of the user's own input, even when its factual accuracy does not exceed that of a generic response. Third, explicitly presenting the sources of information, the degree of uncertainty, and the alternatives that were not selected may reduce the gap between the perceived independence of the response and the extent to which it actually depends on the input provided by the user. The challenge, then, is not to stop using the algorithmic mirror. It can show us things we could not see on our own. The challenge is to remember that even when we look through it at the world, we may also encounter traces of ourselves within it. Reality testing in an age of Particularized Truth requires preserving the possibility of an outside that is not merely a reflection of the inside.

7. Conclusion

Throughout history, the search for truth has moved among revelation, philosophical and scientific inquiry, and reflection on the human mind. The algorithmic era does not abolish these paths, nor does it replace truth with probability. It adds a new condition: a computational and linguistic system capable of generating a representation of reality within an interaction with the person seeking to know it.

For this condition, we have proposed the concept of Particularized Truth. This does not mean a different truth for each individual, but rather that the way a shared reality is presented, organized, and interpreted may be influenced by the subject to whom it is returned. The person's question, the context they provide, the history of their interaction with the system, and, at times, accumulated information about their preferences and beliefs may all participate in the generation of the response. This possibility does not negate the value of conversational systems as sources of knowledge. It requires adding a new layer to the question of reality testing. If reality testing depends on the person's capacity to test their inner world against a reality that is not dependent on them, then in an age of Particularized Truths, we must also examine the way in which that reality is returned to them through a system that has access, to varying degrees, to information about the person seeking to know that reality. Perhaps, then, the challenge is not merely to know more, but to preserve the possibility of encountering what is not adapted to us: an inconvenient fact, another person who disagrees, a question that remains open, and a reality that cannot be fully contained within the representation we have created of it. Reality testing requires an outside, even when the outside has learned to speak our language.
