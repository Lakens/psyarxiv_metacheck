No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Cash transfers are a common poverty alleviation intervention, yet qualitative reports of recipients' lived experiences are limited. Without these perspectives, policy frameworks risk reducing beneficiaries to passive inputs in programmatic equations. This means potentially overlooking the social meanings, moral negotiations, and relational dynamics through which transfers are actually received, used, perceived, and redistributed. To address this gap, we analyzed 74 in-depth interviews with cash transfer recipients and their household members in rural Malawi and Lesotho. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we created four themes: 1) "With my poverty it can't work": Enough to survive, not enough to change, 2) "I can't be eating in my house while my children are hungry next door": Grandmothers and the obligation to share, 3) "I am already worried as to what I will be doing": Psychological relief and stress intertwine, 4) "Sky-falling" and "satanic" money: Notions of deservingness produce stigma and jealousy. Recipients consistently noted improvement in food security, while effects on mental health and social relationships were more mixed. We argue that recipient-centered reports complement and strengthen quantitative evidence about the impacts and mechanisms of cash transfers. We discuss specific policy recommendations, such as larger and more predictable payments, better communication, and sensitive disbursement options, gender-sensitive support, and more active monitoring of psychosocial and community effects in order to mitigate potential negative impacts. Finally, we argue there is a need for a more ambitious focus on development as the key path to poverty eradication.

Introduction

Cash transfers

Cash transfers (CTs) are popular social protection programs. They constitute small, regular payments to recipients in an unconditional or conditional manner (such as by including a condition to attend a healthcare visit in order to receive the transfer). In the last decades, a great deal of attention has been paid to understanding what impacts cash transfers produce 1 . Systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) show that cash transfers are effective in alleviating monetary poverty through increasing household income, total expenditure, and food expenditure 2, 3 . There is also evidence that school enrolment and attendance increase with cash transfer receipt, though with some variability by measure and conditionality 4 . Evidence suggests that cash transfers reduce mental ill health like depression and anxiety 5 though effects also seem to vary, and could be negative, depending on age, gender, and program design 6 . The evidence for impacts on health outcomes is also mixed 7.

Beyond studying effects, it is crucial to unpack how cash transfers work. The study of mechanisms has similarly largely been informed by economics and epidemiological methods, and has relied on interventional data from RCTs, typically with an overt focus on 'objective' measures. However, even though interventional studies do typically incorporate qualitative work especially in early piloting or acceptability evaluations, qualitative findings tend to receive limited prominence in the final published reports, even if they have informed the focus of the quantitative work. Further, a recent systematic review illustrated how even methodologically comparable studies may support different mechanistic explanations for the same relationship -for instance, improving mental health by mitigating exposure to environmental stressors or by feeling economic security or by allowing recipients to focus on longterm goals 8 . Other approaches to understanding CTs' causal mechanisms entail large-scale data linkage efforts, where various types of data about cash transfer recipients' lives are combined, like data about financial circumstances or medical and hospitalization records 9 . Albeit logistically and likely financially burdensome, all of these approaches can produce high-quality evidence. A complementary alternative is asking recipients directly what difference cash transfers make to their lives. This is the approach we take here.

Importance of qualitative work

We highlight four key ways qualitative methods add value to the study of cash transfers: they can capture outcomes and meanings that pre-specified quantitative measures are not designed to detect; they can help illuminate the mechanisms underlying an intervention's effects; they can reveal heterogeneity that average treatment effects (ATEs) obscure; and they can inform the ethicalepistemic considerations that shape our analytical choices.

First, in qualitative work like interviews, respondents have more opportunities to raise what they consider is important to their lives, which can point to experiences not well captured in quantitative work. Interventional evaluations like RCTs typically focus on a focal set of outcomes, measured with instruments decided in advance. Any impacts outside the prioritized outcome set would not be evaluated. By contrast, open-ended qualitative designs can assess a wider range of outcomes, and inform what should be measured and assessed in later studies. This is not only a matter of casting a wider net of measures but may be a matter of casting a different net altogether. For instance, cash transfer recipients describe receiving the transfer in ways that reveal how they interpret its meaning and legitimacy in emic/insider dimensions that are unlikely to be captured via standard survey scales. These may still be consequential for downstream attitudes and behaviors (see later for descriptions of CT as coming "by grace and as a gift" or as "sky-falling money").

Second, qualitative work can help illuminate mechanisms. Economists have cautioned that many quantitative designs including RCTs, are often implemented and assessed in ways that cannot inform us about why interventions work and the underlying mechanisms 10 . When such studies have successfully informed mechanistic understanding, this has been underpinned by a robust theoretical framework against which results can be assessed. Qualitative work is cohesive with and can support this aim: direct reports from individuals can help generate hypotheses and inform theories that can subsequently be further validated and evaluated quantitatively.

Third, qualitative work can speak to heterogeneity that ATEs obscure. Although ATEs can substantiate that an intervention worked, they necessarily collapse heterogeneity across recipients, which makes meaningful variation harder to understand 11 . By contrast, qualitative methods can often provide in-depth insights for each individual and showcase how different people might experience the same intervention differently -for instance, those with existing social community support or those without -and illuminate variance, such as why some might experience large benefits and others may report adverse effects. Here too, qualitative methods can generate new insights that can then be further substantiated with quantitative approaches (e.g. subgroup analyses).

Finally, focusing on qualitative work can promote analyses that are more conscious of their ethicalepistemic implications 12 . As qualitative research can inform more directly about individuals' preferences and variability, it can also inform analytical choices that carry moral implications such as about autonomy, exclusion, non-maleficence 13 . Ultimately, we do not make a case for qualitative methods over quantitative ones or vice versa. Instead, we advocate for evidence triangulation across diverse methodologies, recognizing each approach's strengths and limitations 14.

The current study

Despite their value-add, qualitative studies on the experiences of CT recipients remain rare (certainly in comparison to quantitative work). Previous qualitative work supports that CTs are an acceptable intervention and that positive impacts arise from them, such as increases in income, food consumption, and material assets 15 . However, impacts are contextually shaped: for instance, depending on community and regional context, household size and labor capacity 16 ; gender, sociocultural norms 17 , and in many cases further social protection is required 18 . Given the general underrepresentation of qualitative work in the study of cash transfers, as well as their context-dependent nature, a gap remains in the understanding of the impacts of these interventions globally.

Our study contributes to this emerging body of work by foregrounding the voices of cash transfer recipients and their communities. We focus on two countries, Malawi and Lesotho, that have been understudied in terms of qualitative work. Where such qualitative work exists, it is most often part of a government and/or NGO impact evaluation or report, rather than a focal point or standalone peerreviewed academic paper. Previous qualitative work in Malawi has shown that CTs reduced ganyu/piecework, and allowed recipients to purchase food and have greater income security, though beneficiaries reported the amounts were often insufficient for long-term change 19 . Decreases in ganyu and ability to purchase livestock such as chickens were corroborated by another report 20 which further highlighted a need for "regular and predictable" transfer payments. Another qualitative survey in Malawi, part of a larger mixed-methods investigation, indicated recipients spent most of the transfer amount on food, with other expenses including school-related costs for children, clothing, healthcare, home repairs 21 . In Lesotho the qualitative component of a mixed-method investigation suggested Child Grant Programme (CGP) recipients were able to buy food and cover schooling expenses for children; while recipients noted the increase in income and diet diversity, these changes were short-term, typically lasting for a couple of weeks. This was attributed to relatively small and irregular amounts provided 22 . In further research on the CGP recipients similarly noted concerns regarding the unpredictability and irregularity of the payment schedule; recipients saw the CT value as low and not allowing them to fully meet needs 23 . A combined survey and interview study of Lesotho's pension showed that the pension empowers the economic decision-making of older adults in their households; pensioners supported household members, particularly regarding educational needs 24.

We are interested in exploring the multifaceted lived experiences of cash transfer recipients and communities with a particular focus on the difference a cash transfer may or may not make. We are interested in mapping the potential impacts of cash transfers, whether positive, negative, or neutral, and inclusive of all potential ranges of domains of impacts. Where possible, we want to explore what mechanisms underpin any potential impacts. Our results point to a mix of positive impacts like improved school attendance, increased food security, and negative impacts like stigma, stress, tensions in social relations. They align with a broader literature that similarly shows that CTs alone are insufficient to address structural determinants of poverty. By structural determinants of poverty we mean the institutional, economic, political, legal, social arrangements that distribute power, resources, opportunities, risks across groups, thereby shaping exposure to poverty and the capacity to exit it. Such structural determinants of poverty can include, for instance, governance, macroeconomic and social policies, public service quality, appropriate infrastructure, discrimination, having sufficient economic opportunities and jobs 25 . Altogether, our findings point to the value of recipient-centered evidence for understanding interventional impacts and informing programmatic design.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This is a secondary data analysis of original research conducted in rural villages in Malawi and Lesotho in 2015-2019 26 . The original research group was primarily interested in mapping how cash transfers can shape social relationships, with a focus on people living rurally. Beyond the interviews we draw on here, the wider project also incorporated household profiling, participatory workshops with young adults, and approximately 100 interviews with policymakers and program implementers, enabling the original team to examine cash transfers' effects on household targeting, generational relations, and national-level policy processes. The original study team found that household-based targeting mechanisms often rest on flawed assumptions about household stability and boundedness, generating a sense of arbitrary selection and resentment within communities 27 ; that transfers reaching the elderly are more widely regarded as legitimate, and generate broader benefits for households and communities, than transfers reaching younger adults; and that cash received by young, able-bodied adults is frequently seen as stigmatizing, with young people expressing a preference for paid work or community development opportunities over "free" cash assistance 26 . Our study builds on this foundation. Rather than revisiting household targeting or national-level policy processes, we return to the interviews using reflexive thematic analysis to develop a granular, recipient-centered account of material, psychological, and relational impacts.

We work with in-depth individual interviews with members of households that receive cash transfers: 34 interviews in Lesotho and 40 in Malawi. The interviews include children (9-19 years), adults (20-69) and older adults (70+). In Lesotho 16 interviews were with men/boys and 18 with women/girls. Eleven interviews were with children, and 8 with older adults, with the remaining 15 carried out with adults. In Malawi 18 interviews were with men/boys, and 22 with women/girls. Eighteen interviews were with children, 7 with older adults (70+), and 15 were with adults. For 3 interviews the exact age was not recorded, though all three were grandmothers and we count them conservatively as adults. Note in Lesotho the interview set comprises 31 unique individuals, where one older adult (grandmother), one child (boy), and one adult (man) were interviewed twice during the data collection period. In Malawi the interview set comprises 36 unique individuals, as one young boy, one older woman, and two female adults were interviewed twice during the data collection period.

The majority of interviews took place during April-September, with a minority in January-March. In both Lesotho and Malawi, April through June/August is typically a period of harvest, followed by a period of post-harvest (timing and crop cycles vary somewhat by country and year by year) 28, 29 . Farmers focus on reaping and processing of crops like maize, sorghum, and wheat, groundnuts, tobacco. The interviews from January-March generally correspond to the lean period, when most households are expected to have more depleted food stocks.

Study villages (one in Lesotho and two in Malawi) were purposively selected on the basis of longstanding relationships between the original research team and the communities. In Lesotho, this meant regular visits by members of the original research team since 1993, and in Malawi -since 2007. This meant the interviewing team were trusted in these sites and not associated with the CT. Given concerns that the selected village in Malawi, and its district (Thyolo) might have received more irregular CT payments, a further village (Mulanje District) known to a co-investigator at the University of Malawi was selected, where the CT was known to be more regular. In correspondence with the original team, we understand that in this site, they were considered as associated with the CT scheme and that adult interviewees depicted the scheme more favorably, though children's perspectives were perhaps more frank. We also understand from the original team that in the two main villages nearly all resident members of CT-receiving households were interviewed. In Mulanje, only a minority of CT recipients were interviewed, selected by a person employed to work on cash transfers. Participation was voluntary and not incentivized 30 . All participants consented to taking part, and in the case of young people this entailed consent from their guardian. There were two separate consent forms in the case of participants themselves consenting, and guardians consenting. All interviews were conducted by members of the core original research team 26 accompanied in most cases with interpreters who were students or staff at the local partner universities. Almost all interviews in Malawi were conducted outdoors, as were many in Lesotho. Although others could pass by outside, and families could be interviewed together (particularly children with adults present), most interviews were carried out in private by request of the team. The anticipated average interview length was around 40 minutes, though in practice this varied with a few adult interviews lasting 70-80 minutes. Most child interviews lasted under 20 minutes and used simpler language. Consent was requested for audio recording, and all interviews were transcribed verbatim. Consent forms and interview schedules are openly shared at the same repository alongside the data.

Depending on the interviewer, the interviews were carried out either fully in the local languagestypically Sesotho and Chichewa -or in English with immediate translation to the local language, or fully in English, for those who preferred it. All interviews were transcribed verbatim. Everything said in both English and the local languages was recorded, and a subsequent translation in English was also provided by the original team. Coauthors in our team who spoke the local languages advised us on translation fidelity and context where appropriate.

Our focus is on understanding the effects of cash transfers and the mechanisms through which they impact people's lives. Three different social cash transfer programs (SCTP) are investigated in this study, all of which are unconditional and offered to those meeting certain eligibility criteria (Table 1). Specifically, these are the Lesotho Old Age Pension scheme, a non-contributory old age pension, the Lesotho Child Grants Program (CGP), which covers poor households with at least one child, and the Malawi SCTP Program, which covers ultra-poor and labor-constrained households. In addition to the base unconditional component, the Malawi SCTP includes an education top-up payment that is conditional on children attending school. The Lesotho pension uses categorical, individual-level eligibility: any citizen above 70 years or older who is not already receiving another government pension qualifies. Its primary goal is to reduce poverty for older citizens. The CGP in Lesotho and the Malawi SCTP both assess eligibility at the household level. For the CGP, poor or vulnerable households with a child qualify, where household socioeconomic information and proxy means testing are used together with community-based validation to inform eligibility. Its primary aim is to improve the living standards of vulnerable children and orphans. The SCTP in Malawi targets ultra-poor and laborconstrained households, with the aim of reducing extreme poverty and vulnerability. Various proxy indicators for poverty are considered, as well as if the household has no fit-for-work member or if the ratio of unfit-to-work household members/ dependents to fit-for-work members is 3:1 or higher. The Malawi SCTP is aimed at approximately the poorest 10% within the relevant area. Lesotho's pension is government-funded, whereas the other two programs are government and donor-supported.

Program

Intended Beneficiaries We report the CT amount for each program as a percentage of the GNI per capita. As this measure tracks the average income of a person in that country, we note that in reality the CT amount will likely represent a higher percentage of a household's income. This is because recipients come from poor or labor-constrained households. We also note that there were notable inflation and local currency fluctuations during the study period, so estimates for 2019 should be considered only approximately indicative, and not representative for the full study period. We also note that some participants likely received CTs from other organizations in the past as well. Some reported previously having received cash transfers from the Catholic Development Commission in Malawi (CADECOM) program, which was an emergency relief program in the context of extreme drought and hunger, supported by the World Bank/UN World Food Programme (WFP) and implemented by CADECOM. Another participant reported receiving cash through their traditional authority, funded by the National Association for People Living with HIV and AIDS (NAPHAM) in Malawi. All three programs are currently active as of 2026, and note that these programs and their coverage have since changed (see Discussion e.g. on digital payments and increased coverage).

General analytical approach

We took a reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) approach following the six stages outlined by Braun and colleagues 31 . Seven authors (MZ, JH, TK, CJG, AS, SB, EH) contributed to the analysis, led by MZ.

All seven read the interviews, first engaged with familiarization independently and then during joint online discussions. MZ, JH, TK, CJG, EH contributed with cross-coding (with a mix of semantic and latent coding). This informed the complete coding and later theme creation. MZ, JH, TK led on theme creation with feedback from the team. Researchers could work with their preferred software or by hand, as we did not want to impose any accessibility barriers 32 . We worked collaboratively and with an iterative process, starting from project inception and making decisions about workflow jointly.

We considered RTA suitable for use with secondary data both in general and in our specific case. In general, informative previous work combining RTA with secondary qualitative data [33] [34] [35] assured us of the viability of this approach. We considered it feasible that we can successfully carry out all six key stages of RTA with this dataset. When deliberating between methods, we considered RTA to have advantages over other methods especially when working with secondary data. We thought this approach would promote a reflexive awareness of what we do know and what we do not know at each stage, thus in turn promoting transparency. More specifically, we discussed that emphasis on research subjectivity is particularly useful to us as a source of information when relating to data that we did not collect. RTA allowed us to question how our various cultural backgrounds shape our understanding of the data: for instance, in meetings we discussed how normative it is in our contexts for young children to feel comfortable and offer critical points in the presence of adults and acknowledged how this variation in our own views and experiences affects our readings of children's interviews. We believe that our reflexivity throughout ultimately allowed us to consider many outcome types in a rich multilevel manner, focusing on both positive and negative impacts (e.g. finding both psychological relief and stress).

We took a critical realism framework that allows us to be informed by both realism and constructionism. This view recognizes that while an objective reality can exist independently of us, our understanding of it is shaped by our unique perspectives and circumstances. In our work, a critical realism stance allows us to acknowledge poverty, inequality, social injustice and marginalization as real.

As others have argued, viewing injustice as real allows a clearer path and stronger justification for pursuing social change 36, 37 . Hence, we treated interviewees' experiences as real and true to them, and also recognized that their experiences are shaped by many and likely intersecting aspects, such as contextual, social, cultural, historical, and language factors, as well as individual factors like age, gender, ethnicity, social and economic position. We also discussed the general appropriateness of a critical realist stance as one that is both cohesive with reflexive thematic analysis 31 and that has previously been used meaningfully to understand causal mechanisms with interview data 38 . At the same time, we viewed the literature on mechanisms of impacts from cash transfers as evolving, and so treated this topic in an exploratory manner. We provide further methodological details regarding secondary data, working together, our positionality in our Supplement. We obtained access to the data through the UK Data Service and ethical approval to work with it from the University of Oxford's Social Sciences & Humanities Interdivisional Research Ethics Committee, Reference number 1999152.

Results

We offer a summary of the different impacts and mechanisms that cash transfer recipients reported (Table 2), alongside quotes. Recipients noted multifaceted impacts, covering economic, social, and health outcomes. Many impacts were positive, especially in regard to improving food security and the ability to purchase minor items. Importantly, although participants tended to express gratitude and happiness when receiving the CTs, they also reported negative (iatrogenic) impacts, such as worrying, stress, jealousy, and stigma from their communities, especially from non-recipient neighbors. We note, as a backdrop to all our results, that the conversations captured in this study were sensitive, covering personal finances, relationships, and status within one's community. Recipients noted the difficulty in talking about money ("It seems I am paining my heart" -Lesotho CGP, M, adult) and many were unwilling to directly or immediately talk about negative issues arising from the transfers in their communities or to criticize the programs ("...me dictating that this is how the SCT should run? No! that would be wrong, I am just a recipient " -Malawi SCTP, M, adult). In our reflexive discussions, we noted how the issues of power and context likely affect the interviews. First, from the lens of interviewees being recipients of these aid programs, and thus often relying on the cash transfers for their most basic needs; second, from the perspective of some interviewees not understanding the role of interviewers (in a couple of cases interviewers had to reaffirm they were linked to universities and not linked to the administration of these grants). With this in mind, our findings may reflect a partial and potentially optimistic account of recipients' experiences, as some concerns or negative perceptions may have been left unspoken.

Theme 1: "With my poverty it can't work": Enough to survive, not enough to change

Across all three CTs, recipients articulated a clear boundary between the relief CT provided in tackling acute deprivation and its limits and inability to address structural determinants of poverty. Hunger was the most common and significant issue for all recipients across both Lesotho and Malawi. Universally, basic food security was the focal need and addressing it was the most common priority for all ("this money is mainly and first of all, used for food"-Lesotho CGP, M, adult). CTs enabled families to obtain much-needed food and thus address acute and severe hunger. This was a fundamental positive difference for many participants, that was easy to observe and feel ("It made a big difference since we didn't sleep on an empty stomach anymore." -Malawi SCTP, F, adult). One participant noted that "Before the cash transfer we would eat a single meal in a day, after I receive cash transfer we would eat at least two times a day" (Malawi SCTP, F, adult), reflecting a clear increase in food consumption, though equally pointing to the fact that CTs were not necessarily sufficient for three full meals every day. Recipients most often bought mealie meal, a type of coarse flour, or maize which could be ground to flour. In some cases respondents, particularly in Malawi which is vulnerable to seasonal droughts, noted the difficulty of obtaining food and its very high prices. For some, the CT was essentially enough for a single 50kg bag of maize and they expressed a desire to receive food directly in addition to the cash transfer. Others were able to stretch and make use of the transfer and secure other needed items, typically products for personal hygiene: "When we get the money, it finds us having ran out of everything, so it will just buy maize meal and some soap and that's its end."

(Lesotho CGP, F, adult) Indeed, beyond food, other common needs participants could meet through the CTs included soap, vaseline, lotion, clothes, which allowed them to maintain hygiene, health, and for many a sense of dignity. Where households included children, children's needs were taken into consideration, with CTs covering school fees, school uniforms, notebooks, pencils. For some, CTs allowed them access to healthcare by covering doctor fees, purchasing medication, thus protecting and addressing health needs (see Table 2, material and economic outcomes and educational outcomes). Beyond access to healthcare, participants in Malawi reported that CTs allowed them to stop or decrease their hours of taxing physical labor. Many of the recipients reported having no option but to engage in piecework typically involving casual, short-term agricultural labor, such as weeding or ridging fields. Ganyu/piecework has been discussed as one of the most important sources of livelihood for poor households, although importantly, the need to do ganyu as a way to secure food often limits people's ability to invest in their own farm production, education, or professional development 39.

"The coming in of the money into the house helps to relieve a man from attending to ganyus and instead he is able to use the time that he would spend doing ganyu for other works at home." (Malawi SCTP, F, adult)

Amongst the interviewees were students who reported missing school in order to do piecework to support their families and not go hungry. Cash transfers enabled these students to return to school. Younger adults also noted that their parents (and in general older adults), who received CTs, could work fewer hours of piecework, and that their health benefited ("I think the health of my parents is much better now, because they are getting the money. They are doing piece work less often."-Malawi SCTP, F, adult).

However, across the board, recipients saw the need for deeper change, both in their own proximal environments and within their community. In their own lives, for instance, participants wanted to use CTs to improve their houses, such as through purchasing iron sheets or paying for help smearing the walls of their houses, though this was not possible for everyone ("We can't say that the cash transfer has improved our family as of now, because we haven't bought any livestock, and also because there was a drought some years ago. So when we got the money, we were using it to buy food, clothes for the children and paying school fees" -Malawi SCTP, M, child). Indeed, this illustrates a classic poverty trap -people want to direct their income to more substantial assets and gain upward mobility, but still need to first address their main needs. And so, those who are ultra-poor typically cannot use the CTs for more than securing basic necessities and food they urgently need. Thus, they cannot improve their circumstances more substantially on CTs alone, or as one interviewee summarized: "As for me, like when they said, you should be buying iron sheets from the money that you get from the cash transfer, I said, with my poverty it can't work." (Malawi, SCTP, F, adult)

Nevertheless, some recipients strived to save and make longer-term financial plans. In some cases, this meant trying to buy livestock such as goats or chickens, which were widely considered a form of financial security and investment. Recipients desired an increase in the transfer amount in order to make more transformative choices about their lives and environments. One participant commented that she would use a potential increase to become more independent and self-reliant, particularly around food security: "It would be better if they increase the money so that when you get the money you would invest some in the fields and use the remaining amount to buy maize and soap thereby being assisted" (Malawi, SCTP, F, older adult). On the whole, while being grateful for the transfer, recipients recognize the limits of what they can achieve with the CTs and have a clear understanding of their own circumstances and unmet needs ("Yes, the money really assists her but only for little things not to cater for big projects at home" -Malawi, SCTP, M, child). In this light, CTs can alleviate some, mostly acute forms of deprivation, but do not necessarily open a path to upward socioeconomic mobility for all: "Since my arrival here, I haven't seen any significant thing that was bought with that money." (Lesotho, CGP, F, adult)

Beyond their individual or household needs, recipients expressed desire for greater structural change, especially concerning their communities and broader areas where they live. For instance, recipients gave examples concerning how the land is degraded, there are unsafe rocks, and they wish for those to be secured to allow safe travel and connection to other regions. Many mentioned the poor quality of the soil, and although some were able to buy fertilizer from the CT to improve yield, this did not address the fundamental problem of soil quality and environmental rehabilitation in a long-term fashion. Similarly, some interviewees spoke about the need for improved sanitation and infrastructure more broadly -such as toilets, bridges, electricity, libraries: "We don't even have toilets.

No bridges; when the rivers are over-flowing, children are unable to go to school; Only if we could have bridges." (Lesotho, CGP, F, adult)

Lack of appropriate sanitation, such as access to toilets, illustrated how public health risks can exacerbate economic vulnerability, and that economic interventions given to individuals or families could not address these risks effectively. Similarly, lack of appropriate physical infrastructure, such as bridges and protection against falling rocks, was seen as limiting opportunities to travel safely for work and school. Students in particular noted the absence of electricity and a desire for libraries and books ("maybe they should construct a library for us, so, that we can have access to books as we do not have electricity here" -Malawi SCTP, M, child). Students appreciated that CTs allowed them to attend school, whilst also expressing a desire that their attendance would not depend on the receipt of a cash transfer, recognizing that without it, it would be challenging to attend school. Instead, some expressed a desire for a deeper change, such that governments would support school attendance ("this money helps us greatly with school, if it wasn't because of it, we wouldn't be able to attend school... I wish I could be helped in the sense that, the government should see to it that I attend school, it should help me with that" -Lesotho pension, F, child). Altogether, cash transfers successfully seem to address acute deprivation but on their own were not sufficient to support economic mobility, particularly for the most vulnerable. For many people, there is acute dependence on the CT for survival; many actively look for jobs but do not know where they would find alternative support and are thus reliant on this aid. Increased transfer amounts and complementary interventions could help, but ultimately, what participants speak of is the need for overall development, including governmental support for education, and investment in infrastructure and sanitation.

Theme 2: "I can't be eating in my house while my children are hungry next door": Grandmothers and the obligation to share

Cash transfers formally target individuals (e.g. older adults, children) but in practice are distributed within complex networks of care. Driven by a sense of moral obligation, recipients use CTs to support entire multi-generational households. Across all three CT programs, recipients commonly described using CTs not only to meet their own needs but to support their household (i.e. those living with them), including immediate family members, extended kin, including biological and non-biological relations (aunts, uncles, nephews, nieces, in-laws, adopted children), and sometimes even neighbors (typically through informal reciprocal arrangements of sharing). The money, although nominally received by one individual, was rarely considered a purely individual resource, but instead many depended and benefited from a single CT (Table 2, social and relational outcomes). For CGP specifically, adult recipients deeply felt the importance of ensuring children benefit first, especially concerning food security and school attendance. Nevertheless, beyond these core needs, even child grants were often used to meet fundamental household needs (e.g. soap, clothes, medication) benefitting more than one person. Recipients reported that child grants were predominantly spent on food and the child's needs, though helping extended family where possible was also mentioned.

Most strikingly, grandmothers receiving CTs (generally, pensions in Lesotho and SCTP in Malawi) were central figures in these networks of care and articulated particularly strong commitments to supporting family needs. This echoes previous findings in South Africa on the role of grandmothers' pensions in intrahousehold allocation 40 and broader evidence from Namibia and South Africa showing pensions were used as a redistributive resource to support schooling 41 . Even though these transfers were targeting vulnerable older individuals, typically not working, grandmothers in Malawi and Lesotho used the cash to prioritize their grandchildren's wellbeing. They used CTs to secure school uniforms, shoes, notebooks, pens and pencils, paid for school fees and even school trips for their grandchildren. This was often in addition to supporting the needs of their own adult children. As a mechanism, many older recipients articulated a sense of strong moral or emotional obligations to help their families, and especially their grandchildren -whom they sometimes referred to as simply their 'children', highlighting the extended sense of care 42 . Some older recipients expressed a feeling of impossibility of benefitting from the CTs alone, whilst seeing their loved ones suffering:

"I can't be eating in my house while my children are hungry next door, that is not possible." (Malawi SCTP, F, adult)

Younger family members who benefitted from the redistribution of CTs expressed appreciation for both the love and care, and the tangible financial benefits ("out of her love, she spares some money and gives each one of us a share so we can take care of our individual needs" -Malawi SCTP, M, child). Another grandson explained, "Before we were living without food, or very little, so we were living a miserable life. But after starting getting cash transfer she is able to buy food, paying school fees for my brother and also buying school uniforms for us" (Malawi SCTP, M, child). In this light, despite their own vulnerability and dependency on CTs, grandmothers often become proxy welfare providers, offering both economic and social support. Further, grandmothers also acted as financial planners, advising children and family members on spending and saving -for instance, teaching children about planning for emergencies, such as when they may not be able to work, or may be sick -in other words, learning to balance immediate consumption needs with future uncertainty and need for saving ("she tells us this I will save, in case of emergencies... We listen and we adhere to it for indeed we don't know what tomorrow may bring" -Malawi SCTP, M, child). Decisions around saving and planning for future needs were often sophisticated, given the scarcity many older persons face, and the many immediate needs they and their families have.

We noted that a number of households in both Malawi and Lesotho were grandparent-headed or comprised grandparents and grandchildren only. This coheres with other evaluations of the Lesotho CGP, which found that over 40% of recipient households cared for at least one orphaned child 43 . Grandparents, older neighbors and community members stepped up to care for children that were vulnerable or not looked after, thus taking on an added burden and extending the CT benefit to them: "He was just a child to my neighbour and I did not want him to starve while I am alive, so, I just adopted him."

(Malawi SCTP, F, adult)

Older women therefore became carers for orphaned children, generally expressing a deep sense of community solidarity, of care, and moral obligation. Recipients generally expressed these decisions in simple terms, without doubt or uncertainty about their decisions ("This granddaughter lost her mother. They are three children... Yes, I took her along with her two brothers."-Malawi SCTP, F, adult).

In this light, it is important to understand that targeting individuals in practice means supporting wider family networks, and that sharing with non-targeted members generally reflects conscious choice and functional household economics that address actual family needs, rather than misuse. However, this also means older recipients and especially grandmothers face added pressure and unique burdens. Some of these burdens are logistical, pertaining to the difficulty of accessing and receiving the CT. From one perspective, some of these logistical burdens are universal for all recipients. To obtain the CT, recipients often have to travel substantial distances, and invest both time and resources to reach the disbursement center. For older recipients, added challenges are limited mobility, health problems, and lack of transportation options -the latter meaning that recipients either need to travel significant distances by foot or save money to pay for car transport. One recipient summarized this challenge by saying: "I get there with great difficulty because I am no longer able to walk like before" (Lesotho pension, F, older adult).

Intergenerational tensions were a further burden. Some adult children felt entitled to the CT, seeing it as a household resource that also belonged to them. Some demanded greater contributions and support, while their parents sought to protect their limited resources. As one adult son of a CT recipient commented, "So when it gets here she keeps it, when we tell her we are in need of something, she will be taking it that we are borrowing the money, yet we are going to buy things that will be used by all of us. Or when she is the one buying things, for example, she will buy the smallest bottle of oil, which won't last long because we are many.

That is where we really encounter problems, because it appears she doesn't want the money to get finished" (Lesotho pension, M, adult). Such problems arose when family members had different expectations and needs that could not all be addressed with one CT, and when adults felt dependent on the CT whilst not being able to find work. Importantly, it is worthwhile to note that while it seems CTs provided some empowerment to women, such as improving their capacity to make household purchases and providing a degree of financial autonomy, some recipients noted limited change to more traditional patriarchal roles -many women still saw the men in their homes as heads of families or deferred to them for decision-making, even when the women were the recipients and men did not work, nor receive a CT ("the husband is still powerful in the house because the money just came in by grace and as a gift... The money can come and go while the husband is always there" -Malawi, SCTP, F, adult).

Although not designed to explicitly assess gender differences in spending, the interviews reveal some tentative gendered patterns in how transfers are perceived and redistributed. For some men, transfers were framed through the lens of masculinity and provider identity, particularly for the CGP. In line with other qualitative work in Malawi 44 and Ghana 45 , here too receiving cash was experienced as both a relief and a source of anxiety, as it enabled but also exposed their perceived failure to fulfil breadwinner roles independently. Men in this sample tended to direct transfers toward visible, household-level expenses (e.g., food, school fees etc.) that could be read as fulfilling the provider role, sometimes even framing the money explicitly as not their own ("these grants aren't ours, they are children's" -Lesotho CGP, M, adult). Another father in Lesotho described being asked by other men at a tavern to spend some of the transfer on drinks, deflecting with a reminder: "hey good people whose cash is that?" -Lesotho CGP, M, adult) -a single but vivid account on the social pressure men can face for social spending. Reports of personal expenditure by men were rare. An older man in Lesotho reported spending his pension on food for himself, and the needs of his grandchildren and family, seeing this as "using this money rightfully and accordingly", suggesting adherence to internalized norms about spending. Personal expenditure on clothing or hygiene was a means of reclaiming autonomy and dignity ("I usually see myself that as I walk with other males, indeed I am like them... I would see that I am not in a bad situation, I am not untidy, I am still able to wash the clothes..."-Lesotho CGP, M, adult) These patterns complicate simplistic assumptions that men are "irresponsible" with transfers; rather, their reported spending reflects a negotiation between structural unemployment, masculine expectations, and limited agency. Conversely, women -and grandmothers and older women in particular -emerged as key nodal figures in redistribution networks. They consistently prioritized multi-generational care, using transfers to feed entire extended families, cover grandchildren's school costs, and buffer other household members from shocks. We saw spending as less about individual benefit and more about sustaining networks of care, kinship obligations, positioning them as de facto safety-net managers within precarious livelihoods.

Faced with such burdens, women and especially grandmothers articulated feelings of distress and frustration from being unable to meet all of their own and family needs. This seemed especially acutely felt when there was desire for greater, more substantial change or investment in the youth, once more substantiating the generosity, emotional and moral imperatives that shape CT sharing decisions: "It really doesn't sit well with her, because she still has her own aspiration to accomplish but then the money isn't enough to cater for all that. She still aspires to send [granddaughter] to school but then the money cannot allow her. That is mainly what she aspires to" (Lesotho pension, F, child).

Theme 3: "I am already worried as to what I will be doing": Psychological relief and stress intertwine

CTs are linked to both positive and negative mental health impacts. While recipients note happiness and satisfaction from CT receipt and what the CT allows them to do, they also recognize a dependency on it, uncertainty about what they would do without it, and stress and worry about its disbursement. In this way, psychological relief and stress often co-occur.

CTs brought profound feelings of relief, pride, and restored dignity (Table 2, mental health and wellbeing outcomes). CTs allowed recipients to obtain essential needs for themselves and their families, and thus, CTs provided a sense of worth and equality with others in the community.

Recipients described finally feeling "like somebody", being "happy" and "content", being equal to peers, highlighting the important and far-reaching psychological effects of cash transfers. These effects were noted both by the direct recipients and those indirectly benefiting from them; one recipient noted that her children, who were able to obtain school uniforms and shoes, "always get happy such that they even say they wish it [cash transfer] could come monthly" (Lesotho CGP, F, adult). In one case, a family member also expressed a sense of relief and improvement in the family relationships, as the CT allowed the older family member who received a CT to not rely on and require family support. Such accounts indicate that positive emotional benefits are derived from CTs both directly from CTs and their financial value, as well as indirectly, through what CTs enable as change (e.g. food security, material purchases, access to school, autonomy). Further, being able to purchase food, soap, or school supplies was a signal of self-respect and participation and belonging to broader community life. One recipient noted he no longer felt "fear" but rather was more self-confident and saw himself as equal to his peers: "I usually see myself that as I walk with other males, indeed I am like them" (Lesotho CGP, M, adult).

At the same time, many recipients recognized their current dependence on cash transfers, particularly in the case of pensions or where recipients were especially vulnerable. While recipients expressed gratitude for the CTs, they also shared uncertainty and even stress, particularly about CT continuation. Across the board, recipients expressed a desire that the cash transfer programs continue, with one participant even wishing that her children would not grow up, so her family would continue to receive the transfer: "I am really grateful, we have really been able to address our needs; I at times wish these children weren't growing up, so that it [cash transfer] doesn't decrease" (Lesotho CGP, F, adult).

Such statements illustrate the deep emotional resonance of being able to provide and have greater financial security, whilst also dealing with conflicting sentiments, relating to uncertainty and planning for the future. Relatedly, recipients also recognized that what benefits they could obtain were not limited only by the amount of the CT, but by the frequency of receipt ("Given the fact that it comes after every three months, sometimes we just wish we received it monthly." -Lesotho CGP, F, adult). In this sense, the duration of what a CT can cover is sometimes short, and so wellbeing effects can also decay over time. For instance, many recipients expressed an interest in acquiring productive assets (livestock, fertilizer, field rental) but could not coordinate the irregular small payments into more meaningful lump sums needed for such purchases. As one recipient expressed, "it takes a long time to come, so you can't rely on it and make a plan in advance" (Malawi SCTP, F, adult). This mismatch between desires and inability to make those a reality was also a cause of frustration and sadness. Further, in the context of the CGP in Lesotho, one family member remarked about the children's combination of happiness and frustration: "They get happy because it is no longer like before, but sometimes they will be saying as much as this money is said to be theirs, it mostly buys food, and they can't get the school necessities, hence they are suffering consequently they aren't able to learn well" (Lesotho CGP, F, adult). Such quotes echo earlier themes (cash as helpful but not sufficient for structural change and intergenerational redistribution and potential tensions) while also illuminating the psychological consequences of receiving support that is insufficient for more substantial changes, thus leading not only to positive outcomes but negative ones as well.

Moreover, many recipients express a sense of fear, worry and uncertainty about the future payments, and that the notion of potential discontinuation causes anxiety. One father remarked, "I am already worried as to what I will be doing because I heard like this coming month will be the last one... I am already worried as to whether it's going to change again or what is going to happen" (Lesotho CGP, M, adult). It is notable that many of the practical and logistical aspects of the CT exacerbate stress, such as lack of clear communication about future payment schedules. Although CT programs report regular payment schedules, in practice the nature of disbursement and receipt is irregular -many recipients reported delays of weeks or months, or skipped payments. Both the uncertainty of future receipt and the recognition of dependency on external aid led to stress and worry. As a recipient commented, money and concerns about money become the focal point of one's life: "Basically the life we live now is about money; if it isn't there one lives in greatest difficulty which he or she wonders where will that lead to."

(Lesotho CGP, M, adult)

Stress was also exacerbated by logistical challenges associated with the receipt of the transfer. Broadly, this stress seemed patterned by CT program: pensioners in Lesotho more often worried about weather like snow and the physical toll of travel, whereas CGP and SCTP recipients tended to worry about the unpredictability of disbursement. Participants described having to travel long distances by foot, sometimes for hours, in order to reach the collection centers. For older recipients or those unable to walk due to health problems, this was physically taxing and sometimes completely impossible. Such journeys were sometimes dangerous due to unsafe terrain or weather, particularly in Lesotho's mountain ranges. Few were able to save money and arrange or share a car as alternative means of travel. Weather also affected the announcement that CTs could be collected, sometimes adding to the unpredictability of the receipt date; as one recipient noted: "When the snow has melted, it is then that letters will be dispatched for us to go and collect the money" (Lesotho pension, F, older adult). Some recipients further worried about thieves. This was a concern given that the day when CTs are received is generally widely known. Safety concerns were shared by some, which led to various considerations and practical changes in the lives of recipients about how to receive and store the cash safely ("No, it is not safe to keep the money in the home because someone can steal it from her. There are many thieves here." -Malawi SCTP, F, adult). The fears expressed were particularly profound, as one interviewee remarked: "I sometimes get afraid when I go to receive the money, people will not beat me and take away the money."

(Malawi SCTP, F, older adult).

In this way, CT programs designed to offer relief also introduced new vulnerabilities for some recipients.

Theme 4: "Sky-falling" and "satanic" money: Notions of deservingness produce stigma and jealousy

Beyond individual, household and family effects, CTs also reshaped community dynamics in complex and sometimes undesirable ways. Across Malawi and Lesotho, participants experienced jealousy, gossip, stigma and social exclusion from neighbors who did not receive the transfers. Receipt of CTs altered community hierarchies, whereby recipients gained financial security but also experienced resentment from non-beneficiaries. Negative sentiments were sometimes expressed indirectly, other times through the spread of negative rumors, and sometimes through outward exclusion from community networks. The central idea underlying stigma and jealousy was perceived deservingness.

Recipients discussed how the cash transfers changed how others perceived them, sometimes as "lucky", but more often as "undeserving". Non-beneficiary neighbors questioned why particular households had been selected. In Lesotho in particular, the child grant is commonly referred to as 'seoa-holimo' or 'sky-falling money'. One recipient explained about non-beneficiary neighbors that "they talk very much, they even say we receive seoa-holimo they think we have just received that money, some people are really not at peace" (Lesotho CGP, F, adult). The notion of sky-falling money is underpinned by ideas about not having done anything to deserve the money. This was particularly noted when the recipients were adults who were perceived as able to work and thus obtain income independently. In Malawi, similar discourse was presented by one family, who were told by neighbors the money they received was "satanic", linked to witchcraft and because it was undeserved, that the family would be cursed. In this light, CTs were not merely a financial intervention, but one that introduced social changes and discourse about moral judgment within communities and one that challenges ideas about ability to work, merit, and even divine order. Another boy further commented on the negative burden of gossip: "They trade gossip at us, they may even say that if we continue receiving one day they will come and suck our blood."

(Malawi SCTP, M, child)

In this manner, gossip sometimes extended to threats of violence, compounded on top of already existing threats such as thieves who particularly target cash transfer recipients. Gossip about recipients was often shared in public spaces, and participants expressed a desire to make aspects of the CT receipt less publicly visible. For instance, one interviewee suggested "that the money should be distributed to the beneficiaries from their homes instead of travelling to receive it from elsewhere, where people get insulted" (Malawi SCTP, F, adult). Such statements reflect how seemingly trivial logistical and very pragmatic aspects of CT delivery (e.g. visible collection points, identifiable payment days) had substantial consequences by making recipients' status publicly known and opening them to judgment.

The jealousy of non-recipients was often intertwined in a complex context of expectations of reciprocity. In some villages, households shared food or small cash amounts, lent and borrowed items (e.g. cutlery, plates), creating informal and reciprocal safety nets. For some recipients, continuing to share helped mitigate tensions and preserve social cohesion. Indeed, for some, receipt of the CT acted as a guarantee that the person could pay back a loan or would be able to provide a loan. Other recipients, however, described increased tensions and breakdowns in these community safety nets. Some neighbors refused to share with CT recipients, questioning whether they had real need and thus effectively isolating recipients from community support. Further still, some neighbors (and sometimes extended family members) refused to return money or food. Such ruptures illustrate how CTs affect communities, particularly through notions of deservingness. As a consequence, some recipients preferred to conceal or downplay their cash transfer receipt to maintain social harmony.

Discussion

Main findings

Across four themes, we examine cash transfer interventions as complex programs that have impacts on multiple domains and through a variety of pathways. We understood CTs as having multilevel impacts, affecting both individuals (the recipients), their immediate family members, households, extended families, and wider communities (Figure 1). Beyond advancing our understanding of impacts and mechanisms, our findings make three interrelated theoretical contributions. First, they challenge dominant behavioral and human capital logics of social protection by demonstrating that transfers operate not only through material channels but through relational and moral economies; that is, the meaning and use of cash are mediated by kinship norms, care obligations, and community perceptions. Second, we extend scholarship on informal welfare systems by showing that transfers do not displace informal support networks but are absorbed into them; recipients strategically overlay cash transfers onto existing practices of borrowing, gifting, and mutual aid, sometimes reinforcing and other times straining these networks. Third, we contribute to emerging work on dignity and wellbeing by revealing that recipients evaluate transfers not merely by their purchasing power but by whether they enable socially valued roles, such as feeding one's family, contributing to one's community, or avoiding dependency. This allows us to consider CTs' role beyond material adequacy and also in regard to socially recognized capability.

To move beyond a narrow reading of cash transfers as individual income injections, we build our analysis starting from the concept of moral economy understood as the culturally embedded norms, obligations, and expectations that govern resource circulation within households and communities. Drawing on Thompson 46 and subsequent elaborations in Africanist scholarship, we view transfers not as discrete individual assets but as resources that enter densely woven networks of care and kinship, where they are claimed, shared, contested, and reciprocated according to socially negotiated rules 47 . This lens challenges the standard social protection imaginary of the rational beneficiary who optimizes utility; instead, it reveals recipients as socially situated actors whose decisions are shaped by, for instance, filial piety, gendered care duties, generational hierarchies, and community expectations of solidarity. By attending to these relational dynamics, we argue that conventional theories of impact, focused narrowly on consumption, education, or health outcomes, may miss the deeper social reconfigurations that transfers set in motion: the renegotiation of familial obligations, the redistribution of care burdens, and the subtle shifts in status and dignity within kinship networks. This theoretical move invites social protection scholars to reconceptualize beneficiaries not as isolated individuals but as embedded nodes within moral economies of mutual aid and intergenerational reciprocity. Others have similarly seen households in Malawi and Lesotho are fluid, with people and resources moving across household boundaries 26 and have differentiated between the "imagined" household as envisioned by a social protection scheme in theory as something stable and easily defined, a "documented" household, as captured in national register where funding is directed to, and the "lived" household, distinct from the other two, as something dynamic and changing 27 . In parallel, scholars have also proposed to understand agency as operating at both an individual and a collective level, entailing negotiation by individual people and their local communities 42 . Taking this together, even though programs may in principle target individuals or individual households, this rarely maps onto what recipients perceive and experience, making targeting itself difficult and costly 48 . Awareness of these distinctions can help avoid undermining social protection efforts, and better assessing actual experienced impacts from programs such as CTs.

Returning to the ways we argued qualitative methods can add value in this research area, our results offer concrete illustrations. Unlike some quantitative evaluations, we can consider a wider variety of outcomes as prioritized and reported by recipients. This allows us to both see how universally prioritized food security is as the first and most important use of the transfer, and also to chart a wider array of other domains of impacts, both negative and positives. Our results allow us to inform mechanistic understanding in ways that are complementary and sometimes richer than typical interventional evaluations. For instance, we find that a single outcome like psychological distress arises through multiple distinct mechanisms based on recipients' accounts. Mechanisms like insufficiency of CT amount relative to need or unpredictability of disbursement imply different solutions. Relatedly, stigma was not uniform across all recipients but varied with the perception of deservingness and ability to work, thus being rare for pensions and pronounced for CGP and working-age SCTP recipients. This heterogeneity might be obscured in classical average treatment effects and subgroup analyses would only detect it if notions of deservingness had been hypothesized as a moderator. Programmatic Implications

Research Implications • Multilevel effects: Cash transfers are shared within households and affect community relationships, perceptions, and behaviors. Programs should anticipate and understand this, rather than treating recipients as the sole unit of analysis.

• Increase predictability: Predictable, consistent disbursement timing and amounts could reduce recipient stress and support better financial planning.

• Promote sensitivity: Sensitive disbursement options like mobile payments could reduce theft risk and travel burdens.

• Informative: Qualitative research can be used to meaningfully inform our understanding of cash transfers' impacts and mechanisms.

• Wider use: We advocate for the wider use of qualitative research and recipient-centered accounts, especially when informing interventions or more conventional quantitative research designs used to study impacts and mechanisms, like RCTs.

• Unique strengths: Particular strengths of qualitative methods include helping capture outcomes and meanings that questionnaires might not detect, inform mechanisms, help unpack heterogeneity, and inform ethicalepistemic considerations.

• Complementary protection: Particularly vulnerable households (pensioners, laborconstrained, those exposed to seasonal shocks) will benefit from complementary protections like direct food provision.

• Protective not transformative: Cash transfers alleviate acute deprivation but, in the present study, did not provide transformative, structural changes. Broader developmental commitments are needed to eradicate poverty.

Triangulation: No single method is without limitations, and our central recommendation is to promote evidence triangulation across various distinct methodologies, including both qualitative and quantitative designs.

Results in context

Our findings are positioned in a limited but emerging qualitative literature concerning CTs in Malawi and Lesotho. We corroborate existing findings for these countries 22, 26 , and report positive impacts on increases in food consumption 19, 21 , diet diversity 22 , basic needs 21 , school expenditure 21, 22, 24 , as well as concerns about irregularity and unpredictability of payments 20, 23 . Several of our results corroborate findings by the original study team from their fieldwork in Malawi and Lesotho 26 . We similarly find common intergenerational sharing patterns, stigmatizing perceptions regarding some recipients (particularly for younger, able-bodied people), a recognition of the limits of cash transfers and their insufficiency on their own to address structural determinants of poverty 49 . Our particular focus on impacts allows us to add further nuance to the literature about mental health and wellbeing, as we point to both positive (e.g. happiness, gratitude) and negative (e.g. stress, worry) impacts, and about intergenerational sharing, similarly highlighting both positive (e.g. support for grandchildren and their school attendance) and negative effects (e.g. pressure and intergenerational tension about sharing).

Our further focus on mechanisms also revealed more granular and novel pathways through which impacts arise (Table 2).

Quantitative evaluations of these three CTs also exist. The Lesotho pension scheme was associated with lower poverty incidence and severity in beneficiary households; household size and income were related to the probability of a household exiting poverty 50 . Lesotho's CGP was evaluated in a 24month community-level RCT which found improvements in child wellbeing, more spending on children, a decrease in morbidity in very young children, and protection against food insecurity, though the CGP was not associated with reduction in poverty rates 51 . Note as well, this study, similarly to ours, mentioned payment schedules were sometimes unpredictable over the evaluation course.

Another study showed positive short-term impacts on resilience, driven by the effects for less resilient households; with key pathways including increases in household expenditure and food security 52.

Malawi's Social Cash Transfer was found to reduce depression symptomatology for girls by approximately 15 percent 53 . Further evaluations have found positive impacts on caregivers' wellbeing and spending directed to food, shelter, clothing 54 , increased school enrollments 55 , and protective impacts on diet quantity during lean season, but little improvement in diet quality or economic vulnerability to food insecurity 56 . The Malawi SCTP led to large reductions in participation and total days of ganyu labor 57 . We find our results are generally in line with such results, with recipients' subjective self-report similarly substantiating increases in ability to purchase foods, without necessarily diversifying beyond core essentials (e.g. maize), to support children with school fees and materials, and to reduce ganyu labor.

Moreover, our results sit within a broader, global mixed-methods literature on cash transfers. A central line running across our themes was the appreciation for the many tangible benefits CTs brought, but recognizing their limits. For instance, virtually all recipients noted improvements in food security, though for some the CTs covered staples such as flour or maize, but were not necessarily enough to improve dietary diversity or meet individuals' food preferences. More broadly, as others have argued before, our results similarly point to the idea that cash transfers are a fundamental intervention to support the health and wellbeing of people living in poverty, but they are not a magic bullet that addresses the root causes of poverty 58 . Our findings are in line with findings from an unconditional child grant in Zambia evaluation, where three years post-program ending all impacts faded out; the authors argued that CT programs on their own "cannot vault ultra-poor households permanently out of poverty" and that to do so, productive capacity has to be addressed 59 . A general equilibrium effects investigation in Kenya found that CTs were linked to changes in wages but not changes in land price, landholding, inequality, or firm investment or capital 60 . An examination of cash transfer programs in Ghana, Kenya, Lesotho and Zambia concluded that while CTs can mitigate credit constraints and liquidity, they "cannot replace a holistic economic development strategy", for instance the need to have a functioning market context 61 . Relating to trial evaluations more broadly, we note as well that recipients reported that receiving cash can itself be "costly": recipients noted travelling long distances, sometimes for hours on foot or by paid transport, to reach disbursement points. The costs of time, money, physical effort, and for some exposure to unsafe terrain or risk of theft have to be considered when accounting for the net total value of a cash transfer. In this work, these burdens were particularly noted by older and less mobile recipients. Such hidden costs have also been reported in other CT interventions -such as substantial travel and waiting times 62 , and importance of location 63 for collection. Future trials and government policies regarding cash transfers should entail considerations of delivery mode and accessibility.

Further, recipients note the insufficiency of CTs to address structural factors, such as infrastructure and basic services (e.g. bridges, roads, protection from hazardous terrain, electricity, sanitation), opportunities for economic mobility (e.g. limited employment opportunities, difficulty saving for productive assets), environmental and climate vulnerabilities (e.g. seasonal drought, degraded soil quality), institutional/ public services (e.g. state-supported access to schools, long-term development planning). One manner of considering these reports is through Sen's capability lens 64 . We differentiate between resources like income and the "conversion factors" that determine how effectively a resource can convert into functioning or wellbeing. In our interviews, reports of desired but missing infrastructure like bridges or electricity, sanitation, job opportunities, public policies for education can be seen as environmental and social conversion factors. These limit the extent to which a cash transfer can positively affect wellbeing. For instance, the same cash affords less capability where roads are impassable or where a lack of public education support means CT-funded school fees cannot translate into long-term or consistent school attendance. This is consistent with and corroborates existing frameworks differentiating between protective and transformative social protection programs 65 . Our results suggest that recipients themselves locate the boundary between what cash alone can address like acute deprivation or food insecurity, and what requires further public investment. Importantly, all three programs here have a core aim of poverty reduction and economic protection, and recipients' reports testify to manifold concrete benefits on the individual and household levels. The lack of higher-order, more structural effects may not be surprising, given the programs were not designed with such effects as a goal.

Our findings are also in line with previous research on the root causes of poverty in Lesotho and Malawi, that also points to the need for creating jobs and income-generating opportunities 66, 67 , improving government and institutional support, access to basic services, sanitation, education, promoting gender empowerment 68 . With all this in mind, we have to distinguish between the importance of iterating on cash transfer interventions in order to enhance their cost-effectiveness or reach versus the importance of anti-poverty strategies as tied to broader developmental aims, which consider the prosperity, health and wellbeing of countries and populations 48, 69.

Individual and household factors

From the interviews, we also see the impacts of CTs vary by individual or household characteristics (such as by vulnerability, gender). For those especially vulnerable, such as the sick, the old who cannot work, and the ultra-poor, CTs alone were extremely valuable but not always sufficient to meet all needs. In line with past work, and recipients' own opinions, we believe synergistic interventions, for instance, where food is provided in addition to cash are important particularly for recipients with limited market access or mobility, such as older or disabled recipients, or households who may be more vulnerable to high food-price volatility, such as during lean season 70 . We also saw reports about the gendered aspects of care in the context of CTs. We found that grandmothers in particular shared their cash transfers with family, with a focus on school-going grandchildren's needs. This is in line with past work in South Africa, where grandmothers' -but not grandfathers' -pensions had a large effect on the health, weight, height of granddaughters 40 and in North Macedonia, where women -but not men -who received CTs increased the share of resources spent on food 71 . Importantly, researchers have been critical of some CT programs as implicitly reinforcing gendered expectations of care and child-rearing on women, who are poor and vulnerable themselves 72, 73 . With this in mind, CT provision can be bundled with support to access social services; greater focus should be paid on monitoring gendered outcomes, and fostering gender-transformative programming 74 . More broadly, the presence of grandparent-headed households sits within a broader literature on grandparents raising children and skipped-generations. Previous work in Malawi shows grandparents' particular interest in grandchildren's schooling 75 , and in Lesotho complementary research points to the essential and protective roles grandmothers play for orphaned children especially for school attendance 76.

Further still, recipients whose lives are negatively affected by environmental shocks, such as periods of drought and poor harvest -especially during Malawi's "hunger season", found CTs helpful to secure food and sometimes fertilizer. And so, CTs can likely play an important role in offering some protection to people in the context of exacerbating climate change problems 77 , though this should be paired with broader support for mitigation and adaptation at the community, country, and global levels.

Mental health and stigma

Our findings about mental health impacts and community stigma sit within an emerging literature examining psychosocial impacts. Systematic reviews of the impacts of cash transfers have generally shown positive mental health effects, though with caution for potential increased stress for some recipients 6 , and in line with qualitative reports of exacerbated stress 73 . Self-worth, stress, shame, and stigma have generally been understudied within the field of social assistance, though mixed results such as ours -pointing to both positive and negative effects -exist 78 . It is particularly important to understand the mechanisms through which such effects arise in order to provide appropriate support. Previous work suggests that psychotherapy alone did not improve economic or psychological wellbeing outcomes, and that the effects of psychotherapy and a cash transfer were similar to that of a cash transfer alone 79 . This may be explained if some of the key sources of psychological distress are external and harder to change through an individually-targeted CT. To put our mental health results in a unifying framework, we believe it is helpful to consider which mechanisms are, on one axis, tied directly to the cash transfer and which to its administration, as well as to differentiate, on another axis, between mechanisms tied to the individual or those arising from an external social source. On the logistics-side, CT administration suggested a number of pathways through which negative impacts on mental health could arise: reports of stress and worry were tied to irregular or unpredictable disbursement, varying transfer amounts, difficulty reaching the disbursement location, the public visibility of receiving a CT, including known payment days. On the side of the cash transfer itself, the CT amount was often seen as insufficient, and thus generated stress and uncertainty about how needs will be met. It is also worth considering the multilevel nature of these effects. For instance, although difficulty to travel to a disbursement location arises externally as it depends on a location decided by administrators, travel will be experienced differently depending on individual characteristics. For instance, those who are healthier or can travel in a car will face less of a challenge. It will also be experienced differently based on external factors: for instance, in regard to terrain or seasonal factors like snow. Other important external mechanisms included community perceptions of being undeserving, gossip, threats, isolation, pressure to share the CT, risk of theft. Such considerations regarding specificity and level are relevant to inform interventions. To illustrate based on our findings, if we want to address stigma, it would not be efficient to target recipients and provide additional communication about CT eligibility, as stigma and jealousy arise externally, and so non-recipient community members have to be reached. Taking this all together, a central finding is that the recipients' own reports triangulate with, substantiate, and advance our current understanding of cash transfers' impacts and mechanisms 2, 80 . In this light, we see the inclusion of recipients' own reports as central and necessary to all future work investigating interventional effects.

Limitations

There are notable strengths to our work -like our focus on recipients' own accounts, the triangulation with other existing research, and the rich comparative dataset across two countries and three programs, among others -which we have already mentioned. Our results should also be considered in light of several limitations. First, we highlight that we are limited by already collected data. This meant we could not probe further beyond the existing text, or that we might have missed relevant context from the interviews. Although we had access to the verbatim transcriptions, English translation from the original team, and our own colleagues advising on translation accuracy and context, it is possible that we still missed nuances of what was said. Importantly, non-beneficiaries were not interviewed as part of the primary research and so, spillover effects and community comparisons can only be examined through the views of recipients. We note that recipients' assessments are made without a counterfactual; circumstances would likely be worse without the transfer, and recipients may underestimate benefits already realized. Relatedly, we note it is possible that receiving a CT increases the salience of other unmet needs by making financial support visible and perhaps prompting comparisons (e.g. why is there no funding for roads?). We cannot adjudicate between this expectation-raising account and a more straightforward account of pre-existing unmet need with our data.

As we have individual-level data, we are also limited in terms of what we can say about local labor and crop market dynamics. Recipients in Malawi described reduced participation in ganyu labor, though whether this reduction affects village-level wages and casual labor markets more broadly is unclear from our available interviews. Relatedly, whether CTs shift households' crop-marketing decisions, such as whether they reduce distress sales of unripe crops, was not something recipients discussed directly. Given the importance of the agricultural sector and labor dynamics, both questions are important directions for future research (see Supplement for fuller discussion of existing literature).

Additionally, data collection was completed in 2019, so the perspectives captured here may have since evolved, and would be important to assess in a future update. All three cash transfer programs remain active as of 2026, with greatly expanded coverage in Malawi and an increasing shift toward digital disbursement in Lesotho -a change that directly speaks to addressing the safety and predictability concerns recipients raised. At the same time, both countries have faced substantial economic and environmental shocks since 2019, which have likely exacerbated vulnerabilities. For greater detail on developments since 2019, see the Supplement.

As we do not have access to a record of response rates or reasons for non-participation, we cannot rule out bias in selection or self-selection, particularly for Mulanje, where participation was guided by a local gatekeeper. It is possible that recipients with especially strong positive views were chosen, or that those with especially positive or negative views of the programs were more likely to agree to participate. Further, given that most interviews occurred during or shortly after harvest, participants may have had more cognitive and material bandwidth to consider a wider range of concerns or impacts than they would during the lean season, when scarcity has been shown to narrow attentional focus 81 . In this light, food insecurity concerns expressed here may be conservative relative to what recipients experience during lean season (see Supplement for further discussion of scarcity and seasonality).

Finally, like all qualitative research, the current study did not take place in a context-neutral or relationally-neutral manner. Interviews took place outdoors, and while most were successfully held as private, occasional passers-by were noted, and could lead to perceptions of being publicly visible. Even though there were established relationships with the villages, researchers (both foreigners and from the same countries) do enter them as outsiders to the community; in the case of Western, racialized as White team members, it is possible that they might have been associated with broader development projects or income generating activities 30 . This means we should not treat interviews as context-neutral themselves, but as situated subjective reports. Notably, relating to ideas around social desirability and demand characteristics, it is likely courtesy bias was elicited: that is, some recipients may have withheld criticism so as not to jeopardize a relationship or risk offending a perceived benefactor. More broadly, it is likely that recipients may articulate their views and experience in more socially normative ways.

Transferability

We recognize that the specificity of our two case countries Malawi and Lesotho raises questions about the transferability of our findings. Both are small, predominantly rural, Southern African nations with high poverty rates, noted HIV/AIDS burdens, and extensive labor migration (particularly from Lesotho to South Africa). The cash transfer programs we studied are largely unconditional and targeted at ultra-poor and labor-constrained households, which places them within the mainstream of social protection designs across Sub-Saharan Africa. In this sense, our cases are broadly typical of the region's cash transfer landscape, sharing similarities with programs in Zambia (e.g. Child Grant Programme), Zimbabwe (e.g. Harmonized Social Cash Transfer), Mozambique (e.g. Child Grant, part of Basic Social Subsidy Programme), and parts of West Africa (e.g., Ghana's LEAP). However, we are cautious about over-generalization. The specific cultural configurations of kinship, chieftaincy, and gender relations particularly the strong presence of grandmother-headed households and the cultural salience of provider masculinity may not travel wholesale to settings with different lineage systems, urban-rural dynamics, or religious contexts. Our contribution, therefore, is not statistical generalizability (particularly as it is known in quantitative traditions) but theoretical transferability: we offer conceptual insights around moral economy, care networks, dignity, and deservingness that can inform the design and evaluation of cash transfers in other settings, provided that researchers and practitioners attend to local social and cultural specificities. We encourage future comparative research across more diverse country contexts to test and refine these theoretical propositions, especially whilst engaging local recipients and communities.

Conclusion and policy implications

Our work reveals the complex, multidimensional and multilevel impacts of cash transfers on individuals, households, extended families and communities. Based on our results, we believe that when trying to understand the impacts and mechanisms of cash transfers, qualitative work and work that centers the lived and subjective experiences of recipients should be prioritized alongside more traditional quantitative evaluations of interventions and objectively measured outcomes. Such work has been relatively undervalued but, as shown, can offer valuable complementary and novel insights. Like quantitative work, qualitative research is also subject to its own biases and limitations. With this in mind, we believe stronger triangulation across diverse methods will strengthen inference.

Our findings point to multiple important policy considerations. First, given the multiple insights from interviewees relevant to programmatic design and delivery, it is prudent to involve recipients in program design. Participatory and co-production practices can inform interventions with better understanding of local, cultural, and contextual realities (e.g. intergenerational redistribution and needs, notions of deservingness etc.). For those who are vulnerable and considered ultra-poor, CTs alone can prove insufficient, and complementary interventions are required. For instance, cash transfers could be paired with programs that help individuals obtain productive assets, gain access to employment opportunities, and help with agricultural development to enable and sustain longer-term poverty reduction. For women and those facing additional care burdens CTs could be coupled with complementary empowerment and broader financial inclusion and education support (e.g. access to savings, education and support with controlling finances independently). In order to avoid unintended negative effects, future evaluations of CT programs should also more directly assess the psychosocial and wellbeing considerations we find here, such as evaluating the magnitude and prevalence of stress, experiences of stigma, and social inclusion and ways to mitigate these. These results should inform whether future programming should provide additional psychosocial messaging or support. Many pragmatic and logistical considerations were noted by recipients as well, which could potentially improve the efficiency and impact of cash transfers. For instance, improvements in communication about payment schedules (e.g. the dates of distribution) and program continuity, where possible, will likely provide clarity and assurance to recipients and assist some in helping plan and save. Challenges in traveling to the collection points and the public knowledge of when CTs are received were also noted as issues by recipients. In this light, assessing alternative modes of transfer distribution, such as via electronic banking through mobile phones, is important. Finally, and perhaps most importantly, while our results attest to the multiple positive benefits of cash transfers (particularly in alleviating some of the most acute forms of deprivation, like food insecurity), they equally point to the insufficiency of these interventions for deeper, structural change. People living in poverty are constrained by limited opportunities, exposure to negative environmental shocks, and often unsafe or lacking infrastructure environments. Interviewees recognized this and expressed desire for more substantial change. To accomplish this, broader and more ambitious efforts for development need to occur.

Working with secondary data For this project, we work with secondary (already existing) data. Unlike quantitative research, reanalysis of qualitative data (also called qualitative secondary analysis or QSA) remains generally uncommon (Ruggiano & Perry, 2019). However, as others have argued and demonstrated before, qualitative reanalysis can be informative and lead to both new and deeper understandings (Long-Sutehall, Sque & Addington-Hall, 2011). For instance, previous qualitative reanalyses have been able to offer richer views of interventional effects and mechanisms (e.g. for water, sanitation, and hygiene interventions; Loevinsohn et al., 2015) and explore patient opinions and experiences with important health policy implications (e.g. for guidelines on end of life care; Ziebland & Hunt, 2014). Outside the generation of new insights and interpretation, reanalysis of qualitative data allows us to maximize the value of the data, respect and acknowledge the richness of participants' views, and save research time and costs. Qualitative reanalysis is also particularly appropriate when people or groups are otherwise hard to reach or discuss sensitive topics, as it enables research on otherwise difficult to study issues. Finally, qualitative reanalyses enable comparative research and enhance transparency and trust in research.

We considered all of these factors when deciding on the appropriateness of reanalyzing these data. Ultimately, we found that the data was rich (containing a large number of in-depth interviews), from difficult to access groups and on sensitive topics (such as poverty and cash transfers), and was not previously explored with a specific view of understanding mechanisms and charting impacts. We also discussed the implications of aspects of the research process that we could not directly address, such as the interview process and delivery, including social dynamics between interviewer-translatorinterviewee -we view these as potential limitations common for any qualitative research project (e.g. influencing participants' responses through social desirability bias, misunderstandings due to language barriers, or power imbalances shaping what was shared). We similarly discussed aspects of our research project that we could address. This included, for instance, acknowledging that we can prioritize the use of the originally provided translations for practical accessibility reasons, but that these can be imperfect -and so, also calling on our colleagues with knowledge of the local language to guide and better contextualize our collective understanding of any given uncertain translations. We also discussed the importance of locating and taking an appropriate analytical approach, given both the reanalysis nature of our project and our research goals.

Finally, although working with secondary data does not pose the same ethical issues as primary data collection, it is still imperative to consider ethical principles with the utmost care. Although the data for this project is considered anonymized, such that no real names are used, and the primary researchers provided pseudonyms for everyone, before analyzing the data, we nevertheless considered the plausibility of risks such as human error (e.g. omitting pseudonyms or retaining the use of a nickname or more personal form of address) or deductive inference (such as very specific characteristics or statements allowing the identification of individuals or groups of individuals). With this in mind, we sought and obtained ethical approval to carry out our research from the University of Oxford's Social Sciences & Humanities Interdivisional Research Ethics Committee (SSH IDREC), Reference number 1999152. We obtained data from the UK Data Service Study ID 854106, accessible online from this direct website link: https://datacatalogue.ukdataservice.ac.uk/studies/study/854106?type=reshare#details The data is safeguarded. All researchers from our team who worked with the data accessed it independently, agreed, and followed the data access terms.

Working together

We agreed to start working inductively, guided by interviewees' reports, instead of trying to assess how existing theories or proposed mechanisms may relate to any stated experiences. As we see the coding and analysis stages as inherently subjective, we did not seek to achieve a certain degree of 'objectivity' or 'neutrality' (or even inter-coder reliability) but rather aimed to foster transparent reflection about our positionalities. We supported each other with some semi-structured and optional reflexivity prompts, tips on reflexive memoing and journaling, which we share openly on OSF (https://osf.io/byr54/). By taking a reflexive thematic approach, we focus on critically examining how our assumptions and backgrounds may influence our interpretations of others' lives and how they experience them. During coding, we aimed to actively question how our interpretations are shaped and remain aware of how any of our own experiences or expectations might influence the codes we generate. For instance, this includes noting (in reflexive memos and discussions) how our assumptions are challenged by the interviews, and what emotions or thoughts we have in reaction to the interviews. We tried to remain open to alternative interpretations and seek different perspectives from each other, revisiting the interviews and going back through the coded data when needed. The same spirit extended to theme development, where we sought to develop meaningful patterns, openly acknowledging assumptions and uncertainties rather than simple categories. Indeed, we believe the richness of our discussions and our different view points allowed us to offer nuance (such as acknowledging both positive and negative effects of CTs). We revisited the generated themes iteratively, considering both what meanings are captured and are omitted, and reviewing both earlier coding decisions and the interviews themselves. Outside of the seven researchers primarily involved in coding, all other collaborators were involved in discussions and review of the created codes and themes, with particular sensitivity to cultural and regional appropriateness.

Comparisons across countries and cash transfer programs

While our four themes hold across the full range of interviews, it is worth assessing differences and similarities by country and specific CT programs. In Malawi, the SCTP targets ultra-poor, labor constrained households, irrespective of age. By contrast, Lesotho includes an age-based pension and a low-income household child grant. It is the child grant in particular that is viewed as 'sky-falling money'. Even though these grants offer valuable help, community members see them as 'free money' given to healthy parents, who should be able to provide for their children through work. Thus stigmatizing notions around money that is not justly earned through work as shameful propagate. For pensioners generally there is an understanding that older individuals can no longer work, and pensions as cash transfers are generally more accepted within communities, though not entirely without stigma ("they will be saying this old people receive this money which they don't even know where it comes from"). Although not tied to child grants, similar notions about shameful money are present in Malawi. One girl in Malawi reported that cash transfers were described as 'satanic' from neighbors, and that her mother's leg swelling was tied to witchcraft threats from neighbors with whom the grant was not shared. In this light, the common mechanism driving stigma related to notions of deservingness for those who should otherwise be able to work.

Another similarity between recipients in both countries relates to irregularity in the cash transfer amount or frequency. For instance, one mother who received a child grant in Lesotho summarizes that her "mother's pension was much better because it came monthly", with another recipient similarly expressing dissatisfaction that child grants arrive via irregular payments and sometimes in uncertain schedules. Similar wishes were expressed in Malawi, regarding both timing and amount "Mine is just a request that the program should continue and that there should be consistency and regularity in the time that the money comes say for example every month…. The other thing is about the amount, it is too small because the first time it was K10,000 and then K5000 in the subsequent months." Another recipient in Malawi noted a wish that the payment interval would be regular, and that their household has a break in receiving the CT for 1.5 years. As further context for targeting and disbursement challenges, both in Malawi and Lesotho households -which are often the targeting unit with means testing or community-based approaches for CTs -are dynamic and their composition can change substantially over the years. The primary data collection team found that in Lesotho rural people resided in extended families typically related through a male line. By contrast, in Malawi, households were centered around nuclear, smaller units, in closer proximity to maternal relatives (Ansell, 2019). Importantly, targeting based on sociodemographic indicators (such as income/dependency of a household) becomes more difficult with individuals moving in and out. Perhaps relatedly to the dynamic nature of households, intergenerational and reciprocal sharing arrangements (e.g. grandparent to grandchild, parent to child and grandparents or extended family, neighbor to neighbor) were common in both countries as well.

By contrast, only recipients in Malawi noted they were able to decrease their participation in ganyu labor. In Lesotho, recipients of the Child Grant and pension alike described continuing piecework, predominantly unstable and physically demanding jobs ("thereafter [after the CT has been spent] I am still fending for myself by leveling sites for people or extracting stones for them, or like when it is beginning of ploughing season, I will just work with people in the fields.") We note regarding preferences for social protection programming, recipients of the Malawi SCTP commonly noted preferring food over cash. There was less of an informative signal in Lesotho, where some expressed a preference for cash, but many did not comment. These differences are likely underpinned by differences in local context, such as by the varying impacts of lean seasons, market opportunities, inflation among others.

Finally, travel burdens and logistics around obtaining the CT were present in both countries. Recipients in both Malawi and Lesotho mentioned considerations about distance and difficulty in reaching disbursement locations. However, this tended to be more elaborated upon in Lesotho, one of the countries with the highest average elevation in the world, specifically in regard to terrain and altitude -with snow closing roads, or letters delayed until 'the snow has melted', and considerations about distances required to walk to Maseru, hiring cars/taxis being prominent.

Local labor and crop market dynamics

Recipients in Malawi described how CT shaped their labor and productive investment decisions: most notably, how they decreased ganyu work. These reports are subjective individual-level accounts, and they leave two related questions about local market dynamics. The first concerns labor markets. Given that recipients can reduce their ganyu labor, it becomes important to understand how such reductions affect local casual labor markets. Direct evidence on this is limited. One study of an agriculturally framed CT in Malawi showed day ganyu supply decreased among CT recipients, while it increased for non-recipient households; further village-level ganyu wages rose, driven by increased demand for ganyu (Ambler et al., 2018). Importantly, this transfer entailed a single large payment for agricultural purposes and is thus different than the regular, smaller, unconditional transfers covered in our paper. With this in mind, the applicability of this mechanism should be treated tentatively and evaluated in further studies.

The second concerns crop markets. One potential mechanism not directly captured in our qualitative data is whether cash transfers affect households' participation in crop markets, including decisions about when, whether, and to whom to sell crops. This mechanism is particularly relevant in settings where smallholders face substantial price variation and liquidity constraints that may shape their selling decisions. Some recipients described increased investment in production -such as fertilizer purchases, land rental, and livestock as a buffer stock (e.g. buying goats as protection against financial uncertainty, or as a means to store value to later fund roofing work), though others commented the amounts were not sufficient for such productive investments. However, on the whole recipients did not discuss cropsale timing or marketing decisions more explicitly. To our knowledge, this pathway similarly remains unresolved in the wider literature. A quantitative evaluation including evidence from two of the same programs we capture here -the Social Cash Transfer in Malawi, and the Child Grants Programme in Lesotho -finds significant production gains but no significant effect on crop sales in Lesotho, and an increase in the likelihood of selling crops but no effect on how much crops were sold or their value in Malawi (Daidone et al., 2019). Their theoretical framework treats this effect as ambiguous a priori. That is, on one hand, cash transfers could increase commercialization (market participation), by giving households the means to overcome the transaction costs of selling (e.g. transport, time, market access). On the other hand, cash transfers could reduce distress sales, where liquidity-constrained households are forced to sell crops prematurely, before they are fully ripe, to meet urgent cash needs (e.g. sales of 'green' maize (FEWS NET 2002). Taken together, this points to an important gap: understanding whether and how transfers relax liquidity constraints sufficiently to alter crop-marketing behavior remains an important question for future research.

Seasonality

We considered the possibility of seasonality bias, whereby the shared timing of interviews may affect patterns of self-reported outcomes. In our case, most interviews spanned the harvest and post-harvest period, with few also being carried out during the lean period. This spread across the year assures us that, on the whole that is, when considering all interviews, a variety of reports across the agricultural calendar are captured. At the same time, it remains possible that seasonality shaped individual interviews. Experimental work on scarcity has shown that farmers demonstrate diminished cognitive performance on a variety of tasks before harvest (when poorer) as compared to after harvest (when richer; Mani et al., 2013). This was interpreted in a 'poverty as a cognitive tax' framework, where poverty-related concerns 'consume' mental resources, leaving less cognitive resources for other tasks (Mullainathan & Shafir, 2013). In the context of our work, which was predominantly during/recently after harvest, we speculate that such a framework might mean more participants were able to consider a wider net of concerns due to having more cognitive bandwidth. Conversely, it might also mean some concerns were less salient or underestimated due to seasonality -even though virtually all CT recipients brought up food insecurity, the degree to which this may affect them likely differs across the seasons and food poverty concerns may be even higher than what is reported here during the lean seasons.

Developments since 2019

All three programs described here are still active with the same core implementation policies. The Malawi Social Cash Transfer Program (SCTP) Mtukula Pakhomo has scaled and reaches all 28 districts. 2026 data details that the program supports over 290,000 households with over 1.1 million primary and secondary beneficiaries, and with MKW 14,936,026,000 transferred between January and June 2026 (Mtukula Pakhomo, n.d.-a). The program is funded by the Malawi government, World Bank, KFW, Irish Aid and the European Union. As of 2023, individuals received an average amount of MKW 14,919 (US$ 8.8) disbursed through physical-or e-cash payments, monthly or bi-monthly. (Mtukula Pakhomo, n.d.-b). The cash transfer amount is dependent on members per household, with an additional small education bonus conditional on the number of children attending primary and/or secondary school. For 2023, a single member household would receive MKW 8,000; a 2-3 member household would receive MKW 11,500, and a 4 or more-member household would receive MWK 12,000. The educational bonus entails MWK 2,000 per child in primary school and MWK 4,000 per child in secondary school. Reports from the Ministry of Gender, Children and Community Development of Malawi corroborate the spending patterns participants report in our study: the most common purchases are food, school fees and educational supplies; CT reduce hunger and allow more children to attend school instead of working to support their families (Malawi Ministry of Gender, Children and Community Development, 2022).

During the COVID-19 pandemic the government arranged an additional emergency program -the COVID-19 Urban Cash Intervention (CUCI) -which supported vulnerable families affected by income loss in its four major cities (Blantyre, Lilongwe, Mzuzu, Zomba). Payments began in February 2021, and an estimated 138,000 households received 3 months of transfers totaling MWK 105,000 (Masi, 2026). A qualitative evaluation of this CT is also in line with some of our findings: the CUCI reduced recipients' participation in casual labor (ganyu), and community tensions developed regarding program eligibility criteria (Malawi Ministry of Finance and Economic Affairs, 2024). There is growing traction of unconditional cash transfers, including through initiatives like GiveDirectly or Canva's cumulative (since 2021) commitment of USD$150 million in unconditional cash transfer funding to poor households in Malawi (Agbetiloye, 2026).

Beyond economic disruptions from Covid-19, notable climate shocks in recent years included substantial flooding after Cyclone Idai (2019), Tropical Storm Ana and Cyclone Gombe (2022), Cyclone Freddy (2023), and El Niño-related drought and dry spells (2023 onwards). These negatively affected crops, livestock, infrastructures and homes, while also increasing food prices (American Institutes for Research, & Centre for Social Research, 2023). As further current context, high inflation rates and food inflation rates, as well as the 2023 devaluation of the Malawi kwacha, were highlighted by FAO as intensifying food insecurity and limiting household's resources (Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2023). Malawi is experiencing elevated food and fuel costs, foreign-exchange shortages, weak economic growth, debt pressures, constrained generation of jobs, and increasing climate vulnerability (FEWS NET, 2026). These pressures are compounded by noted dependence on external donor financing for health and social protections programs (UNICEF Malawi Social Policy Team, 2024) and declining development assistance (African Development Bank, United Nations, & World Bank Group, 2025). Recent work by the African Development Bank, the United Nations, and the World Bank group provided policy recommendations, focusing on macroeconomic stability, creating jobs, building human capital and resilience, and strengthening infrastructure for growth. Two of these policy recommendations -namely the need for job opportunities and infrastructure -were highlighted as needed by the CT recipients in the current study as well.

In Lesotho, the Old Age pension remains in effect. The nominal amount has steadily increased since the timing of the interviews reported here, and as of the 2026/2027 budget release stands at M 1,000 per month (approximately USD$60). Notably some pensioners have expressed that these amounts are not sufficient to fully meet food and household requirements (echoing findings from Theme 1; Lesotho News Agency, 2026). The Child Grant in Lesotho similarly remains in effect, though the amount provided has remained relatively stable and high inflation has affected its value. At present the child grant will provide a family with 1-2 children M600.00 quarterly/every three months. A family with 3-4 children receive M750.quarterly, and a family with 5 or more children receive M900.00 quarterly. A report in 2022 estimated that this CT represented only 8% of the average monthly household consumption for that year, down from 21% in 2013 (in line with interviewees who had reported the amount was not sufficient to meet all needs (Binci et al., 2022). Since 2023 the Government of Lesotho has made commitments to prioritize improving case management and complaints, as well as extending a mobile payment pilot (Binci et al., 2022). By the end of April 2025, an estimated 49% of eligible child grant households (approximately 20,000) were receiving their quarterly grants through mobile platforms, a notable increase from 0.7% in 2023 (UNICEF Lesotho, 2025). Other forms of social protection in Lesotho include the Disability Grant, Public Assistance and cash in kind, and an Orphans and Vulnerable Children Bursary.

In the period since 2019, Lesotho's economy has also been strained. Estimates from the World Bank suggest that in 2020 Lesotho's GDP had contracted by 8.2% (World Bank, 2025). Climate shocks have included El Niño related droughts (particularly affecting mazing production, bringing it down to 60% below the average for 2024 (FAO, 2025). Droughts and poor infrastructure have been linked with ongoing water insecurity in Mafeteng, disrupting essential services like health facilities and schools (Motaung, 2025). As of 2026, about 15% of the rural population are facing acute food insecurity, as a consequence of climate shocks like drought, heavy rain and hail storms, livestock diseases (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, 2026). In July 2025, the government declared a two-year "state of disaster" in response to job losses, high youth unemployment (almost 50%), and further economic uncertainty brought about from the imposition of US tariffs on Lesotho's exports, particularly affecting the textile sector and its predominantly female labor force (Ngcobo & Fihlani, 2025). Cuts to financial aid programs (particularly regarding USAID and PEPFAR projects) have put further burdens on health services. In the face of such challenges, social protection programs for the vulnerable remain critically important, in addition to the calls CT recipients themselves have madefor broader development, addressing structural determinants including among other employment opportunities, food security, public infrastructure.
