Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Burnout-and depression-like functional collapse is usually interpreted through isolated biological systems, such as hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal signalling or inflammation. This may miss state-dependent coordination across systems, particularly within individuals whose cognitive and/or sensory processing carries a high physiological cost. High Processing Cost Phenotypes (HPCPh), previously described by us, span neurodivergent and high-ability profiles, including autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, learning disorders and giftedness.

We present a systems-physiology framework derived from longitudinal within-person observation of 12 individuals followed in routine private clinical practice, comprising 532 laboratory collections over 10-25 years per person. Each collection is read as a physiological state and successive collections as transitions. Across the series, multiple regulatory systems changed configuration within individuals, and physiologically related marker pairs repeatedly departed from their expected relations. In three examples, multisystem configurations shifted back after changes in context over days, months or one year. Over the last nineteen months of follow-up, in the collections where serum osmolality was measured together with sodium, glucose and urea, the serum osmolal gap (measured minus calculated osmolality) reached 10 mOsm/kg or more in 14 of 36 collections (14 of 34 when collections with C-reactive protein of 5 mg/L or more are set aside), in 8 of the 12 people, while measured sodium stayed between 133 and 140 mEq/L. The gap opened on transitions and was closed in stable containment, and the urine-to-plasma osmolality ratio distinguished collections taken on the way out of containment from those taken on the way in.

We interpret this pattern as multisystem regulatory containment: a coordinated reduction and redistribution of physiological throughput when information-processing demand exceeds sustainable capacity, rather than failure of a single system. We name this regime the Third Allostasis: a Displacement of Regulatory Purpose in which, with energy supply intact, neuronal and peripheral throughput are lowered so that the command centre stays viable. M The particles that open the gap are, in this model, compatible organic osmolytes, which cells release within minutes and recover over days; the osmometer counts them in full, and the sodium electrode cannot see them. M We also introduce simple visual tools for reading routine laboratory data longitudinally within the person. This retrospective case series is descriptive and hypothesis-generating. It does not establish specificity to HPCPh or normative longitudinal trajectories. Its purpose is to make a recurrent, context-responsive and potentially reversible physiological pattern visible and testable. If confirmed prospectively, this framework may be particularly relevant to neurodivergent and high-ability children and adults whose functional decline is poorly captured by conventional cross-sectional interpretation, and may inform both clinical care and the design of environments better matched to individual processing capacity. This may matter most in children, in whom such changes can have more lasting consequences.

Introduction

Responses to sustained load rely on coordinated biological mechanisms that adapt the organism to changing demand [1] [2] [3]. These responses recruit interacting regulatory systems-neuroendocrine, osmotic, metabolic, immune and vascular-whose joint behaviour, rather than any single pathway, maintains stability under varying conditions [4] [5] [6]. In the steady state, these systems obey relations that any textbook states: within one person, under feedback control, TSH rises when free T4 falls [7]; transferrin saturation and ferritin broadly move in the same direction when iron homeostasis is undisturbed [8]; eosinophils fall and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio rises when cortisol rises, a shift shared with catecholamines and inflammation [9, 10]; and serum sodium, the main determinant of plasma osmolality, tracks it when glucose and urea are in range [11]. The last of these pairs holds under a condition the others do not carry: calculated osmolality doubles sodium to stand for itself and its anions and adds only glucose and urea, whereas the osmometer counts every dissolved particle per kilogram of water, so electrically neutral particles outside that count raise osmolality without moving sodium [11, 81]. Cells adjust their own volume with particles of this kind, the compatible organic osmolytes [82, 83], and in this model it is their passage through plasma that parts the two (see Framework and Methods). M The present series makes departures from these relations visible within the person, as the area between two curves drawn on the same scale.

Much of clinical research has approached stress-associated states through symptom-based categories and through single axes measured in isolation, most often the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis or inflammatory markers. This framing has limited the integration of regulation across systems within the individual. In particular, little attention has been given to individual physiological set points, to the narrow within-person range of many analytes, and to the variability of thresholds for sensory, cognitive and environmental load [12] [13] [14].

Routine laboratory interpretation typically reads one value at a time against a population reference range [15, 16]. For most analytes, that range is a property of the population, not of the person; it is wide enough to contain most healthy adults, and a substantial change within one person can occur entirely inside it [12, 13, 17, 18]. A person whose morning cortisol halves, whose transferrin saturation moves from the top to the bottom of the range, or whose urinary specific gravity drifts from concentrated to dilute has undergone a marked within-person change, and every one of those values may still sit inside the printed interval [11, 14, [19] [20] [21]. Sodium adds a further case: a value of 138 mEq/L sits inside the printed interval, yet in a plasma whose measured osmolality is 300 mOsm/kg it holds a smaller share of the osmotic space, and standardized to a reference osmolality of 290 mOsm/kg it corresponds to 133.4 mEq/L, in the hyponatremic range (see Methods).

Across diverse sources of sustained load, whether cognitive and sensory demand, prolonged threat, or systemic and structural stress, patients present with overlapping features: fatigue, autonomic instability, diffuse somatic symptoms and functional collapse [22] [23] [24]. These presentations are often grouped under broad functional categories, in part reflecting the difficulty of establishing a unifying physiological framework that integrates multiple systems and individual regulatory thresholds. These individual differences matter most where processing capacity and physiological cost are both high. The High Processing Cost Phenotypes (HPCPh) framework, described in previous work, proposes that in a broad group of individuals-including the categories currently grouped under neurodivergence, high ability, and other profiles marked by domains of heightened capacity-information processing carries a higher energetic cost [25 -27]. In these individuals, domains of highly efficient processing may coexist with increased physiological vulnerability when demand remains high, and prolonged overload may eventually present as functional collapse, depression-like states, or predominantly somatic symptoms [25, 28]. Containment under such conditions is not proposed as unique to any phenotype. What stood out in our clinical observations was the laboratory pattern and its speed. In a practice that sees many patients with burnout-and depression-like presentations, this coordinated pattern-with several systems shifting within the same collection and shifting back within days to months when context changes-has so far been observed only in individuals whose profile is consistent with the HPCPh framework. The 12 individuals reported here were drawn from approximately 40 such individuals on ethical and confidentiality grounds, not on the basis of their laboratory course. The series documents the pattern and its reversal; it was not designed to test whether the pattern is specific to HPCPh, and that question remains open. Whether its speed is related to that architecture, or to the extracellular-matrix property proposed as an upstream constraint [27], also remains open.

Previous work in this line developed parts of this framework, initially in terms close to standard physiology. Functional Iron Blockade was first described with population cut-offs and a minimum duration of three months [29]. Over subsequent years of longitudinal follow-up, recurrent ferritin elevation without inflammation or another recognized explanation became too consistent to be treated as idiosyncratic, prompting a closer examination of iron transport within the person. As the same pattern recurred across individuals, reduced iron transport, rather than sequestration, emerged as the physiological reading. Once we began to read the laboratory profile as a possible physiological state, rather than each marker as an isolated fluctuation, the other markers progressively came into view as parts of the same pattern-the same shift in perspective used throughout this article. Cortisol and DHEA-S dynamics and hydro-osmotic regulation were described as components of a containment response [25, 28], and the extracellular matrix was proposed as an upstream constraint on neural topology and volume transmission [27]. A preliminary report on five of these individuals described functional reopening with expansion of dynamic range after load reduction [28]. What is new here is the extension to ten mechanisms in 12 individuals, the collection as the unit of analysis, and reversal documented across three time scales. This third version adds the layer beneath the same ten mechanisms. It describes the inorganic ions and compatible organic osmolytes with which cells adjust volume and excitability, the hormones that move them, including vasopressin and insulin, and the compartments (cell, interstitium and lymph, endothelium, kidney) through which a change of regulatory state reaches the laboratory tube. In the model, this layer is what makes the ten mechanisms move together as one state. M Against this background, the unresolved question is how sustained load of diverse origin converges on shared regulatory states, and how those states can be recognized and reversed. We propose that when load exceeds capacity, regulation does not simply fail; it contains. Throughput is reduced in a pattern consistent with priority for the central nervous system [30, 31]. What is defended, in this reading, is a membrane-bioenergetic budget: restoring the sodium and potassium gradients after each signal takes 50-70% of the ATP that grey matter spends on signalling [26, 84], and lowering firing is the only cut large enough to balance it (see Framework). M This containment mode shares features with the survival mode described for type 1 allostatic overload [3], in which growth, maintenance and repair are restricted [32], although here it arises from a different kind of load (see Discussion). We call this mode the Third Allostasis: beside the energy scarcity of type 1 and the social conflict of type 2 [3], an overload of information, met with the energy source intact, displaces the purpose of regulation from external performance toward keeping the command centre viable. M The same low values have been interpreted in classical allostatic models as an inadequate response [33]. Reversal alone does not distinguish the two readings, since an inadequate response can also recover. What favours the containment reading in this series is the configuration itself: albumin never fell below 3.9 g/dL while the growth axis and iron transport were restricted, a pattern more consistent with selective allocation than with generalized failure.

In this article we present a systems-physiology framework for a specific class of burnout-and depression-like functional collapse states observed in HPCPh, grounded in longitudinal within-person observations of 12 individuals followed in routine private clinical practice. The framework describes coordinated modulation across ten regulatory mechanisms: iron-redox regulation; osmotic regulation consistent with vasopressin-linked modulation; circadian neuroendocrine dynamics; thyroid regulation and peripheral deiodination; the growth axis; albumin and protein synthesis; glucose and insulin economy; prolactin; neurovascular and coagulation markers; and mineral and cofactor status. The Framework groups them as Core Buffer Mechanisms (CBM), Peripheral Buffer Mechanisms (PBM) and the Metabolic Signature, and calls the autonomic and vestibular systems, which carry the state between centre and periphery, Coupling Mechanisms. Two features organize the reading. First, the unit of analysis is the collection: one collection, with the systems measured on the same morning, is read as a state; two collections of the same person as a transition; and each value is read against the person's own series and against the other values of the same collection [15, 17]. Second, temporality is state-dependent rather than fixed by the mechanism. Speed was one of the features that drew our attention clinically: the mechanisms do not follow a fixed hierarchy of fast and slow responses, as an earlier formulation proposed [28]. In our observations, iron can move within days to months; return tends to be slower the longer the state has persisted and while overload continues, with each axis beginning to return after a change in context at its own pace [34] [35] [36] [37]. The model adds containment hysteresis: the state arms fast and disarms slowly, in part because cells release their organic osmolytes within minutes and take days to recover them (see Framework). M This question may be particularly relevant in neurodivergent populations, including autistic people, people with ADHD or learning disorders, high-ability individuals, and profiles combining these features. Across these groups, processing capacity may coexist with a high physiological cost of sustaining input, adaptation and performance under environments that are poorly matched to the individual. Recognizing the biological mechanisms through which overload is contained may therefore have implications not only for clinical care, but also for how educational and other environments are designed. The present series does not test whether these mechanisms are specific to neurodivergence; it shows how they can be read within individuals in whom high processing cost is clinically evident. This framework does not introduce new biological pathways. It reads established regulatory systems together within a state-dependent, within-person architecture and places one clinical point at its centre: when these states occur, they can be recognized in routine laboratory data and can reverse when context changes. Making them visible may help shift attention from what a person is expected to tolerate toward what their physiology shows they can sustain.

Framework

CORE MODEL -MULTISYSTEM REGULATORY CONTAINMENT Establishing common ground: a note on "textbook" physiology. Throughout this article, by the textbook refers to the direction expected from established physiology when conditions known to alter that particular relation are not operating. It does not imply that two markers are proportional, that they change at the same speed or by the same amount, or that their longitudinal correlation has been established. Different pairs are related for different physiological reasons: some by direct feedback, as with TSH and free T4; others because they report different components of the same regulated system, as with ferritin and transferrin saturation. The drawings assume no population trajectory. They ask only whether, within the same person over time, the pair moves broadly as expected from its physiology, or parts ways. When a pair repeatedly departs from that expected direction, the departure indicates that an additional determinant, not accounted for by the pair's usual physiological relation, is operating.

A coordinated multisystem allostatic response to contain information-processing overload. The ten mechanisms below are separated only for description, but are read as components of the same regulatory state. We propose iron and water as central mechanisms; the other eight are read with them in the same collection. We name this response the Third Allostasis: a Displacement of Regulatory Purpose in which, with the energy source intact, the usual physiology is turned from external performance toward keeping the command centre viable; the compatible organic osmolytes, their vasopressin command and containment hysteresis, set out below, are what move the ten mechanisms together (see Discussion). M We name the four groups used throughout. The Core Buffer Mechanisms (CBM) are the central systems that lower physiological throughput: the vasopressin-commanded osmotic buffer, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, the iron-redox buffer and the thyroid axis, with prolactin, the vitamin D-parathyroid hormone axis and the growth axis placed by their distance from the centre. The Peripheral Buffer Mechanisms (PBM) are the interfaces that absorb load when the core does not suffice: skin, gut, muscle and fascia, endothelium and glycocalyx, lymphatics, red-cell volume and the monocyte-eosinophil interface. The Coupling Mechanisms-autonomic and vestibular-join perception, discharge and connection, and act feed-forward. The Metabolic Signature, deliberately without an acronym, is the same regime seen through metabolism: glucose handling, insulin, the economy of protein synthesis and the filtration read-outs. The separation is descriptive: the ten mechanisms below are these same systems, read in these groups. Statements marked M are mechanistic propositions of the model; the convention is stated in Methods.

(i) Iron-redox regulation Circulating iron can become restricted through physiologically different routes, and transferrin saturation helps distinguish how iron is moving through the system. In containment, transport appears throttled: saturation falls while ferritin holds or rises, with no increase in iron stores sufficient to account for it and with C-reactive protein (CRP) at non-inflammatory levels, usually low. In reopening, ferritin and saturation tend to move together again as the state approaches equilibrium; in some people CRP rises slightly during this phase, usually remaining below 3 mg/L. When overt inflammation or infection is present, CRP rises as expected, showing that the inflammatory response itself remains available. Across these trajectories, ferritin often follows context more closely than a storage-only reading would predict, parting from or rejoining saturation according to the phase.

(ii) Osmotic regulation (vasopressin-linked) Urine concentration follows vasopressin [11, 19], whose osmotic threshold can shift within the person [36]. The index Ht 3×Hb (haematocrit minus three times haemoglobin; see Methods) moves between collections while red-cell distribution width (RDW) and mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH) hold: a change of state, not of production. Read over longer periods, the same index also reflects the background state. A change of sign that settles across successive transitions is read as a shift in background-the level at which the person is anchored -rather than as fluctuation.

Why the cell cannot buffer volume with inorganic ions. By the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz relation, any change in free intracellular K , Na or Cl moves the resting membrane potential and gates voltage-dependent channels; ⁺ ⁺ ⁻ and free inorganic ions, carrying net monopolar charge, raise ionic strength (I = ½ cz²), which screens the Σ electrostatic interactions that hold protein structure and, depending on the ion's place in the Hofmeister series, destabilizes the fold or precipitates the protein [82, 85]. Cytosolic electroneutrality is therefore supplied not by free inorganic anions, which account for less than a quarter of the anion charge, but by organic polyanionsmacromolecular carboxylates, Mg-ATP² , phosphocreatine, glutamate and aspartate-under Stewart's ⁻ quantitative acid-base chemistry and the Gibbs-Donnan equilibrium [86]. In the same logic, reactive polyvalent cations are locked inside organelles: labile iron is oxidized and mineralized within ferritin [87, 88], and calcium is sequestered in the endoplasmic reticulum and in mitochondrial calcium-phosphate granules [89]. The general solution is the same throughout this regime: the particle is taken out of the osmolality without being taken out of the cell.

The compatible osmolytes themselves. Taurine and betaine carry a positive and a negative charge within the same molecule, so their net charge is zero and their contribution to ionic strength is nil, while the fixed separation of those charges gives them permanent dipole moments of the order of 10-15 debye, against 1.85 for water; alongside them the cell uses the neutral polyol myo-inositol [82, 83]. Dipolar solutes of this kind raise the local dielectric permittivity of the medium and hold proteins in their native fold by preferential exclusion [90, 91]. We extrapolate the consequence that routine chemistry cannot see: at the concentrations a contained cell reaches, the same accumulation that cushions volume raises the voltage cost of firing. M The command path. Under central vasopressin acting on astrocytic V1a receptors, activity-dependent water flux through aquaporin-4 swells the astrocyte [92]; the swollen cell opens volume-regulated anion channels, hexamers in which LRRC8A is obligatory and in which the presence of the LRRC8D subunit is what makes the pore permeable to neutral and zwitterionic solutes [93] [94] [95] [96]; the taurine released then acts at extrasynaptic GABA A receptors and at glycine receptors and raises the neuronal firing threshold [97] [98] [99]-clinically, the protective fog described in the Discussion. Each link of this chain comes from a different bench, and the model assembles them into one command pathway. M The arm sits on the waking side, with noradrenaline high and the extracellular space small; the glymphatic wash belongs to depressurization, when vasopressin and noradrenergic tone recede together [100].

The osmostat is anticipatory, and blind to these particles. Circumventricular osmoreceptors in the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis and in the subfornical organ transduce effective tonicity through mechanosensitive channels-an N-terminal splice variant of TRPV1, and TRPV4-and are therefore blind to organic osmolytes that equilibrate across the membrane [101] [102] [103]. Magnocellular vasopressin neurons, in turn, are driven feed-forward by oropharyngeal, gastrointestinal and circadian signals seconds to hours before plasma osmolality moves [104] [105] [106]. We extrapolate one step further: the same feed-forward machinery can be addressed by the detection of environmental mismatch itself, so that osmotic command is recruited as a danger signal and not only as a water-balance signal. M The non-osmotic arm of the axis is already established at the pituitary, where vasopressin acts on the corticotroph through V1b receptors [107], and copeptin is its accessible marker.

Three distinct swellings. "The cell swells" names three different events, and conflating them is the commonest misreading of this physiology: swelling by solute entry, when amino acids and ions enter coupled to sodium under hormonal command, as in the hepatocyte, and water follows-anabolic [108]; swelling by a stopped pump, as in hypoxia and spreading depolarization, where ATP fails and sodium leaks inward, with lactate and acidosis [109]; and swelling by osmolyte held under command, with the pump working, zero net charge, no lactate and no acidosis. This series is looking at the third, which is also the only one that reverses on a timescale of days.

The renal arm of the same axis. When vasopressin command recedes, aquaporin-2 is internalized within hours and free water leaves, so the urine marks the phase faster than the serum does [11]. Filtered taurine, betaine and myo-inositol are avidly reclaimed by proximal sodium-coupled cotransporters whose adaptation is in Vmax and follows the osmolyte content of the renal cortex itself [110]; and myo-inositol oxygenase, expressed overwhelmingly in the proximal tubule, is a low-affinity, low-turnover enzyme-an overflow valve rather than a fine clearance route [111]. A sustained plasma load therefore spills into the urine, and the gap closes from the renal end. M (iii) Circadian neuroendocrine dynamics (HPA axis) The salivary cortisol curve usually changes shape before morning serum cortisol moves. In our observations, the first changes are in the shape of the day: the awakening response becomes less prominent; the curve inverts or loses its evening nadir; it falls early and abruptly, in a cliff-like drop; or it responds paradoxically. These are often the first signs that the axis has stopped responding in the usual way, which may make curve shape particularly informative in the early phases of containment, when load first outruns capacity, and in children. Flat curves tend to mark later phases [28]. On the way back, the curve can peak off-hour or take unstable, unusual shapes before stabilizing.

(iv) Thyroid regulation and peripheral deiodination TSH follows a central set point rather than free T4 [38]; peripheral T4 metabolism can shift toward reverse T3 [34, 39]. In this series, TSH may remain low even when free T4 and free T3 are also low, consistent with a centrally driven reduction in thyroid output rather than a compensatory rise in TSH. At other points, particularly during reopening, TSH rises while free T4 and free T3 are already normal or high within the person's own series, consistent with central drive leading the recovery of thyroid output. The relation is therefore read dynamically within the person: TSH can pull the axis down during containment and upward during reopening, and a single collection captures whichever point of that transition is under way.

One line places the thyroid in the membrane budget: active T3 is the main transcriptional accelerator of the Na /K -ATPase, so a centrally driven reduction in thyroid output lowers pump density-in overt hypothyroidism ⁺ ⁺ the number of ouabain-binding sites in human skeletal muscle falls by about half [112]-and, with the central axis inside the reference range, the model predicts a proportionally smaller but directionally identical reduction [7]. M A second layer is peripheral deiodination. When sufficient T4 is circulating, diversion toward reverse T3 can provide a finer level of regulation of thyroid hormone action. In our observations, this is often most visible during reopening or more stable states, once thyroid output has been released, but it can also occur during containment. Reverse T3 is therefore read as a context-dependent peripheral mechanism, not as a stand-alone marker of containment.

The two gates can be named. The central gate is the hypothalamic tanycyte, where local type-2 deiodinase raises hypothalamic T3 and blunts thyrotropin-releasing hormone and TSH despite low-normal free T4 [113]; the peripheral gate is type-3 deiodinase, a transcriptional target of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 and of local α cellular stress, which diverts T4 to reverse T3 cell by cell [114, 115]. Read together, they are the two levels at which the axis can be held down without disease of the gland [38].

(v) Growth axis (GH/IGF-1) IGF-1 falls under sustained load, as under energy restriction [35, 40], and in several individuals rose within weeks of a change of context. Its weight is greatest during development, when the GH/IGF-1 axis supports the growth and development of bone, muscle [41] and brain [42].

The brake has a described mechanism: during sustained containment, hepatic growth hormone receptor signalling is uncoupled post-transcriptionally-fibroblast growth factor 21 lowers active STAT5 and induces SOCS2 and IGFBP1-so IGF-1 falls with pituitary reserve preserved [40, 116]. We extrapolate the trade-off to development, where ATP diverted from linear growth and maturation toward membrane containment would appear as a reversible deceleration that tracks context rather than endocrine disease [80]. M The present series carries no paediatric data, and the prediction is stated so that it can be tested.

(vi) Albumin and protein synthesis During containment, protein synthesis often appears to be economized as part of the organism's management of load and demand, while albumin remains preserved. A generalized pathological reduction in protein synthesis would be expected to lower albumin as well; the pattern observed in this series (see Results (vi)) is therefore more consistent with selective allocation of synthesis than with global synthetic failure. [43, 44] Albumin is the exception that defines the economy. It is the main non-volatile weak acid of the system and the guarantor of sub-glycocalyx oncotic pressure [86, 117], and we read the translational economy of this regime as selective at the level of the transcript, sparing the products that hold the vascular compartment together while the rest is paused. M The bench counterpart of that pause is graded: mild stress blocks initiation through eIF2α phosphorylation [118], while severe stress, osmotic stress included, produces an adaptive pause in which loaded ribosomes stall at the initiation codon and messenger RNA is preserved for resumption within minutes [119]. Protein synthesis is among the largest discretionary costs a cell carries [120], and a factory stopped but still assembled is the cheapest reversible state available. This is the inverse of the acute-phase response, in which albumin falls while CRP and fibrinogen rise [121, 122]. These processes, together with glucose handling and the filtration read-outs, are what we call the Metabolic Signature.

(vii) Glucose and insulin economy Glycated haemoglobin, which integrates glucose over months, typically stays below the reference limit and is stable within each person; fasting glucose holds its own band most of the time but rises in some phases, after stress or under overload. Insulin does not follow glucose: in some individuals it stays very low; in others it rises transiently and markedly, compatible with transient peripheral resistance that spares glucose for the brain [31]. We propose that, in these states, glucose handling relies to a greater extent on routes that do not depend on insulin.

Insulin is read here as an intercompartmental transport hormone, with two triggers that are not glycaemic in origin. Beta cells carry V1b vasopressin receptors, with receptor identity settled pharmacologically and genetically [123], and the insulinotropic effect of vasopressin is permissive to glucose rather than independent of it-absent at very low glucose and larger as glucose rises [124]-so vasopressin commands exocytosis at ordinary fasting glycaemia. SWELL1/LRRC8A, in turn, converts cell volume into a depolarizing chloride current, acting in synergy with KATP closure [125, 126]. That channel is the route glucose itself uses, so any osmotic swelling of the beta cell recruits the same pathway and releases insulin with no rise in glucose at all, and when beta cells shrink on volume decrease at reopening, insulin falls to its nadir. M The same axis is visible epidemiologically through copeptin [127] and in animal models of vasopressin-driven metabolic load [128].

What the secreted insulin then does has three arms: it translocates and activates the Na /K -ATPase in muscle, ⁺ ⁺ liver and astrocytes within minutes [112]; it stimulates sodium-coupled neutral amino acid and osmolyte uptake through System A/SNAT2, itself induced by hypertonicity [129, 130], supplying the sodium driving force that the osmolyte transporters need; and it cuts urinary sodium excretion by about half within an hour at clamped normal glycaemia, with no change in filtration rate or aldosterone [131], which on the molecular bench corresponds to stimulation of the proximal sodium-bicarbonate cotransporter [132]. The model therefore predicts that when insulin reaches its nadir at the onset of reopening, the renal antinatriuretic restraint is released and sodium leaves in the urine together with the osmolyte wash. M The reactive phase is the same reserve seen from the other side: after a large contextual overload, transient peripheral insulin resistance holds glucose high for a short period with insulin present rather than absent, so that glucose is reserved for the tissues that take it without insulin [30, 31, 133].

(viii) Prolactin In several individuals, prolactin rose without another apparent cause, most often as brief peaks during reopening and, less often, for longer stretches during containment. Prolactin is a fast, self-limiting stress signal-its rise stimulates tuberoinfundibular dopamine, which suppresses it [45]-and central prolactin dampens hypothalamic -pituitary-adrenal responsiveness [46]. The pattern observed here is compatible with a short-acting brake on state transitions. Two non-pharmacological triggers account for these peaks. Hypo-osmolar swelling of the lactotroph itself raises intracellular calcium and releases prolactin in pituitary tumour cells, while in perifused normal pituitary cells the same hypo-osmolar stimulation persists without extracellular calcium [134, 135]: swelling is the trigger, with more than one downstream route. And the model proposes that vasopressin spillover during command transitions recruits the lactotroph along the route oxytocin is already known to use [45]. M This is why prolactin appears on both legs of the loop, and why a transient rise here is read as a brake on the transition rather than as pituitary disease [46].

(ix) Neurovascular and coagulation markers We propose that sustained high-gain processing increases neurovascular and haemodynamic demand. Shear stress is itself a major endothelial signal: under sustained laminar flow, it induces an endoprotective phenotype involving KLF2/Nrf2, eNOS, thrombomodulin and fibrinolytic regulation, while the endothelial glycocalyx acts as a mechanosensor and antithrombotic surface [47] [48] [49]. Under persistently high informational throughput, the endothelium may therefore function as a peripheral buffer for the mechanical and haemostatic consequences of increased flow.

Glucose has a second, non-caloric job at this interface. Through the hexosamine pathway it is the building substrate of the glycosaminoglycan chains of the glycocalyx and, through O-GlcNAcylation, the cell's own nutrient sensor: the same flux that builds the layer reports abundance [49]. Excess free glucose degrades what it builds -six hours of experimental hyperglycaemia in healthy humans halves the systemic glycocalyx, releases hyaluronan and activates coagulation, and co-infused N-acetylcysteine prevents it [136]. We extrapolate the corollary that matters here: a transient glucose excursion can degrade the layer, and raise fibrin turnover, in a person with entirely normal insulin sensitivity. M The matrix and the second circulation. Under the revised Starling principle, oncotic opposition sits immediately beneath the glycocalyx and, in most peripheral beds, nearly all interstitial fluid and extruded solutes return through the lymphatics [117]; the negatively charged interstitial meshwork binds sodium electrostatically without the osmotic obligation of water [137] [138] [139], with a storage rhythm of its own on a scale of weeks [140]. The same layer is the vessel's mechanosensor: once it is degraded, the endothelium no longer adjusts its hydraulic conductivity to shear [141]. Local hypertonicity is read by TonEBP in interstitial monocytes and macrophages, which secrete vascular endothelial growth factor C and expand lymphatic drainage [138, 139], and marrow release of those monocytes runs through sympathetic 3 signalling, described in chronic stress with β inflammatory monocytes [142]; the model reads the monocytosis of this series in a non-inflammatory register, as recruitment for sodium handling and matrix repair. M The post-mitotic delay of about a day and a half before the classical monocyte leaves the marrow accounts for the lag between a phase change and the count [143], and in the lumen non-classical monocytes clear microparticles and damaged endothelial cells from the same surface [144, 145].

The natriuretic switch and the shear sensor. Natriuretic peptides disarm vasopressin centrally, inhibiting the firing of vasopressin neurons [146] [147] [148], and in the collecting duct, inhibiting the hydro-osmotic response at a step upstream of cyclic AMP formation [149]; at the microvascular wall, atrial natriuretic peptide drives controlled shedding of the glycocalyx [150], while endothelial guanylyl cyclase A signalling governs the intravascular-tointerstitial volume partition-the tap itself [151]. Alongside the atrial, volume-driven peptides there is a local one: C-type natriuretic peptide is the endothelium's own natriuretic hormone, released by shear stress [152, 153]. The model proposes shear as one of the local triggers for sodium release, coupling the fall in flow during containment to the fall in nitric oxide in both the vessel and the lymphangion [47]. M A second feature of the series is the occurrence, outside a typical inflammatory or thrombotic context, of patterns usually interpreted within those settings. These patterns recur in individuals followed outside this series and remain to be investigated.

For one of those patterns the model names a source: cross-linked fibrin deposited in the interstitial matrix is cleaved by plasmin and drained through the lymph into the bloodstream, so D-dimer, a marker of fibrin turnover with many non-thrombotic origins [154], would here track the osmolal gap rather than any coagulation parameter. M The heparan sulfate lost from a remodelled glycocalyx is the vessel's own heparin, the binding site of antithrombin [136, 150]-the mechanistic link between an insult to the layer and a rise in fibrin turnover without thrombosis.

(x) Mineral and cofactor status Minerals and cofactors, among them magnesium, calcium, parathyroid hormone and vitamins, vary within the person across these states (see Results (x)). They were not the focus of this work and deserve dedicated study.

One of them has a defined place in the membrane brake. The species hydrolysed by the Na /K -ATPase is the ⁺ ⁺ Mg-ATP² chelate [155], and extracellular magnesium is the voltage-dependent plug of the NMDA receptor pore ⁻ [156]; the model proposes that sustained high-frequency firing together with the renal washout of the transition period depletes the intracellular magnesium pool exactly when the system is least able to pay for recovery, which makes magnesium the cheapest point of intervention in the whole chain [74]. M Zinc and copper act at the same synaptic gate, zinc inhibiting NMDA receptors in a subunit-dependent way [157] and copper blocking GABA A currents with high affinity [158].

Together, these ten mechanisms define the markers tracked within the person.

STATE-DEPENDENT TEMPORALITY

An earlier formulation, built to stay close to established physiology, ordered the domains by timescale: osmotic fast, neuroendocrine intermediate, iron-redox slow, and the growth axis slower still [28]. Following a growing number of individuals over longer periods made it evident that, in this type of response, timing belongs to the state rather than to each system. This is what we propose here.

Speed becomes visible in the window of a change in state or context, and the same axis can be fast in one window and slow in another (see Results).

Earlier work found a pattern of return compatible with hysteresis [28]. From a clinical observation recorded before any calculation, we propose that the longer a state has persisted before the context changes, the slower the return tends to be. Timing is also strongly shaped by context. In our observations, reopening and return toward equilibrium generally do not proceed while the context that overloads the person remains in place. This is one more reason to recognize the state rather than reading each marker separately.

Containment hysteresis-arms fast, disarms slow-is the reason timing belongs to the state. Efflux is passive and costs nothing: volume-regulated anion channels dump taurine, betaine and myo-inositol down a thousandfold gradient in minutes [94]. Return requires sodium-coupled cotransport-two sodium ions for taurine, three for betaine, two for myo-inositol-under TonEBP induction [159, 160]. In rat brain after correction of hyponatremia, electrolytes re-accumulate within 24 hours with overshoot while organic osmolytes take five days or more [161, 162]; we extrapolate that window to the human systemic compartment, where the same transporters, the same stoichiometries and the same transcriptional control operate, and read the multi-day persistence of the plasma gap as its first systemic read-out. M There is no human systemic measurement of that reuptake: a gap in the bench literature, stated here as such.

The asymmetry is by design, not by poverty, and it gives the two clocks. Because the intracellular compartment holds about twice the volume of the extracellular one, each 1 mmol/L released by cells appears as about 2 mmol/L in plasma and interstitium together, and recollecting a gap of 20 mOsm/kg costs well under one part per thousand of a day's ATP: the open gap is the cheap state and the cushioned one is the expensive one. M The urine runs on the fast clock-vasopressin has a half-life of minutes and the collecting duct answers in hoursand the plasma on the slow one, so the urine says where the person is and the serum where the person has been; read this way, the gap is a marker of flux rather than of state, against a distribution centred near 2 ± 6 -mOsm/kg in hospital patients [81] (Methods).

STATE TRANSITIONS

We propose that the observed dynamics reflect non-linear transitions between regulatory states rather than gradual deterioration. Transitions can be abrupt (see Results, Reversal on three time scales). Under sustained load, the organism recruits successive, highly articulated containment mechanisms that appear to buffer the central nervous system (CNS) against collapse [30, 31].

Information-processing overload is a physical load. Escape points-windows in which input falls and the system can disengage and sleep-depend on perceived safety [50, 51]. Interpersonal support is also one of the conditions the CNS registers as safety; both therefore have physiological consequences. The full set of containment mechanisms serves survival and is profoundly responsive to context and load. In HPCPh, sensory and/or cognitive overload can place the CNS itself under unsustainable demand.

The load can be stated in the currency the cell uses. Between 50 and 70% of signalling ATP in grey matter goes to restoring transmembrane sodium and potassium gradients [26, 84], so sustained high-gain capture and the recruitment of Bridges push transmembrane flux toward the kinetic ceiling of the pump. When potassium efflux outpaces active reuptake, extracellular potassium accumulates, depolarizes neighbouring membranes and moves the network toward the threshold of the spreading depolarization first described by Leão [109, 163, 164]. Lowering firing is the only cut large enough to meet the budget, and it has to come before the ceiling is reached, which is why the response is feed-forward rather than reactive. M The triggers usually lie in the person's Islands-domains of high-gain capture, prolonged plasticity and/or extensive multimodal convergence-when input exceeds processing capacity, and/or in the Desertsimplementation bottlenecks that limit the efficient stabilization of rapid, automatized or serial execution-when persistent environmental demand exceeds bridging capacity. Bridges are the distributed compensatory pathways required to translate processing within Islands into external performance across Desert domains [25].

As the mismatch persists, set points and physiological accommodations can shift into a more pervasive containment state, with lower-throughput metabolic responses, in line with what has been described as a persistent cell danger response [52]. At the extreme, what we observed resembles a hibernation-like state. Even then, the response can be read as adaptive. In active-inference terms, when the organism's internal generative model increasingly predicts that available actions are unlikely to change the expected outcome, continued investment in active responding becomes progressively less advantageous [53, 54]. This converges with the neuroscience revision of learned helplessness, in which passivity is the default response to prolonged uncontrollable aversive events, whereas what is learned is control [55].

Once the systems settle into a more stable freeze-like regulatory state-the hibernation-like state-adjusting the context becomes a necessary starting point. In our clinical experience, reopening remains possible, but the way back becomes a process shaped by the physiological consequences of prolonged overload and mismatch. Two features tend to track the duration and intensity of the preceding state: how much flexibility the mechanisms still retain, and how much time, effort and consistency are required for return to an active, engaged state . Both worsen when escape points, recognition of the nature of the overload, and interpersonal support are absent.

Two levels of variation: background and fluctuation. Within each person, the signal of each axis can be read at two levels. The background is the slow component: a prevailing level with inertia and regulatory memory, the level at which the person is anchored over months. The fluctuation is the fast component: short-range variability around that background, visible within a delimited window. In our experience, prolonged containment-and, at the extreme, the hibernation-like state-appears as a persistent background with flattened fluctuation amplitude. Reopening appears as fluctuation regaining amplitude, the background changing level, or both, with no fixed order between the two. What recovery restores is flexibility: the capacity of the system to move.

Read with the two clocks, these two levels have a measurable counterpart: the urine index (see Methods) reports the fluctuation of the water axis within hours to days, while the plasma gap keeps the memory of the previous phase, so a single collection can show a background and a movement at once.

The clinical course of these states, from reactive overload to containment and reopening, is described in the Discussion.

Methods

Setting and selection

This is a retrospective case series from a single psychiatric practice offering combined treatment with a focus on psychotherapy, which also provides one-off consultations and evaluations. Within the much larger population seen in the practice, approximately 40 individuals were identified as having a profile compatible with the HPCPh framework [25]. Twelve were invited on ethical grounds (no foreseeable risk to their care; identity protection feasible) and consented. They were chosen on ethical and confidentiality grounds, not on the basis of their laboratory course, and none was selected in advance for study. Some individuals were not included because they would be too easy to identify, including patients with more extensive laboratory follow-up and some in whom the mechanisms were most evident and the reversals clearest. Among those were children and cases from the same family, including monozygotic twins. This came at the cost of showing the mechanisms in their clearest form. We chose to protect the people involved and to present the mechanisms, even if less strikingly, so that they can be studied further on more suitable research grounds. Some individuals were followed regularly; others were seen in consultation only, with a review of all available laboratory records and no follow-up or treatment by the practice. Care was provided by different clinicians over time. Some individuals received an approach directed by the HPCPh framework; others never did: they were identified as compatible with the profile from past clinical records. The series is therefore heterogeneous, by design.

The study does not set out to give definitive answers or to meet a standard of statistical purity. Its aim is to present the mechanisms so that they can be studied and recognized in clinical practice, since they appear to be reversible and are rarely identified. This choice also follows the principles of bioethics (beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice and autonomy): to benefit as many people as possible with the least potential harm to the individuals in the series. For the same reason, minimal clinical history is given; in a private practice, people are easy to identify. Protecting them was a central concern of the study and shaped several decisions on methods and on how results are presented.

All tests were ordered, and all treatment decisions were made, on clinical grounds only. The decision to report these cases came afterwards, as the importance of the profile and its potential impact became evident, and the invitation to take part and the reporting took place only after a protocol for the case series had been carefully drawn up and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital de Clínicas de Porto Alegre. Understanding what was happening, delineating the phenotype and seeing the response to treatment all took place within routine clinical care. This is also why some individuals have tests that others do not.

Five of the 12 individuals were included in the first version of this preprint [28].

Ethics, consent and identity protection The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Hospital de Clínicas de Porto Alegre (CAAE 99556626.7.0000.5327). All participants gave written informed consent, signed on paper, in addition to earlier verbal consent and an online record.

As stated above, ethical and confidentiality concerns were a priority in the design of this study. Consent covers publication of laboratory results, age and sex, with minimal identification. Diagnoses are reported as counts, never linked to individuals, because the series is small and comes from a single practice. Calendar dates are not reported; time is given as intervals (days, weeks, months) to protect identity. Individuals are named in the text only by their role in the series.

The laboratory series Collections were ordered as part of each person's care, by different physicians in different circumstances over the years, not by protocol, and the interval between them is that of the clinical schedule. Early-morning collection is the local standard; when tests were requested by the practice, the instruction was to collect in the morning, within two hours of waking. The reading uses the laboratory report only. No collection was excluded: collections with high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) ≥ 5 mg/L remain, with the CRP value reported alongside, because when there is inflammation the collection responds, and this is shown as well. Results reported as below or above a limit (such as "< 0.1") were not converted into numbers. When there are two results on the same date, both are kept; where a calculation requires a single value, the rule is stated with it. An empty cell means the test was not done. Most collections were run in one of two laboratories; each collection was assigned to a laboratory from the signature of the reference ranges printed in its report, without naming the laboratory.

The laboratory sees blood, not brain. Plasma is about one quarter of the extracellular compartment and roughly one twelfth of total body water, and the blood-brain barrier, the astrocyte and the interstitial gel stand between the event and the tube. What the report shows is the shadow, cast in plasma, of a redistribution occurring on the other side of those boundaries. This is one more reason why the collection, read as a whole, is the unit of reading: no single value in the tube is the event itself.

The collection as the unit of state The unit of reading is the collection: one morning, with all systems measured on it read together. One collection is a state; two collections of the same person are a transition. Each value is read against the person's own series and against the other values of the same collection, never alone against a population range. A single analyte's trajectory is read inside the collection, not only against its own earlier values.

Why the collections are drawn The tools described below were built to show what the clinical reading had already found. Numerical summaries were tried first and set aside: standardizing each value against the dispersion of its own series, or statistically adjusting one marker for another as a covariate, removed the very variation under study. What carried the information was less the level of a test than the form of its movement, and the way several tests moved together or apart from one collection to the next.

For patterns of this kind, with non-linear interactions across systems, figures, colours, lines and marks allow a simultaneous view of the data that the serial presentation of information compresses. In our view, the reduction of a phenomenon to a single number is a central problem of present-day practice and research, above all for multisystem processes and for functional, adaptive mechanisms. What follows is a structure for reading, meant to be adapted, not a fixed procedure. The descriptions below show the tools we built to read the states and the possibilities they open; they are examples, and each can be changed as long as the basic principles of the dynamics of the states are respected. Every choice along the way is an example rather than a rule: the measure of centre (mean, median or another), the scale on which values are placed, the pairs that are drawn. The most suitable and accessible format, compatible with the individual, group or population under study or in clinical evaluation, should be chosen; where we suggest that something can be changed, we say so.

How the collections are shown The table-figure. Each person's series is shown as a table-figure: one column per collection, one row per test (Figure 2; all people, Figure S1). Days since the first collection shown are printed at the top of each column. Days elapsed since the previous collection are repeated on an interval row placed at the middle of the table, with the same number of rows above and below it, and on more than one row when the table is tall, so that the timing can be read anywhere in the column. Time is given in days; no figure carries a calendar date. Each cell is coloured by its position on the common scale described below, aiming to make tests with different units visible on one scale; the number is printed inside the cell. Blue is low, orange is high, and orange does not mean bad. hs-CRP is shown by category only: green up to 3 mg/L, yellow above 3 and below 5, red at 5 or above; a result reported only as below 5 is hatched green. A white cell means the test was not done in that collection; a hatched grey cell means it was done but has no position on the scale. The dashed line marks a change of context declared by the author. Tests are grouped by system, in the same fixed order for every person: hs-CRP, then iron, the red cells, the water axis, the HPA axis, the thyroid, the growth axis, the white cells, prolactin, the platelets, glucose and insulin, and albumin. Each table-figure shows a window of the series, stated in its legend; a collection in which only one test was done is not shown as a column.

The choice of tests, their grouping and their order was the one that gave the authors the clearest reading. With the rationale of the systems and axes kept, the order and the tests placed in the table-figure can vary; the method is the reading of the column, with the rows organized by system or by dispersion. In the clinic, when one person is being read, other techniques can be just as useful, for example a conditional-formatting gradient applied to the values themselves (one colour at the low end, white at the centre, another colour at the high end), which can reveal nuances of the background and fluctuations that were not visible before.

The area between the curves. The internal control of the collection is a set of textbook pairs, defined only by their classical regulators: TSH × free T4 [7]; ferritin × transferrin saturation [8]; morning cortisol × neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio, × eosinophils and × monocytes [9, 10]; ACTH × cortisol; and serum sodium × osmolality [11]. Both tests of a pair are drawn on the same scale over time, in days since the first collection shown. Where the textbook runs the pair in opposite directions, one of them is turned over (100 minus its position on the scale): free T4 against TSH, and eosinophils and monocytes against cortisol. Under the textbook relation, the two curves would then move together. The area between them is shaded. It is drawn, not measured: no number is attached to it, and nothing is compared with another person. Clinically, what matters is the drawing itself, and above all what we call the "joint view of the areas": all the areas of one person seen together, where the holes open, where they close, and whether they open together. For research, the areas may be measured and explored, for example to look for connections between pairs and for reciprocal influence, as long as the exploration stays descriptive. The pairs are drawn in two layouts. In the first, one pair per figure and one person per panel: ferritin × transferrin saturation (Figure 3), TSH × free T4 (Figure 4) and the other pairs (Figure S3); here a pair is drawn when the person has at least four collections with both tests. In the second, one person per figure and one chart per pair, each pair starting at its own first collection, with the dashed line marking the same moment in every chart (Figure S2); this second layout is the joint view of the areas. Like the table-figure, it is a reading tool. The rationale is to make the variations visible as a landscape: opposite movements do not cancel each other, as they would in a difference or a mean, and the slope of the curves (the speed of change) and the amplitude of the gap are meant to be seen at the same time, all the information at once. The geometric view is kept on purpose: it protects the reading of the collection as a network, in which every axis acts on the others in ways too entangled for a summary number to hold without losing what is clinically useful. Simple proportions. At the foot of the table-figure, two proportions are printed as examples: "TSH if free T4 were 1" (TSH ÷ free T4) and "ferritin if saturation were 20%" (ferritin × 20 ÷ saturation). Each is shaded by its distance from the person's own median: white at the median, darker with distance, black when the distance exceeds half the width of the reference interval printed on the report for the test in whose units the proportion is expressed (TSH for the first, ferritin for the second). They only show that, for a fixed level of one test, the other varies widely within the person. They are not textbook indices and do not replace them. They are examples of one move: fix one value of a pair and see how the other is distributed against it over time. The same kind of proportion can be built for any pair whose regulator is known, with its rationale stated, and applied to other parts of the system in the same way; here it is shown in this form to demonstrate the variation and the application. The proportions were not adjusted for how the variability of a test may change across its range, which ideally would be taken into account, and should be where it is known and where population studies exist; here the aim is visual, and the deviation expected from that source would in any case be far smaller than what appears. It serves the understanding of that one person's biology; it asserts no general truth.

The osmolal gap and sodium at a reference osmolality. Where the collection carries a measured serum osmolality, two further quantities are computed, and they are declared here as the proportions above are. The serum osmolal gap is measured osmolality minus calculated osmolality, 2 × Na + glucose/18 + urea/6 ⁺ (sodium in mEq/L, glucose and urea in mg/dL), computed only when the four inputs come from the same collection; only osmolality measured by the laboratory enters, never an osmolality calculated by the laboratory and printed on the report. The equation is stated because the size of a gap, and with it any prevalence, depends on the equation used [81]. With the same terms and ethanol added, in consecutive adult patients enrolled prospectively at an inner-city hospital, the gap was centred at 2 ± 6 mOsm/kg [81], and its biological variation within the person has been measured directly, weekly over five weeks in healthy volunteers sampled fasting in the morning [165]. A gap of 10 mOsm/kg or more therefore lies at the edge of the 95% interval; as a reading mark, the threshold of 10 is a conservative choice, not a test. The second quantity is the move of the proportions above applied to the serum sodium × osmolality pair [11]: "sodium if osmolality were 290", Na@290 = 290 × Na ÷ measured osmolality. The distance 140 Na@290 is the sodium yielded to other ⁺ particles, and twice it, 2 × (140 Na@290), approximates the gap across the range observed in this series; the two are exactly equal when measured osmolality is 290 mOsm/kg and glucose/18 + urea/6 sums to 10 mOsm/kg. Like the 1 and the 20% of the proportions above, 290 mOsm/kg and 140 mEq/L are reference points and can be changed. Neither quantity is printed on the figures; both are used in the text (Results (ii)), where the gap states, in mOsm/kg and with glucose and urea accounted for, the departure that the serum sodium × osmolality pair shows as an area (Figure S3e). Two indices. Two indices are also printed at the foot of the table-figure: haematocrit 3 × haemoglobin (white at zero, green below, red above) and neutrophils ÷ lymphocytes (white from 1 to 2, red above 2, green below 1). Both are defined below under "Widely available markers". They are examples. Other indices built on the same principles may be used, and are encouraged, to explore the dynamics of the organism under assessment.

A third index, in the same spirit: urine osmolality ÷ serum osmolality, measured in the same collection. It is read here with three marks, as an example: below 1, the urine is more dilute than plasma and free water is being released; around 1.5, equilibrium; above it, and clearly at 2 or above, the urine is held concentrated under vasopressin command. It needs only two tests that any routine laboratory runs, and it is not printed on the figures; it is used in the text (Results (ii)). Because the gap marks flux rather than state, the index is what places a collection with an open gap on the way into containment or on the way out (Framework, State-dependent temporality). M Margins between collections. Where a reading needs a margin between two collections, the margin comes from the within-person biological variation of each test. For this article, as an example, we chose the margin of the European Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (EFLM; 95% margin) [16, 18], which seemed to us the best available. The laboratories' analytical imprecision is not declared in the reports and was assumed to be half of the within-person variation. The margin is offered as a suggestion, where it is useful. The principle comes first: take a within-person margin from the best available source, and choose what serves the patient or the population at hand, at the lowest cost and the greatest feasibility.

The common scale: the reference interval printed on each report To place tests with different units on one drawing, each value is expressed as its position relative to the reference interval printed on the laboratory report of that collection: 0 at the lower limit and 100 at the upper limit. The interval is the one in force for that test, in that laboratory, at that time; method and reference interval were taken from the reports themselves, laboratory by laboratory and period by period (Supplementary methods). No population dataset beyond the report's own interval is used; that interval, itself derived from reference individuals, serves here only as a ruler. The reading is within the person, and the report's own interval keeps each value in the frame in which it was produced and read: the assay, the laboratory and the period. The scale is used for movement only, never as a verdict on level: a person is not "higher" or "lower" than anyone. It serves the readings within the person: the span a test covers across the series, the limits crossed, and the step between consecutive collections. It is an experiment in reading, a cartography: one possible ruler among others, not recommended over any other. For reverse T3 the ruler is approximate: the range of the most frequent laboratory.

The printed range itself is a convention, which is one more reason not to read it as a verdict. By the guideline that laboratories are meant to follow, a reference interval is the central 95% of values from at least 120 reference individuals [56]. One healthy person in twenty therefore falls outside it by construction. In practice a laboratory may instead transfer the manufacturer's interval and check it on about twenty people, or derive one from its own patient database with statistical filters [56, 57]. For ferritin, a systematic review traced the lower limits of the five most used assays back to their source studies [58]. Of all the studies it included, half had not explicitly screened for people at risk of iron deficiency, and half cited no guideline. The absence of explicit, wellestablished definitions and standards in the literature, which may owe something to the succession of commercial assays with differing parameters, makes population references complex to obtain, and it cannot be inferred that they apply to a specific individual. They are useful for detecting what falls far outside the normal: serious or structural disease, infection. This is one more reason why efforts such as this one, which grew out of the study of the high-processing-cost phenotypes, seem to us clinically important, especially for a medicine oriented to the individual, and one that takes into account what is emerging as ever more relevant, the network relations among the systems [6].

Reading rapid changes Every rapid change between two collections was put through four questions, in order. Does the collection itself explain it (hs-CRP, a dual result, a pre-analytical note, a change of laboratory)? Does textbook physiology explain a change of that size in that interval, with a named mechanism and a plausible magnitude? Does a known underlying condition of the person explain it? If none does, the change is recorded as a change of context until shown otherwise. The burden falls on an alternative explanation. Where measured osmolality is available, the first question also asks whether the particles in the tube are accounted for: glucose and urea enter the calculation, and the classical causes of an osmolal gap-ingested alcohols and other exogenous osmoles, ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, renal failure, and pseudohyponatraemia from high plasma lipids or protein-are considered, through the same collection where it carries the test and otherwise through the person's known condition, before the gap is read as anything else.

Background and fluctuation State varies on at least two levels. The background is the band in which a test predominantly sits over a period of the person's series; the fluctuation is the oscillation within that period. Both are read within the person. A background that stays fixed in one band for long periods, with little fluctuation, is read differently from one that moves between bands. The period is not defined in advance, and no window is declared before the reading. Its length depends on the context, not on a mechanism with a time course of its own: in this framework the mechanisms are adaptive and their form is flexible, and no disease process or structural damage sets the window beforehand. No one can say in advance when a context will change, or whether it will. That is why the figures, the table-figure and the colour gradients are useful here in place of a fixed rule. How much can be seen depends on how many collections there are, which is one reason why systematic longitudinal follow-up matters, above all in the cases that have resisted explanation.

Widely available markers Red-cell index Ht -3×Hb. Hematocrit minus three times hemoglobin is zero at a mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) of 33.3 g/dL. Above zero, the red cell carries more volume for the same hemoglobin; below zero, less. Because whole blood is re-equilibrated in the analyser's isotonic diluent before sizing, the index reads state in hours: the volume the cell itself holds for each gram of hemoglobin. Red-cell distribution width (RDW), which reflects differences in size among the cells present and therefore production over weeks to months, serves as its control: a change of the index without a change of RDW is a change of state, not of production. The mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), stable within the person, is its second control: with MCH unchanged, what moves the index is water; a falling MCH raises the index as well, which is why the iron of the same collection decides the reading. The red cell regulates its own volume, through K-Cl cotransport and through Piezo1 and the Gardos channel [166], and that regulation is what the index reports.

How the four routine instruments differ. Four instruments of the routine laboratory answer four different physical questions, and where they disagree the disagreement is read rather than discarded. The osmometer counts every dissolved particle per kilogram of water, whatever its charge: by freezing-point depression, the clinical standard, a zwitterion such as taurine or a polyol such as myo-inositol lowers the freezing point exactly as a sodium ion does, and the measurement carries no water-fraction effect. The ion-selective electrode answers only to the free ion it is selective for, so zwitterions and polyols generate no potential at it, and sodium bound electrostatically to polyanions is not counted. A direct electrode measures sodium activity in plasma water; an indirect, diluted electrode returns concentration per volume of plasma and carries the water-fraction effect, and the two configurations disagree mostly where plasma protein is abnormal, above all when it is low [167]; in this series albumin never fell below 3.9 g/dL (Results (vi)). The sodium method is the one printed on each report and is listed, laboratory by laboratory, with the reference intervals (Supplementary methods). The haematology analyser dilutes whole blood many-fold into a fixed isotonic diluent before sizing the cells; red-cell water equilibrates across aquaporin-1 in a fraction of a second, so a swelling caused merely by hypotonic plasma vanishes inside the diluent, whereas solutes that have entered the red cell, such as glucose, are not erased by the dilution, and sodium, which does not enter, leaves no trace. What persists in the diluent is the volume the cell itself holds, and this is what Ht 3×Hb reads.

What the strip reads, and the urinary counterpart of the gap. The reagent strip measures specific gravity through the change in acidity of a polyelectrolyte driven by the ionic concentration of the urine, read by a pH indicator [168]. Uncharged particles-zwitterions, polyols, urea, glucose-add nothing to ionic strength; the strip is not affected by glucose, whereas the refractometer is; the strip may be raised by albumin and reads low in alkaline urine [20, 168]. A strip specific gravity low for the urine osmolality measured in the same sample, with the pH read on the same strip taken into account, therefore identifies a load of uncharged particles in the urine; because urea is the main uncharged particle of normal urine, that reading needs urinary urea from the same sample. The urinary counterpart of the serum gap is measured urine osmolality minus [2 × (Na + K ) + ⁺ ⁺ urea/6], with glucose/18 added when glycosuria is present; its usual range is wide, because ammonium is not measured, so a low value is informative and a high value alone is not. It requires urinary sodium, potassium and urea in the same sample as the osmolality, which few collections of this series carry (Results (ii)). Read together, an osmometer that counts every particle, an electrode that counts only free charge, a cell counter that reequilibrates the sample before sizing it and a strip that reads ionic strength alone disagree in a specific, predictable pattern when the extracellular compartment carries uncharged, osmotically active particles; the pattern of their disagreement, not any single value, is what is read.

Iron

Iron was read with the collection, never with one index alone. What is read is the relation between transferrin saturation and ferritin: whether the two curves move together, as the textbook states, or part ways. The two modes follow from that relation and from the rest of the collection. The same holds in time: hepcidin acts on serum iron within hours [169, 170] and serum iron varies across the day, so hepcidin, interleukin-6 and hs-CRP are read only against the iron panel of the same collection or of the same order, and no statement on their coupling is made across orders.

What we do not do

There is no hypothesis testing and no control group. This is a case series that shows; it does not prove. We do not compute z-scores, tests or p values, and nothing is compared with another person or with a population as a verdict on level. The figures show; the text describes. We do not reduce the phenomenon to a number: where an area or an index is computed, it is for exploration and description, never as a test or a verdict. We do not state absolute truths about any person's biology; every figure supports the understanding of that one person. No organism is in the same state twice, so each collection is read as a state in its own right, not as a sample from a fixed distribution.

Laboratory methods, as printed in the individual reports. Almost all collections were processed by one laboratory, whose individual reports state serum and urine osmolality by freezing-point depression (cryoscopy), sodium by direct potentiometry on a dry-chemistry slide, ionized calcium by a dedicated ion-selective electrode, urine density by reflectance of the reagent strip, hormones by electrochemiluminescence and hs-CRP by latexenhanced immunoturbidimetry; free testosterone is calculated. The second laboratory, used by two individuals, states osmolality by freezing-point depression, sodium by potentiometry and urine density by refractometry or by an automated strip reader. The method was checked report by report: Supplementary Table S15 marks, for each collection, the osmolality method found in the individual report. The laboratories do not make the method visible where clinicians read: the cumulative report prints every osmolality under one name and states no method, and a value the laboratory calculated appears there beside measured ones. We flag this practice: a physician following a patient over years cannot tell from the cumulative report which numbers came from the osmometer, and the method should be printed wherever the result is.

The reading tools The reading tools are themselves a result of this series: they made visible, in routine laboratory data, the pattern that the clinical reading had already found.

The collection as a column. Each collection is read as a whole, as one column of the table-figure : all the tests of one morning side by side, with the days elapsed and the change of context marked (Figure 2; all people, and the second period of the one-year example, Figure S1). The areas between the curves. The areas between paired curves (Figures 3 and 4) make visible the decoupling from the expected relation: what the organism is doing to survive no longer follows the usual relation between the two markers. Something else is coordinating the system. The two clocks. Two measurements that any routine laboratory runs place a collection on the loop between containment and reopening: the urine index, urine osmolality ÷ serum osmolality drawn in the same collection, which follows vasopressin action within hours [19], and the serum osmolal gap, measured osmolality minus the osmolality calculated from sodium, glucose and urea (Methods). How to read collections and track HPCPh states and transitions: the "easy way" A notable application of the framework, still to be tested and validated both in research settings and for its clinical usefulness in routine care, is that HPCPh mechanism patterns can be tracked, directly or through proxies, on tests most people already have: the blood count, urinalysis and the usual blood chemistry. The steps below apply specifically to tracking HPCPh containment and reopening patterns. Population reference values can and should remain the reference when the aim is to evaluate disease, damage or abnormal function outside adaptive responses. Figure 2 shows three people read this way.

1. Gather results by date, side by side. Put each day's results in one column, with the dates in order from left to right. 2. Read one day at a time, as a whole. Look at all the tests from the same morning together, not one test at a time. 3. Compare with the person's own earlier results, not with the printed range. A value can change a lot and still stay inside the range printed on the report. For adaptive multisystem containment, what matters is how it compares with the person's earlier results. 4. Notice what moves together, and what parts ways. Some pairs of tests are expected to move together, or in opposite directions. When they do not, write it down.

5. Read the whole sequence. If something seems decoupled from the expected patterns, look at the load in the person's context and at their Islands, Deserts and Bridges profile. The story is in the series; one value, on its own, rarely tells it.

Reading routine results this way, even by hand, is free for clinical and research use, for non-commercial purposes. Especially in long-standing cases that do not meet a clear diagnostic category, or that respond poorly to standard protocols, it may open a path to explore and understand the person.

They serve here as a quantified reference, because the iron removed is known: about 600 to 800 mg per course. Ferritin did not follow it. After the first three courses it fell 2.5 to 12 times more than the iron removed would predict; after the fourth it did not fall; and between the first two courses, with no iron removed, it rose by about 600 ng/mL in nine months (Table S13). In this person, serum ferritin did not behave as a measure of iron stores. In this individual, who runs higher ferritin levels, the dissociation between ferritin and transferrin saturation did not differ in pattern from the others, and saturation followed context rather than iron stores; the indices were read in the same way as the rest of the series.

Where the inflammatory route was looked for, it was not there. Interleukin-6, the cytokine that drives hepcidin in inflammation [169], was measured six times in five of the twelve people and lay between 2.0 and 2.7 pg/mL, every value below the limit printed on the report (3.4 pg/mL); in four of the six the iron panel was drawn in the same collection, and in a fifth in the same order eight days earlier, with transferrin saturation from 27% to 73% and ferritin from 67 to 565 ng/mL. Hepcidin was measured twice, in the second individual, about three months apart: 20.8 ng/mL in the collection that completed an order whose iron panel, drawn eight days earlier, showed ferritin of 565 ng/mL and saturation of 73%, and 8.5 ng/mL in a collection that carried its own panel, with ferritin of 464 ng/mL and saturation of 51.5%. Interleukin-6 was 2.6 and 2.7 pg/mL and hs-CRP 0.88 and 1.09 mg/L in the two hepcidin collections. Hepcidin was high with saturation high, and lower as serum iron came down, with no inflammation in either column; because hepcidin acts on serum iron within hours, each value is read against the iron panel of its own order (Methods).

Read with these values, the restraint of iron in this series runs without the inflammatory pathway, and it moves our earlier formulation [29] away from it. Two non-inflammatory tiers, carried by the model from the bench into this series, account for what the columns show. M At the gut, apical absorption depends on an intestinal HIF-2α stimulus, the one that raises duodenal cytochrome b and DMT1 in iron deficiency [171]; when that stimulus is not called, the absorptive machinery stays low and absorbed iron is held in enterocyte heavy-chain ferritin, a gate that operates without hepatic hepcidin [172]. Inside the cell, iron is oxidized and mineralized within the ferritin cage; circulating ferritin, secreted largely by macrophages and relatively iron-poor, rises as a signal of retention rather than as a mirror of stores [173]; and ferritinophagy, through the cargo receptor NCOA4, delivers ferritin to the autophagosome for the release of its iron [88], the route by which, in the model, iron returns to transferrin when the mismatch resolves. M The iron-regulatory proteins act the other way round, repressing ferritin translation in a cell that wants iron [174]; containment, with saturation falling while ferritin holds, is the complementary configuration. The model proposes the gut gate as the brake of prolonged containment and the ferritin cage, with its NCOA4 release, as the buffer of transitions: the body stops letting iron in before it starts holding the iron it has. Step by step.

What follows is an illustration rather than a result in its own right: an exercise showing how different the pattern and the dynamics are from the textbook, and how a rule of three, which any clinician can do at the bench with the two tests most often requested, TSH and free T4, can indicate movement where no other method is available, always read together with the other parameters. Within the person, over 95 steps between consecutive collections from 11 people, TSH moved on average far less per unit of free T4 than the textbook log-linear relation predicts (0.27 against 1.73 per ng/dL). In 19 of the 95 steps TSH moves outside what free T4 called for, and in 13 of these 19 it moves the opposite way. The signed mean deviation of TSH is +0.036 (ln) and the mean absolute deviation 0.411, eleven times larger: the movement exists and cancels out when pooled, because it goes both ways. The steps are long (median 163 days), and the margin depends on the assumed analytical error. None of the tools shown here is definitive, and none is meant to be precise; they indicate movement Reverse T3. It appears in 11 of the 12 people, in 1 to 7 collections each, on dates with free T4 and, in all but one collection, TSH. Discussion

The present series suggests that a specific class of burnout-and depression-like functional collapse states is more accurately read as a state-dependent reconfiguration of distributed physiological regulation than as dysfunction of a single pathway. Read collection by collection and against each person's own series, ten regulatory mechanisms shifted together, parted from the relations they obey in the steady state, and, in the three examples of reversal, returned toward them when the context changed. Within this architecture, collapse does not emerge from the failure of individual systems. It emerges from a coordinated reduction of throughput, a containment in which growth, maintenance and repair are restricted in a pattern consistent with priority for the central nervous system. Containment, not failure As argued in the Introduction, the configuration of the collection favours the containment reading over that of an inadequate response [33]: in this series, albumin is preserved while the growth axis and iron transport are restricted. This retreat was described fifty years ago as conservation-withdrawal, a regulatory process that economizes when exhaustion threatens [60]; the present series adds its laboratory face and its reversal with context. The regime is cold: in the collections that carry this configuration, interleukin-6 was normal wherever it was measured and hs-CRP stayed at non-inflammatory levels, and lactate was not raised in any collection with an open osmolal gap in which it was drawn, so that, with albumin preserved, nothing in the panel describes inflammation, lysis or catabolism [121]. Albumin is spared, in this reading, because it is the main non-volatile weak acid of plasma and the guarantor of oncotic pressure beneath the endothelial glycocalyx (Framework (vi)) [86, 117]. M The measure of health in this reading is not a value inside the printed range, but flexibility: the capacity to move between states and to return. This framework is consistent with, but extends beyond, established models of stress adaptation [2] and allostasis, in which stability is achieved through change [3, 5]. Unlike single-axis models, it explicitly incorporates:  multi-axis coupling  state-dependent temporality, rather than a fixed temporal hierarchy  state-dependent reconfiguration  within-person reading, with the person's own series as the primary standard of comparison

The framework also departs from existing accounts of load. Neither type of allostatic overload described by McEwen and Wingfield [3] fully captures what is observed here. Type 1 arises when energy demand exceeds supply; here energy is not simply lacking-the organism spends energy to reduce mitochondrial throughput, shut systems down and protect the brain. Type 2 arises when energy intake is sufficient or even excessive, in the presence of social conflict or other social dysfunction, and does not trigger an escape response; here the load is not simply conflict, but the amount and nature of the information that reaches the person. Escape points matter for that reason: when the person can escape, input falls.

The comparison with ischaemia makes the difference concrete. In ischaemia, arterial oxygen and glucose collapse, and the brain recruits its most archaic silencing brakes-adenosine, the opening of ATP-sensitive potassium channels, channel and spike arrest [178, 179]-but in the mammalian brain, with the source at zero, they only buy minutes. Here, arterial substrate and oxygen are intact, and the threat comes from the rate of ionic and computational demand placed on the membrane pump (Framework, State transitions). The same button is pressed, with opposite yields: in anoxia, lowering firing postpones the collapse; under processing overload, it resolves it. M The cheapest brake is also the most physical: the compatible organic osmolytes held by the contained cell raise the voltage cost of firing (Framework (ii)). M Containment is a strategy, not an agony.

The load here is of a third kind: informational. It is processing load, determined by capture bandwidth: by what each person's antennae (the features of their architecture that amplify what is captured; see the clinical course below) take in from a given environment, and by the mismatch between that input and what their architecture can process at that moment. It is not a generic excess of information. Predictive accounts of stress introduce another relevant concept: uncertainty itself carries a cost for the brain [61]. Uncertainty, however, is inherent to human life, and its cost varies across individuals. It does not specifically describe the amount, intensity or nature of incoming information, nor the cost of processing it; those are the dimensions that drive the states described here.

We call the coordinated, feed-forward shift that this load recruits the Displacement of Regulatory Purpose: the Third Allostasis. Hunger has a place and the social environment has a time; information has neither. Type 1 answers a scarcity of energy, and Type 2 a sufficient or excessive intake in the presence of social conflict [3]; the third answers a demand for processing that approaches the ceiling of the membrane pump while the energy source is intact, and it runs cold. M The physiology recruited is the usual one; what is displaced is its purpose, from external performance toward keeping the command centre viable. The compatible organic osmolytes, the vasopressin command that holds them in place and the hysteresis with which they are released and recollected (Framework (ii) and State-dependent temporality) are the mechanism that makes the ten mechanisms move together. M The thyroid illustrates the distinction. Thyroid allostasis describes adaptive changes in thyrotropic regulation under strain [38]; in this series, thyroid regulation tracks the management of processing bandwidth and can respond rapidly once input is identified and brought into a range the person can sustain. The reason the thyroid belongs in this picture is quantitative: active T3 sets the transcription of the membrane Na /K pump [112, 180], ⁺ ⁺ so restraining thyroid drive lowers the standing cost of the very process the state is trying to afford (Framework (iv)). M The load comes from capture bandwidth. Through high-gain domains (Islands), the same environment delivers more input to this phenotype than to others [25]. The bandwidth is a gain: it supports pattern detection, multimodal convergence and, in many individuals, high ability [25]. The cost lies in control. Keeping a wide-band system below its limit demands fine gating and background suppression [25], and the energy is spent on that containment, keeping the system from failing.

A second cost: translation The same architecture has a second side. In the matrix hypothesis [27], a less dense extracellular matrix, including the perineuronal nets that hold mature circuits in place, favours high-gain capture in some domains and limits the efficient stabilization of rapid, automatized or serial execution in others [25]. The result is not a lesion but an uneven landscape: peaks of capture (Islands) beside domains where execution is not automatized (Deserts). Nothing is broken; the function is routed elsewhere. Regions that are not the primary substrate of a function are recruited to carry it, as in the alternative routes that compensated readers use [62], and what would ordinarily run automatically runs under supervision [25]. This is a second cost, independent of the first. It adds to the energy spent containing input on the Island side, and its size depends on the final architecture: which domains are Islands, which are Deserts, and how far apart they sit [25]. When the budget falls, as with fatigue, the Bridge is what stops working. At the far end of this range, in high capture-low translation configurations [25], the cost of translation may exceed what bridging can supply. Clinically, the first sign is a rise in executive difficulty. If demand keeps rising, including well-intended additions such as multiple concurrent therapies or pressure to keep pace with academic content, load and masking increase and executive difficulty deepens. What we have observed is that, beyond this point, the capacity to retain new information falls, as the system sheds whatever it can. In school, academic and occupational settings this can close into a vicious circle: the less is retained, the more demand is added. Children are the most exposed, since neither they nor their families can simply step out of the setting.

Masking

Masking deserves a place of its own. It is rarely counted as load, yet it was one of our biggest surprises, and one of the most valuable clinically. Our clinical experience has consistently shown that masking seems to exact a biological price far higher than externalizing behaviour, and for some people the laboratory shows it to be the heaviest load of all. This alone could change many trajectories that stay hidden, literally masked, behind what seems more socially acceptable. The cost is silent, and it can be life itself: literally, or through extreme loss of function in people in whom it was not expected. These trajectories are often read as refractory depression, as a personality disorder or as somatic complaints, or never receive attention for what actually takes away their right to health and to life itself. Masking should be explored in dedicated studies, and soon.

The clinical course: reactive overload, containment and reopening The phases below describe how the axes behave at the two levels defined in the Framework, background and fluctuation, as we have observed so far.

Reactive overload. In our experience, short-lived excessive allostatic load is more reversible, compatible with a stage in which set points have not yet shifted pervasively. Mechanisms remain more flexible and return to the person's own levels. In some individuals, while the need for allostatic regulation is not yet acute, the system first settles into a more active position, closer to active coping: cortisol remains active and reactive, with morning serum cortisol normal to high. However, even when morning serum cortisol is preserved under overload, we frequently observe the salivary cortisol curve beginning to lose its typical dynamics early, sometimes inverting, together with insomnia, cognitive looping and an underlying anxiety-like state. Yet even in this more agitated state, patients usually report a subjective sense of exhaustion related to the load, which can be intense and accompanied by varying degrees of functional decline, often involving loss of executive function. In the cases we have followed, DHEA-S typically falls first, sometimes alone, while cortisol remains preserved, a pattern compatible with a shift of steroidogenesis toward cortisol at the 17,20-lyase step [63]. This initial, shorter phase is also marked by greater flexibility: mechanisms still alternate, switching on and off with the external context and with whether the person's biological support, such as nutritional status, is sufficient. The water axis emerged as the fastest containment mechanism: patients report compatible symptoms-fog, headache, a subtle yet extreme fatigue-that at first revert almost immediately once the overload ceases, but persist for progressively longer windows as the context remains above the person's processing threshold. Ferritin usually rises in this phase; later, in many individuals but not all, it falls. This phase does not occur in every individual with an HPCPh profile: when load strikes far above processing capacity, the system drops directly into containment, and the risk is high from the outset.

Cognitive symptoms go beyond executive function. Fog is common and closely tied to the water-handling mechanisms. It feels stunned and anaesthetized, somewhat dissociative, consistent with a function of shielding the person from excess input. It is usually transient, but in more chronic states it can become pervasive. In heavier and more pervasive overload, the capacity to retain new information can fall markedly. This, too, is reversible. Both appear to be part of the system's contingency mechanisms, potentially raising the threshold for input and sparing the central nervous system from lasting damage. Iron restriction is a candidate worth assessing here. It may contribute to the marked drop in cognitive capacity that we often see in people with an HPCPh profile under heavy overload. Our hypothesis is that this occurs through mitochondria deprived of iron [64].

Containment. When overload becomes sustained, or the pressure to process input remains too close to the limits of the person's CNS architecture and current state without sufficient breaks, preserving the viability of the central nervous system becomes the urgent target. All other systems are recruited into a coordinated response, by whatever means are possible or necessary, since no life is possible apart from its command centre. What we observe suggests that, as the risk approaches irreversible damage, the cost of projecting and trying new scenarios to escape the overload becomes unbearable, and/or successive experiences bring the predicted chance of success of any active attempt close to zero. The response then becomes a sequence of shutdowns and cost-lowering mechanisms that places the body in a depression-like state of biological apathy. Here, cutting energy supply and less necessary production is adaptive: under those circumstances, it appears to emerge as the best strategy available to the organism for survival.

Crossing this line works as a gate: mechanisms that fluctuated move into a more pervasive background. One marker is the point at which cortisol stops holding and falls, but it is neither always the first switch to be turned off nor the only one. How long cortisol holds varies: in one individual under sustained endurance training, it remained preserved while the growth axis fell. The salivary curves frequently register the change first, with morning serum cortisol sinking only later, into what we called a hibernation-like state [28, 65]. DHEA-S, typically already low, can fall even further.

The laboratory picture points in the same direction across systems. Alongside functional iron blockade (FIB), the water axis and the HPA axis also frequently enter containment mode, acting as brakes; the growth axis falls as well, as reflected in IGF-1. The thyroid shows a consistent downward command: TSH varies independently of free T4 [38], and peripheral T4 metabolism shifts toward reverse T3 whenever there is T4 available for conversion and the load threatens the system. In some individuals, insulin secretion is economized: fasting insulin stays low relative to glucose, at times at very low levels. Insulin moves with volume rather than with glucose: vasopressin acts on the beta cell through V1b receptors, permissively to glucose and already at ordinary fasting glycaemia [123, 124], and swelling opens the beta cell's volume-regulated anion channel, the route glucose itself uses [125, 126], so that the swings follow the phase of the water axis with glucose unchanged (Framework (vii)). M Protein synthesis is economized as well: albumin is spared as a rule, while effort and substrate shift toward what is essential. White blood cells show paradoxical responses, described in Results (iii).

At the centre of the movement, both in the fluctuations and in the background being established, water and ion regulation become less flexible when the mismatch is sustained without sufficient escape points-breaks from high or misaligned input-in a pattern compatible with vasopressin taking over control from the reninangiotensin-aldosterone system. The water axis then loses fluctuation amplitude, most markedly in the later, hibernation-like state. With vasopressin in command, the cell holds a cushioned configuration of compatible organic osmolytes, the astrocyte included, while plasma osmolality is given by sodium as usual: the osmolal gap is closed, and what has changed is inside the cell and in the urine (Framework (ii)). M This is why stable containment can look unremarkable on a panel (Results (ii)). Concurrently, withdrawal of iron from the mitochondrial scene intensifies, holding the organism in a more restricted scenario in which the body spends energy to cut off energy: immersed in an inescapable external overload beyond its control, mitochondria are shut down as the available means of containing a thermodynamic condition that threatens the system. Here, iron is blocked without overt inflammation [29], apparently at every step, not only at absorption. Ferritin, which earlier marked iron being withdrawn from circulation and stored until the environment allowed the brake to be released, often begins to fall because iron is no longer allowed in; every iron marker can fall, as far as anaemia.

Reopening. First, whatever triggers and sustains containment must be removed, or at least scaled down or rearranged, until the demand for input and processing becomes one the person can sustain. Only then can the internal environment return to a more flexible, less restricted pattern. The "biological sensor" that triggers and shuts down containment mechanisms behaves as if it lies within each compartment, between compartments, and in the load and organization of transmission. This is what makes it chemically and biophysically possible to release the defences and climb back from a frozen defensive state toward a more permissive exchange between the person and the environment.

That exchange-the way the person, and every system the person is made of, relates to what comes from outside, processes and rearranges it, and initiates, or does not initiate, an executive output in return-is set largely by the body's reading of safety, external and internal. In HPCPh, safety depends on the fit between two things. One is the person's architecture: where and how their Islands, Deserts and Bridges are configured. The other is the amount, intensity and pitch of information in the context: the nature of everything that strikes from outside and, to some degree, from inside-interoceptive reading, projection of scenarios and adjustment of prediction-with the different sources of information continuously triggering and modulating one another. The gain of function, the type of processing-parallel rather than linear, for example-and the other characteristics that build the topographic composition of Islands, Deserts and Bridges and shape the emerging phenotype are what we have called the "antennae": they amplify the amount of information captured and/or processed at once. They emerge from each person's architecture [25], and it is not up to the person to turn them off.

In practice, reaching more stable and functional states, and/or finding the way toward reopening, begins with mapping the architecture. We identify what the antennae capture and where the points of overload lie. We then build strategies, which we call Bridges. Some are external, built together with the patient. Others are internal, already the patient's own, and we strengthen, stimulate, adapt or optimize them. Their purpose is to offset Deserts, manage context and add resources at an energy cost the person can afford at that moment. A sufficient biological substrate for reconstruction is essential-for example, vitamin D, folate, magnesium, B vitamins, and enough protein and fat in the diet. Circadian signals such as sunlight and/or programmed light that simulates sunrise can be particularly effective. Exercise is always adjusted to the phase and phenotype. It can be five minutes of stretching or a 40-minute run; at times it is simply getting out of bed. In our experience, what opens the gate to the way back is fitting each step to the state, far more than any universal prescription.

Each path is therefore unique, as was the path that led the person there. This is close to what precision medicine and precision psychiatry mean. In many cases, with management adjusted to the person, cortisol recovers quickly. IGF-1 rises within weeks, as a marker of reconstruction. Iron is usually fast once the context has truly become safer. It also depends on cellular memory, however: after a long, sustained collapse, adjustments across systems on the way back can be difficult. Reopening is not free. Some transitions are hard. In one person, a set of parameters pointed to salt and water balance returning to control by the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, accompanied by marked symptoms that closely resembled the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion. The transition is hard for a mechanical reason: the two brakes do not hand over in step. As vasopressin command withdraws, aquaporin-2 is internalized and the water that was being held leaves [11] (Framework (ii)); natriuretic peptides disarm the axis in the hypothalamus and in the collecting duct [146, 149] (Framework (ix)); and angiotensin II, the most potent dipsogen known, drives thirst before the other arm has taken over [181]. M One brake releases before the other catches: this is the clinical face of the exit leg, the part of the loop in which the osmolal gap opens widest (Results (ii)). After a long containment, releasing one brake taxes the other systems until their set points adjust. Fatigue and oscillating body temperature can accompany a recovery that the collection already shows to be under way.

Symptoms come in cycles, in step with laboratory transitions: dysautonomia, insomnia, muscle pain and headache, among others. They can look like a recurrence, but often they are a different set. Their carriers are the Coupling Mechanisms-the autonomic [22] [23] [24] and vestibular systems, which sense the medium, act before the load arrives and carry a central change into distant organs-which is why one regulatory state is felt in several places at once. From outside, the course looks like a staircase, with an occasional step back: the context pushes further than the moment can hold, or a release goes too far and crosses the line of safety, and the organism pulls the brakes once more. The first emergence from a long shutdown is the hardest. When input and processing are kept at levels suited to the person's current state, the organism's "trust" in releasing the brakes seems to grow. The laboratory mirrors this: systems do not open all at once when the context allows, which surprised us at first. When things go well outside, the permission to live and to create comes back-with support, escape points and, above all, the person's ownership of their own "instruction manual", together with family education and adaptation of routines by employers, schools and universities to the person's profile of capture and processing. The path has a cost, but it is possible, and in our experience the cost tracks the duration of containment: brief and light when collapse was short and fast, longer and more laborious when it lasted.

Reading the network in routine examinations Network physiology holds that system-level behaviour arises from interactions among partially coupled subsystems rather than from isolated mechanisms [6]. The framework aligns with this view, in which coupling between organ systems, measured through continuous signals, reorganizes as physiological state changes [6]. We propose that this reorganization can also be read, at lower temporal resolution, in routine laboratory collections. A single collection records the value of each marker, not the coupling between systems; coupling becomes visible across successive collections of the same person. The area between the curves is a way of reading that network from routine examinations. Each textbook pair states the direction in which two markers are expected to move (by the textbook, in the sense defined in the Framework); drawn on one scale, the two curves show how far they part within the person, and the table-figure shows the whole collection at once. In this series the pairs part in a consistent way: TSH does not go where free T4 asks it to go, but where the centre asks it to go; the white-cell series moves against cortisol for long stretches; ferritin and saturation part with inflammation quiet. Sodium and measured osmolality part as well, and the departure becomes visible only because four routine instruments answer different physical questions (Methods): an osmometer that counts every particle, an electrode that answers only to the free ion it is selective for, a cell counter that re-equilibrates the sample before sizing it, and a strip that reads ionic strength alone [168]. They disagree in a specific, predictable pattern when the extracellular compartment is carrying uncharged, osmotically active particles. M Because the cell releases these particles within minutes and recollects them over days, through sodium-coupled transporters, the disagreement opens across a transition and closes when the state settles (Framework, State-dependent temporality). M The method does not look for the most probable explanation; it looks for what does not fit, and treats it as signal. Morning serum cortisol, the marker most often ordered, tells the least of this story: it sinks only when the state has gone far down, while the salivary curve changes first. The curve tells where the person is; the serum value, where the person has already been.

Neuroplastic signalling may follow the same state-dependent course: in earlier work in this line, peripheral brainderived neurotrophic factor was raised early after trauma and fell with time and with recovery [66, 67], a dynamic not measured in the present series.

What changes in practice Three points follow for practice. First, the state can be read with what any clinic already orders: the index Ht -3×Hb, read with red-cell distribution width and urine specific gravity (see Methods), is a state signal that needs no osmometer. Where serum osmolality is measured, the same logic puts a second state signal within reach: four numbers already on a routine chemistry report and one subtraction give the osmolal gap, and a urine osmolality drawn the same morning says which leg of the loop the collection belongs to (Methods; Results (ii)). The question put to the test also changes: asking what is missing leads to the reference range and to "everything is normal"; asking how much it costs to function this way leads to the column. Second, the person can serve as their own control. In one individual, a year of standard care, including a course of electroconvulsive therapy, preceded the change of approach without response; the reversal followed the change of approach, with no new pharmacological treatment introduced during the transition (see Results).

Third, the basics come first. Vitamin D was below 30 ng/mL at some point in every person of this series, and below 20 ng/mL, the lower limit printed on the report for the healthy population, in eight of the twelve; it is inexpensive to measure and to correct. We have proposed elsewhere that, in these phenotypes, low 25-OHvitamin D that persists despite supplementation may reflect higher metabolic consumption [29], a high-turnover state [27]. In our clinical practice, magnesium, calcium and parathyroid hormone also fluctuate around changes of state (see Results (x)), and lower magnesium levels appear to matter for how the state settles; keeping vitamin D and magnesium sufficient is part of the ground on which any reopening is built. In children followed outside this series, parathyroid hormone can be very low during these states. These fluctuations were not the focus of this work and need dedicated study. Magnesium is where the mechanism is clearest: as Mg-ATP² it is ⁻ the obligatory substrate of the membrane pump [155], and it is the voltage-dependent plug of the NMDA receptor [156], which is why keeping it sufficient is the cheapest point of intervention in the whole chain (Framework (x)). M The general form of the point matters more than any single measure: an intervention that answers a budget problem by ordering the system to spend more works against the state it is meant to treat.

The literature points the same way. Lower 25-OH-vitamin D is consistently reported in autism and in attentiondeficit/hyperactivity disorder [68, 69], in children and in young adults [70]. Given the same dose, in the same city and season, autistic children raised 25-OH-vitamin D less than a comparison group [71]; genetic liability to autism was associated with lower vitamin D, and not the reverse [72]; and in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, low 25-OH-vitamin D was not accompanied by the rise in parathyroid hormone seen in controls [73]. Magnesium is a cofactor of the enzymes that metabolize vitamin D [74], and urinary magnesium loss is higher in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and autism [75]. To our knowledge, no study has measured vitamin D turnover in these phenotypes, and none has examined vitamin D in high-ability profiles. What this series adds is the course within the person (see Results (x)): physiology and clinical course point the same way.

Why cross-sectional studies miss it A cross-sectional sample pools people in containment with people in reopening. Saturation falling in one and rising in another cancels out in the mean, and TSH moving in whichever direction the centre calls for becomes noise. The step-by-step TSH exercise (see Results (iv)) illustrates this cancellation: the signed mean deviation stays close to zero while the mean absolute deviation is eleven times larger. Fifty years after Engel called for a medical model that would include the person [76], the person still lies outside the unit of analysis; this series proposes one, the collection read against the person's own history.

The textbook relations between the pairs, TSH and free T4 or ferritin and transferrin saturation, rest, as far as we could trace them, on three kinds of data. One is physiological experiments and interventions, in which one member of the pair is changed and the other follows. Another is cross-sectional samples, in which each person contributes one point and the cloud of points has a slope. The third is treated patients followed while treatment is adjusted. Healthy people have been followed over time, but each analyte's variation was described on its own, over weeks to a year [13, 14]; the biological-variation literature compiles such studies analyte by analyte [16, 18]. For the two members of a pair drawn together within healthy people over years, the closest we found is a cohort with ferritin and transferrin saturation measured twice, twelve years apart [77]. A slope across people need not be the slope within any one of them. For TSH and T4, population set-point data suggest a more complex inverse relation than physiological studies do [78]; in other fields, group-level structure has been argued not to generalize to individuals [79]. This is why the figures show each pair against the textbook relation and not against a within-person reference: we found none. Such a reference could be built from routine laboratory databases, in people with several collections in which both tests were drawn together.

Beyond the clinic Understanding these mechanisms is essential to develop strategies aligned with each person's profile, with benefit not only to the individual but also to families, schools, workplaces and institutions, which often lack a physiological frame for these situations. A biological counterpart, readable in multisystem markers, can support decisions for the individual at a time when discussions rely mostly on diagnostic categories that offer no mechanistic basis for them.

For schools, universities and workplaces, particularly in full-day, high-demand settings, this reading may point to conditions that can be designed in advance rather than corrected afterwards: the load of input, the availability of escape points, and the fit between what is demanded and each person's architecture, including profiles that combine high ability with neurodivergence. For health systems, the markers come from low-cost tests that most people already have, and trajectories that now take years of consultations, investigations and treatments without response might be recognized earlier. None of this was measured here: a series of 12 cases cannot estimate effects on learning, on staying in school or at work, or on cost. It indicates where to look, and what a prospective study in educational and occupational settings would need to follow.

Testable predictions What to investigate follows from what is shown. The predictions below are clinical and within the person. They do not assume that people respond alike: direction and magnitude differ from one person to the next, and what is shared is the dynamics. Each prediction states what a person's own series should show if that part of the framework is correct. The predictions are independent, as expected of a proposal in development: one can fail without invalidating the others.

1. Masking has a biological cost. In a person whose load comes largely from masking, the series should move toward containment even while outward behaviour and function appear preserved, and should move back when masking can be reduced.

2. Background and fluctuation. In prolonged containment, the fluctuation of each axis should lose amplitude while the background persists. A quiet series is not necessarily stability; it can itself be a state. Reopening should appear as fluctuation regaining amplitude, the background changing level, or both.

3. Shape before level. On the way into containment, the shape of the salivary cortisol curve over the day should, in most cases, change before morning serum cortisol does; on the way back, the order need not hold. A morning serum value within the reference range should not rule the state out.

4. Pairs drift from the textbook and approach it again. Within the person, textbook pairs should move away from their expected physiological relation as containment sets in. In the later phases of reopening, as the system approaches equilibrium, they should move closer to that relation again, without a common direction or magnitude across people.

5. The gap belongs to movement. Within the person, a serum osmolal gap of 10 mOsm/kg or more should appear in collections taken during transitions and close during stable containment, and the urine-toplasma osmolality ratio measured in the same collection should separate a collection taken on the way out from one taken on the way in. Where copeptin is drawn in the same collection, it should follow the calculated osmolality rather than the measured one.

6. The urinary sibling. One to two days after a plasma peak, the same event should appear in the urine as a urinary osmolal gap and as a low strip specific gravity beside a high measured urinary osmolality. Testing it requires only that urinary sodium, potassium and urea be ordered in the same sample as urinary osmolality; a quantitative urinary amino acid profile from that sample would name taurine directly.

7. The other side of the barrier. Where brain proton spectroscopy at high field with spectral editing is available, the myo-inositol and taurine signals should be higher in stable containment than after reopening, and should fall in the window in which the plasma osmolal gap opens.

8. Containment, not failure. In containment, albumin should remain preserved even when other syntheses are economized. Generalized synthetic failure would be expected to lower albumin as well. 9. Insulin moves with volume, not with glucose. In a person followed through a transition, fasting insulin should swing severalfold with fasting glucose and glycated haemoglobin unchanged, following the phase of the loop read in the urine index rather than glucose; C-peptide drawn with insulin in one contained and one exiting collection should separate beta-cell firing from hepatic clearance.

10. Fibrin turnover tracks the wash, not the clot. Where D-dimer is raised outside a thrombotic context, it should follow the osmolal gap across collections of the same person rather than any coagulation parameter.

11. Context turns the axes together. When input is brought into a range matched to the person's architecture, axes that share no known pathway should turn within the same window, in step with symptoms. When context again exceeds what the person can sustain at that moment, they should step back together, each following its own timescale: the staircase seen from outside.

12. The length of the state sets the way back. The longer and more fixed the background in containment, the slower the return and the greater the propensity for marked transitional symptoms, depending on which axes predominate in that person. A later emergence should usually be easier than the first.

13. Cognition returns when load falls. When load is reduced and adequate biological substrate is available, executive dysfunction and other cognitive symptoms-including fog and impaired retention of new information-should improve within the person, without requiring an additional intervention directed specifically at cognition. 14. Treatment that ignores the load. Treatments that usually work in other conditions should succeed less often here, or may even worsen the state, when they do not take information-processing load into account, particularly when the treatment itself adds to that load.

15. Cross-sectional designs will miss these dynamics. When people in different states and moving in opposite directions are pooled, the movements cancel in the mean.

Each prediction can be tested independently. Where a given prediction is wrong, the corresponding movement should not appear consistently within the person's series-for example, textbook pairs should remain coupled, fluctuation should not change systematically with state, or no turn should occur around a change in context.

Children, in whom the growth axis operates within developmental windows that do not recur [80], are a particularly important population in which to test these predictions first. This is a case series, not a trial: it shows a recurrent pattern, highly responsive to context, so that it can be studied, and soon.

Limitations

This is a retrospective, undesigned case series from office practice. The timing of sampling was set by clinical care, not by protocol: tests were ordered when they seemed to make a difference, and what was not ordered does not exist. There is no control group and there are no statistical tests, by choice: the series describes, and power calculations do not apply to a description. The ruler is the person themselves, which requires long series; people with few sampling occasions contribute less. Several participants were receiving magnesium, vitamin D and B12 supplementation by clinical decision, and some were on medication, so the variability of these axes is not read as demand. Conclusions and Outlook

Twelve people, one lens. Read collection by collection and against their own history, they show a pattern that reading each system in isolation does not see: variation beyond what textbook physiology expects, systems moving together and against the textbook relations, temporality that depends on state rather than on the clock, and reversal when the context changes, over days, months or a year. This is a case series that shows rather than proves, with widely available routine tests as its backbone, and it shifts the unit of analysis from the static marker to the dynamic physiological state. We call the state it describes the Third Allostasis: a Displacement of Regulatory Purpose toward keeping the command centre viable. What this version adds is the layer beneaththe ions the cell may not move, the organic osmolytes it moves instead, and the compartments they cross-so that the water axis can be read on a routine panel with four numbers and one subtraction. High processing cost exists, it leaves a mark on the body, and the mark reverses.

The outlook is prospective and within the person. The predictions above can be tested in high-frequency longitudinal series. Children, left out of this series to protect identity, are where this matters most and where it should be studied first.

Its use need not stop at the clinic: schools, universities and workplaces are where much of the load is set.

The figures are offered as a way of looking: a means of relearning how to read routine results within the person, which clinicians and researchers can use and extend, and which claims nothing beyond what is drawn.
