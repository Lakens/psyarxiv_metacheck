Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Language, Cognition, and the Way We Thinkhinges on it being both pervasive in and important for cognition, with the two often being linked. As the argument goes: if inner speech is ubiquitous, it must be important, and if inner speech is important, everybody must have it. However, the observation that some people experience little to no inner speech makes this foundational notion less solid. Individual differences in inner speech have in recent years come into focus both in the public sphere and in the inner speech research field. While viral posts and clips are generally framed in a sensationalist manner rather than in the spirit of scientific inquiry, the specific perspectives and questions that come up in such public discussions are highly relevant for researchers as well. Scientists and philosophers have studied differences in how people experience "thinking" for a long time (see e.g., Aveling, 1927; Bartlett, 1927; Pear, 1927; Roth, 1965), and a subsection of the inner speech research field is already occupied with describing how the characteristics of inner speech within individuals vary by task demands (Fernyhough, 2004; Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011; Theodorakis et al., 2000; Yao, 2025). However, trait-level differences in the experience and use of inner speech have received less attention. The finding that some people experience little or even no inner speech deserves attention since, if true, it has serious consequences for theories about the (facilitative and even necessary) role of inner speech in human cognition generally. In this commentary, I will first outline the current evidence for the existence of people with anendophasia, as we have coined the phenomenon in question, and then contextualise anendophasia with regard to some of the functions of inner speech presented in Kompa's Language, Cognition, and the Way We Think. I will argue that the existence of anendophasia is a serious challenge for theories claiming that consciously experienced inner speech is necessary for metacognition, self-reflection, self-knowledge, and reflexive, higher order, conscious thinking. I will then discuss four possible ways of dealing with this challenge.

When one does empirical work on inner speech (as we have done in e.g., Nedergaard, Christensen, et al., 2023; Nedergaard et al., 2021; Nedergaard, Skewes, et al., 2023), it gradually becomes clear that a minority of participants do not find the concept of "talking to themselves in their heads" at all intuitive. They often even insist that they do not do it. In part inspired by such claims, my colleague Gary Lupyan and I decided to test whether the reported absence of inner speech has any behavioural consequences (Nedergaard & Lupyan, 2024). We recruited one group of people who scored very low on the Verbal Factor of the Internal Representations Questionnaire (Roebuck & Lupyan, 2020) -which targets propensity to use inner speech in everyday life -and a control group of people with high scores on the same factor (high scores being typical of the general population).

[div-01]

We referred to the people in the former group as "people with anendophasia", anendophasia being defined as the experienced lack of inner speech in everyday life. We then tested these two groups on four behavioural tasks where we hypothesised that differences in inner speech would result in differences in behaviour. Participants with anendophasia turned out to be worse at verbal working memory and covert rhyme judgments -two tasks requiring a high degree of phonological precision represented in inner speech -but did not perform differently when switching quickly between simple arithmetic tasks or making perceptual discrimination judgments. Interestingly, when people with anendophasia reported talking out loud to solve the rhyming or verbal working memory task, their performance was indistinguishable from that of the participants reporting high levels of inner speech. This suggested that it really was the "pronouncing" of the wordseither covertly or overtly -that made the difference. Our study was the first to attempt targeted recruitment of people experiencing little to no inner speech and the first to examine whether such subjective reports of atypical inner speech phenomenology have behavioural correlates. Overall, our study indicated that people who report experiencing less inner speech do behave differently in theoretically sensible ways.

Given the potential theoretical consequences of the existence of people with anendophasia, we might also want to consider other sources of evidence than one behavioural study. Such sources do exist, although some of them provide relatively indirect evidence: first, people with aphasia -language impairment resulting from neurological lesion -can lose and regain their inner speech, both in parallel with and separately from their outer speech (Fama et al., 2019; Fama & Turkeltaub, 2020; Langland-Hassan et al., 2015; Sierpowska et al., 2020). This can be measured using covert rhyme judgments, covert syllable counting, and so on, but notably is also reflected in subjective reports about everyday use of inner speech (Alexander et al., 2024; Tichborne et al., 2025). While this is not evidence that people with "congenital" anendophasia exist, it is evidence that individuals can tell the difference between having and not having inner speech. This is important because one of the common arguments against taking reports of anendophasia seriously (and reports of other experiential differences such as aphantasia) is that the reports reflect a lack of metacognitive insight or misinterpretations of terms rather than real differences in phenomenology (Michel et al., 2025; Schwitzgebel, 2011). Second, Descriptive Experience Sampling (DES; Heavey & Hurlburt, 2008; Hurlburt & Akhter, 2006; Hurlburt & Heavey, 2006), widely considered the gold standard for investigating phenomenological experience, has shown that some people never have experience samples containing inner speech (Heavey & Hurlburt, 2008; Hurlburt & Bass-Krueger, 2025).

Since DES is sampling-based, this is of course no guarantee that such people never could innerly speak, but the DES findings are nonetheless striking. Russell Hurlburt, the inventor of DES, has stated that 'I am confident, based on my DES fidelity-aspiring investigations of about 500 persons, that many people never, or almost never, innerly speak in everyday situations' (Hurlburt, 2026, p. 2). Third, a few recent studies (Gao et al., 2026; New et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026) have attempted to directly elicit inner speech in large samples of participants using different kinds of prompting and have found that between approx. 1% and 5% of participants report experiencing no inner speech even when explicitly asked to produce it.

Of course, we do not know the extent of the overlap between the individuals who report no DES prompts with inner speech, the individuals who are not able to generate verbal imagery voluntarily, and the participants in our study who reported low propensity for everyday use of inner speech. It seems likely, however, that the overlap is substantial. Based on these outlined sources of evidence, I would argue that we are licensed to have as our working hypothesis that some people do not consciously experience inner speech in everyday life.

For most of the claims in Language, Cognition, and the Way We Think concerning how inner speech can function as a cognitive tool, the existence of people who do not experience inner speech is not a serious problem. Inner speech can serve various scaffolding functions (as mnemonic aid, as a means for controlling attention, as a planning tool, etc.) for those that have it while those that do not can plausibly function using alternative strategies. The challenge for inner speech theorists, then, would be to be specific about how facilitative inner speech is supposed to be (i.e., what should be the difference between using it and not using it), which exact properties of inner speech make it helpful (its linear, sequential nature, its hierarchical structure, its categorical structure, or something else entirely), and which alternative strategies with equivalent properties are possible. The real potential issue arises with claims that inner speech is necessary for this or that cognitive function, most notably featuring in some of the accounts of the role of inner speech in "higher-order" cognitive functions sketched out in section 3.2 Metacognition, Consciousness, and Self-knowledge of Kompa (2024). These theories state that consciously experienced inner speech is constitutively necessary for (some cases of) metacognition, self-reflection, self-knowledge, and reflexive, higher order, conscious thinking. In the following, I will briefly examine three such specific cases -Bermúdez' ideas on inner speech and "thinking about thinking", Morin's ideas on inner speech and self-reflection, and Kompa's own ideas on inner speech and deliberative, goal-directed thinking. Bermúdez (2003 Bermúdez ( , 2018) ) argues that language in general is crucial for the kind of thinking that can be characterised as "thinking about thinking" -in fact, he claims that 'we can only think about thoughts when they are linguistically formulated' (Bermúdez, 2018, p. 199). This kind of thinking includes attributing thoughts to others and reasoning about how thoughts relate logically to each other. For example, he argues that to consciously evaluate the relations between our judgments and the evidence we have for them, we must be able to "hold in mind" thoughts about judgments and thoughts about evidence to assess these relations. He emphasises that we can draw conclusions and make decisions based on evidence without language, but that we need language to reason about our reasoning. In order to evaluate our own reasoning, Bermúdez argues that this reasoning must be in a format that we can be conscious of, i.e. either image-like or language-like, and that this format must be structured in a way that can represent (logical and inferential) relations between representations (e.g., X is supported by Y evidence but Z evidence goes against it, A believes that P so is likely to act in M ways even though P is false). Since images do not, according to Bermúdez, have the required structural properties to represent inferential relations and the like, and since a (putative) Fodorian Language of Thought is not consciously accessible, the format must be natural language sentences. The existence of anendophasia presents an obvious issue for this kind of idea since people with anendophasia appear able to engage in complex reasoning (and reflect on this reasoning) both in social and other contexts at a sufficiently high level that their behaviour is not easily distinguishable from that of people with higher levels of inner speech.

A related idea -pertaining to what may be considered a subset of "thinking about thinking" -is the notion that inner speech is crucially involved in self-reflection, a position defended most extensively in contemporary psychology by Morin (e.g., Morin, 2005 , 2018; Morin et al., 2011; Morin & Brinthaupt, 2026; Morin & Hamper, 2012). Morin and colleagues' position is neatly summarised in the following quote (Morin, 2018, p. 276): 'a significant part of describing, defining, and understanding our self is done through extensive internal verbal conversations we have with ourselves about any and all aspects of our self.' Morin further argues that inner speech plays a causal role in implementing a host of functions revolving around and shaping the self, although it is not entirely clear whether he believes that inner speech is necessary for these functions. In some places, he states that he does not believe that inner speech is a prerequisite for awareness of an inner self (Morin, 2018, p. 281), while in other places he argues that 'higher, more conceptual (abstract) pieces of self-information require self-verbalization in order to be captured by the self' (p. 281, emphasis mine). Like Bermúdez, who emphasises that much of reasoning can and does take place without inner speech, Morin also states that some form or level of self-reflection -what he calls "perceptual (sensory) self-information" -is possible without inner speech. However, this perceptual self-reflection is of the sort exhibited by non-human animals and preverbal infants, anchored in immediate perceptual experience, which means that we cannot appeal to that notion to explain how people with anendophasia appear able to form, maintain, and reflect on their self-concept. Presumably, Morin's prediction about people with anendophasia would be that, all else being equal, they should have poorer self-reflection and a less realistic and complex self-concept ('it is likely that not using inner speech for introspective purposes will impede self-reflective activities and the development of a realistic and wellarticulated (i.e., complex) self-concept (Morin & Joshi, 1990)', p. 281). One could argue that people with anendophasia still learn about themselves through language, but they do so through social speech rather than inner speech. However, Morin claims that the social world is necessary but not sufficient for full-blown human self-reflection and self-knowledge -we need to verbalise self-statements to be self-aware outside social situations. Learning about oneself without inner speech is, according to Morin, like navigating through a dark room without turning the flashlight on.

Kompa does not appear to take a firm stance on these accounts, although she declares herself sympathetic to the idea that inner speech is necessary for complex deliberation involving trains of propositional thoughts and the application of learned inference rules (Kompa, 2024, p. 5). In fact, she points out several weaknesses of accounts claiming that inner speech is necessary to make us conscious of this or that, most importantly that they assume that there is a prior, language-independent thought to be revealed through inner speech. She says that this would commit us to a Fodorian Language of Thought (which should be 'our last resort', ibid, p. 147) and a translation manual. Of her own claims in Language, Cognition, and the Way We Think, the only one that comes close to positing a constitutive, necessary role for inner speech is the claim that 'some instances of thinking may be instances of inner speaking and vice versa' (p. 5). Kompa says that activities such as goal-directed, deliberative reasoning constitutively involve inner speech, and that 'activities such as these (often) exploit the linguistic properties of language or utilize language-bound social practices such as argumentation, question-answer protocols, or dialogue.' (p.

130). By the 'linguistic properties of language', she means semantic and syntactic properties, i.e. that lexical concepts are combined in a compositionally or syntactically structured way. Like Bermúdez and Morin, she also mentions that 'there are also (rudimentary forms of) causal and analogical reasoning which might not implicate linguistic capacities' (p. 130). However, appeal to these rudimentary forms is, like in the case of Morin's self-reflection, not sufficient to explain the indistinguishable-from-typical reasoning abilities of people with anendophasia. Whether or not the existence of people with anendophasia is really an issue for Kompa's ideas mentioned here hinges on 1) whether she believes that these instances of thinking which are instances of inner speech are necessary for ordinary human functioning, and 2) whether she believes that we need to be consciously aware of these instances for them to fulfil their functional roles. I do not think she believes either (but I will discuss the latter point in more detail below). Nevertheless, if Kompa is right, then people with anendophasia should show measurable differences in their deliberative, goal-directed reasoning.

Thus, it seems that the existence of people with anendophasia presents an issue for these three accounts positing an important and even occasionally necessary role of inner speech in metacognition, self-reflection, and deliberative reasoning. This is especially the case since people with anendophasia appear to be able to engage in these processes at a level that is on the surface indistinguishable from people with typical levels of inner speech. One conclusion that could be drawn from this is that inner speech is simply not necessary or particularly important for these processes. However, since the authors cited above (and many others) have made careful arguments to the contrary, it is worth first considering some potential alternative solutions: 1.

Inner speech fulfils the same functions for metacognition, self-reflection, and deliberative reasoning in people who do not experience inner speech, it just does so subconsciously.

Since we have to rely on self-report to assess the experience of inner speech, we cannot in principle know if people who report no inner speech actually do engage in it unconsciously. One possible solution to the challenge posed by the existence of people with anendophasia could thus be to say that they do use inner speech but are not conscious of it. Since Kompa (2024) wants to define inner speech mechanistically and not phenomenologically, I gather she would favour a solution that emphasises what inner speech does (what functions it fulfils) rather than how inner speech is experienced which might make her sympathetic to this solution. However, I do not think this is a good solution for two main reasons: first, I am not comfortable with the notion of unconscious inner speech since it is empirically intractable (even more empirically intractable than inner speech already is!) and arguably indistinguishable from a Language of Thought.

Second, most of the useful functions of inner speech -and in particular those argued for by Bermúdez and Morin -depend on conscious access. Bermúdez even explicitly states that the inner speech episodes he is referring to are conscious inner speech episodes. Kompa, however, does not require conscious access for her ideas of the constitutive role of inner speech in deliberative, goal-directed thinking but she does run into trouble trying to distinguish thinking that is linguistically structured from thinking that is structured in a different way which means that it would be incredibly difficult to test whether inner speech is happening or not -and this becomes even more of a thorny issue if the linguistically structured thought completely resists conscious access and thereby report.

2. People with anendophasia have inner speech but they do not hear it and therefore do not identify it as such.

This is related to the above potential solution but subtly different. It could be the case that inner speech activity is going on to the same extent in everyone, but that some people's auditory imagery is not phenomenologically vivid enough for them to be able to identify the inner speech as (voiced) speech. There could be syntactic and semantic structure determined by a specific natural language in their inner speech, but it would be without sensorimotor imagery (a recent paper estimates that this kind of inner language occurs in a non-trivial proportion (17.5%) of responses to prompts to think about a specific topic; Krempel et al., 2026). Rather than a problem of conscious access (as the above potential solution), this would be a problem of reliable reports. If people with anendophasia have inner speech but do not hear it, they could still be conscious of and feel a sense of agency related to inner speech episodes and be able to use them for self-reflection, reasoning, and metacognition, but they should have problems with tasks that rely specifically on the sensory features of an inner voice. Some findings lend credibility to this idea, for example that people who report less inner speech also report less auditory imagery overall (and fewer earworms), and that especially tasks requiring a high degree of phonological detail are negatively impacted (Nedergaard & Lupyan, 2024). However, while inner speech and auditory imagery are positively associated, it is possible to experience inner speech and no auditory imagery and vice versa (e.g., New et al., 2026) so the two are not always linked. Further, people with anendophasia often claim that their thoughts are not "effable", i.e., not straightforwardly translatable to language, which should not be the case if their thoughts are already linguistically formulated but just lack phonological encoding. If it were true that people with anendophasia either experience "soundless" inner speech or do not have conscious access to it at all, this would presumably make them less likely to use inner speech intentionally so we would still predict that their behaviour would be different from that of people with typical inner speech.

3. Linguistic structures in the mind can still subserve metacognition, self-reflection, and so on (offline) even if there is no (online) inner speech activity.

Several authors have argued that language serves as a cognitive scaffold throughout development (e.g., Bowerman & Choi, 2001; Gentner, 2010; Pu et al., 2025; Quine, 1960), that it functions much like a ladder to help us reach more sophisticated reasoning skills, theory of mind skills, and metacognitive skills. Once the skills are acquired, the ladder is no longer needed. This could explain why for example theory of mind development and language development seem so closely intertwined (De Villiers, 2000; de Villiers & Pyers, 2002; Lohmann & Tomasello, 2003; Milligan et al., 2007) while there is no robust evidence for online use of inner speech in solving theory of mind tasks in adulthood (Dungan & Saxe, 2012; Forgeot d'Arc & Ramus, 2011; Nedergaard, Wallentin, et al., 2023; Samuel et al., 2019). Since people with anendophasia also acquire social speech and develop as creatures steeped in language, it is plausible that language subserves higher-order cognition "offline" in them as well. However, this would not be enough to save the idea that conscious inner speech is necessary which, as discussed above, is the case for many of the posited useful functions of inner speech. It would also not be a very elegant theory that would have to posit that inner speech plays "online" roles in some individuals but "offline" roles in others.

4. Inner speech does not serve metacognitive, self-reflective, or deliberative reasoning functions in people without inner speech, and they have to externalise to achieve those functions (writing things down or speaking out loud).

Both Bermúdez, Morin, and Kompa claim that much of reasoning and self-reflection can take place without inner speech, so it is actually a fairly limited set of cases where there is something to be explained for people with anendophasia. It is therefore possible to hypothesise that such individuals would speak out loud (either to themselves or to others) or write down their thoughts in these cases where it arguably is necessary to make the thoughts graspable and order them sequentially. Whether they do this is of course an empirical question. There is already some tangential support from our 2024 study where participants with anendophasia found some of the tasks much easier if they talked out loud during the tasks. People with anendophasia also more informally often report things like 'I speak out loud to hear my thoughts' (DW, personal communication) and 'I have felt the need to talk out loud to myself… it's easier to make sense of the mess of thoughts "in my head" when I literally hear what I'm thinking' (TN, personal communication). I believe this potential solution would appeal to Bermúdez, since he states that he is not claiming that thinking about thinking is only possible through inner speech -that it could also be through visualising writing or through speaking out loud or writing something out (Bermúdez, 2018, p. 203-204).

Of these possible solutions, the latter seems most plausible to me, although any one of them or a combination are still possible pending further empirical investigations. Currently, the two most informative ways forward would be: 1) To investigate to which degree thought activity (perhaps especially reasoning and self-reflection) is linguistically structured (semantically and syntactically if not phonologically) in individuals with anendophasia and whether they are able to report on this. One way of doing this would be to test whether their thoughts are in fact as "effable" as those of people with typical levels of inner speech, that is whether they can relatively quickly and easily put their spontaneously occurring thoughts into words. 2) To test whether individuals with anendophasia are in fact more likely to talk out loud or write things down in the specific subset of cases that Bermúdez, Morin, and Kompa refer to (deliberative, goal-directed reasoning, abstract, context-independent self-reflection, and conscious metacognition).

Earlier in the present commentary, I argued that the existence of anendophasia, if taken seriously, should prompt us to be more specific and explicit about what exactly it is about inner speech that is supposed to help and to what degree. To shed further light on this issue, I believe it would also be worth considering cases where it is not beneficial to think in a language, especially in light of the fact that many people with anendophasia claim that thinking in inner speech would be burdensome and time-consuming.

Although Kompa does not discuss this in much detail in her book, she does list it as one of the possible ways that language and cognition could be related (p. 3): '[language and cognition] are related, but language hinders or distorts thought. One might, for example, argue that the variety and richness of thought cannot be captured in language and that linguistically formulating thoughts cannot help but result in loss and impoverishment.' I suspect that many people with anendophasia would be sympathetic to this idea. There are indeed some specific cases where it appears that thinking in language is detrimental. For example, verbal overshadowing happens when linguistic re-coding of a non-linguistic stimulus distorts the memory of the stimulus and blurs the visuospatial details (Alogna et al., 2014; Lane & Schooler, 2004; Schooler & Engstler-Schooler, 1990). There are also some verbal interference studies suggesting that verbal interference sometimes makes you better at solving a task like mental transformation (Brandimonte et al., 1992a (Brandimonte et al., , 1992b;; Hitch et al., 1995; Pelizzon et al., 1999) and faster at decision-making, presumably because you skip a linguistic re-coding step (Evans & Brooks, 1981; Forgeot d'Arc & Ramus, 2011; Phillips, 1999).

In this commentary, I have discussed the evidence for and potential consequences of the existence of anendophasia, the phenomenon where some individuals experience very little or no inner speech in their everyday lives. I argued that anendophasia presents a serious problem for theories positing that consciously available inner speech is necessary and important for certain "higher-order" cognitive functions and outlined four potential solutions. Considering the notion of anendophasia alongside the points raised in Kompa's book generates many empirical challenges such as figuring out whether the thoughts of people with anendophasia are less "effable", i.e. easily translatable to language, how to measure inner speech activity that is not phonologically specified, and whether people with anendophasia externalise by talking out loud or writing things down in those cases where we would predict that speech/a linguistic format is necessary (sequential, deliberative reasoning, evaluating one's own thinking, consciously reflecting on one's self-concept, etc.) One thing is certain: we can learn a lot about inner speech from people who do not have it.
