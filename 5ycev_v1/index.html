Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

Abstract

While research on workplace ostracism has largely focused on social, physical, and cyber modalities, emerging scholarship highlights linguistic ostracism as a distinct and under-recognized modality. It presents unique complexities due to its high attributional ambiguity, generally non-purposeful nature, and the requirement that a subgroup of two or more individuals speak a shared language unfamiliar to the target. This chapter proposes a multimodal perspective on workplace ostracism by situating linguistic ostracism alongside social, physical, and cyber modalities. Through a comparative analysis of these four modalities, the chapter identifies both shared aspects and key points of divergence. It also considers how they may interact, potentially amplifying their impact when they co-occur. By positioning linguistic ostracism as a complementary and essential form, the chapter aims to expand current theoretical frameworks and underscore the value of a multimodal approach to understanding workplace ostracism, particularly in linguistically and culturally diverse work environments.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, research on social exclusion has grown considerably, with consistent findings highlighting its harmful effects on individual, interpersonal, and group outcomes (Chen et al., 2025; Smart Richman & Leary, 2009). These experiences are not only socially but also physically painful (Williams, 2007a). Building on this foundation, scholars have increasingly explored how experiences of ostracism and rejection unfold within organizational settings, where exclusionary behaviors can have serious consequences for both employee well-being and broader workplace functioning (Bedi, 2021; Howard, Cogswell, & Smith, 2020). This growing body of research has positioned workplace ostracism as not only a social concern, but as a critical organizational issue that warrants attention alongside other forms of counterproductive work behavior (Robinson et al., 2013).

To better understand the nature of exclusion, researchers have distinguished between two broad forms: rejection-based and ostracism-based exclusion (Wesselmann & Williams, 2017; Williams, 2007a). Rejection involves overt signals of devaluation, such as being mocked, stigmatized, or openly told one is not wanted (Blackhart et al., 2009). These actions communicate direct negative attention and typically carry clear interpersonal or group-based intent. Ostracism, in contrast, involves being ignored or left out without explanation (Williams, 2007a). Rather than receiving explicit negative feedback, individuals experiencing ostracism encounter silence, absence of acknowledgment, or subtle nonverbal cues that render them socially invisible. Although both forms of exclusion can be distressing, they differ in how the exclusion is delivered and interpreted. In this chapter, we focus explicitly on ostracism-based exclusion and its various manifestations in organizational life.

The conceptualization of ostracism has evolved substantially over time, becoming more refined and context specific. Initially, Gruter and Masters (1986), in a special issue of Ethology & Sociobiology, framed ostracism as a phenomenon where individuals are deliberately excluded by a group to maintain the group's cohesiveness and survival. Subsequently, Williams (2007) broadly defined ostracism as the act of "ignoring and excluding individuals or groups by individuals or groups" (p. 427). Ferris et al. (2008) shifted the focus toward subjective experiences by describing ostracism as "the extent to which an individual perceives that he or she is ignored or excluded by others" (p. 1348). Robinson et al. (2013) further refined this definition within workplace contexts, specifying ostracism as occurring "when an individual or group omits to take actions that engage another organizational member when it is socially appropriate to do so" (p. 206).

Across these perspectives, a consistent characteristic emerges in that ostracism fundamentally involves being ignored or excluded when inclusion is socially expected (Gruter & Masters, 1986; Williams, 2001). As a result, contemporary research increasingly differentiates ostracism from related aversive workplace behaviors such as aggression, incivility, mobbing, and bullying, emphasizing its distinct nature and unique impacts (Ferris et al., 2008; Ferris et al., 2017; Robinson et al., 2013; Williams, 1997). This definitional evolution underscores the growing recognition of ostracism as a subtle, yet impactful form of interpersonal mistreatment in organizations, and one that is shaped by institutional norms, power dynamics, and structural factors that give rise to its distinctive psychological and organizational consequences.

In addition to definitional refinements, emerging research has also illuminated the functions that ostracism can serve. Hales et al. (2016) proposed that exclusion often serves specific purposes for the ostracizer or group, such as protecting the group from individuals perceived as problematic, signaling to members that their behavior must change to remain included, or removing those who resist correction. Similarly, Nezlek et al. (2012) found that self-protection is a common motive among those who engage in ostracism. Together, this work suggests that ostracism may function to punish norm violations or safeguard the ostracizer's social standing, emotional stability, or group cohesion. These functional perspectives complement definitional approaches by highlighting that ostracism is not only an interpersonal behavior but also a strategic social process shaped by group needs and individual motives.

While ostracism is often treated as a general construct, relatively little attention has been given to systematically examining the distinct modalities through which ostracism is enacted in the workplace. Yet meaningful differences exist in how ostracism is enacted, perceived, and interpreted. As research moves beyond broad definitions, it is crucial to explore how different modalities of ostracism (e.g., physical, social), differ in expression and in impact. Adopting a multimodal perspective may reveal that different modalities of ostracism elicit distinct psychological and behavioral responses, shaping outcomes in ways that a unitary view fails to capture.

Each modality of ostracism-physical, social, cyber, and linguistic-shares the common thread of social disconnection and the potential to threaten basic psychological needs (Robinson et al., 2013; Williams, 2007). While physical, social, and cyber ostracism have been widely recognized in existing models (Williams, 1997 (Williams, , 2001)), linguistic ostracism has received comparatively little attention despite its growing relevance in today's diverse and multilingual workplaces (e.g., Fiset & Bhave, 2021; Kulkarni, 2015). Linguistic ostracism involves exclusion rooted in the loss of access to shared language or communicative understanding, often unfolding through subtle or unintended acts that still leave individuals feeling ignored or disconnected. In contrast, physical ostracism refers to exclusion from spaces or activities; social ostracism arises through interpersonal neglect or rejection; and cyber ostracism occurs in digital environments, where individuals are ignored or excluded from online communication channels. Linguistic ostracism, however, represents a distinct and meaningful form of exclusion that warrants inclusion in modality-based frameworks of ostracism. By formally recognizing linguistic ostracism as a unique modality, scholars and practitioners can more fully capture the diverse exclusionary experiences present in contemporary organizational life.

In the following section, we explore the dominant model of ostracism in organizations, Williams' Need-Threat Model (2001 , 2007 , 2009) which typically includes physical, social, and cyber modalities. We also acknowledge the broader Smart Richman and Leary (2009) framework on social rejection, which has gained traction and complements Williams' model by offering a more generalized perspective on exclusionary experiences. While these categories have shaped much of the existing research, we propose integrating an emerging and increasingly important form: linguistic ostracism. By doing so, we aim to broaden the discussion of exclusion in organizations, particularly in diverse and multicultural workplaces where language dynamics play a central role. We draw attention to how language barriers and exclusionary communication practices can create unique experiences of ostracism that may go unnoticed within existing models. This chapter examines where linguistic ostracism overlaps with, diverges from, and enriches the broader ostracism framework, offering new insights for both theory and practice in today's global and multilingual organizational environments.

Modalities of Ostracism

Williams ' (1997, 2001) Need-Threat Model offers a structured framework for understanding the different ways individuals experience ostracism in the workplace. The model outlines four dimensions: motive, quantity, causal clarity, and visibility, each of which helps differentiate how exclusionary behavior occurs and affects individuals. Together, these dimensions illustrate that ostracism is not a uniform experience but one that varies across context and interpretation, influencing the severity and nature of its psychological and interpersonal effects.

In terms of motive, ostracism may be defensive, such as avoiding a colleague to sidestep conflict after a disagreement; punitive, when a team deliberately excludes someone who violated a norm; oblivious, where an employee from a marginalized background is overlooked not due to any specific incident but because they are perceived as unimportant. Sometimes, exclusion may not be intended (not ostracism) or could be socially expected (role-prescribed), such as not engaging in small talk with everyone in a busy office.

The quantity of ostracism ranges from partial, such as limiting interaction to formal meetings but excluding someone from social gatherings or email threads, to complete, where a colleague is consistently ignored both socially and professionally. Causal clarity refers to how clearly the reasons for exclusion are understood by the employee. In some cases, such as after receiving a formal warning, the cause of the exclusion may be obvious. In other situations, employees may be excluded without any clear explanation, leaving them uncertain and confused about why they are being ignored.

The visibility dimension captures different ostracism modalities. Initially, visibility encompassed two primary modes: physical and social ostracism (Williams, 1997). Physical ostracism involves observable acts of exclusion, such as a colleague being deliberately left out of team meetings or relocated away from colleagues without clear reasons. Social ostracism refers to subtler interpersonal exclusion, such as coworkers regularly ignoring an individual's contributions during group interactions despite being physically present. This model was subsequently expanded to include cyber ostracism, which captures exclusion occurring in virtual environments (Williams, 2001; Williams et al., 2000; Williams et al., 2002). Examples of cyber ostracism include not receiving responses to emails, being deliberately excluded from virtual meetings, or ignored within social media interactions or direct messages. Although physical, social, and cyber ostracism differ in their specific mechanisms and modes of delivery, they share a common characteristic: the perception of being ignored or excluded in ways that threaten core psychological needs, such as belongingness and self-esteem.

Recent scholarship has further attempted to expand the visibility dimension by recognizing linguistic ostracism as a distinct yet related form of exclusion (Fiset & Bhave, 2021). Linguistic ostracism occurs when individuals perceive exclusion through language-related mechanisms, such as conversations or documents conducted in languages or jargon that they cannot understand or easily engage with. Although linguistic ostracism differs in its specific methods from physical, social, and cyber modes, they all disrupt social connections and elicit negative emotional and cognitive responses (e.g., Dotan-Eliaz et al., 2009). Collectively, these various modes of ostracism underscore the multifaceted ways in which exclusion is communicated and experienced within contemporary workplaces.

Importantly, ostracism threatens four basic psychological needs: belonging, selfesteem, control, and meaningful existence. These needs are central to employee well-being and workplace functioning, and ostracism typically reduces positive affect while increasing negative affect (Ferris et al., 2008; Williams, 2009). In line with Williams' (2007a) model, these threats can elicit fight, flight, freeze, or tendand-befriend responses, and were further elaborated on by Sharma and Dhar (2024). Fight responses may include confrontational coping such as directly challenging the perpetrator or engaging in aggressive or deviant acts to regain control, although these strategies often escalate conflict and hinder recovery. Flight responses reflect avoidance and align with avoidance coping, which involves withdrawing from interactions or situations that might trigger further exclusion. Freeze responses involve emotional numbing or passivity and can resemble ramification-alleviation coping, where individuals focus on managing internal distress through emotional support, mindfulness, or reframing rather than attempting to change the situation. Tend-and-befriend responses involve seeking support or forming alternative connections and can include influential coping strategies such as ingratiation, prosocial behavior, or political behaviors that aim to rebuild acceptance and shape social dynamics more constructively.

Coping with ostracism is a dynamic process that depends on individual characteristics such as neuroticism, interpersonal sensitivity, and the need to belong, as well as organizational features including hierarchical culture and the presence of supportive leadership (Sharma and Dhar, 2024). These personal and contextual factors shape how employees interpret exclusion, the coping strategies they adopt, and whether they sustain, modify, or discontinue those strategies over time. Consequently, coping responses are fluid and multi-layered, closely tied to perceptions of resource loss and relational meaning, and are central to understanding how employees manage different forms of workplace ostracism.

The model also accounts for antecedents such as individual traits like neuroticism or organizational factors like hierarchical culture, mediators such as the target's attribution of intent or personal coping style, and reactions to ostracism, which were updated to include fight, flight, freeze, and tend-and-befriend (Williams, 2007a). Importantly, ostracism is understood to threaten four basic psychological needs: belonging, self-esteem, control, and meaningful existence, all of which are essential to employee well-being and workplace functioning (Ferris et al., 2008; Williams, 2009). Williams (2009) later built on his Need-Threat Model of ostracism by proposing a three-stage temporal model to describe how reactions to ostracism evolve over time. In the reflexive stage, individuals experience an immediate, automatic reaction to being excluded, marked by a universal and largely uncontrollable psychological pain and a sharp threat to their core psychological needs. This is followed by the reflective stage, where individuals begin to appraise the situation more consciously, making attributions about why the exclusion occurred and choosing how to respond (e.g., seeking re-inclusion, withdraw, retaliate, or turning to others for support). If exclusion persists and efforts to reconnect fail, the resignation stage may follow. At this point, individuals may psychologically disengage, experiencing emotional numbness, learned helplessness, or long-term alienation from the workplace.

Building on this foundation, the present chapter extends the Need-Threat Model by examining how linguistic ostracism fits within the visibility dimension of ostracism (Williams, 1997 (Williams, , 2001) ) and by exploring how modalities differ in the ways exclusion is enacted, perceived, and interpreted (see Table 1). The chapter compares linguistic ostracism with other established modalities such as physical, social, and cyber, identifying both its distinctive features and areas of overlap, and considers how these modalities may co-occur, potentially amplifying their psychological and social effects. Together, these insights offer a more nuanced understanding of how exclusionary practices are communicated and sustained through language, and the implications these dynamics hold for employee well-being, inclusion, and organizational functioning.

Physical Ostracism

Physical ostracism refers to the exclusion of an individual through bodily or spatial separation from others (Williams, 2001 , Lustenberger & Williams, 2009). It includes generally deliberate actions that remove or distance the individual from a social setting entirely. It is characterized by intentional actions that distance a person from a social context altogether. These actions can range from subtle, everyday behaviors such as avoiding eye contact (Böckler et al., 2014), turning away, or failing to invite someone to join a group, to more institutionalized forms, including banishment, exile, or solitary confinement (Harvey et al., 2019; Zadro et al., 2008; Williams, 2001). Although understudied in organizational contexts, this form of exclusion is reflected in workplace measures, such as the Workplace Ostracism Scale item "Others left the area when you entered" (Ferris et al., 2008).

In the workplace, physical ostracism is often more overt and less ambiguous than other modes of exclusion because it involves clear, observable spatial separation (Chen et al., 2025). Unlike social ostracism, which can be subtle and open to interpretation, physical ostracism includes unmistakable actions such as being consistently excluded from meetings, or experiencing situations where coworkers deliberately leave the area upon your arrival (Ferris et al., 2008; Harvey et al., 2019). These modes of ostracism do not require the constant presence of others, as the act is carried out through the individual's physical removal or displacement. As a result, physical ostracism in organizational settings tends to carry low attributional ambiguity as employees typically recognize they have been excluded and can clearly identify how it was enacted. The visibility and permanence of this separation leave little room for doubt about the exclusion or its intent.

Some scholars have proposed that being physically removed from a group may reduce the pain of rejection by limiting exposure to the social interactions that reinforce one's exclusion (Williams, 2001). In these cases, individuals may turn their attention toward alternative tasks or environments, offering some psychological distance from the exclusion. However, others contend that physical ostracism may be even more painful, precisely because the excluded individual is aware that others are continuing to engage in meaningful interactions in their absence (Hales & Williams, 2021). Regardless of the mechanism, empirical research has shown that physical ostracism is associated with a number of negative interpersonal, affective, and psychological outcomes such as increased anger, and reduced feelings of control and self-esteem (Nezlek et al., 2012).

Social Ostracism

Social ostracism refers to situations in which individuals are ignored, excluded, or given the silent treatment even though they remain physically present with others (Williams, 1997 (Williams, , 2001)). In contrast to physical ostracism, where the target is physically removed from the setting, social ostracism operates through the withholding of verbal or non-verbal engagement (e.g., refusing to return a greeting, averting eye contact, or allowing ideas to die in conversation (Ferris et al., 2008; Harvey et al., 2019). William James (1890 /1950) once wrote that being socially ignored can provoke such intense feelings of despair that physical pain might seem preferable. Indeed, social ostracism has been likened to social death (Williams, 2007b) because it threatens the fundamental human need for belonging.

A defining feature of social ostracism is its subtlety and attributional ambiguity. Perpetrators can deny intentionality by claiming distraction or unawareness, and bystanders may not notice the behavior at all (Robinson et al., 2013). Although people often detect ostracism early (Eisenberger & Lieberman, 2004; Spoor & Williams, 2007; Williams & Zadro, 2005), it is less likely to be punished for more active forms of workplace harassment (O'Reilly et al., 2015), as perpetrators can easily deny any wrongdoing by claiming distraction or unawareness (Robinson et al., 2013). In addition, unlike workplace incivility or bullying, which are typically overt and aggressive, social ostracism is conceptually distinct and involves covertness and omission (Ferris et al., 2017; Fiset & Robinson, 2018; Robinson et al., 2013). As such, it may be unintentional, episodic, and difficult to prove. Social ostracism is often subtle and marked by attributional ambiguity, which allows perpetrators to plausibly deny exclusionary behavior and makes it difficult for others to detect (Robinson et al., 2013). In workplace settings, it is typically expressed through the withholding of verbal or nonverbal engagement, such as ignoring greetings or avoiding eye contact. These behaviors are captured by the Workplace Ostracism Scale (Ferris et al., 2008), which includes items such as "Others ignored you at work" and "Your greetings went unanswered." Because social ostracism occurs in the presence of others, it requires physical co-presence but not a defined subgroup, as it can be enacted by individuals or groups (Williams, 2007a).

Social ostracism in organizational settings carries serious implications for both individual well-being and broader organizational functioning. Meta-analytic evidence further indicates that ostracism is associated with a wide range of negative interpersonal, affective, and psychological outcomes, including workplace deviance, low task performance, reduced wellbeing, and diminished job satisfaction (e.g., Bedi, 2021; Howard et al., 2020). Although the behavior is often subtle and difficult to identify, its psychological toll can be severe, with lasting effects on both the individual and the workplace environment.

Cyber Ostracism

Cyber ostracism refers to being ignored, excluded, or left out through digital communication channels, including emails, messaging platforms, virtual meetings, and social networking sites (Williams et al., 2000). Cyber ostracism is increasingly relevant in today's remote and hybrid work environments, where a significant amount of workplace interaction occurs online (Kassner et al., 2012). Unlike physical exclusion, and similar to social exclusion, cyber ostracism is often more subtle in visibility and ambiguous in intent, making it harder to detect and address. It can be as simple as being left off an important email thread, not receiving responses in chat threads, or being ignored in a virtual meeting.

Cyber ostracism can be carried out by individuals or groups in digital settings, either through intentional acts such as ghosting or ignoring messages, or through more passive behaviors like failing to acknowledge someone's input during virtual meetings or group chats (e.g., Williams et al., 2002). A central characteristic of cyber ostracism is its high attributional ambiguity, as targets often struggle to determine whether the exclusion was intentional or accidental (Chen et al., 2025). This ambiguity is amplified by the asynchronous nature of online communication (Li et al., 2022; Mattila et al., 2013), where individuals are physically separated and unable to observe or hear the ongoing interactions between others (Smith & Williams, 2004). In such contexts, delays or silence may not seem overtly hostile but can nonetheless be deeply felt. Common situations, such as being overlooked on collaboration platforms like Slack or Teams or not receiving responses to emails, can evoke strong feelings of exclusion (Harvey et al., 2019). In the absence of clear, immediate cues, individuals are left to interpret the meaning behind the silence, which can lead to rumination, uncertainty, and emotional distress (Chen et al., 2025; Schneider et al., 2017).

Even brief instances of online exclusion, such as those simulated in the "Ostracism Online" paradigm (Wolf et al., 2015), can result in significant emotional distress, affecting both short-term wellbeing and long-term perceptions of organizational belonging. Although some research suggests that cyber ostracism may be less aversive than social ostracism (Williams et al., 2002) and may even lead to increased online work engagement under certain conditions (Yang et al., 2022), the broader evidence highlights it as a serious organizational concern. Cyber ostracism has been linked to adverse interpersonal, affective, and psychological outcomes including heightened job stress, reduced mental wellbeing, and increased aggression (Liu et al., 2018; McCord et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2020).

Linguistic Ostracism

Linguistic ostracism is defined as "instances where focal employees perceive that others at work have rejected and/or excluded them by using a language that they do not comprehend" (Fiset & Bhave, 2021, p. 431). What distinguishes linguistic ostracism from other modes of exclusion is its reliance on language as the medium through which exclusion is enacted. That is, targets are symbolically excluded when coworkers converse in a language the focal employee does not understand, thereby denying them access to participation, interaction, and shared meaning within the group (Carter-Sowell et al., 2021; Neeley et al., 2009). A common example involves colleagues switching from the shared corporate language (e.g., English) to another language (e.g., Serbian) in the presence of an employee who does not comprehend it (Dotan-Eliaz et al., 2009; Kulkarni, 2015).

Because language serves as a salient cue for social and cultural group membership, such exclusion can also trigger group-based categorizations (Bhave & Fiset, 2021). In these instances, targets may not only experience interpersonal rejection but may interpret the behavior as signaling boundaries between linguistic, ethnic, or national groups. As a result, linguistic ostracism can activate social identity and categorization processes, prompting individuals to protect both their personal and group identities. Prior research suggests that linguistic exclusion may elicit negativity directed at perpetrators as well as at the broader group to which they belong, including heightened perceptions of group-based threat and prejudice (Hitlan et al., 2006; Hitlan et al., 2016). Linguistic ostracism is also conceptually distinct from cases in which language indirectly contributes to other forms of exclusion. Some employees, for example, may be physically or socially excluded because they are perceived as linguistically incompetent due to limited fluency or a noticeable accent (Carter-Sowell et al., 2021; Kulkarni, 2015; Kulkarni & Sommer, 2015). In these cases, language operates as an antecedent to exclusion. In contrast, linguistic ostracism is enacted directly through communicative choices that isolate the target in real time, which makes language itself the immediate medium of exclusion.

Organizational research characterizes linguistic ostracism as generally nonpurposeful and attributionally ambiguous (Ferris et al., 2017; Robinson et al., 2013). For example, in a recent study, Fiset (2022) found that 81% of participants who reported speaking in a non-mutually understood language at work did so for nonsurreptitious reasons, including reducing communication process loss and building solidarity with others who speak the same language. Although these behaviors may not be maliciously intended, they are often perceived as exclusionary and are both subtle and attributionally ambiguous because the target cannot understand the content of the conversation and is therefore unable to determine whether the exclusion is intentional or benign. This uncertainty about others' motives can heighten psychological distress and social tension. Such experiences are associated with a range of negative outcomes, including reduced group commitment (Hitlan et al., 2006), heightened frustration, anger and suspicion toward coworkers (Hitlan et al., 2016; Kulkarni, 2015), reduced performance (Fiset & Bhave, 2021; Fiset & Bhave, in press), and greater language-related misunderstandings in the workplace (Fiset, 2023; Fiset, Bhave, & Jha, 2024). In this regard, linguistic ostracism fits within broader definitions of workplace ostracism, which involve the omission of socially appropriate engagement (Robinson et al., 2013).

Nonetheless, linguistic ostracism is considered a specific form of workplace ostracism that is empirically distinct from the broader construct (Fiset & Bhave, 2021; Robinson et al., 2013). It occurs when two or more individuals communicate in a language that the target does not understand, creating a language-based subgroup and resulting in a many-to-one form of exclusion (Dotan-Eliaz et al., 2009). In contrast, other modes of workplace ostracism can be enacted by a single individual (Ferris et al., 2008; Williams, 2001). The group-based nature of linguistic ostracism may heighten its psychological impact by reinforcing the target's outsider status, especially in multicultural teams, where language serves as a key marker of identity and belonging (Kulkarni, 2015). Because the exclusion is enacted by a subgroup rather than a single individual, it is particularly likely to elicit group-level affective responses and interpretations, further differentiating linguistic ostracism from other ostracism modalities that do not typically produce such categorization effects.

These dynamics are particularly salient in diverse and multilingual workplaces, where language often functions as a proxy for cultural inclusion. Immigrant employees who are not fluent in the dominant workplace language, or who speak it with a distinct accent, may be especially vulnerable to exclusion (DelCampo et al., 2011; Newman et al., 2012). In such contexts, linguistic ostracism can contribute to broader patterns of marginalization, undermining individual well-being and organizational cohesion.

In the next section, we provide a detailed analysis of how linguistic ostracism fits into the broader ostracism literature and contributes to multimodal understanding of its effects in organizations. We further argue for its conceptual distinctiveness, the methodological and attributional complexities it introduces, and the unique challenges it poses. We follow this with a discussion on future research as well as practical implications for how this multimodal lens can help leaders foster more inclusive and equitable interactions in organizations.

Understanding Ostracism Through a Multimodal Lens

In practice, workplace ostracism is likely to unfold in a multimodal fashion and that makes it difficult to pinpoint where one form ends and another begins. Consider an employee who enters the lunchroom and finds their colleagues engaged in a vibrant discussion. As they approach, no one acknowledges their presence (social ostracism), the conversation continues uninterrupted in the unfamiliar language (linguistic ostracism), and the group averts their gaze and subtly shifts their bodies away, failing to make space at the table (physical ostracism). Another example occurs in virtual environments, where individuals may be excluded by group members who continue interacting in a language the individual does not understand, creating a simultaneous experience of cyber and linguistic ostracism (Hitlan et al., 2016). While each act might appear minor or justifiable in isolation, the cumulative experience generates a potent sense of exclusion that is difficult to deny.

Despite growing recognition of ostracism's negative consequences, existing research tends to study exclusion as a monolithic experience, artificially isolating it into discrete categories, such as cyber, social, or physical, for the sake of experimental or conceptual clarity (e.g., Hartgerink et al., 2015), or merging multiple modes into the same study (Ferris et al., 2008). While this approach has yielded important insights, it fails to capture the real-world complexity of how exclusion is enacted and perceived in organizational life (Williams et al., 2000). Just as workplace mistreatment research has moved toward intersectional and cumulative frameworks (e.g., Dhanani, & Bogart, in press), ostracism research must shift toward a layered, integrative model that considers how modality and motive intersect in specific exclusion episodes. For instance, a meaningful unit of analysis could be an exclusionary event rather than an exclusionary mode, enabling researchers to identify how multiple modes of ostracism manifest within a single encounter.

Discussion

Taking a multimodal perspective to the study of ostracism is particularly important because it gradually reduces the attributional ambiguity that might otherwise allow individuals to dismiss isolated incidents (Meral et al., 2023). For example, when a colleague averts their gaze, an employee might interpret the behavior as mere distraction. However, when that behavior is accompanied by exclusion from email threads or side conversations in which others engage freely, a recognizable pattern of exclusion begins to form, and one that is increasingly difficult to overlook or explain away. This issue is especially pronounced in cases of linguistic ostracism, where targets are excluded from interactions conducted in a language they do not understand, leaving them unable to access the content or discern the intent behind the exchange (Fiset & Bhave, in press; Fiset & Bhave, 2021). In multilingual workplaces, such incidents are often minimized as cultural misunderstandings, obscuring their cumulative harm. Beyond creating ambiguity, linguistic ostracism can also trigger group-based categorizations because language often signals cultural, ethnic, or national group membership (Hitlan et al., 2006 , Hitlan et al., 2016). Targets may therefore interpret exclusion as both interpersonal rejection and a threat to group identity, which can elicit defensive reactions and negativity toward perpetrators and the broader group they represent.

These group-based processes further indicate that linguistic ostracism may evoke stronger intergroup affective responses than other modalities of workplace exclusion. This possibility is especially relevant for organizations that value diversity and inclusion, as language-based exclusion may inadvertently heighten intergroup tensions and undermine efforts to foster psychological safety and cohesive collaboration (Tenzer & Pudelko, 2015). For this reason, it is essential to examine the broader context in which ostracism occurs to better assess the perceived or actual intent behind exclusionary acts.

Practical Implications

Adopting a multimodal lens allows organizations to detect exclusionary behaviors that may otherwise go unnoticed (Fiset & Boies, 2018). When exclusion is viewed through only one form, such as being ignored in conversation or left out of an email, it is often dismissed as a misunderstanding or isolated incident. However, considering social, physical, cyber, and linguistic modes together enables a more accurate diagnosis of patterns that unfold across different workplace contexts. This perspective helps managers and HR professionals identify employees who may be experiencing consistent but subtle modes of exclusion, even when no single act seems severe on its own. By embracing a multimodal approach to workplace ostracism, it can help managers improve the ways they and their organizations gather and respond to employee feedback. Traditional surveys and complaint mechanisms often fail to capture the complexity of exclusionary experiences, particularly those that occur through digital platforms or language-based interactions. By expanding survey instruments and reporting tools to include items that reflect multiple modalities of ostracism, organizations can better identify and address concerns. For example, including questions about feeling left out of virtual meetings, ignored in group chats, or excluded from conversations conducted in unfamiliar languages allows for a more nuanced understanding of employee experiences. This perspective also helps organizations develop more targeted inclusion strategies. Rather than relying on one-size-fits-all interventions, companies can tailor their training and intervention efforts to specific contexts and exclusion risks (Scott & Duffy, 2015; Tenzer & Pudelko, 2015). In hybrid teams, this might include establishing clear norms for digital communication to prevent cyber ostracism. In multilingual teams, it may involve developing a language strategy where the team sets shared language expectations to reduce linguistic ostracism (Fiset & Bhave, 2023; Neeley & Kaplan, 2014). At the same time, a multimodal perspective supports training efforts that help employees recognize how small, seemingly benign actions (e.g., avoiding eye contact or switching languages mid-conversation) can collectively contribute to feelings of exclusion. By raising awareness of how exclusion can operate across different modalities, organizations can reduce attributional ambiguity, foster more intentional inclusion, and strengthen psychological safety for all employees.

Future Research Directions

To advance a more nuanced understanding of exclusion in organizational contexts, future research could adapt and integrate existing measurement tools, such as the Workplace Ostracism Scale (Ferris et al., 2008), the Workplace Exclusion Scale (Hitlan & Noel, 2009), and the Linguistic Ostracism Scale (Fiset & Bhave, 2021). These could be used alongside newly developed or refined instruments designed to capture physical and cyber modes of ostracism. Such an approach would enable researchers to systematically assess both the distinct and overlapping effects of different exclusion modalities and to evaluate whether each form accounts for unique variance in predicting key organizational outcomes. For instance, researchers could explore whether linguistic and cyber ostracism exacerbate negative experiences among members of multilingual virtual teams, particularly in relation to team collaboration and knowledge hiding. This work would build upon existing research by clarifying the additive or interactive impact of multimodal ostracism within complex workplace environments.

Researchers might also employ experience sampling methods or diary studies that incorporate modality tagging, enabling participants to record ostracism episodes and indicate whether the exclusion occurred through social, physical, linguistic, or digital means, or some combination of these. Such critical incident techniques are well suited to identifying patterns of multimodal exclusion over time and can support examination of ostracism as a dynamic, evolving process. This is particularly useful when viewed through the lens of Williams' (2009) three-stage temporal model of ostracism, which includes the reflexive, reflective, and resignation stages. Research on linguistic ostracism, in particular, calls for more precise conceptual understanding of its many-to-one structure, its high attributional ambiguity, and its culturally embedded nature. By broadening the methodological toolkit and expanding theoretical frameworks, researchers can more accurately map the complex social dynamics of workplace exclusion and contribute to the development of more inclusive and responsive organizational practices.

Building on this, future research should explore whether these motives are equally relevant across different modes of ostracism. Each form is shaped by a distinct communicative context that may influence both the intent of the ostracizer and the interpretation of the target. For example, cyber ostracism may be more easily justified as a passive or ambiguous act of self-protection, while physical or social ostracism may more clearly communicate punishment or rejection. Linguistic ostracism introduces a unique aspect to this model by integrating linguistic familiarity and ease (Fiset, 2022). In multilingual environments, individuals may switch to a shared language not necessarily to exclude, but to increase conversational comfort and efficiency among fluent speakers. Expanding the current taxonomy of ostracism motives to include this consideration and others could deepen our understanding of how exclusion operates in diverse, multilingual settings and help distinguish between deliberate exclusion and behavior driven by communicative or cognitive preferences.

In addition, power plays a central role in workplace ostracism, which can function as a tool for asserting control and limiting others' influence (Fiset et al., 2017). As Robinson and Schabram (2017) highlight, ostracism can be used to manipulate organizational dynamics. For example, an employee may give silent treatment to exclude others from important information or social networks, thereby weakening their influence while enhancing the power of the one excluding them (Gamian-Wilk et al., 2017). Importantly, unlike related constructs such as mobbing or bullying, where power imbalance is a defining feature, ostracism can be enacted by individuals or groups at any level of the hierarchy. It may occur upward (e.g., subordinates excluding a supervisor), downward (e.g., managers excluding employees), or laterally among peers. This flexibility distinguishes ostracism from forms of mistreatment that rely on hierarchical asymmetry and helps explain its wide range of manifestations within organizations. This is further evidenced by research showing that even individuals in positions of power, such as managers, can be involved in ostracism as targets (Robinson et al., 2013; Walsh et al., 2019), as sources (Jahanzeb et al., 2021), or as interveners (Fiset & Boies, 2018). These patterns illustrate that ostracism both shapes and is shaped by power, and that ostracism can reinforce, challenge, or reshape existing power structures depending on who enacts it and under what circumstances. These dynamics can be observed across multiple modes of ostracism, including linguistic, cyber, social, and physical ostracism. Future research could explore how power interacts with these distinct modes to shape the experience and consequences of being ostracized at work.

The emergence of linguistic ostracism as a distinct modality of exclusion invites scholars to consider additional, under-explored modes of ostracism that may not align neatly with the dominant social, physical, or cyber typologies. One promising direction for future research is the systematic integration of information exclusion, which refers to the experience of being deliberately or unintentionally left unaware or out of the loop of important knowledge known to others (Jones et al., 2009). Although typically studied in the context of communication breakdowns or group performance, information exclusion shares important features with recognized ostracism modes, including its subtlety, attributional ambiguity, and capacity to threaten belonging and perceived status (Jones et al., 2009; Jones & Kelly, 2010; Jones et al., 2021). Unlike social ostracism, where interaction is withheld entirely, information exclusion involves selective engagement where targets may be included in conversation but excluded from the informational content that allows full participation. Future research could explore whether information exclusion is both conceptually distinct from other ostracism modalities (Jones et al., 2021) and whether it interacts with them to intensify negative outcomes. Positioning information exclusion as a complementary modality may help expand existing models of ostracism and shed light on the diverse ways individuals are marginalized in organizational settings.

Conclusion

In sum, this chapter advances the conceptualization of workplace ostracism by arguing for a multimodal perspective that recognizes the distinct yet interconnected forms through which exclusion is enacted and experienced. By examining physical, social, cyber, and linguistic ostracism in relation to one another, we highlight how these modalities vary in visibility, intentionality, and impact, while collectively threatening employees' fundamental psychological needs. Linguistic ostracism, in particular, introduces a vital aspect to this framework by highlighting how language influences individuals' experiences of inclusion and exclusion in the workplace. As organizations become more diverse, distributed, and digitally connected, ostracism is increasingly likely to occur across multiple modalities, sometimes simultaneously. Viewing ostracism through this layered framework allows researchers to develop more precise theories, supports practitioners in creating more inclusive interventions, and strengthens the legitimacy of a multi-modal understanding of workplace ostracism.
