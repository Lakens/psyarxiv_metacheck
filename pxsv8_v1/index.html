Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

research_questions Research question: Does ability grouping influence how adolescents think about peers and the school system?

SUMMARY OF THE STUDY Participants will see 3 animated videos that provide information about a fictional student who has a low, medium or high score on a test. This information is accompanied by an ability group (low, medium, high) or not (between-subjects). Next, participants will answer questions about this peer and the school system (e.g., endorsement of the use of ability grouping in schools).

Hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1
We hypothesize that participants are more willing to meet up with (1a) and feel closer to (1b) targets who obtained a high score compared to targets who obtained a low score and we expect that this effect is stronger when ability groups are salient (vs not salient; interaction performance and grouping for meeting up (1c) and social closeness (1d)).

Hypothesis 2
We hypothesize that participants’ own ability group influences their willingness to meet up and feelings of social closeness: When ability groups are salient (vs. not salient) we hypothesize that adolescents are more willing to meet up (2a) and feel closer to (2b) peers who are in their ability group compared to peers who are not.

Hypothesis 3

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Lay summary

Schools worldwide sort children into ability groups to improve achievement, but little research has tested how this affects children's social lives. We showed Dutch adolescents (10-18 years) short animated stories about peers who did well, intermediate, or less well at school. Some adolescents saw peers described only by their performance, others saw peers who were also labeled with an ability group. Without labels, performance did not change how lower-and intermediate-achieving adolescents felt about peers. With labels, these adolescents often felt less close to peers in other ability groups and adolescents of all groups were more likely to defend a school system with ability grouping. Ability grouping may thus create social divides and reduce contact between children from different backgrounds.

The Social Consequences of Ability Grouping in Schools

Schools worldwide group children according to their presumed ability. Schools and teachers use ability grouping because it allows them to offer tailored education, which they assume fosters learning and achievement (Francis et al., 2020; Jerrim et al., 2024; Johnston et al., 2024). Psychological research, however, has shown that group distinctions have pervasive consequences for children's social cognition and behavior. Yet, little research has addressed how ability grouping causally impacts social processes at school (Papachristou et al., 2022; Terrin & Triventi, 2023). Integrating educational, sociological, and psychological research, the current preregistered experimental study aims to fill that gap. Dutch adolescents (10-18 years) were introduced to peers with different achievement levels at school. We manipulated whether their achievement was accompanied by an ability group label (low, intermediate, high) and assessed whether ability group labels changed adolescents' social preferences (i.e., feelings of social closeness, willingness to meet up), their predictions about contact between peers, and their attitude toward school systems that use ability grouping.

Ability Grouping

Ability grouping is implemented worldwide but can take different forms (Francis et al., 2020; see Sierksma & Brummelman, 2026). When children are younger (i.e., primary school), ability grouping happens within classrooms, often by assigning children to high, intermediate, or low-ability groups for mathematics and literacy. When children are older (i.e., secondary school onwards), schools can assign children to different tracks for different courses, or to an ability group for all courses (e.g., vocational vs. academic). Children in different ability groups sometimes go to separate schools. Other times they go to the same school but are housed in different classrooms or parts of the building. Despite these different forms, there is a common denominator: Children are grouped based on prior achievement or presumed abilities.

Educational and sociological research on the impact of ability grouping has predominantly focused on two core outcomes: achievement and self-views. Research focused on the impact on achievement shows ability grouping often does not affect it (Jerrim et al., 2024; Terrin & Triventi, 2023) or that it lowers achievement of children assigned to lowability groups (Allende et al., 2024; Deunk et al., 2018; Francis et al., 2020; OECD, 2016; Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010). Research focused on the impact on self-views shows that ability grouping can benefit self-views of children in high-ability groups, but harm those assigned to low-ability groups (Chmielewski et al., 2013; Francis et al., 2017; Marks, 2013; McGillicuddy & Devine, 2020). The opposite can also occur: Children assigned to lowability groups feel more positive about their ability because these groups more readily allow for downward comparisons (i.e., Big-Fish-Little-Pond-Effect; Fleischmann et al., 2023; Marsh, 1987; Mulkey et al., 2005; Wan et al., 2024). Overall, research to date makes clear that ability grouping can lead to intrapersonal changes in achievement and self-views.

Psychological Theory

Drawing on social-and developmental-psychological research on grouping, we propose that ability grouping may also change interpersonal processes at school. When children are assigned to an ability group, this introduces a new group distinction in their daily life. Extensive psychological research shows that group boundaries profoundly change social cognition and behavior (e.g., Dunham, 2018; Rhodes & Baron, 2019). Starting at ages 3 to 5, children prefer those who belong to higher-status groups or their in-group (Shutts, 2015).

And even novel, seemingly unimportant groups, such as those based on T-shirt color, bias who children like or how they perceive them (Dunham, 2018).

Why do group distinctions change social cognition and behavior? One prominent account holds that group distinctions give rise to social essentialism (Gelman, 2003; Rhodes & Moty, 2020), the belief that groups share a stable, underlying essence. Social essentialism entails that people (including children) believe groups are fixed and membership is caused by internal factors. Essentialism also fosters the belief that people within a group are fundamentally alike and categorically distinct from members of other groups (Rangel & Keller, 2011). Essentialism emerges early. By age 3, children already treat animals and gender as having underlying essences, and with age this reasoning can extend to race and ethnicity (Diesendruck et al., 2013; Gelman, 2003; Rhodes & Moty, 2020). Salient group labels, perceptual markers and generic language foster essentialism in children (Birnbaum et al., 2010; Gelman & Heyman, 1999; Rhodes et al., 2012; Segall et al., 2015). These processes are often present in schools that use ability grouping. Students in the same ability group are, for example, taught in separate classrooms or parts of the building, making group membership visible during the school day. Ability groups are also commonly labeled (e.g., "top set", "set 3"). Ability grouping in secondary schools therefore creates conditions under which essentialism is likely to develop (see also Sierksma & Brummelman, 2026). Emerging evidence indeed suggests that children may essentialize ability groups. One study found that U.S. children (6-11 years) think that when peers are assigned to ability groups, this is because of internal and stable causes, and that changing ability groups is difficult (Muradoglu et al., 2026).

Building on this work, we postulate that ability grouping may also change children's social preferences, predictions of contact between peers, and their attitudes about ability grouping. There is robust evidence that (salient) group boundaries and social essentialism lead children to prefer in-group over out-group peers and foster a belief that members of a group are alike (Dunham, 2018; Rhodes & Baron, 2019; Rizzo et al., 2022). A striking example of this tendency is shown by a study (Bigler et al., 1997) in which U.S. children (6-9 years) were assigned to 'meaningless' color groups when they attended a summer school. In the experimental conditions, teachers made functional use of the groups (e.g., assigning tasks based on groups; resembling ability-grouped schools). After four weeks, children in the experimental conditions saw individual members of the same color group as more similar to each other, and members of different color groups as more dissimilar. Children also liked peers in their in-group more and assigned more positive traits to them.

Existing Evidence on the Social Impact of Ability Grouping

Existing empirical research concurs with the idea that ability grouping has social consequences. Large-scale sociological studies suggest that ability grouping influences how and whether peers like each other (Buchmann & Dalton, 2002; Lorenz et al., 2020). Belgian adolescents report, for example, that most of their friends are from their own ability group, few friends are from other groups, and that they like their own group most (Dekeyser et al., 2024a; Spruyt & Van Droogenbroeck, 2025). And in qualitative studies, children also express liking their group most and say things like "I don't really think about going into a different class-I like my class" (Hallam et al., 2004) or that moving to another ability group would make them feel "out of their comfort" (Francis et al., 2020, p. 127).

These studies, however, cannot establish the causal impact of ability grouping on interpersonal processes: They lack control conditions in which grouping is absent, and allocation to ability groups is not randomized, which means selection effects influence outcomes (i.e., ability groups are confounded with other differences; Papachristou et al., 2022; Wan et al., 2024). Our approach addresses this issue. We randomly assigned adolescents (enrolled in secondary school) to a condition in which a peer's ability group was made salient or one in which it was not. Of course, our data cannot speak to how assigning adolescents to ability groups changes interpersonal processes. But the study does allow us to isolate the causal effect of ability group salience on how children think about their peers and ability grouping in general.

Social Preferences, Contact Predictions, and Attitudes About Ability Grouping

We formulated four hypotheses grounded in psychological theory on grouping and essentialism. First, adolescents might like higher-achieving peers more in general, as research suggests higher-status and competent peers are often liked most (Palacios et al., 2024; Shutts, 2015; Wentzel et al., 2021). Salient ability group labels might amplify this tendency, as they make group status unambiguous and could trigger negative stereotypes about low-ability groups (Bardach et al., 2023). Second, we expected that adolescents' own ability group would influence their social preferences. Specifically, essentialism and salient group boundaries foster in-group bias (Rhodes & Baron, 2019; Rhodes & Moty, 2020) and thus ability grouping may lead adolescents to feel closer and more willing to meet up with peers in their in-group compared to those in their out-group.

Turning from adolescents' own preferences to their beliefs about others, our third hypothesis is based on research that suggests salient group boundaries and essentialism lead children to think group members are alike (Rhodes & Baron, 2019; Rhodes & Moty, 2020).

As a consequence, adolescents may, independent of their own ability group, be more likely to predict that peers want to meet up with in-group peers compared to out-group peers when ability group labels are salient.

We also assessed the impact of ability group labels on adolescents' attitudes toward school systems that use ability grouping. Grouping and essentialism often make people more willing to keep the status quo (Jost, 2020; Kraus & Keltner, 2013; Roberts et al., 2017). Adults, for example, who endorse essentialist views are also more in favor of policies that perpetuate social hierarchies (Mandalaywala et al., 2018; Roberts et al., 2017). And when U.S. children (4-5 and 7-8 years) think that status differences between novel groups have internal causes, a prime feature of essentialism, they evaluate disparities as more fair (Hussak & Cimpian, 2015). Ability grouping and essentialism might thus not only impact how adolescents feel about peers but also how they feel about the practice itself. Our fourth hypothesis states therefore that when group labels are salient, adolescents may see a grouped system as better or more efficient than schools that do not use grouping. We also explored the reasons adolescents gave for their attitudes toward ability grouping and examined the impact of their own ability group, as adolescents in high-ability groups might be more motivated to uphold a social structure that favors them (Jost, 2020; Kraus & Keltner, 2013; Rizzo et al., 2022; Schenk et al., 2026; Srivastava et al., 2025).

The Present Research

Does ability grouping influence how adolescents think about peers and the school system? We assessed this question in Dutch adolescents aged 10 to 18 years enrolled in secondary school. In the Netherlands, children are allocated to ability groups when they start secondary school. Assignment is based on a final test that children take at the end of primary school (10-12 years) and teacher advice. There are three main ability groups: VMBO (preparatory vocational, lower achievement), HAVO (senior general, intermediate achievement) and VWO (university preparatory, higher achievement). Children typically remain in the same ability group until the end of secondary school (i.e., age 16 to 18).

Adolescents saw an animated vignette which introduced them to a peer who either performed well, intermediate, or less well at school (within-subjects). We manipulated whether achievement levels were accompanied by ability group labels used in the Dutch system. Importantly, we manipulated not only ability group labels but also achievement levels. This allows us to establish the causal impact of ability group labels beyond achievement differences (i.e., a low-ability group also conveys lower achievement, a highability group also conveys high achievement). Afterwards, we assessed adolescents' social preferences, predictions of contact between peers, and their attitude toward ability-grouped classrooms. For social preferences, we included measures that assessed willingness to meet up and social closeness, to tap into adolescents' concrete action tendencies as well as their more general feelings.

Method Participants

Data collection was conducted by a survey company (Ipsos I&O) for a set period, and we preregistered that we would include all adolescents who wanted to participate during that time, with a minimum of 300. A total of 338 adolescents participated, and 6 were excluded because they were not enrolled in secondary school. Our final sample (N = 332) included adolescents between 10 and 18 years old (M = 14.55, SD = 1.95; 51.2% were boys, 47.6% girls, 0.9% non-binary, and 0.3% specified being genderfluid). Participants were from all educational levels (ability groups: 41.3% VWO (high), 21.7% HAVO (intermediate), 22.9% VMBO (low), 1.5% vocational track below VMBO, 12.7% mixed level) and were relatively evenly distributed over grades (22.9% year 1, 16.6% year 2, 17.2% year 3, 19.9% year 4, 12.3% year 5, 11.1% year 6). Our sample was predominantly Dutch: 97.3% indicated being born in the Netherlands. The median net income of caretaker 1 (n = 294) was €2500 to €2999 (n = 294) and for caretaker 2 (n = 259) €3000 to €3499 (median gross income in the Netherlands in 2024 was €3225 per month; CBS, 2026).

Educational level for caretaker 1 was 0.6% primary school, 11.4% high school, 25% secondary vocational education, 33.1 Bachelor's degree, and 27.7% Master's degree or Ph.D (2.1% missing). For caretaker 2 educational level was 0.6% primary school, 9.6% high school, 32.8% secondary vocational education, 19% Bachelor's degree, and 28.3% Master's degree or Ph.D (9.6% missing). We conducted power sensitivity analyses. For the simple regression we used an analytic approach with the pwr package (Champely, 2006). For the mixed models, we used simulation-based power analyses with the simr package (Green & MacLeod, 2015; 1000 simulations). These showed we had sufficient power to detect small to medium effects for all main effects and interactions (see Supplemental materials and code on ResearchBox). The study was approved by the ethics board of the university where this research was conducted (nr. 25-0484).

Transparency and Openness

We preregistered our study design, hypotheses, and data analysis plans on OSF, https://osf.io/cs372/overview?view_only=61eee4b9fe9e451e96b9ab9a0c9fb0f2, and we made our materials, data, and code available via ResearchBox at https://researchbox.org/11042 (CODE: UCGCUT).

Design

A 2 (grouping: yes vs no; between-subjects) by 3 (peer performance: high, intermediate, low; within-subjects, order randomized) design was used. The targets were gender matched to participants' gender (adolescents who indicated non-binary or 'other' were randomly assigned to a male or female version). Names of targets (females: Isa, Roos, Tess; males: Stan, Tim, Luc) and performance level were counterbalanced.

Procedure and Measures

Participants completed the study at home on their phone or laptop. This study was part of a larger project; we only discuss (preregistered) variables and analyses relevant to this study. Participants saw three animated videos (~30 sec.) in which we introduced them to a peer (see Figure 1). They saw a hand drawing an animated character and read: "This is [name target]. When she/he was in 6th grade in elementary school, she/he scored [150, 250, 380] out of 400 on the final test. She/he is now attending secondary school [and in a low/intermediate/high-ability group]." To manipulate performance level, we used fictitious scores (150, 250, 380) that do not match real-life test scores in the Netherlands. To manipulate ability groups, we used labels that secondary schools in the Netherlands use: VMBO for low ability, HAVO for intermediate ability, and VWO for high ability. After each video, participants answered questions about social closeness, willingness to meet up and they predicted contact between peers (order randomized). At the end of the experiment, adolescents' attitude toward ability grouping was assessed. We also assessed adolescents' essentialist ideas about ability groups (see supplemental materials), but exploratory analyses showed no consistent impact. We therefore do not include the measure here. Answers were provided on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 'certainly not' (1) to 'certainly' (5).

Social Closeness

To measure social closeness, we used the Inclusion of the Other in Self (IOS) scale (Aron et al., 1992). We told participants: "How do you see [name peer] and yourself? Choose the image below that best matches your answer." The answer scale consisted of 7 options with two increasingly overlapping circles, one of the circles had the word "me" in it and the other circle the name of the peer (see ResearchBox). Higher scores represent feeling closer to the peer.

Predicting Contact Between Peers

To measure how ability grouping influenced adolescents' thinking about peer relations in general, we asked them to predict, for each target, how likely it was that they would meet up with low-, intermediate-, and high-achieving peers ("Do you think [name target] likes to meet up with students who scored around [150/250/380; order randomized]?"

In the grouping condition, performance levels were accompanied by an ability group label (i.e., VMBO, HAVO, VWO). The three questions were answered on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 'certainly not' (1) to 'certainly' (5).

Attitude Toward Ability Grouping

To assess whether ability group labels change how adolescents think about ability grouping, they read: "(name peers) secondary school is considering doing away with separate classes for students who scored low, intermediate, or high on the final test in 6th grade. Instead, they want students scoring low, intermediate, or high [or: in the low-, intermediate-, high-ability group] to be in the same class for all subjects." Adolescents then answered whether they thought that was a good idea (5-point Likert scale ranging from 'certainly not' (1) to 'certainly' ( 5)).

We also asked participants to explain their answer (open-ended).

Adolescents' answers were coded into 10 categories by two coders (see Table 1). Coders could assign multiple codes per answer. There was substantial agreement between coders (κ = .795, p < .001), and we therefore used the coding of the first coder in our analyses.

Analyses

Analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 2026.01.0-392). We used the following packages: haven (Wickham et al., 2015), dplyr (Wickham et al., 2014), psych (Revelle, 2007), tidyverse (Wickham et al., 2019), lme4 (Bates et al., 2015), lmerTest (Kuznetsova et al., 2017), emmeans (Lenth & Piaskowski, 2017), readxl (Wickham & Bryan, 2015), and irr (Gamer et al., 2005). Willingness to meet up and social closeness were analyzed with mixed linear models, including a random intercept for participant identity to account for conditions nested within participants (Muradoglu et al., 2023); attitudes toward ability grouping were analyzed with simple linear regression.

For willingness to meet up and social closeness, models included ability grouping (grouping = 1, no grouping = -1) and performance (dummy-coded, low performance as reference) as fixed predictors. To examine the influence of participants' own ability group, we also ran separate models for ability groups (low, intermediate, high), using participants' own ability group as reference category. For predicting contact between peers, we ran the same models but matched the performance reference category to the vignette peer, allowing us to compare predicted contact between similar-and dissimilar-performing peers. For attitude toward ability grouping, we used simple linear regression with ability grouping (grouping = 1, no grouping = -1) as the predictor.

Exploratory analyses examined the influence of participants' own ability group on predicting contact between peers (separate models by ability group) and attitudes toward ability grouping (dummy-coded, low-ability group as reference). And, using logistic regression, we explored whether ability group labels influenced adolescents' justifications for their attitudes toward ability grouping. Results

Willingness To Meet Up

Results for willingness to meet up are reported in Table 2 and Figure 2. In general, adolescents were less willing to meet up with a low-performing peer compared to highperforming (p = .002) and intermediate-performing (p = .009) peers. Ability group labels did not influence whether adolescents wanted to meet up with low-compared to intermediateperforming peers (p = .669). But ability group labels did influence whether adolescents wanted to meet up with low-compared to high-performing peers (p = .004). Follow-up analyses showed that when group labels were salient, adolescents were equally willing to meet up with high-and low-performing targets (b = -0.01, SE = 0.09, p = .996, 95% CI [-0.22, 0.20]). When group labels were absent, adolescents were more willing to meet up with high-performing than low-performing peers (b = -0.38, SE = 0.09, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.60, -0.17]).

Impact of Adolescents' Own Ability Group.

To examine how adolescents' own ability group influenced their willingness to meet up, we ran separate models for participants in low-(n = 75), intermediate-(n = 70) and high-(n = 137) ability groups. Peers' performance levels in the videos influenced willingness to meet up in each ability group.

Adolescents in low-ability groups were less willing to meet up with high-performing (p < .001) than low-performing peers, but did not differentiate between low-and intermediateperforming peers (p = .169). Adolescents in intermediate-ability groups were less willing to meet up with low-performing than intermediate-performing peers (p = .032), but did not differentiate between intermediate-and high-performing peers (p = .812). Adolescents in high-ability groups were less willing to meet up with low-performing (p < .001) and intermediate-performing (p < .001) peers compared to high-performing peers. Thus, adolescents wanted to meet up most with peers who had similar performance levels to them. Did the presence of ability group labels amplify this tendency? For adolescents in low-ability groups, there were significant interactions for ability grouping and performance (low vs. intermediate, p = .028; low vs. high, p = .001). Follow-up analyses showed when group labels were absent, adolescents in low-ability groups were equally willing to meet up with low-, intermediate-, and high-performing peers (low vs. intermediate, b = -0.12, SE = 0.22, p = .854, 95% CI [-0.62, 0.39]; low vs. high, b = 0.20, SE = 0.22, p = .615, 95% CI [-0.31, 0.71]). However, when ability groups were salient, adolescents in low-ability groups were more willing to meet up with low-achieving peers compared to intermediate-and highachieving peers (low vs. intermediate, b = 0.50, SE = 0.17, p = .013, 95% CI [0.09, 0.91]; low vs. high, b = 1.11, SE = 0.17, p < .001, 95% CI [0.70, 1.52]).

For adolescents in intermediate-ability groups, there was no significant interaction for grouping and low vs. intermediate performance (p = .096). The interaction for grouping and high vs. intermediate performance was significant (p = .049), but none of the follow-up tests for this interaction reached significance (all p's > .198), although adolescents were marginally more willing to meet up with intermediate-performing peers when ability group labels were salient compared to when they were not (b = 0.45, SE = 0.24, p = .057, 95% CI [-0.01, 0.92]).

For adolescents in high-ability groups, the salience of group labels did not change their willingness to meet up (high vs. intermediate, p = .240; high vs. low, p = .435). Thus, adolescents in high-ability groups were more willing to meet up with similar-achieving peers regardless of whether group labels were present.

Social Closeness

Results for social closeness are reported in Table 2 and Figure 2. In general, adolescents felt less close to low-performing peers than high-performing (p < .001) and intermediate-performing (p < .001) peers. The salience of ability groups did not influence how close adolescents felt toward low vs. intermediate-performing peers (p = .770). Ability group labels did influence how close adolescents felt toward low vs. high-performing peers (p = .008). Follow-up analyses showed that when group labels were present, adolescents felt closer to high-performing than low-performing peers (b = -0.39, SE = 0.08, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.58, -0.19]) and this pattern was even stronger when group labels were not salient (b = -0.70, SE = 0.09, p < .001, 95% CI [-0.90, -0.50]).

Impact of Adolescents' Own Ability Group.

We then conducted analyses to understand the impact of adolescents' own ability group on social closeness. Performance information influenced social closeness. Adolescents in low-ability groups felt closer to lowperforming than high-performing peers (p = .008), but did not differentiate between intermediate-and low-performing peers (p = .225). Adolescents in intermediate-ability groups felt closer to intermediate-performing peers than low-performing peers (p = .003), but did not differentiate between intermediate-and high-performing peers (p = .741).

Adolescents in high-ability groups felt closer to high-performing peers compared to low-(p < .001) and intermediate-performing (p < .001) peers.

The presence of group labels changed feelings of social closeness for adolescents in low-and intermediate-ability groups. For adolescents in low-ability groups, there was a significant interaction for grouping and high vs. low performance (p <.001). Follow-up analyses showed that when group labels were absent, adolescents felt equally close to lowand high-performing peers (b = -0.11, SE = 0.19, p = .831, 95% CI [-0.55, 0.33]). But when group labels were salient, adolescents felt closer to low-performing than high-performing peers (b = 0.76, SE = 0.15, p < .001, 95% CI [0.40, 1.11]). The interaction for grouping and intermediate (vs. low) performance was not significant (p = .078).

For adolescents in intermediate-ability groups, there were significant interactions for ability grouping and performance (low vs. intermediate, p = .029; high vs. intermediate, p = .011). Follow-up analyses showed that when group labels were absent, adolescents felt equally close to intermediate-vs. low-performing (b = 0.09, SE = 0.14, p = .813, 95% CI [-0.25, 0.42]) and intermediate vs. high-performing peers (b = -0.32, SE = 0.14, p = .067, 95% CI [-0.66, 0.02]). When group labels were salient, however, adolescents felt closer to intermediate-compared to low-performing peers (b = 0.58, SE= 0.17, p = .003, 95% CI [0.18, 0.98]), but did not differentiate between intermediate-and high-performing peers (b = 0.25, SE = 0.17, p = .314, 95% CI [-0.15, 0.65]).

For adolescents in high-ability groups, group labels did not change how close they felt to low-performing (p = .435) or intermediate-performing (p = .236) peers. Findings thus show that adolescents in low-and intermediate-ability groups feel less close to peers in other ability groups when ability group labels are salient. In contrast, adolescents in high-ability groups feel closer to high-performing peers regardless of group labels.

Predicting Contact Between Peers

Performance information influenced adolescents' predictions of contact between peers (see Table 3). Adolescents expected low-performing peers to be more willing to meet up with low-performing peers compared to intermediate-(p < .001) and high-performing (p < .001) peers. For intermediate-performing peers, they predicted more willingness to meet up with intermediate-performing peers compared to low-(p < .001) and high-performing (p < .001) peers. And for high-performing peers, they predicted more willingness to meet up with highperforming peers compared to intermediate-(p < .001) and low-performing (p < .001) peers.

Group labels did not change adolescents' contact predictions for low-and highperforming peers (all ps > .076). For intermediate-performing peers, there was a significant interaction for grouping and high (vs. intermediate) performance (p = .048). Discussion

Ability grouping is omnipresent across the industrialized world. Yet, research suggests that it can backfire and lead to detriments in children's academic achievement and self-views (Francis et al., 2020). Why that is, is not clear. Insight into the interpersonal consequences of ability grouping is a necessary but often overlooked component to understanding why ability grouping is not always effective. Here we show that the simple act of adding an ability group label to descriptions of peers or the school system radically changes adolescents' social preferences and thinking about ability grouping. This speaks to the powerful role of ability grouping in shaping adolescents' social life and has important implications for schools and teachers.

Ability Grouping and Social Relations

Ability group labels changed adolescents' social preferences. Without ability group labels, a peer's achievement did not impact how close children in low-ability and intermediate-ability groups felt toward them. When ability group labels were salient, however, this shifted. Specifically, adolescents in low-ability groups felt less close to peers in high-ability groups and were less willing to meet up with peers in high-and intermediateability groups. Adolescents in intermediate-ability groups felt less close to peers in lowability groups and were somewhat more willing to meet up with peers in intermediate-ability groups. Thus, even though peers clearly differed in achievement, achievement alone often did not drive adolescents' social preferences. This provides causal evidence that ability grouping changes how children relate to one another, and may lead to segregated social networks, in particular when children belong to low and intermediate-ability groups.

Another striking finding is that ability group labels changed how children thought in general about the practice itself. When we introduced them to the idea that schools would no longer separate children based on their achievement (group), they opposed that idea more when ability group labels were present and justified their opinion by referring to differences in intelligence. Ability group labels may thus have led adolescents to view achievement differences as having deep, internal causes (Gelman, 2003; Rhodes & Moty, 2020). This is also reflected in their justifications: "the difference between the low and high ability group is huge, they can't go together", "the least intelligent person will drag the group down", and "then the high ability group starts acting bossy, the ability groups become insecure, and things turn into a mess." These findings support the notion that ability grouping fosters essentialism and the belief that members of the ability groups are more alike and do not mix well (Rhodes & Moty, 2020).

Our research thus suggests that ability group labels can foster achievement-based social segregation. Students assigned to different ability groups, however, do not only differ in achievement. Research shows that children from disadvantaged backgrounds (i.e., lower socioeconomic and immigration background) are overrepresented in low-ability groups (Hallam & Parsons, 2013; Hartas, 2018; Moller & Stearns, 2012; Muijs & Dunne, 2010; Van de Werfhorst & Mijs, 2010). If ability grouping creates divides between students who differ in achievement, it may thus also reduce willingness for contact between children belonging to different societal groups. Contact is one of the most effective ways to reduce prejudice and stereotypes (Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). Salient ability group boundaries could thus have negative downstream consequences beyond the classroom and decrease societal cohesion.

Our findings also connect meaningfully to issues in educational inequality.

Specifically, disparities in educational performance related to socio-economic status or immigration background take root at an early age and widen throughout development (Brummelman et al., 2024; OECD, 2016). Adjusting education to children's ability is often implemented to reduce such inequality. But if ability grouping leads to segregated social networks and system-justifying tendencies in youth, it may amplify negative stereotypes about low-ability groups and promote social exclusion (Hussak & Cimpian, 2015; Roberts et al., 2017). Social exclusion and negative stereotypes, in turn, can lead to underperformance, reduced motivation, and feelings of loneliness and anxiety (Brummelman & Sedikides, 2023; Ladd & Kochenderfer-Ladd, 2016; Rubin et al., 2006). Interpersonal changes set in motion by ability grouping may thus exacerbate achievement inequality rather than reduce it.

High-Ability Groups and Third-Party Predictions

Adolescents in high-ability groups consistently preferred high-performing peers and were unaffected by ability group labels. Why? Assignment to a high ability group is perceived positively by adolescents (Houtte et al., 2012; McGillicuddy & Devine, 2020) and, as people are generally inclined to see themselves in a positive light (Sedikides et al., 2021), doing well at school may be more central to their self-concept (Pop et al., 2016). Research suggests that when traits are central to a person's self-concept, they impact social perceptions more readily than when traits are peripheral (Sedikides et al., 2021; Sedikides & Skowronski, 1993). Thus, we speculate that for adolescents with high achievement levels at school, achievement more readily colors how they see others, as compared to adolescents in low-or intermediate-ability groups, for whom achievement is a less important dimension (Dekeyser et al., 2024b).

Adolescents' third-party predictions about the social preferences of their peers were impacted by achievement: They expected that similar-achieving peers were more likely to meet up. In contrast to our hypotheses, however, ability group labels did not amplify this tendency. One reason for this may be methodological. The questions we used to predict contact always included the exact score each peer had. Adolescents may thus have used a simple similarity heuristic (Laursen, 2017), and group labels added very little to this tendency. It could also be that adolescents are somewhat aware of the tension and negative stereotypes about ability group labels (Bardach et al., 2023) and that, when thinking about others, they were inclined to provide non-stereotypical, socially desirable answers. Indeed, they sometimes said similar-achieving peers would be less likely to meet up when vignettes included ability group labels versus when they did not. We recommend that future studies aim to replicate the findings with more ambiguous portrayals of peers, which could reduce adolescents' concern for social desirability.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

Our research has several strengths, including its experimental design, a comprehensive set of dependent variables (i.e., first-person, third-person, system-view), and a wide age range. Our research also has limitations, which point to important directions for future work.

We studied intentions and perceptions and focused on ability grouping at secondary school in the Netherlands. An important next step is to examine actual behavior and real interactions between children of different achievement levels and groups across diverse cultural contexts. Experimental virtual reality paradigms are well suited to this goal, as they combine ecological validity with high experimental control (Schenker et al., 2026). Such studies should also more closely examine the type of school that adolescents go to. Ability grouping can be implemented between-or within-schools, and in the latter, students assigned to different groups have more opportunities for contact (Blanco-Varela et al., 2024; Eikeland & Ohna, 2022), which often reduces group-based biases (Davies et al., 2011; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006). However, when all ability groups are housed in the same school, it could also make group distinctions more salient, leading to stronger biases (Houtte et al., 2012; Sierksma & Brummelman, 2026). Similarly, growing up in more unequal societies fosters competitiveness (Sommet et al., 2023) and thus makes achievement differences highly salient regardless of group labels. Exploring these contextual factors is important to understand when ability group labels are more and less influential in shaping social relations.

Another important next step is to examine underlying mechanisms and developmental change. We propose that ability grouping promotes social essentialism (Sierksma & Brummelman, 2026) but did not test it directly. Previous studies suggest that children perceive ability groups as having an internal cause and as stable (Muradoglu et al., 2026), but future studies should test whether they also perceive less variability within groups compared to between groups (Rhodes & Moty, 2020) and whether such essentialist notions set in motion group-based biases. Testing young children, preferably before they have been assigned to ability groups, could also help us better understand the changes ability group assignment sets in motion.

Practical Implications and Conclusion

Schools and teachers who aim to promote an inclusive school environment could work toward making ability groups less salient. They may, for example, avoid using ability group labels during instruction and make them less visible (i.e., in hallways, learning materials; see also Sierksma & Brummelman, 2026). Making group distinctions less salient may also be achieved by fostering contact and collaboration between children placed in different ability groups (Allport, 1954). Schools could, for example, teach children assigned to different ability groups in the same classroom and at comprehensive schools (Van de Werfhorst, 2018). This might reduce essentialist notions and signal that group boundaries are malleable and groups heterogeneous.

Research to date has mostly examined the intrapersonal impact of ability grouping.

Learning at school, however, is not an individual endeavor, and social processes are key to achievement and motivation (Duraiappah et al., 2021; Zimmer & Toma, 2000). Our research suggests that ability grouping fosters social segregation and leads adolescents to see the system that produces this segregation as more legitimate. Understanding these social consequences is essential for schools that want to tailor education to students' needs without deepening the divides between them.
