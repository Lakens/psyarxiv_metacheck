Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Reliability Estimation and Reporting Practices in Ambulatory Assessments and Other Intensive Longitudinal Designs: A Meta-Analysis

Reliability Estimation and Reporting Practices in Ambulatory Assessments and Other Intensive Longitudinal Designs: A Meta-Analysis including ecological momentary assessments, experience sampling studies, and daily diary studies -have become an increasingly popular tool in psychological research (Fritz et al., 2024).

Ambulatory Assessments (AA) and other intensive longitudinal designs (ILDs) -

While these designs have permeated various fields in psychological research (e.g. personality psychology, Conner et al., 2009; organizational psychology, Beal, 2015; developmental psychology, Neubauer & Schmiedek, 2020), they feature particularly prominently in fields of mental health and emotions (Löchner et al., 2026; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023). Part of this increase in popularity can likely be attributed to the growing awareness that the possibility of capturing thoughts, feelings, behaviors as they unfold in individuals' daily lives in or close to real time is invaluable to better understand psychological processes in ways that cannot be accomplished in single-occasion or laboratory-based assessments (Hamaker, 2012; Laurenceau et al., 2026; Reis, 2012; Schwarz, 2012).

To fully leverage the potential of these designs requires, however, that they can assess the constructs of interest in a psychometrically valid fashion. This includes the reliability of the measurements as well. Reliability, understood broadly as the proportion of true score variance in observed scores or the replicability of a measurement, is a foundational psychometric property of any measurement. In the context of ILDs, however, reliability poses challenges that are qualitatively different from those encountered in traditional, singleoccasion assessment. Specifically, the arguably most prominent reliability estimators (internal consistency estimators such as Cronbach's α or McDonald's ω) have not been developed specifically for use in ILDs and require adjustments before these estimates can be reasonably interpreted in this context (Geldhof et al., 2014; Lai, 2021). Importantly, the total variance in the observations in ILDs comprises variance between persons and variance within persons, which makes it necessary to report reliability by level of analysis.

Recently, Castro-Alvarez et al. (2026) have provided an overview of different internal consistency estimators, which is a highly valuable resource assisting empirical researchers to select the appropriate estimate for their specific need and apply the chosen estimator to their data. Still, with the large number of different possibilities, it might be challenging for researchers to keep track of the most appropriate estimate for a given context. This might be particularly relevant, as reporting the reliability of the measurement often boils down to reporting only the relevant estimates in the Methods section of an article, ostensibly without providing directly relevant information for the substantive conclusions drawn (which is typically the goal of the respective paper). Lacking awareness of the relevance of reporting reliability has been one of the most frequently reported reasons for researchers not reporting this estimate (Vogelsmeier et al., 2024). However, reliability is essential for interpreting results from the collected data as not accounting for psychometric issues can bias parameter estimates and thereby threaten the validity of the conclusions drawn (Schneider & Junghaenel, 2023; Siepe et al., 2026). Furthermore, from the perspective of building cumulative knowledge in science, reporting reliability estimates is invaluable in aiding future work to build on and improve measurement practices (Flake & Fried, 2020; Parsons et al., 2019).

What is currently lacking from the literature is an overview of the current state of the art of reporting practices of reliability estimates in ILDs across different fields of psychological constructs. In the present work, we report results from a meta-analysis of 500 empirical ILD articles published over 10 years to examine reporting practices and trends therein. With this study, we aim to provide an overview of reporting practices in a representative sample of studies using ILDs in heterogeneous contexts and a broad class of assessed constructs. We thereby extend pioneering work in this area that has focused on reliability reporting in subfields such as emotion research (Brose et al., 2020; Castro-Alvarez et al., 2026) or the assessment of personality states (Horstmann & Ziegler, 2020). In addition to examining whether reliability estimates are reported, we also examined the numerical estimates. Estimating Reliability in ILDs

The concept of reliability defined as the replicability of a measurement seems somewhat at odds with the key benefit of ILDs which focus on capturing changes in individuals' experiences (Kuppens et al., 2010). Consequently, test-retest reliability as a popular estimate of reliability in more traditional research designs lends itself less well in this context. It is worth pointing out, however, that test-retest procedures can provide valuable insight also into the reliability in ILD when applied within the same measurement occasion (i.e., repeating the same items within the same beep assessment or with a short delay; see Dejonckheere et al., 2022). Using this approach requires, however, that this retesting is explicitly implemented in the study design. In principle, then, estimating the test-retest correlation allows estimating the reliability of single item measures as well. This is particularly useful because the individual measurement occasions in ILDs are kept brief by necessity to keep participant burden manageable (Eisele et al., 2022). If no repeated measures within a given measurement occasion have been implemented, reliability could also be estimated in the context of a first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) model as shown by Schuurman & Hamaker (2019).

While appealing, these suggested reliability estimates are typically not featured outside the methodological literature: In a recent review of reliability reporting in 124 empirical articles assessing mood, affect or emotion (Castro-Alvarez et al., 2026), not a single article reported such a model-based reliability estimator. Instead, different internal consistency estimators were prominently represented in those articles. The most frequently reported indices of internal consistency are arguably Cronbach's α or McDonald's ω (Revelle & Condon, 2019). Multilevel extensions of internal consistency indices have been proposed to account for the hierarchical structure of repeated measurements (Geldhof et al., 2014; Lai, 2021). The rationale for these extensions is that (co)variances among repeated observations obtained in ILDs reflect variation at both the between-person and within-person levels.

Between-person variation reflects systematic differences between individuals (e.g., some individuals report higher scores on an item on average), whereas within-person variation reflects fluctuations around an individual's own typical level across measurement occasions.

Importantly, between-person and within-person (co)variances are statistically orthogonal and cannot be used interchangeably to infer associations at the other level (Molenaar, 2004; Voelkle et al., 2014). Consequently, reliability in ILDs should be considered separately at the within-person and between-person levels which can be done by using information on between-person and within-person variances and covariances (Geldhof et al., 2014; Lai, 2021). At the within-person level, internal consistency reflects the extent to which item scores covary in their temporal deviations from an individual's expected or typical level.

High within-person internal consistency would indicate that the items reliably capture this common source of temporal variation. At the between-person level, internal consistency reflects the extent to which individuals' average scores on one item covary with their average scores on the other items. High between-person internal consistency therefore indicates that individuals who report relatively high levels on one item across the observation period also tend to report relatively high levels on the remaining items.

Alternative approaches to reliability estimation have also been proposed for ILDs (Castro-Alvarez et al., 2026). One of the earliest approaches was introduced by Cranford et al. (2006) and is based on Generalizability Theory (G-Theory; Brennan, 2001). From this perspective, an individual j's response to item i at measurement occasion t (Yᵢⱼt) is decomposed using a three-way analysis of variance model with person, time, and item as crossed factors. The variance components associated with these effects can be used to derive different reliability coefficients (see Cranford et al., 2006, for details). Alternatively, withinperson reliability can also be examined through variance decomposition in a three-level model. In this specification, items (Level 1) are nested within measurement occasions (Level 2), which are nested within individuals (Level 3; see Nezlek, 2017; Shrout & Lane, 2012).

While these different internal consistency estimators (Cronbach's alpha, McDonald's omega, G-Theory estimates, estimates based on a three-level variance decomposition) represent all estimates that were reported in the articles surveyed by Castro-Alvarez et al.

(2026), the authors highlight additional ways to estimate reliability in ILDs: P-Technique (with or without autoregressive effects; see Fuller-Tyszkiewicz et al., 2017), the mixed-effects trait-state-occasion model (ME-TSO; see Castro-Alvarez et al., 2022) and the two-level random dynamic model (2RDM; see Xiao et al., 2026). One key aspect differentiating these approaches from the internal consistency estimators is that they provide person-specific within-person reliability estimates. While advantageous on many levels, this does not appear to be a procedure that is implemented in empirical research papers (yet).

Reporting Practices in ILDs

The call to report psychometric information in empirical work is not new (e.g., Wilkinson, 1999). For the comparatively young field of ILDs, this has been echoed as well (McNeish, 2026; Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2020). However, prior review work suggests that this call has not been fully answered in most empirical articles. In their review, Brose et al. (2020) examined whether reliability estimates were reported in 50 articles published in Emotion between 2005 and 2017. Their results revealed that reliability estimates were reported in 29 (=58%) of all articles, but only in ten of these articles (20% of all included articles), the authors stated that the reported estimate refers to the level-specific (withinperson) reliability estimate. Castro-Alvarez et al. (2026) included 131 studies from 124 articles (published 2018 or later) in their analyses. They included only articles that assessed constructs related to affective well-being (emotion, positive affect, negative affect, mood).

Consistent with Brose et al. (2020), results showed that 73 studies (55.7%) reported reliability estimates, but in only 27 (=20.6% of all studies) an estimate was reported that accounts for the nested data structure. It is somewhat surprising that reliability reporting seems not to have increased over the years, in particular given that software code to obtain these estimates has become more available over the last decade. One aspect that complicates the comparison of reporting rates between Brose et al. (2020) and Castro-Alvarez et al. (2026) is that different populations of articles were included in the two reviews (only papers published in Emotion vs. a larger sample of ILD studies). In the present work, we aimed to target changes in reporting standards over time more directly, by drawing a large, representative sample of research articles using ILD that were published within the last decade. Furthermore, these two studies targeted specifically reporting of reliability of the assessment of affect. Although affective states are among the most frequently studied constructs in ILDs (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023) it remains unclear whether the previously found reliability reporting practices also generalize to other constructs that are assessed in ILDs. Horstmann and Ziegler (2020) surveyed research targeting personality states; six out of 24 studies (=25%) included in that review reported reliability separately by level of analysis, providing rather similar estimates to the field of emotion research. In research on psychosis, Bell et al. (2024) report in their meta-analysis (EMA studies with samples of > 50% individuals with diagnosed psychotic disorder), that 33 of the 68 included studies reported any data on reliability or validity, with 20 studies reporting internal consistency estimates. In that study, it was not further differentiated whether the reliability estimate was reported separately by level (within vs. between), however.

In sum, prior work examining reporting practices of reliability in ILDs suggests that about 50% of studies report some form of reliability index, yet only about 20-25% decompose reliability estimates into within-person and between-person components. The current work seeks to examine whether these numbers reported in specific subfield of psychological research (emotion research, clinical psychology, personality) generalize to a representative sample of ILD studies published between 2015 to 2024. Furthermore, we aim to investigate trends in reporting practices over time. We expect (preregistered Hypothesis 1) that the proportion of studies reporting reliability separately by level has increased over time, given the increasing availability of software and tutorials (e.g., McNeish, 2026; McNeish et al., 2021) that researchers have at their disposal to compute these estimates.

Reliability Estimates of Affective States

Affective states are an immensely important construct in ILDs: Wrzus and Neubauer (2023) showed that the study topic emotion was by far the most prevalent topic studied in the 477 articles included in their meta-analysis. In many studies, researchers assess positive affect with several positive emotion items (e.g., cheerful, happy, content), and negative affect using a set of negative emotion items (e.g., sad, angry, downhearted). Using several items per construct allows estimating reliability by means of internal consistency. Notably, regardless of the specific estimator, all internal consistency estimates share a fundamental assumption. Specifically, they are based on a reflective measurement model, in which a latent construct is conceptualized as the common cause of variation in the observed indicators. Under this model, low internal consistency may be interpreted as evidence that the measure does not reliably capture the underlying construct. Such an interpretation is warranted, however, only if the reflective measurement model provides an appropriate representation of the relation between the indicators and the construct. If this assumption is violated, internal consistency may provide misleading assessments of reliability, as imposing a reflective measurement model when it does not adequately represent the measurement process can result in inappropriate or misleading conclusions (Rhemtulla et al., 2020).

The appropriateness of a reflective measurement model might be somewhat questionable for negative affect on the within-person level. Suppose a researcher aims to investigate whether external events, such as minor daily stressors (Almeida, 2005), influence an individual's momentary negative affect (operationalized using several indicators, such as feeling angry, sad, tense, and frustrated). In a reflective measurement model, the indicators are assumed to be exchangeable manifestations of an underlying latent negative affective state.

Hence, increases in the latent state are expected to produce corresponding increases across the indicators, resulting in positive within-person covariation. For example, a momentary increase in sadness relative to an individual's typical level would be expected to coincide with increases in feeling angry, frustrated, and tense. This pattern, however, may not adequately characterize momentary affective experiences. Different events may elicit qualitatively different emotional responses. A traffic jam, for example, may increase anger without necessarily increasing sadness, whereas an interpersonal conflict may elicit frustration without producing a corresponding increase in other negative emotions. Accordingly, the individual indicators may exhibit only weak within-person associations at best. Under such circumstances, low internal consistency does not necessarily indicate poor reliability, but rather reflects the fact that the indicators do not share a common within-person source of variation. This distinction is critical because measurement error and lack of interitem covariation are conceptually different phenomena. If measurement error is negligible but the indicators represent distinct manifestations of momentary negative affect, an internal consistency coefficient may nevertheless be low. While in general the same issue of non-interchangeable items might also occur for positive affect, the issue of a non-reflective measurement model might be clearer for items assessing negative affect, as positive information in general is more similar to one another (more "dense"; Unkelbach et al., 2008).

Consequently, different positive emotions (cheerful, happy, content) might be clustered more strongly together and hence covary more strongly over time than different negative emotions (sad, angry, downhearted, frustrated).

These considerations tentatively suggest that within-person internal consistency estimates might be larger for scales assessing positive affect than for scales assessing negative affect (preregistered Hypothesis 2). If this is in fact due to a false assumption of a reflective measurement model, then adding more items to the scale would not improve internal consistency: In general, adding more items to a scale will increase the internal consistency as long as the items are all interchangeable (and therefore intercorrelated) indicators of the latent construct. However, if added items do not covary with the other items of the scale, this will not lead an increase in the internal consistency. We therefore predict that the positive effect of number of items per scale on the within-person internal consistency (preregistered Hypothesis 3a) will be smaller for negative affect than for positive affect (preregistered Hypothesis 3b).foot_1

The Present Study

In the present meta-analysis, we examined reporting practices in published, peerreviewed articles using ILDs. We targeted specifically studies in which self-report measures were assessed repeatedly in individuals' daily lives. In doing so, we assessed the current state of the field and identified the degree to which recommendations for reliability reporting (e.g., Trull & Ebner-Priemer, 2020) have been adopted. Specifically, we examined (a) which reliability estimators are actually being used, (b) whether researchers distinguish between within-person and between-person levels of analysis, and (c) whether this pattern has changed over time between 2015 and 2024. We further tested a set of preregistered hypotheses concerning the correlates of reported within-person reliability, including trends over time (H1), differences between positive and negative affect scales (H2), as well as the differential effect of the number of items on reliability for positive vs. negative affect (H3a, H3b).

At this point, about 300 articles of the main coding phase had been coded and the data had been accessed to spot-check potential issues in the data coding. Preliminary descriptive analyses had been conducted, but none of the preregistered analyses had been conducted. Search of Studies and Inclusion Criteria

Figure 1 depicts the process of searching and screening the eligible articles. We searched the databases PubMed, PsycArticles and PsycInfo for relevant articles published between 2015 and 2024. The search was conducted on January 18 2025 with the following search terms (in title, abstract or keywords): "ambulatory assessment" OR "experience sampling" OR "ESM" OR "ecological momentary" OR "EMA" OR "diary" OR "daily diary"

OR "e-diary" OR "intensive longitudinal" OR "micro-longitudinal" OR "micro longitudinal".

We limited the search to publication years 2015 -2024, and peer reviewed articles published in English, excluding dissertations. We further amended the search by including references reported in the quarterly literature review on ambulatory assessment research that is shared via the listserv of the Society for Ambulatory Assessment (SAA, e.g. as done by Ottenstein & Werner, 2022). In a next step, the abstracts were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: Empirical studies using ILD methods (e.g., ambulatory assessment, experience sampling, ecological momentary assessment, daily diary), operationally defined as studies in which at least one assessment per day over at least five days was taken or in which at least 20 assessments were taken within a period shorter than five days; Published between 2015 and 2024 in peer-reviewed journals; written in English; Reports on psychological constructs (operationally defined as at least one self-report measure) intensively over time. Exclusion criteria were specified as: Non-empirical papers (e.g., reviews, theoretical articles); metaanalyses; studies not employing ILD methods, not including human study participants or not reporting on psychological constructs; studies that do not contain at least one self-report measure assessed intensively over time; book chapters, dissertations, publication in non-peerreviewed journal; publications in languages other than English. Trained student research assistants and the authors of this work screened the abstracts via SyRF (Bahor et al., 2021).

For each, the title, authors, journal, and publication year were presented alongside the inclusion and exclusion criteria. For each presented abstract, the coders decided whether this publication should be included or excluded based on the information presented.

Given the large number of hits obtained, we deviated from the preregistration in that the screening was conducted by only one coder. We deemed this step necessary to complete the screening in time. Further, given the large sample and our preregistered choice to select a subsample of 500 articles from it (see below), erroneously excluding articles at this step seemed unproblematic as only a subsample of all eligible articles would make it to the final analytic sample. Erroneously including an article would be resolved in the data extraction step in which falsely included articles would be replaced during coding (as preregistered).

As the screening still took longer than anticipated, human screening of the abstracts was terminated after 8,783 abstracts (51.6%) had been screened. The remaining 8,222 abstracts were screened by a large language model (LLM) that had been trained on the human rated abstracts (see Henninger et al., 2026, for a tutorial on using LLMs for abstract screening). We fine-tuned PubMedBERT, a language model pretrained on biomedical abstracts (Gu et al., 2021). The model was trained to minimize false exclusions because losing a relevant study was considered more costly than retaining an irrelevant one, and it only issued a decision when it was confident; uncertain cases were returned to human coders. The LLM provided suggestions on a three-way scheme (exclude / review / include) for the remaining abstracts; of these, 4,021 (48.9%) were excluded, 2,423 (29.5%) were included, and 1,778 (21.6%) were routed to human reviewers. In an audit of the LLM's results, 302 abstracts were screened by humans. We found that the model wrongly excluded only one of 179 articles (0.6%) and wrongly included 2 of 112 abstracts (1.8%), and its disagreements with human coders more often revealed human coding errors than model errors. Full details on the LLM are reported in Online Supplement S1, and the code is available on https://github.com/jpsnijder/RILDA_LLM. This process resulted in a total sample of 6,339 eligible articles. As preregistered, we selected 500 articles randomly, stratified by publication year, from this full sample. We also selected an additional 50 articles that were used to train the coders. The remaining 5,789 articles served as potential replacements in case any of the 500 selected articles were deemed to be ineligible during the coding process. A total of 69 out of these 500 articles were replaced by other articles (published in the same year as the replaced article).

Coding of the Studies

Seven student assistants coded 481 articles, while the second author coded the remaining 19 articles. All coders received extensive training before commencing the coding of the 500 articles. Raters received the pdfs of the articles and a standardized coding manual with instructions on the information that should be extracted from each article. Entering this information was further aided by a standardized R script that asked for the relevant input and created a csv file with all relevant information for each article and coder.

Coder training

In a first training session (2 hours), the coders were trained on the type of information that they were asked to extract from the articles and how this information can be entered into the R script. This session was hosted by the authors of this work and also featured them reporting on the coding of three practice articles. This session and later training sessions were recorded so that raters could rewatch the training if necessary. Each rater then received three articles to code (one of which was coded by all raters). In the next training session (1 hour), raters' questions were discussed as were discrepancies between raters in the coding of the joint article, and 10 new articles per coder were assigned for practice (five articles were coded by all raters). The procedure was repeated for a third time; after the third training session, 10 new articles were assigned for practice per coder (five articles were coded by all raters) and questions were discussed in a last training session (1.5 hours) before the coding of the 500 main articles commenced.

Coding proper

The 500 articles were assigned to coders in batches of 10-20. In each batch, 1-3 of the assigned articles were assigned to all coders that were active at the time (due to personnel fluctuations, not all coders were active across the whole coding phase), resulting in 30 articles (6%) that received ratings from multiple coders and were used to estimate inter-rater reliability. Of the originally assigned articles, 69 were replaced because -after discussion between the rater and the authors -they turned out to be ineligible based on the inclusion criteria. After the rating phase had officially ended, 19 articles remained uncoded. These were then rated by the second author of this work (this step had not been preregistered).

Coded information

We developed a standardized coding manual (available at https://tinyurl.com/RILDAWP1) that explained the type of information that should be extracted from each article. This information can be grouped into three levels: information on the article level included for instance, title, publication year, article doi, and the number of studies reported in the article. The study level information comprised for instance the sample size, study duration and compliance. Within each study, several constructs can be reported and information on each construct was instructed to be coded, for instance, the construct name, the response scale used and -most important for the present work -which reliability estimate was reported for this construct.

In a first step, coders were prompted by the R script to enter the article information, the number of studies included in the article, and -for each study reported -the number of constructs and names for all constructs in all studies reported in this article. The R script then created a csv file with the appropriate number of columns and rows in which the coders were instructed to manually enter all the remaining information.

Article level Basic information.

A short title (the first three words of the title), author name (last name of the first author), the publication year and the article doi were coded in a first step.

The coders also entered their initials to match the codings to the coder.

Information to prepare the csv file. Coders entered the number of studies that were reported in the article. Next, the R script asked for each study for the number of constructs that were reported. Similar to our inclusion criteria, only constructs that were assessed in an intensive longitudinal fashion were relevant here (e.g., baseline measures that were assessed only once did not count towards the number of constructs). Next, the R script prompted the coders to enter the construct name for each construct in each study. Constructs should be named as they were named in the study.

Study level Sample information.

Coders entered information on the sample age (mean, SD, minimum, maximum), gender composition (percentage of participants identifying as female), and the included sample size. Additionally, the sample type was coded with the categories: student population; recruited via MTurk; general population; clinical population (if there was at least one clinical group recruited); and other. Finally, raters coded whether there was any indication that (part of) the data had been used in a previous publication (dichotomous variable: yes or no).

Study design.

Coders retrieved information on the number of study days, the intended number of assessments per day, the number of completed assessments per participant, the total number of assessments across all days and participants. As there is some heterogeneity with respect to which information on study duration and density is typically reported, we chose to offer different possibilities of coding this information. Coders were instructed to adapt the number of assessments per construct in case constructs were assessed with different frequencies (e.g., sleep quality might be assessed 1x per day, negative affect 5x per day).

Additionally, we assessed the reported compliance, if study participation was incentivized (categorical variable: no incentives; financial incentives (incl. vouchers); other incentive; unclear), if there was a compliance-based incentive (e.g., staggered incentives; dichotomous variable: yes or no). Finally, coders retrieved information on the total number of items per beep and the average completion time per beep.

Construct level

Of interest. For each construct in each study, coders rated first, whether the construct is of interest to the present meta-analysis. In the first step (see Information to prepare the csv file) every construct that was assessed in an intensive longitudinal fashion was entered. In this second step, only self-report measures of psychological constructs were relevant and should be coded further. Raters were given examples of constructs that are not of interest to this study: physiological parameters (e.g., movement, heart rate), sensor-based data (e.g. GPS, app usage data), or (self-report) data of objective characteristics of one's environment (e.g.: "are you currently alone or with others"? "Where are you currently"?). The variable of interest was dichotomous (yes vs. no). For all constructs that were not of interest, all other construct level information was not coded. The subsequent information was only coded for constructs of interest. Of the total 2,883 constructs, 2,436 (84.5%) were coded as "of interest" and only these constructs will be reported in this paper. In all 535 studies, there was at least one construct of interest reported (range: 1 -18, mean = 4.55, SD = 3.05, median = 4).

Assessment.

For each construct (of interest), the number of items, the number of reverse coded items, and the response scale (categorical variable: dichotomous, categorical, Likert scale, visual analogue scale (VAS) / slider, other) were coded. For Likert and VAS, the scale minimum and maximum were also coded. The time frame of the item instructions was assessed as a categorical variable with the categories "now" (e.g., how do you feel right now?), "hour" (e.g., how did you feel in the past hour?), "since the last assessment" (e.g., how did you feel since the last beep?), "day/night" (e.g. how did you feel today? How did you sleep last night?), "other", and "unclear". Lastly, coders assessed whether the construct was assessed using items / a scale as it had been used in prior work or not.

Sampling scheme.

We coded sampling scheme on the construct level rather than the study level, as it might differ among constructs within a study (e.g., in studies that employ a mix of event contingent and fixed sampling scheme). This information was coded as categorical variable with the options "fixed", "random", "semi-random", "event", "mixed", and "other".

Reliability.

Coders first rated whether any reliability estimate for the construct has been reported (dichotomous: yes or no). Next, more information on the estimator was obtained and coders were given a fixed set of options to report the reliability estimate: "alpha"

or "omega" should be selected for reports of Cronbach's α or McDonald's ω, respectively.

The option "mlcfa" was given for cases that specifically referred to Geldhof et al. (2014) or Lai (2021) and reported reliability estimates based on multilevel factor analysis. We added this option to allow coders to more easily identify these estimates as multilevel versions of α and ω, respectively. The option "g" should be selected for estimates based on Generalizability Theory (Cranford et al., 2006). The category "decomp / mlm" was selected for estimates based on multilevel decomposition sensu Nezlek (2017) or Wilhelm & Schoebi (2007).

Model-based estimates (e.g. derived from the Multilevel Measurement Error Vector AR(1) model or the 2RDM) were coded as "model" and test-retest estimates as "retest". Two more options were given for other estimates ("other"), and instances in which it remained unclear, which estimate was reported ("unclear"), respectively.

Next, coders provided information whether reliability estimates were reported separately by level (with the options: "yes", "no, combined", and "unclear"). The numerical reliability estimate was then entered. The within-person reliability and between-person reliability estimates, respectively, were entered in case reliability was reported separately by level. If the estimated reliability was reported combined across levels (or if separate by level was unclear), reliability was entered as other reliability.

Inter-rater agreement

Thirty articles were coded independently by four or five coders. Each coder's entries were matched to common coding units (one per construct within a study) using a manually curated mapping (see Online Supplement S2), yielding 260 units. Agreement was computed using Krippendorff's α (Hayes & Krippendorff, 2007; Krippendorff, 2004) which accommodates any number of coders, different coders per unit, and missing ratings, and it weights disagreements appropriately for nominal, interval, and ratio variables. Confidence intervals (95%) were obtained through bootstrapping which resampled articles 1,000 times.

Alpha ranged from .81 to 1.00 across 14 coded variables (see Table S2b), so all estimates reached the .80 threshold recommended for drawing conclusions from coded data (Krippendorff, 2019). Agreement was high for the key variables: sample size, α = .99, 95% CI [.98, 1.00]; number of items, α = .94 [.86, .99]; reliability estimates not separated by level, α = .99 [.93, 1.00]; whether a reliability was reported, α = .96 [.91, 1.00]; and type of reliability coefficient, α = .94 [.78, 1.00]. Within-and between-person reliability estimates were coded for only 10 units each (α = .91 [.31, 1.00] and .81 [.53, 1.00]), so these values were estimated with larger uncertainty.

Data preparation

The precise steps described in this section were not preregistered. The individual csv files were merged. For articles coded by multiple coders, inconsistencies between coders were resolved by either selecting the codings given by the majority of the raters or in case of a tie the rating provided by the most consistent raters overall (see Online Supplement S2 for details). For Hypotheses 2 and 3b, the appropriate constructs needed to be grouped into the classes negative affect and positive affect, respectively. This was done manually as the construct names reported in the individual articles were inconsistent. The first author of this work grouped each construct label into one of 23 different construct clusters (e.g. stress, social aspects, sleep; see Online Supplement S3 for details). Constructs were grouped into the clusters negative affect / positive affect if the construct name entered by the coders indicated a negative / positive emotional state, but not a specific emotion (depression, anxiety, happiness, etc.) or affect with unclear valence (e.g., affective well-being, mood). In most cases (e.g., state_positive_emotions, intensity_of_positive_emotionality, daily_negative_affect) this could be resolved directly from the provided codings. Given the importance of these construct clusters for the study hypotheses, for codings that suggested that they might belong to these clusters (e.g., dna, paa), the original papers were consulted before they were assigned to a construct class.

Last, because the information on reported reliability estimates was essential to this study, the first author of this work double checked all information on type and numerical estimates of reliability for all constructs for which the coders indicated that reliability was reported (1001 constructs, 292 studies, 272 articles). Data Analyses

We used brms (Bürkner, 2017) with default settings for priors and number of iterations for all hypothesis tests. For our first research hypothesis, a dichotomous variable was created indicating whether for at least one relevant construct within the study, reliability was reported separately by level (coded as 1) or not (coded as 0). This variable was used as the dependent variable in a logistic regression model with linear and quadratic publication year (centered on 2020) as predictors. We deviated from the preregistered approach in which we intended to estimate a two-level model (with studies nested within articles) because this model ran into convergence issues (most likely due to the small number of articles reporting > 1 study).

For the second research hypothesis we retained only constructs that were coded as either positive affect or negative affect, and for which within-person reliability estimates were reported. The size of the estimated within-person reliability was used as dependent variable in a linear two-level model (with observations nested within studies), which was predicted by the independent variable PA (coded as 1 if the construct was coded as positive affect, 0 if it was coded as negative affect). For hypothesis 3a, only constructs were used for which withinperson reliability estimates and the number of items used to measure the construct were reported. Number of items (centered on 3) was used as the sole predictor in a two-level model.

For hypothesis 3b, the dichotomous PA variable was added, as was the PA x number of items interaction. In these models, we also omitted the preregistered third level (article), given that most articles reported only one study. For all models we considered a model parameter to be statistically meaningfully different from zero if its 95% credible interval excluded zero.

Results

Description of included articles, studies, and constructs

The vast majority of the included articles reported results from one study (n = 468; 93.6%). In 30 articles, two studies were reported, while only one article reported three and one article reported four studies. The number of articles in the sample increased across publication years, with 38 articles from 2015 included and 69 articles from 2024, mirroring the general increase of studies employing ILDs reported previously (Fritz et al., 2024).

Descriptive statistics on the study and construct level are reported in Table 1. Sample sizes ranged from 1 to 9,175 (median = 101, MAD = 75.61). The average length of the study was 18.96 days (median = 10 days, MAD = 5.93), but there was substantial heterogeneity in study duration (range: 1 -365 days). Number of planned assessments per day ranged from 0.5 (= one assessment every other day) to 38, with an average of 3.41 assessments per day (median = 2.5, MAD = 2.22). Study compliance was explicitly reported in 268 studies (=50.1% of all studies) and the average compliance reported was 79.73%, closely matching results from previous meta-analyses (Ottenstein & Werner, 2022; Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023).

Across all studies, 2,436 constructs of interest were reported, of which 179 were grouped into the cluster positive affect, and 220 were grouped into the cluster negative affect (see Online Supplement S3 for the sizes of the other construct clusters). Fixed (n = 970) and semi-random sampling schemes (n = 705) were the most often employed sampling schemes.

Most constructs were assessed either with respect to the past day or night (n = 712) or with respect to "right now" (n = 691). Likert scales (n = 1,627) were the most prominent scales, followed by VAS (n = 351) and dichotomous items (n = 140). There was a wide range of number of items used to assess each construct. Overall, 882 constructs (41.9% of all constructs of interest) were assessed with one item. The median number of items used per construct of interest was 2 (mean = 3.40), and 77% of all constructs of interest were assessed with 4 items or fewer. The use of at least one inverse coded item was reported for only 66 constructs (2.7% of all constructs of interest). Reliability estimates for positive affect and negative affect

On average, the reported within-person reliability estimate for all positive affect constructs was .780, the corresponding average for all negative affect constructs was .755 (see Figure S5a; descriptive statistics of the reported reliability estimates separately by construct clusters are reported in Online Supplement S5, Table S5 and Figure S5b). In line with hypothesis 2, this descriptively small difference in estimated reliability was statistically meaningful, b = .031 [.004, .058] (see Table 3). To examine whether the difference in reliability estimates is specific to within-person reliability, we ran an additional (nonpreregistered) sensitivity analysis. In this multilevel model, the three reliability ratings were stacked into one variable which was predicted by construct group (factor with two levels: positive affect vs. negative affect) and reliability level (factor with three levels: within-person vs. between-person vs. other). Results (see Online Supplement Table S6) showed that the difference in within-person reliability for positive affect vs. negative affect was descriptively comparable to our preregistered model, but the 95% CI covered zero, b = .025 [-.002, .052].

Importantly, there was no evidence that the difference in reliability for positive vs. negative affect was different for between-person reliability estimates, b = -.006 [-.046, .035], or other reliability estimates, b = .027 [-.011, .061], respectively.

In a last model, we examined whether the positive link between number of items and within-person reliability would be stronger for positive affect than for negative affect (hypothesis 3b). There was no evidence for the postulated construct cluster x number of items interaction, however, b = .001 [-.011, .012] (see Table 3).

Discussion

ILDs have been gaining popularity as an important tool to examine within-person processes in individuals' everyday lives. Self-reports captured in these designs are -not unlike measures in other designs -not free of measurement error, however. To evaluate and improve measurement and ultimately enhance the validity of the conclusions drawn from ILDs, it is important to quantify the amount of measurement error of the measures on both the between-person level and the within-person level.

Trends in Reporting over Time

The present meta-analysis examined reliability reporting practices and trends therein in 500 articles using ILDs published from 2015 -2024. Previous work showed that only about 50-60% of studies employing ILDs reported any reliability estimate for their measures collected in an intensive longitudinal fashion, and only about 20-30% reported estimates that allow to evaluate reliability separate for within-person and between-person level, respectively (Brose et al., 2020; Castro-Alvarez et al., 2026; Horstmann & Ziegler, 2020). Results from the present meta-analysis yielded very similar results: In 286 (53.5%) of the 535 studies in the sample, at least one reliability estimate for an intensive longitudinal construct was reported, and only 20.4% of included studies reported level-specific reliability estimates. The similarity in these prevalence rates between our study and earlier work suggests that reliability reporting practices in a representative sample of ILD research across different fields are comparable to the practices found in emotion research (Brose et al., 2020; Castro-Alvarez et al., 2026) and personality research (Horstmann & Ziegler, 2020).

Our results extend previous work by showing that reporting level-specific reliability estimates has increased from 2015 to 2024. This change in reporting practices seems to be driven by a change in the type of reliability reporting rather than a global increase in reporting any reliability. For instance, in 2015, 20 studies reported any reliability (50% of all studies from 2015) and 5 reported level specific estimates (12.5%). In 2024, 36 studies reported any reliability (51.4%), but a larger share of them (24 studies, that is, 34.3% of all studies from 2024) reported level specific estimates. Sensitivity analyses suggest that this pattern of results was not driven by a possible change in abandoning single-item or two-item measures in more recent studies.

If anything, more recent studies tended to exclusively rely on fewer than three items per construct more often than earlier studies. When considering only studies that reported at least one construct measured with at least three items, the change in reporting practices was virtually identical compared to the full sample. This change in reporting practices therefore might indicate an increasing awareness of the relevance of level specific reliability estimates supported by an increasing availability of tools and resources to obtain these estimates more easily.

Evaluation of Estimated Reliability

The size of the reported reliability estimates varied across studies and constructs. On the within-person level, the median reliability was .78, with 50% of the reported reliability estimates falling between .68 and .84. Reliability estimates on the between-person level were larger, with a median of .94 and an IQR from .89 to .98. Higher reliability on the betweenperson level is expected because all repeated assessments are aggregated in the estimation of the person mean, reducing the measurement error by 1 / t (with t being the number of repeated assessments; see Cranford et al., 2006) 3.

This discrepancy emphasizes the need to report reliability estimates separately by level: when reporting only a combined reliability, the resulting estimate is based on a blend of within-and between-person covariation of the items (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002) that does not inform the reader about the relative amount of true between-person variation or true within-person variation of the assessment. Depending on the research question, researchers might be interested in using the measure to assess between-person differences in a construct (e.g., do some individuals sleep longer than others across the study period?) or within-person differences (e.g., does the same individual sleep more on some nights than on others?).

Whereas the former (between-person) differences will be typically assessed with higher reliability, many studies using ILDs are particularly interested in the latter (within-person) differences: Understanding within-person dynamics of a construct as well as potential predictors and outcomes thereof are typical research questions targeted in ILDs (Laurenceau et al., 2026). For these research designs, sufficient within-person reliability of the measure is arguably more important than between-person reliability.

Going one step further, in just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs; Nahum-Shani & Murphy, 2026) researchers aim to identify the most opportune moments to trigger interventions in daily life. For instance, researchers might aim to trigger a mindfulness intervention specifically in moments with elevated stress. healthy states (i.e., low stress) could be determined and the intervention could be triggered, whenever the individual's momentary stress level falls outside this corridor. This use case arguably requires an even more precise assessment of within-person fluctuations in stress than studies examining the within-person associations between time-varying variables: Error-prone assessments of a momentary state in a JITAI would lead to both false alarms (intervention triggered when it is not opportune) and omission errors (opportune moments missed) thereby rendering the presumed benefit of these designs limited.

The two use cases (assessment of momentary states for analyses of associations with other constructs vs. as triggers for interventions) demonstrate that any benchmarks of "good"

or "sufficient" reliability strongly depend on the use case. This is neither a new realization nor specific to ILDs: Nunnally (1978) suggested that for early-stage research, reliabilities of .70 can be deemed sufficient, whereas in applied settings much stricter criteria should be applied (>.90) -highlighting that a targeted minimal reliability depends on the context of use. This nuance in interpretation of these values might, however, sometimes not be fully acknowledged in the interpretation of the obtained reliability estimates of an empirical study.

We therefore emphasize here that our findings do not suggest that within-person reliability above .78 (the median) or .84 (the third quartile of the distribution) indicate "good" or "sufficient" reliability. The values reported in this work are a descriptive summary of estimates reported in previous work. They can be used to give an indication of how a reported reliability estimate stands relative to previous estimates. No "cut-off" or "target values" of within-person reliability can or should be delineated from this work.

The Measurement of Affective States

Because affective states are the most often reported constructs in ILDs (Wrzus & Neubauer, 2023), we targeted within-person reliability of positive and negative affect in more detail in this work. Building on the idea that different negative affective states might be more heterogeneous than different positive affective states, we tested the hypothesis that within-person reliability (which is almost exclusively estimated as within-person internal consistency; Castro-Alvarez et al., 2026) will be lower for scales capturing negative affect than for scales capturing positive affect. This hypothesis was supported in our preregistered analysis. However, the difference in reliability was descriptively small 4 (.780 vs. .755) and the difference was no longer statistically reliable after controlling for the number of items in exploratory analyses.

Hence, whereas this general signal detected in the main analyses suggests reliable differences in within-person reliability for positive and negative affect, there is likely substantial heterogeneity surrounding this average signal that should be examined in future work. For instance, the included studies differed in the specific emotion items that were included in the negative and positive affect scales. Including "tense, stressed, annoyed" as negative affect items might be expected to yield higher internal consistency than "tense, sad, lonely". Differences in temporal resolution (feeling negative affect "right now" vs. "today") could also impact the extent to which affective states co-vary within individuals.

Future research is needed to target the question if or when the assumption of a reflective measurement model is less tenable for negative affect than for positive affect.

Strengths and Limitations

The present results are based on a large sample of 500 published articles featuring ILDs. By drawing this sample randomly from a full population of eligible articles, the results are expected to generalize across the non-included articles. However, the population of this meta-analysis comprised published articles only. Study characteristics and reported reliability might be different in unpublished work. The decision to include only published work was intentional to understand reporting practices in published research articles, hence the present 4 To further contextualize the size of this difference: The difference of between-person and within-person reliability estimates for these two constructs was more than five times as large (.137 and .143) than the difference in within-person reliabilities for positive affect and negative affect (.025).

findings do not necessarily reflect study characteristics in ILDs in general, but only in published ILD work.

Some further limitations of the present work need to be considered. First, while the inter-rater reliabilities suggested adequate agreement in the ratings between the trained coders, the agreement was not perfect, adding some error variance to the coded data. Second, not all information that was planned to be coded was reported consistently in all articles. For instance, the number of inverse coded items was reported for only a small fraction of constructs. This limits the degree to which the unique contributions of different study, sample or construct characteristics for reliability reporting and the size of the estimated reliability can be examined. Overall, such combined effects were not part of the analyses conducted in the present study (which targeted a select set of predictors only). We invite future research to use the data published alongside this work to examine unique effects of multiple characteristics on reliability reporting. Third, the associations reported in this work showed the between-article or between-construct associations. For instance, the link between the number of items and reliability suggests a small between-construct effect (constructs that were assessed with more items yielded somewhat higher reliability estimates). This information cannot be transferred to within-construct associations: we cannot infer by how much the estimated reliability of a given construct would change if we included one more item. Fourth, the included articles varied greatly with respect to the research questions they aimed to address. Some articles were specifically designed to address within-person associations among time-varying variables, while in other studies, the ILD was more of an "add-on" to capture between-person differences in a construct in individuals' everyday lives. Different research questions might beget different ways in which reliability is reported. No information on the research questions or research context of the articles was coded, and therefore these potential differences cannot be targeted in the present data set.

Recommendations and Conclusion

While reporting any psychometric information for constructs assessed in ILDs has not changed over the ten-year reporting period and remained at a prevalence of about 50% in published research, there has been a shift towards a larger share of articles reporting reliability separately for the within-and between-person level. Whereas this trend is positive, the proportion of articles reporting separate reliability estimates per level remained below 40% even in the most recent years included in this meta-analysis. For future work to efficiently build on previous measurements in ILDs, having detailed information on the psychometric properties of the measurement is invaluable. Initiatives like the ESM item repository (Kirtley et al., 2018) which collects items and scales used in previous research would benefit greatly from incorporating this information into their database. This requires that sufficient psychometric information is reported in the empirical work. We therefore emphasize the need for and relevance of reporting psychometric information on measures in ILDs separately for the within-person level and the between-person level. We also acknowledge that there may be cases when a reliability estimate is not readily available. For instance, for single-item items or formative measurement constructs, reporting a reasonable reliability estimate may prove difficult. In such instances, explicit statements why no psychometric information was provided would be very helpful. This would further aid in increasing transparency in the reporting of psychometric practices.
