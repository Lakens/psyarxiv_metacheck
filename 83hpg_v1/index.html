How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Retrospective reports of childhood maltreatment are widely used in research and clinical settings, yet little is known about the long-term stability of these reports in adulthood. We investigated the consistency of adults' recollections of childhood maltreatment over 16 years in 1,696 Finnish adults who completed the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF) in two population-based surveys in 2006 and 2022. All CTQ-SF subscales showed substantial stability (latent r = .71-.86), but only 4% of participants responded identically to all items. Contrary to expectations, participants did not report less maltreatment overall in 2022; instead, the reports became more dispersed, and more participants reported believing that they had been abused. Depression and anxiety symptoms on each occasion were associated with reporting more maltreatment on that occasion, particularly emotional abuse and neglect, consistent with mood-congruent memory processes. These findings highlight the importance of considering current psychological functioning when interpreting retrospective maltreatment reports.

Introduction

Much of what is known about childhood maltreatment and its consequences in adulthood is based on adults' retrospective reports (Baldwin et al., 2019; Hardt & Rutter, 2004). These reports are often given many years after the events took place, and the emotional state of the person at the time of reporting may influence what is recalled (Everaert et al., 2022). If reports of childhood maltreatment change with the current state of the person, part of the well-documented association between reported childhood maltreatment and psychopathology in adulthood may reflect the present rather than the past (Colman et al., 2016).

Findings are mixed regarding how well people can remember past emotional events (van Giezen et al., 2005). It is well known that memories are reconstructed and, therefore, sensitive to distortion due to, for example, misleading information and suggestibility (Loftus et al., 1978; Loftus & Palmer, 1974). Nevertheless, research suggests that childhood events of psychological significance can be recalled accurately into adulthood and that individuals who have suffered childhood maltreatment remember trauma-related events well (Goodman et al., 2010 (Goodman et al., , 2019)). It is, however, also well established in memory research that forgetting occurs over time, a phenomenon known as the 'forgetting curve' (Ebbinghaus, 1885 (Ebbinghaus, /1913)). This suggests that memories become less accessible and detailed as time passes, particularly for events that are not frequently rehearsed or emotionally significant. In the context of childhood maltreatment memories, this basic principle of memory would predict a general trend towards fewer reported experiences over time. van Giezen et al. (2005) examined how consistent people's memories of emotional events are. They found that in studies on flashbulb memories (i.e., detailed memories of situations where one first learned about a shocking event) and in experimental studies, memories of emotional events were either stable or diminished over time. In studies on CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT victims of assault or war exposure, memories of emotional events tended to be amplified. The consistency of memory of emotional events decreased with a longer time interval between the first and second measurement. These intervals varied between two days and three years in the studies that were reviewed. Discrepancies in memories of emotional events may thus become more likely over time. However, since these intervals were at most three years, little is known about the consistency of memories over the decades across which childhood experiences are typically reported.

Only a few studies have examined how consistent adults' memories of their childhood maltreatment experiences are over long periods (>1 decade), and to the best of our knowledge, none has used the CTQ-SF. This is a significant knowledge gap, since much research on childhood maltreatment is based on adult study participants' subjective recollections of events that often took place more than a decade earlier. Hence, gathering knowledge about how consistent adults' memories of their childhood maltreatment experiences are over a long period would also provide valuable information about the reliability of retrospective measurements of childhood maltreatment.

Much research on childhood maltreatment relies on retrospective reports (Pinto Pereira et al., 2021; Wielaard et al., 2018), and several studies support the method (Brewin et al., 1993; Pinto Pereira et al., 2021; Wielaard et al., 2018). Nevertheless, in a meta-analysis by Baldwin et al. (2019), there was poor agreement between prospective (e.g., official records gathered at the time of the event) and retrospective (e.g., questionnaires about past events) measures of childhood maltreatment. This does not necessarily mean that the retrospective reports are less valid, since prospective measures have been shown to underreport cases of childhood maltreatment (Kalin et al., 2022; see Scott et al., 2009, for review). Retrospective reports may even be the more relevant measure for some purposes, as psychopathology in adulthood has been found to be more strongly associated with subjectively reported than with CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT objectively recorded maltreatment (Danese & Widom, 2020; Reuben et al., 2016). In any case, poor agreement with prospective measures is a question of validity, whereas whether the same person gives the same account of their childhood when asked again is a question of reliability, which is important for interpreting retrospective reports.

Of the retrospective measures of childhood maltreatment, the short form of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ-SF; Bernstein & Fink, 1998; Bernstein et al., 1994; Bernstein et al., 2003) is the most widely used. The CTQ-SF measures five domains of childhood maltreatment: Emotional Abuse (EA), Physical Abuse (PA), Sexual Abuse (SA), Emotional Neglect (EN), and Physical Neglect (PN). The questionnaire has been shown to be a generally reliable and valid measure of childhood maltreatment in both clinical and community samples (Bernstein et al., 2003; Cammack et al., 2016; Gerdner & Allgulander, 2009; Hagborg et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2013; Shannon et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2021).

The test-retest reliability of the CTQ-SF has also been shown to be good over shorter time periods. Hagborg et al. (2022) found that the test-retest reliability ranged from substantial to almost perfect in a sample of adolescents, with an interval of one year between measurements. Among adolescents, Wang et al. (2022) found good test-retest reliability for the total CTQ-SF and the EA and EN subscales over four weeks, but moderate reliability for the PA, SA, and PN subscales. In a sample of patients with schizophrenia, with an interval of 11 months on average, Xiang et al. (2021) found excellent test-retest reliabilities for the total CTQ-SF and all subscales except EN, which had moderate test-retest reliability. Kim et al. (2013) also found high test-retest reliability over 4 weeks in a sample of individuals with schizophrenia for the total CTQ-SF and all subscales except PN. In a sample of patients with bipolar disorder, with an interval of 18 months, Shannon et al. (2016) found excellent testretest reliabilities for all subscales except SA, which had moderate reliability. Finally, CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT Cammack et al. (2016) studied the test-retest reliability of the CTQ-SF in women before and after pregnancy, with the median time between measurements being 35 months. The EA, PA, and SA subscales had good reliability, while the EN and PN subscales had moderate reliability. Only a few studies have followed adults over longer intervals, all with other instruments. Wielaard et al. (2018) followed older adults over six years. In a New Zealand birth cohort, reports of childhood physical and sexual abuse given at ages 18 and 21 showed only moderate agreement (κ ≈ .45), with frequent false-negative reports (Fergusson et al., 2000). In a Canadian population survey, 7,466 adults reported childhood adversities in 1994/1995 and again in 2006/2007, and new reports on the second occasion were associated with developing depression and with increasing psychological distress and stress (Colman et al., 2016). Finally, Goltermann et al. (2023) found that CTQ reports were highly stable over two years in healthy and depressed adults, and that changes in depression severity explained only a small proportion of the variance in CTQ scores, mainly in the emotional maltreatment subscales.

Previous studies on the stability of the CTQ-SF also have some methodological limitations. Firstly, the time intervals have been short, from four weeks to about three years.

Secondly, stability has mostly been assessed with correlations between scale scores. Pearson correlations do not take systematic changes in mean levels into account, and identical scale scores on two occasions can result from endorsing different experiences, which means that stable scale scores can hide inconsistencies in what is reported. Thirdly, studies of change in reports have relied on difference scores, which combine the two occasions into one number and make it difficult to know which occasion an association is due to (Edwards, 2001).

Finally, comparing scores across occasions requires that the questionnaire measures the same constructs in the same way on both occasions, which has rarely been tested over long time intervals (but see Wang et al., 2022).

Since true childhood experiences cannot change, observed discrepancies between two temporally separate adult recollections of the same childhood experiences must be associated with other factors. Therefore, in the present study, in addition to calculating the agreement between recollections of childhood maltreatment experiences by comparing two adulthood recollections measured 16 years apart, we also explored how symptoms of depression and anxiety, as well as alcohol consumption, were associated with memory discrepancy. Since these were measured on both occasions, it was possible to test whether participants reported more maltreatment on the occasion on which they had more symptoms, as would be expected if the reports depend on the state of the person at the time of reporting. We also examined the agreement at the level of items, scale scores, classifications, and latent variables, and tested whether the CTQ-SF measured the same constructs in the same way on both occasions.

Research shows that memory is mood-congruent (Bower, 1981), so that an individual's current emotional state influences the retrieval of autobiographical memories.

People are more likely to recall experiences that match their current emotional state (e.g., individuals experiencing depression or anxiety may be more prone to recall childhood experiences that were negative). Individuals with current depressive symptoms have been shown to show biased recall of emotional information, particularly recalling less positive information, according to a meta-analytic review by Everaert et al. (2022). In a meta-analytic review by Herrera et al. (2017), it was suggested that highly anxious individuals also recall less positive information and that they, compared to individuals with low anxiety, tend to recall threatening information selectively. Applied to childhood maltreatment, the current emotional state could affect both whether particular experiences come to mind at all when completing a questionnaire and how ambiguous experiences, such as the emotional climate of one's family, are perceived. In both cases, the same person would report more maltreatment when distressed than when not.

Other factors have also been identified that can potentially disturb autobiographical memory processes. Alcohol-dependent participants had lower scores than controls in tests of the accuracy of episodic (but not semantic) autobiographical memories from their childhoods (Nandrino et al., 2014). If heavy alcohol consumption impairs the retrieval of such memories, it could be expected to lead to fewer reports of childhood maltreatment.

Reports of childhood maltreatment could also change because people come to perceive their childhood differently. Attitudes towards the treatment of children have changed considerably during the past decades. In Finland, for example, physical punishment of children was banned by law in 1983, and reports of physical punishment have declined markedly in later birth cohorts (Österman et al., 2014). Personal experiences, such as psychotherapy or becoming a parent, may also lead adults to reassess how they were treated as children. Such reassessment could be expected to affect items asking respondents to evaluate their experiences (e.g., whether they believe they were abused) more than items describing specific acts. According to fuzzy-trace theory, the gist of an experience is also retained longer than its details (Brainerd & Reyna, 2002), which could likewise lead evaluative reports to remain stable or increase while reports of specific events fade. Finally, not all changes in responses are equivalent. A change from never true to rarely true means that an experience is reported for the first time, whereas a change from sometimes true to often true only concerns its frequency, and respondents may also use the response scale differently on different occasions. Hence, changes in reports need to be examined at the level of items and response categories, and not only at the level of scale scores.

Aims and Hypotheses

The present study aimed to test whether people have a consistent perception of their childhood maltreatment experiences over a period of 16 years in adulthood, using the CTQ-SF as the measure of childhood maltreatment. Since the present study used data from two CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT large surveys from 2006 and 2022, both including data from multiple questionnaires, it was also possible to test some hypotheses about factors that could be associated with discrepancies in reports of childhood maltreatment. Measures of symptoms of depression and anxiety and of alcohol consumption were available for both time points.

Based on previous research, we expected that: (1) Participants would, due to forgetting, report fewer abuse experiences at the later (2022) measurement occasion.

(2) Participants would report more childhood abuse experiences on the occasion on which they had more symptoms of depression and anxiety, so that symptoms in 2022 would be associated with more abuse experiences being reported in 2022 than in 2006, and symptoms in 2006 with more abuse experiences being reported in 2006 than in 2022. This expectation was based on the idea that depressive and anxious states during retrieval would result in a higher likelihood of retrieval of negative, mood-congruent memories.

(3) Participants would report fewer childhood abuse experiences on the occasion on which they consumed more alcohol. This expectation was based on alcohol consumption impairing retrieval of episodic memories.

In addition, we explored which items were most sensitive to current affective states, whether items asking respondents to evaluate their experiences changed differently from items describing specific events, and whether a tendency to minimize maltreatment in 2006 predicted later changes in reporting.

Childhood Maltreatment

Childhood maltreatment was measured retrospectively with a Finnish translation of the CTQ-SF (Bernstein & Fink, 1998; Bernstein et al., 1994; Bernstein et al., 2003), prepared with back-translation and used previously with the 2006 data (Hollerbach et al., 2018). The questionnaire has 28 items, of which 25 measure five domains of childhood maltreatment: EA, PA, SA, EN, and PN. The remaining three items form a Minimization/Denial (MD) scale, which was included in 2006 but not in 2022. The wording of the items and the response options were identical in the two surveys.

Each of the five subscales consists of 5 items, such as being hit so hard that one had to see a doctor or go to the hospital for PA and family members saying hurtful or insulting things for EA. All five EN items and two PN items (having someone to take care of and protect one, and someone to take one to the doctor when needed) are positively worded and were reverse coded. Each of the three abuse subscales also includes one item asking whether CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT respondents believe that they were physically, emotionally, or sexually abused. We refer to these three items as evaluative items, and to the remaining 12 abuse items, which describe specific acts, as event items. Participants respond to the statements using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never True to5 = Very Often True) and the score for each subscale ranges from 5 to 25. The cutoff scores for at least low-to-moderate maltreatment are EA ≥ 9, PA ≥ 8, SA ≥ 6, EN ≥ 10, and PN ≥ 8 (Bernstein & Fink, 1998). The MD scale was scored as the number of MD items answered Very Often True (range 0-3; Bernstein & Fink, 1998).

The CTQ-SF has been shown to be a generally reliable measure of childhood maltreatment in both community (e.g., Gerdner & Allgulander, 2009; Hagborg et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022) and clinical (e.g., Gerdner & Allgulander, 2009; Hagborg et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2021) samples. Furthermore, the CTQ-SF has been examined for criterion-related, concurrent, convergent, discriminant, construct and structural validity, and the validity of the CTQ-SF as a measure of childhood maltreatment has generally been found satisfactory (Bernstein et al., 2003; Gerdner & Allgulander, 2009; Hagborg et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2022). In the present study, the internal consistency was good to excellent for EA, SA, and EN (Cronbach's α = .84-.92), lower for PA (α = .68 in 2006 and .76 in 2022), and lowest for PN (α = .56 and .69; Table 1). Ordinal α, which is more appropriate for skewed ordinal items (Zumbo et al., 2007), ranged from .78 (PN in 2006) to .98 (SA).

Symptoms of Depression and Anxiety

The Brief Symptom Inventory 18 (BSI-18; Derogatis, 2001) measures symptoms of depression, anxiety, and somatization. The depression and anxiety subscales were included in both the 2006 and the 2022 survey and each consist of 6 statements (e.g., "Feeling worthless"), which respondents rate according to how much they have been distressed by each symptom during the past 7 days, on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = Not at All to4 = CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT Extremely). The scores range from 0 to 24, and the scores of participants with one missing item were prorated. The depression and anxiety subscales of the BSI-18 have been shown to have good internal consistency and are considered to be valid measures of depressive and anxiety symptoms (Franke et al., 2017). In the present study, α was .84-.86.

Alcohol Consumption

The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT; Babor et al., 2001) consists of 10 items that measure alcohol consumption, potential dependence on alcohol, and alcoholrelated harm. In the present study, only the three items that measure alcohol consumption (AUDIT-C) were used. The AUDIT-C was included in both surveys.

The AUDIT-C consists of the questions 1) "how often do you have a drink containing alcohol", 2) "how many units do you drink on a typical day when you are drinking", and 3) "how often do you drink 6 or more units on one occasion". Participants respond to the questions by choosing one of the five response alternatives depicting a range from low (0) to high (4) alcohol consumption. The scale ranges from 0 to 12, and a score of 4 or more for men, and 3 or more for women, is considered a symptom of hazardous alcohol use.

Participants who reported never drinking alcohol did not answer the two follow-up questions and were scored 0 on them.

The AUDIT is widely used and has been shown to be a reliable and valid measure of problematic alcohol use (Allen et al., 1997; Bush et al., 1998). In the present study, the internal consistency of the AUDIT-C was modest (α = .59 in 2006 and .61 in 2022), which can be expected for three items that measure different aspects of drinking.

Measurement Invariance

Longitudinal measurement invariance of the CTQ-SF was tested with ordinal confirmatory factor analyses of the five-factor model on both occasions, estimated with weighted least squares and mean-and variance-adjusted test statistics (WLSMV; Flora & Curran, 2004), with residual covariances between repeated items. Because high response categories were sparse, the two highest categories were merged for all items, and three items endorsed by fewer than 4% of participants (items 9, 17, and 21) were scored as never versus ever true. Following Wu and Estabrook (2016), we compared configural, threshold-andloading, and threshold-loading-and-intercept (scalar) invariance models. Because χ² difference tests are highly sensitive at this sample size, we judged invariance mainly by the change in the comparative fit index (ΔCFI ≤ .002; Meade et al., 2008) and used score tests of individual equality constraints to locate noninvariance, freeing a constraint only if it was significant after Bonferroni correction. Under the final model, latent means and variances in CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT 2022 were estimated relative to 2006 (fixed at 0 and 1, respectively). The same procedure was applied to the BSI-18 subscales.

State-Dependence of Reports

Because difference scores of scale totals mix consistent and inconsistent responses and are difficult to interpret (Edwards, 2001), we quantified inconsistency at the item level.

For each participant, we computed the difference between the 2022 and the 2006 response to each item (after reverse-scoring) and summed the positive differences (upward inconsistency: more maltreatment reported in 2022) and, separately, the absolute values of the negative differences (downward inconsistency: more maltreatment reported in 2006), across all 25 items and within each subscale. We also counted the items that changed from never true to any endorsement, and vice versa.

Each inconsistency index was regressed on depressive symptoms in 2006 and in 2022, entered simultaneously, controlling for sex and age; models with anxiety symptoms and alcohol use were specified in the same way. If reports depend on the affective state at the time of reporting, upward inconsistency should be predicted by 2022 but not by 2006 symptoms, and downward inconsistency by 2006 but not by 2022 symptoms. Because the indices are bounded by the level reported on each occasion, we repeated these models controlling for the CTQ-SF total score in 2006 or in 2022, and with negative binomial regression. To remove random measurement error, we then estimated latent variable models for each subscale, with latent CTQ-SF and latent symptom factors on both occasions under the final invariance model. The 2022 CTQ-SF factor was regressed on the 2006 factor and on symptoms on both occasions and, in a second model, the 2006 factor on the 2022 factor and on symptoms on both occasions, controlling for sex and age. Finally, to identify the items most sensitive to current affect, we regressed each standardized item response on the same item on the other occasion, on symptoms on the same and on the other occasion, sex, and age, CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT pooling the two directions in one model with a common coefficient for same-occasion symptoms.

Evaluative Versus Event Items and Minimization

We compared the mean change on the three evaluative items with that on the 12 event items, and examined whether MD in 2006 predicted later inconsistency, controlling for the 2006 CTQ-SF total score and depressive symptoms on both occasions.

Discussion

We examined whether adults have a consistent perception of their childhood maltreatment experiences over 16 years in a Finnish population-based sample. To our knowledge, this was the longest time interval studied in a follow-up study on the CTQ-SF.

Exploring a long interval is relevant, since people are often asked about their childhood maltreatment experiences many years after the events took place. Overall, the reports were stable in rank order, particularly when measurement error was taken into account, but they were far from identical, as only 4% of the participants gave the same response to all items on both occasions. The changes were also systematic. The reports on each occasion reflected the participants' depressive and anxiety symptoms at that time, most strongly for emotional CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT abuse and neglect; the reports spread out over time rather than increasing or decreasing overall; and the evaluative items, which ask whether the respondent believes they were abused, became more common while the reports of specific events did not.

Stability and Change Over 16 Years

Overall, participants responded relatively consistently to the CTQ-SF subscales.

Responses correlated with large effect sizes (r = .56-.69) between 2006 and 2022, and the latent correlations, which are corrected for measurement error, were higher (.71-.86).

Previous studies, which have had shorter time intervals between measurements (4 to 140.7 weeks), have shown that the CTQ-SF has good test-retest reliability (Cammack et al., 2016; Goltermann et al., 2023; Hagborg et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2013; Shannon et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2022; Xiang et al., 2021). The somewhat lower correlations in the present study are in line with the finding that the consistency of memories of emotional events decreases with longer time intervals (van Giezen et al., 2005), and the latent correlations show that much of the inconsistency was due to measurement error on each occasion rather than to changes in what participants recalled.

Although cross-time correlations were relatively large at the group level, they allow for substantial variability at the individual level. The agreement for individual items and for the classification of participants by the cutoff scores (κ = .34-.50) was considerably lower, and comparable to the agreement between reports of abuse given three years apart in the Christchurch cohort (Fergusson et al., 2000). Hence, dimensional scores are more reliable than categorical classifications. Caution is advised when applying these findings at the individual level -such as in court cases -because some individuals' perceptions of their childhood maltreatment experiences can differ substantially over time.

Based on the basic principle that forgetting increases over time, we expected the participants to report fewer abuse experiences overall in 2022 compared to 2006. This CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT hypothesis was not supported at the level of the subscale scores. However, participants did not report substantially more abuse experiences either. Although the observed means increased slightly, the latent means changed little and in no uniform direction (slightly lower for emotional abuse, slightly higher for emotional neglect, and not different for the other subscales), and the increase in the observed scores mainly reflected a greater spread of the reports, so that some participants reported less maltreatment in 2022 than in 2006 and others more. Fading memories and decreasing distress may explain the former, and a reassessment of childhood experiences the latter. The increase in the evaluative items supports the latter interpretation, as the proportion of participants who believed that they had been physically or emotionally abused increased markedly, whereas reports of specific events, such as being hit so hard that it left bruises or marks, did not. This could be related to societal changes reflecting increasingly negative attitudes to childhood abuse, including attention to its negative impact on mental health. In Finland, physical punishment of children was common until the 1980s and has since become much less common and less accepted (Österman et al., 2014). This may have resulted in the participants reassessing their childhood experiences and identifying some experiences as abusive that they did not previously identify as such. The evaluative items increased mainly among participants whose reports of events also increased, and they rarely decreased when reports of events decreased. This is in line with fuzzy-trace theory, according to which the gist of an experience is retained longer than its details (Brainerd & Reyna, 2002), so that once a person has concluded that they were abused, this conclusion may remain even when memories of specific incidents fade. Indeed, reports of specific events decreased slightly on average, and markedly for the item about being hit so hard that it left bruises or marks (from 34.6% to 24.7%), which is the pattern that forgetting would predict. Some forgetting of specific events may thus have occurred but been masked in the subscale scores by the increase in the evaluative items. Since the evaluative items are part CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT of the abuse subscales, such changes alter the subscale scores without any change in the reported events, which contributed to the noninvariance of the PA subscale.

Reports Reflect the Current Affective State

We tested whether depression and anxiety symptoms were associated with changes in the participants' perceptions of their childhood maltreatment experiences and found support for our second hypothesis. When the report on one occasion was held constant, participants reported more maltreatment on the occasion on which they had more depression or anxiety symptoms. This was true in both directions, after taking into account that the inconsistency indices are limited by the level reported on each occasion, and in latent variable models that remove random measurement error. The fact that the pattern was found in both directions is important. A stable trait such as negative affectivity, which is associated with both psychopathology and reports of adverse childhood experiences, would lead to the same association between symptoms and reports on both occasions, but it cannot explain why the reports follow the symptoms present on the occasion on which they are given. These findings are in line with those of Herrera et al. (2017), who suggested that highly anxious individuals recall less positive information, and with the meta-analytic findings of Everaert et al. (2022), which suggested that individuals with depressive symptoms experience bias when recalling emotional information. They also extend the findings of Colman et al. (2016), who found that new reports of childhood adversity were associated with developing depression, and of Goltermann et al. (2023), who found small effects of changes in depression on CTQ scores over two years, mainly in the emotional subscales. The effects were modest (standardized coefficients of about .1 to .3 in the latent models), but they were found in a population-based sample with mostly low levels of symptoms and could be larger in clinical samples, in which symptoms vary more. Participants' current negative affective state could, therefore, facilitate or bias their perceptions of their childhood maltreatment experiences.

A clear pattern in the results was that emotional abuse and neglect were more affected by depression and anxiety symptoms than other aspects of childhood maltreatment experiences. Again, this finding is perhaps not surprising given that recall of emotional aspects of such experiences can be expected to be more malleable than recall of actions (i.e., physical or sexual abuse). Also, the positively worded physical neglect items about having someone to take care of one seemed to be more malleable than the items describing physical or sexual abuse. However, the results do not show whether this reflects mood-congruent memory or mood-congruent responding, that is, whether distressed respondents retrieve more negative memories or describe the same memories more negatively. A general tendency to agree with statements is unlikely to explain the results, since symptoms were associated with more reported maltreatment on both positively and negatively worded items, but a tendency to respond more negatively on a particular occasion cannot be ruled out with two occasions and self-report measures alone. In either case, cross-sectional associations between retrospectively reported maltreatment, particularly emotional abuse and neglect, and current psychopathology are likely to be inflated by the shared influence of the current state, and studies using retrospective reports should therefore measure current symptoms and take them into account. In clinical assessment, reports of emotional maltreatment given during a depressive episode should be interpreted with this in mind.

We found no support for our hypothesis regarding alcohol consumption, which was expected to negatively impact the retrieval of episodic memories. Alcohol consumption was not consistently related to the reports on either occasion. The AUDIT-C measures the frequency and quantity of drinking in the general population, and few participants drank at levels that could be expected to impair autobiographical memory (Nandrino et al., 2014).

Finally, participants who minimized maltreatment in 2006 did not report more maltreatment CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT later, which speaks against the possibility that later reports reflect a delayed willingness to disclose experiences that had been denied earlier.

Strengths and Limitations

The present study has several strengths. Firstly, to the best of our knowledge, it has the longest time interval between measurements of any follow-up study of the CTQ-SF. The results, therefore, give more information about how consistent adults' memories of their childhood maltreatment experiences are over a longer time. Secondly, the sample size was large, and the item wording and response options were identical on both occasions. Thirdly, depression and anxiety symptoms were measured on both occasions, which made it possible to test whether the reports followed the symptoms on the occasion on which they were given.

Finally, the analyses were conducted at the level of items, scale scores, and latent variables, and they took the clustering of participants within families into account.

The present study also has some limitations. Firstly, the mean scores of the CTQ-SF and each subscale were relatively low (see Table 1). Memories of negative events seem to be more vivid or detailed than memories of positive or neutral events (Bowen & Kensinger, 2017), and since most of the participants of the present study had low scores on the CTQ-SF, their recollection of these aspects of their childhood may be less accurate than in, for example, a clinical sample of individuals who have suffered more severe abuse. This could lead to many participants responding inconsistently to the CTQ-SF across the two measurement occasions. The low scores also mean that the estimates for PA and SA in particular were based on relatively few participants who reported any such experiences.

Secondly, the participants also had relatively low levels of symptoms of depression and anxiety, which may have limited the size of the associations between symptoms and reports.

Thirdly, the internal consistency was low for PN (α = .56 in 2006 and .69 in 2022) and modest for the AUDIT-C, which weakens the associations with these measures. Fourthly, CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT with only two measurement occasions, state-dependent reporting cannot be fully separated from measurement error or response styles specific to one occasion, and since the symptoms and the reports of maltreatment were given in the same survey, part of their association on each occasion may reflect shared method variance. Fifthly, the surveys differed in format. In 2006, participants could respond on paper or online and the MD items were included among the other items, whereas in 2022 the survey was online only, required a response to every item, and did not include the MD items. The changes did not differ between participants who had responded on paper and online in 2006, but other differences in format could have contributed to the greater spread of the reports in 2022. Sixthly, the participants who took part in both surveys were a minority of the original sample and may differ from those who did not. Finally, consistency is not the same as accuracy, as a report that is the same on two occasions may still be incorrect, and we had no prospective records with which to validate the reports (Baldwin et al., 2019).

Concluding Remarks

In conclusion, adults' reports of childhood maltreatment are stable enough over 16 years to rank individuals, but they do change over time. The reports spread out, more participants come to believe that they were abused without reporting more specific events, and reports of emotional abuse and neglect in particular reflect the person's affective state at the time of reporting. Researchers and clinicians who rely on retrospective reports of childhood maltreatment should therefore take the current state of the person into account when interpreting such reports.

Constraints on Generality

The Digital and Population Data Services Agency of Finland (https://dvv.fi/en) does not keep records on residents' race, but it does keep records of their native language. According to official statistics, only 10.8% of Finland residents were foreign-language speakers in 2024 (e.g., https://stat.fi/en/publication/cm1jg8tr20lco07vwvoif9s6i), and Finland is also an CHILDHOOD MALTREATMENT REPORTS AND CURRENT AFFECT ethnically homogenous country in international comparison. In the 2006 data collection (i.e., time point 1 of the present study), only native Finnish speakers were contacted with an invitation to participate in the survey, and it is hence likely that an overwhelming majority of the participants were ethnic Finns. Participants were not asked about their race or ethnicity in either survey. Furthermore, there were more than twice as many women participants as men participants. The homogeneity of the sample used in the present study, the lack of data on race and ethnicity, and the underrepresentation of male participants potentially limit the generalizability of our findings. The sample was drawn from Finnish-speaking twins and their siblings identified through the Central Population Registry of Finland. We expect the findings to generalize to adults in the general population of the other Nordic countries and of other Western European countries with similar norms about child rearing. Whether they generalize to countries in which corporal punishment of children is more widely accepted, which may affect which experiences are labeled as abuse, and to clinical and forensic samples, in which maltreatment and symptoms are more common and more severe, remains to be tested.
