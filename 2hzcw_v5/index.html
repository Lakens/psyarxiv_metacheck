Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Social camouflaging among autistic adolescents has increasingly been recognized, but its developmental and family context remains unclear. Parental affiliate stigma and adolescents' internalized stigma may be relevant correlates. This study examined associations among these factors, including maternal and paternal patterns.

Methods: A cross-sectional online questionnaire was conducted with 50 autistic adolescent-mother-father triads in Japan. Adolescents reported social camouflaging and internalized stigma, while mothers and fathers reported affiliate stigma. Path analyses were conducted, adjusting for adolescent age and autistic traits.

Results: Adolescents' internalized stigma was positively associated with social camouflaging. Both maternal and paternal affiliate stigma were positively associated with adolescents' internalized stigma in separate models and remained independently associated when modeled simultaneously. In the combined model, the estimated indirect association with social camouflaging through internalized stigma was statistically significant only for paternal affiliate stigma.

Conclusions: These findings highlight the relevance of adolescents' internalized stigma and parental affiliate stigma to social camouflaging in autistic adolescents. They also underscore the importance of examining both maternal and paternal stigma-related experiences within the family context.

Background

Social Camouflaging and Internalized Stigma among Autistic Adolescents

Adolescents are sensitive to social evaluation [1]. Autistic adolescents exhibit elevated risk for peer difficulties and bullying, which are associated with internalizing difficulties [2] [3] [4]. Social camouflaging refers to strategies used to mask autistic characteristics or compensate for social difficulties to meet neurotypical social expectations [5]. Social camouflaging has also been observed among autistic adolescents [6, 7].

Social camouflaging may vary across developmental, gender, and sociocultural contexts. Gender differences reported among Western adolescents have not been consistently observed in East Asian samples, including Japan [8] [9] [10] [11]. Japanese cultural emphasis on relational harmony and conformity may shape autistic individuals' experiences of difference and family responses to autism [12] [13] [14]. A cross-cultural study of autistic adults reported nonlinear associations between social camouflaging and generalized anxiety, depression, and well-being in Japan, whereas the UK sample showed linear associations [15]. These findings emphasize the importance of examining social camouflaging within the Japanese sociocultural context.

Greater social camouflaging has been associated with higher levels of anxiety, depression, and stress among adolescents [6]. Among autistic individuals, social camouflaging has been associated with social anxiety and lower psychological well-being [16]. Associations among social camouflaging, social anxiety, and depression have also been reported among autistic adolescents in Taiwan, another East Asian setting [17]. Social camouflaging has additionally been associated with self-alienation and diminished self-esteem [18, 19] and suicide-related risks [20].

Consistent with the self-stigma model [21], stigma-related experiences may contribute to camouflaging by shaping negative self-evaluations and rejection expectations [22]. Internalized stigma refers to the internalization of negative societal attitudes and stereotypes into one's self-concept, resulting in negative selfevaluations and emotional distress [21], and has been associated with concealment and avoidance-oriented coping in autism [23]. Turnock et al. [24] proposed that enacted stigma may contribute to the individual's internalized stigma and the family's affiliate stigma. Affiliate stigma refers to the internalization of societal devaluation by those closely associated with a stigmatized individual, such as family members [25]. The Affiliate Stigma Scale was originally developed and validated among caregivers of individuals with intellectual disabilities or mental illness [25]. Subsequent studies have also used this scale to examine affiliate stigma among parents of autistic children [26, 27]. Perceived stigma is associated with greater social camouflaging among autistic adults [28, 29]. However, few previous studies examined these associations, and family-related stigma processes have rarely been examined.

Affiliate Stigma within the Family System

In this family context, stigma-related meanings may become linked to adolescents' self-evaluations and social adaptation, including social camouflaging. Mothers tend to assume greater responsibility for daily caregiving, education, healthcare, and emotional interactions, whereas fathers may be more likely to engage in play-oriented and directive interactions and may take a more detached or pragmatic approach to coping; notably, parenting stress may be comparable between mothers and fathers [13, 30, 31]. These differences in parental roles and coping may shape how stigma-related experiences are perceived and expressed within the family. However, it remains unclear whether maternal and paternal affiliate stigma are independently associated with adolescents' internalized stigma when both are considered simultaneously.

Parental psychological states are mutually influential within family relationships [32], while stigma is socially constructed through social interactions and may be internalized by parents through discrimination and prejudice associated with their family member's autism [27, 33]. Additionally, parents of autistic adolescents may experience persistent stigma-related stress [34], associated with psychological distress and parenting strain [35] [36] [37]. Therefore, these stigma-related experiences may become embedded within family interactions. From a developmental-ecological perspective, the family remains a central microsystem during adolescence [38], and parenting attitudes have been associated with self-esteem among autistic children and adolescents [39]. This may be particularly salient during adolescence, when sensitivity to social acceptance and rejection is heightened [1]. Thus, given previous findings linking perceived stigma to social camouflaging [28, 29], adolescents' internalized stigma may also be associated with social camouflaging. Together, these considerations provide a rationale for examining associations among parental affiliate stigma, adolescents' internalized stigma, and social camouflaging.

Methods

This cross-sectional, web-based observational study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Participant Recruitment

The participants were recruited via a mailing list operated by LITALICO Inc., a company specializing in developmental support services. The mailing list comprised approximately 10,000 registered members. Data were collected between October 2024 and January 2025. Valid responses were obtained from 137 families.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This study recruited families of autistic adolescents aged 12-18 years with a formal diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) by a psychiatrist, child and adolescent psychiatrist, or pediatrician. Parents were defined as adults who cohabited with the autistic adolescent and assumed a parental role, regardless of biological relatedness. Maternal and paternal respondents were classified according to the parental role reported in the questionnaire; therefore, these labels do not necessarily correspond to gender identity or sex assigned at birth. All participants were required to understand the study information in Japanese, provide informed consent, and complete a web-based questionnaire. Adolescents who previously participated in autism-specific mental health interventions were excluded. Additionally, families were excluded if a parent could not complete the questionnaire owing to a severe mental disorder.

Following data screening, families were excluded if any data were missing from the adolescent, mother, or father; if the adolescent was ineligible based on educational status or residence; or if multiple adolescents from the same family responded. Only the first submission was included if duplicate responses were identified. The analytic sample size was determined by the number of complete adolescent-mother-father triads available. Requiring complete data from all three family members substantially reduced the sample, particularly because paternal responses were frequently missing. Demographic information was collected using separate web-based questionnaires for parents and adolescents.

Measures Used for Adolescents

Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire, Japanese Version

Social camouflaging was assessed using the Japanese version of the 25-item Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire [40, 41]. The CAT-Q comprises three subscales-Compensation, Masking, and Assimilationand is rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("strongly disagree") to 7 ("strongly agree"). The original and Japanese versions exhibited excellent internal consistency (α = .93 and .94, respectively) [40, 41].

Given the focus on overall camouflaging and limited evidence for subscale validity among adolescents, only the total score was analyzed. In this sample (n = 50), internal consistency of the total score was good (α = .89).

Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness, Japanese Version

Internalized stigma was assessed using the Japanese version of the 29-item Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness (ISMI) [42, 43]. The ISMI comprises five subscales-Alienation, Stereotype Endorsement, Discrimination Experience, Social Withdrawal, and Stigma Resistance-and is rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("strongly disagree") to 4 ("strongly agree"). The Japanese version demonstrated high internal consistency (α = .88) [43]. Previous studies have used adapted shorter versions of the ISMI to assess autism-related internalized stigma among autistic adults [44, 45]. In the present study, participants were instructed to interpret references to "mental illness" as referring to autism, and scores were interpreted as indicators of autism-related internalized stigma [44]. The Stigma Resistance subscale showed low internal consistency in the original version (α = .58) [42] and has been suggested to represent a construct distinct from other ISMI domains [46], with similar findings for the Japanese version [43]. Therefore, only the remaining 24 items were used. Factor analytic and bifactor studies supported the ISMI-24 total score as predominantly reflecting a general internalized stigma factor [47]. Therefore, this study used the ISMI-24 and only analyzed the total score. In this sample (N = 50), internal consistency was excellent (α = .96).

Autism Spectrum Quotient-10 (AQ-10)

Autistic traits were assessed using the 10-item short form of the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ-10), derived from the original 50-item AQ [48]. The Japanese AQ was developed through translation and back-translation [49]. Responses are dichotomously scored (0 or 1), yielding total scores ranging between 0 and 10, with higher scores indicating greater autistic traits. The full AQ and Japanese short form exhibited internal consistency of approximately α = .85 and .65, respectively. In this sample, internal consistency was α = .57.

Measures Used for Parents

Affiliate Stigma Scale for Caregivers, Japanese Version

Parental affiliate stigma was assessed using the Japanese version of the 22-item Affiliate Stigma Scale for Caregivers [25]. The Affiliate Stigma Scale has also been used to assess autism-related affiliate stigma among parents of autistic children [26, 27]. Items are rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("strongly disagree") to 4 ("strongly agree"). The Japanese version was developed through translation and backtranslation, with approval from the original authors, and demonstrated high internal consistency (α = .93; Aya Taniguchi, personal communication, 19 December 2025). In this study, parents were instructed to interpret references to "intellectual disability" as referring to autism, and scores were interpreted as indicators of autism-related affiliate stigma. In this sample, internal consistency was excellent for both maternal (N = 50) and paternal (N = 50) respondents (α = .96).

Procedure

This study was conducted online between 31 October 2024 and 29 January 2025. Participants completed a web-based questionnaire at home using their own devices after parents provided informed consent and autistic adolescents provided assent. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Chiba University Pearson correlations were used to describe bivariate associations among maternal and paternal affiliate stigma, adolescents' internalized stigma, and social camouflaging. Path analyses were conducted using structural equation modeling in R (version 4.5.1) [50] using RStudio (version 2025.09.1+401), lavaan package [51].

Model specification was guided by the self-stigma model [21]. Maternal and paternal affiliate stigma were examined separately and jointly to estimate their independent associations with adolescents' internalized stigma while accounting for their covariance. Indirect associations with social camouflaging through internalized stigma were estimated. In the primary models, direct paths from parental affiliate stigma to social camouflaging were fixed to zero, consistent with the hypothesized indirect association model. Age and AQ-10 scores were included a priori as theoretically relevant covariates of internalized stigma and social camouflaging, and all exogenous predictors were allowed to covary. Analyses were conducted across gender because the sample was too small and unevenly distributed for reliable gender-stratified analyses; sex assigned at birth was not collected. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted without adjustment for age and AQ-10 scores.

Given the relatively small analytic sample (N = 50), the reliance of conventional maximum-likelihood inference on large-sample asymptotic theory, and the potentially non-normal sampling distribution of indirect effects [52, 53], bootstrap estimation was used to obtain standard errors and confidence intervals for the model parameters. Confidence intervals were based on 10,000 bootstrap resamples, and an effect was considered statistically significant when its 95% bootstrap confidence interval excluded zero. Regarding reported standardized estimates, bootstrap standard errors and confidence intervals were calculated from the bootstrap distribution of the standardized coefficients.

Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. CFI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .06, and SRMR ≤ .09 were interpreted as indicating good fit, following the criteria proposed by Hu and Bentler [54]. However, TLI and RMSEA may perform poorly in small samples [54] and RMSEA may be problematic in models with very low degrees of freedom [55]. Given the small sample size and low model degrees of freedom, the Bollen-Stine bootstrap test was used to assess overall model fit [56], based on 10,000 bootstrap resamples.

An exploratory Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the detection performance and significance patterns of direct and indirect associations in the parent-combined model with 50 family triads, following established simulation-based approaches [57, 58]. Normally distributed data were generated under 21 hypothetical population scenarios varying the standardized maternal and paternal paths to internalized stigma (β = .20-.40), the path from internalized stigma to social camouflaging (β = .20-.50), and the correlation between maternal and paternal affiliate stigma (r = .30, .50, or .70). Scenarios included both equal and unequal parental effects. Paths from age and autistic traits to both outcomes were set to β = .20, while other exogenous correlations were set to zero. For each scenario, 500 datasets (N = 50) were generated, and standardized effects were evaluated using 95% percentile bootstrap confidence intervals based on 2,000 family-level resamples per dataset [59].

Discussion

Consistent with H1a and H1b, both maternal and paternal affiliate stigma were associated with adolescents' internalized stigma in the single-parent models, and internalized stigma was associated with greater social camouflaging. Consistent with H2, both maternal and paternal affiliate stigma remained independently associated with internalized stigma when modeled simultaneously. However, only paternal affiliate stigma exhibited a significant indirect association with social camouflaging through adolescents' internalized stigma after accounting for maternal affiliate stigma. This paternal pattern was also observed in the unadjusted sensitivity analyses, whereas the corresponding maternal associations were not.

Internalized Stigma's Role in Social Camouflaging Among Autistic Adolescents

The findings suggested that social camouflaging may function as a self-regulatory strategy associated with internalized social evaluation. Previous studies among adults consistently reported that perceived social stigma was associated with increased social camouflaging among autistic individuals [22, 28, 29]. Extending these findings to adolescents, this study's results suggested that social camouflaging is closely associated with internalized stigma, particularly during a developmental period marked by heightened self-awareness and sensitivity to social evaluation [1, 60].

Furthermore, in this study, both maternal and paternal affiliate stigma were associated with adolescents' internalized stigma, and each remained independently associated with internalized stigma when considered simultaneously. This interpretation aligns with affiliate stigma, which emphasizes the internalization of stigma through relationships with stigmatized others [25], as well as with studies indicating that parents' stigmarelated distress is associated with autistic young individuals' psychosocial adjustment through everyday interactions [32, 61].

Additionally, these findings are consistent with the self-stigma model [21] and broader accounts of stigma as embedded within social and interpersonal contexts [62].

However, given the cross-sectional design, these associations may also be reciprocal, and the directions specified in the model should be interpreted as theoretically informed rather than as evidence of temporal ordering.

Maternal and Paternal Patterns of Affiliate Stigma

A possible explanation for the more consistent paternal pattern is that differences in mothers' and fathers' social and emotional roles within the family may make paternal affiliate stigma more readily conveyed to adolescents' own social self-evaluations. Mothers are typically more involved in daily caregiving [31, 63], and maternal emotional states may be associated with adolescents' emotional experiences through repeated daily interactions, particularly when affiliate stigma co-occurs with parenting stress [64] [65] [66] [67]. Additionally, mothers have been reported to use more problem-solving and positive reappraisal strategies, seek social support more frequently, and exhibit more sensitive and responsive parenting than fathers [13, 30].

Conversely, fathers' attitudes and evaluations may be more closely associated, in some families, with broader social norms and evaluations of self-worth [31, 68]. This interpretation is broadly consistent with previous studies linking parental emotional distance or rejection to adolescents' self-esteem and internalizing outcomes [69] [70] [71], as well as studies documenting stigma and blame experienced by fathers raising autistic children [72, 73]. From this perspective, fathers' negative perceptions of autism or concerns regarding social evaluation may be more readily reflected in adolescents' perceptions of how they are evaluated by society as autistic individuals, thereby relating to internalized stigma and social camouflaging. However, these processes were not directly assessed and remain hypothetical.

The associations between parental affiliate stigma and adolescents' internalized stigma may be particularly meaningful in the Japanese cultural context. Japanese parents reportedly exhibit less responsive and affectionate parent-child interactions than Western parents [13], and being different from others is often perceived negatively among autistic individuals in Japan [14]. In addition, traditional gendered divisions of family roles remain relatively persistent in Japan [63, 74], which ranked 118th of 148 economies in the 2025

Global Gender Gap Index [75]. These findings suggested that cultural expectations surrounding conformity, social evaluation, and gendered family roles may provide an important context for understanding how parents' stigma-related experiences relate to adolescents' self-stigma.

However, these within-family mechanisms cannot be established from cross-sectional data, and maternal and paternal roles vary across families and cultures. Therefore, the more consistent paternal pattern should not be interpreted as indicating greater importance, but as a preliminary finding that maternal and paternal stigmarelated experiences may relate to adolescents through different family pathways. Larger longitudinal studies are required to examine these processes.

Theoretical Contributions

The findings extend adult-focused accounts of stigma-related camouflaging by indicating that internalized stigma is associated with social camouflaging during adolescence, supporting the self-stigma model within a developmental context [21, 28, 29].

Additionally, the findings situate these processes within family relationships, as parental affiliate stigma was associated with adolescents' internalized stigma, consistent with ecological and sociological accounts of stigma [38, 62]. The parent-combined analysis further emphasizes the importance of including both parents, particularly given that previous studies linked paternal roles to social norms, self-worth, and adolescents' internalizing outcomes [31, 68, 70, 71].

These findings extend individual-level accounts of social camouflaging by situating stigma-related processes within developmental, familial, and cultural contexts.

Clinical Implications

Sustained camouflaging is associated with negative self-concept, anxiety, and depression [6, 11], whereas internalized stigma may reflect negative self-evaluations and erosion of authentic self-concepts [1, 25].

Clinically, these findings emphasize the importance of addressing self-evaluative processes underlying camouflaging rather than solely focusing on behavioral change. Interventions fostering self-compassion and self-acceptance may help reduce self-negation and psychological distress [76], particularly given the association between camouflaging and perceived social evaluation [10].

Previous family-based autism studies and interventions predominantly focused on mothers, with fathers remaining under-represented [77]. Moreover, mothers reportedly seek social support more frequently than fathers following their child's autism diagnosis [30]. Given that Japanese fathers of children with developmental disabilities may report higher developmental expectations than mothers [78], this raises the possibility that paternal stigma-related experiences may influence how fathers engage with diagnosis and support. Future studies should examine whether affiliate stigma is associated with reluctance to seek or engage with support services.

Finally, given that parents are embedded within broader social structures of labeling and discrimination [62], clinical efforts should extend beyond the family to include initiatives aimed at reducing societal stigma, consistent with the neurodiversity paradigm and the social model of disability [79, 80].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the matched adolescent-mother-father data are a key strength of this study, allowing maternal and paternal affiliate stigma to be examined simultaneously. However, requiring complete triads yielded a modest analytic sample (N = 50), particularly because paternal responses were frequently missing. Although bootstrap confidence intervals were calculated, the estimates of indirect effects should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size. Second, recruitment through a developmentalsupport provider's mailing list, combined with the triad requirement, may limit generalizability to families with less engagement with developmental-support resources or families in which fathers did not participate.

Therefore, replication in larger samples is needed.

Third, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding causal direction. Although the analyses were theoretically guided by a model in which parental affiliate stigma was associated with adolescents' internalized stigma, alternative and bidirectional pathways may exist. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify temporal ordering and potential reciprocal associations.

Fourth, the analyses focused on a specific relational structure linking parental affiliate stigma, adolescents' internalized stigma, and social camouflaging. The paternal pattern remained evident in the unadjusted sensitivity analyses (Additional file 3); however, future studies should compare alternative model specifications and include additional psychological factors, such as anxiety, depression, and self-concept.

Fifth, formal measures of support needs, adaptive functioning, and language ability were not collected.

Cognitive assessment information was only available for a subset of participants, was based on parent report, and could not be independently verified; therefore, it was not included in the statistical analyses.

Consequently, it remains unclear whether these clinical characteristics may have contributed to the observed associations.

Sixth, the modest sample size and uneven gender distribution precluded reliable gender-stratified analyses [8, 9], and sex assigned at birth was not collected. Larger studies should examine these gender-related differences more explicitly.

Seventh, reliance on self-report measures may have introduced common method bias, potentially inflating associations among adolescent-reported variables. Although the ISMI and Affiliate Stigma Scale were originally developed for other populations, their prior use in autism research provides a rationale for their application in the present study. Nevertheless, further validation of their autism-referenced Japanese versions would be valuable.

Eighth, although this study examined family-level factors, social camouflaging is influenced by multiple contexts, including peer and school environments [18]. Future studies should adopt integrative frameworks that consider stigmas across social contexts.

Finally, given that all participants resided in Japan, the findings may reflect Japanese cultural norms, including interdependence and relational harmony [12], as well as patterns of association between camouflaging and mental health reported in Japan [15]. Therefore, it remains unclear whether these findings can be directly generalized to other sociocultural contexts.

Conclusions

This study examined the associations between social camouflaging, internalized stigma, and parental affiliate stigma among autistic adolescents. Internalized stigma was consistently and positively associated with social camouflaging, suggesting a close association between self-directed stigma and social camouflaging during adolescence. Additionally, parental affiliate stigma was associated with adolescents' internalized stigma, emphasizing the family context's relevance to self-evaluative processes. When maternal and paternal affiliate stigma were examined simultaneously, both remained independently associated with adolescents' internalized stigma, whereas the estimated indirect association with social camouflaging was statistically significant only for paternal affiliate stigma. These findings emphasize the importance of considering both parents when examining stigma-related processes among autistic adolescents, while suggesting that paternal stigma-related experiences warrant further investigation.
