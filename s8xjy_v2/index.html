Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Contemporary workplace arrangements may weaken employees' ability to personalize their workspace, anticipate its conditions, and regulate access to it, yet no dedicated instrument directly measures the resulting experience of perceived deterritorialization. This article reports the development and initial structural validation of the French-language Perceived Deterritorialization Scale (PDS). In Study 1, 340 French office employees completed an initial 26-item pool. Principal-axis factoring with Oblimin rotation supported three correlated dimensions: spatial identity loss, environmental instability, and territorial intrusion. Fifteen items were retained, accounting for 49.2% of the variance in the extracted solution. In Study 2, an independent sample of 225 employees completed the retained items. Confirmatory factor analysis showed that the correlated three-factor model fitted substantially better than unidimensional, two-factor, and orthogonal alternatives, robust χ²(87) = 109.39, p = .053, CFI = .982, TLI = .978, RMSEA = .034, 90% CI [.000, .052], SRMR = .044. Internal consistency was acceptable across dimensions (α = .818-.840; composite reliability = .820-.843). Supplementary analyses provided provisional evidence of partial scalar invariance across individual, open-plan, and flex-office configurations and indicated a stable retained core but a less stable itemexclusion boundary. These findings provide initial evidence concerning the PDS's internal structure and consistency. External convergent, discriminant, criterion, and predictive validity, as well as temporal stability, remain to be established.

1. Introduction

Contemporary workplaces are increasingly organized around shared, reconfigurable, and nonassigned environments. Open-plan offices, activity-based settings, flex offices, and hybrid arrangements can reduce employees' control over where they work, how long they occupy a particular workstation, and whether they can personalize or protect that space. Research has associated such workplace configurations and spatial transitions with privacy dissatisfaction, distraction, stress, and reduced environmental satisfaction (Bodin Danielsson & Bodin, 2008; de Croon et al., 2005; Kim & de Dear, 2013; Morrison & Macky, 2017). These findings establish the relevance of workplace design for employee experience, but they do not provide a dedicated measure of the territorial experience that may accompany changes in the use and allocation of workspaces.

Existing instruments assess related aspects of workplace experience, including workplace attachment (Rioux, 2006), environmental satisfaction (Moffat et al., 2016), perceived privacy (Kim & de Dear, 2013), and functional comfort (Vischer, 2007). However, these measures do not directly operationalize employees' perceived loss or weakening of the territorial conditions through which they express professional identity, anticipate the availability and organization of their workspace, and regulate access to it. We refer to this experience as perceived deterritorialization. Its theoretical foundations, proposed dimensional structure, and conceptual boundaries with neighbouring constructs are developed in the following literature review. These distinctions are conceptual at this stage and should not be interpreted as evidence of external discriminant validity.

A measure of perceived deterritorialization is needed because office type alone is a contextual descriptor rather than a measure of employees' subjective territorial experience.

Employees working in the same spatial configuration may differ in the extent to which they experience weakened spatial identification, reduced environmental predictability, or difficulty regulating territorial boundaries. A construct-specific instrument is therefore required before the relationships between perceived deterritorialization, its antecedents, neighbouring constructs, and possible outcomes can be examined empirically.

The present research reports the development and initial structural validation of the French-language Perceived Deterritorialization Scale (PDS). The development procedure was organized with reference to established recommendations for scale construction and evaluation (Boateng et al., 2018; DeVellis, 2017). Study 1 examined the structure of an initial 26-item pool among 340 French employees and applied prespecified retention criteria to identify a more parsimonious item set. Study 2 evaluated the resulting structure in an independent sample of 225 French employees through confirmatory factor analysis and comparison with three alternative measurement models. Additional analyses examined internal consistency, the separation among the proposed dimensions, measurement invariance across office configurations, and the sensitivity of item-selection results to sampling variation and to the operational application of the retention criteria.

Accordingly, the present studies provide evidence concerning the PDS's internal structure and internal consistency. They do not establish external convergent or discriminant validity, criterion or predictive validity, temporal stability, responsiveness to environmental change, or the psychometric equivalence of an English-language version. Until these properties are examined in independent research, the PDS should be regarded as a candidate Frenchlanguage measure with initial structural support.

2. Literature review

>Literature review

2.1. Workplace territoriality and the erosion of territorial functions

Territoriality refers to the processes through which individuals or groups claim, personalize, regulate, and defend their relationships with physical space (Altman, 1975). In workplace settings, these processes include the use of spatial markers, personal objects, routines, and boundary-regulation behaviours through which employees establish a recognizable relationship with their work environment (Brown et al., 2005). Workplace territory should therefore not be understood solely as formal ownership of a workstation. It also concerns the practical and symbolic functions that a relatively stable spatial domain can provide.

A functional account of workplace territoriality distinguishes identity-related and instrumental functions. Personalization allows employees to express aspects of their professional identity and establish continuity between themselves and their surroundings. Territorial markers can communicate occupancy and provide visible indications of how a space is associated with a particular person or role (Altman, 1975; Brown et al., 2005). At the same time, relatively stable occupancy can support environmental predictability by allowing employees to anticipate the availability, arrangement, and use of spatial resources. Territorial boundaries also contribute to the regulation of access, interruption, and social interaction, thereby linking territoriality to privacy-regulation processes in organizational environments (Altman, 1975; Sundstrom et al., 1980). Together, these functions depend on what we term territorial conditions: opportunities for personalization, continued or predictable occupation, and control over access to one's workspace.

Contemporary office arrangements can alter these conditions. Open-plan offices reduce physical enclosure and make access to individual work areas more permeable. Shared-desk and flex-office systems additionally separate employees from continuously assigned workstations, potentially limiting personalization and making the availability or configuration of workspace less predictable. These arrangements do not necessarily produce the same experience for every employee. Organizational rules, storage provisions, booking systems, team practices, and the degree of choice retained by employees may preserve some territorial functions even in nonassigned environments. Office configuration is therefore a contextual condition that may facilitate or constrain territorial experience, rather than a direct measure of that experience.

Empirical research nevertheless indicates that office configurations and spatial reorganizations are associated with privacy dissatisfaction, distraction, increased environmental demands, reduced perceived resources, and less favourable evaluations of the work environment (Bodin Danielsson & Bodin, 2008; de Croon et al., 2005; Kim & de Dear, 2013; Morrison & Macky, 2017). These findings establish the relevance of privacy, control, interaction, and environmental satisfaction within contemporary workplaces. They do not, however, directly assess whether employees perceive an erosion of the identity-related, predictive, and boundaryregulation functions supplied by workplace territories. The functional account of territoriality therefore provides the theoretical basis for defining perceived deterritorialization as a subjective experience, rather than treating office type or spatial reorganization itself as the construct.

2.2. Defining perceived deterritorialization and its conceptual boundaries

Building on the functional account of workplace territoriality, we define perceived deterritorialization as the subjective experience that the territorial conditions supporting one's relationship with the workspace have been weakened or removed. It concerns employees' perceptions that their workspace no longer adequately supports identity expression, environmental predictability, or the regulation of territorial boundaries. The construct therefore refers neither to a particular office configuration nor to spatial change in itself. Rather, it captures how employees experience the erosion of territorial functions within their working environment.

Perceived deterritorialization is conceptually related to, but not equivalent to, workplace attachment. Workplace attachment refers to an affective bond with the workplace that develops through continuing interactions between an individual and the work environment (Rioux, 2006).

Perceived deterritorialization instead concerns a perceived deprivation or weakening of territorial conditions. The constructs consequently differ in both their temporal logic and their psychological focus: attachment concerns a bond formed over time, whereas perceived deterritorialization concerns the perceived availability or loss of spatial conditions supporting territorial experience. They should not be treated as opposite poles of a single continuum.

Conceptually, an employee may report little attachment to a workplace while nevertheless perceiving restrictions on personalization, predictability, or boundary regulation; conversely, an attached employee may not experience deterritorialization while those territorial conditions remain available.

Perceived deterritorialization also differs from environmental satisfaction. Environmental satisfaction instruments evaluate employees' appraisal of multiple features of the workplace and the extent to which the environment meets their needs or expectations (Moffat et al., 2016). Such evaluations may encompass comfort, functionality, layout, or ambient conditions. Perceived deterritorialization has a narrower relational focus: it concerns the weakening of employees' ability to establish, recognize, and regulate a territorial relationship with their workspace. An employee may therefore be broadly satisfied with the physical quality of an environment while still perceiving a loss of territorial continuity or agency.

Perceived deterritorialization is likewise distinct from functional comfort. Comfort concerns the extent to which a setting supports the physical, functional, and psychological requirements of the work being performed (Vischer, 2007). A workstation may be well equipped, ergonomically adequate, and quiet while offering no opportunity for personalization, no continuity of occupation, and no control over access. Comfort therefore concerns the fit between the environment and the activity, whereas perceived deterritorialization concerns the availability of the conditions supporting a territorial relationship with that environment.

Finally, perceived deterritorialization overlaps conceptually with perceived privacy but is not reducible to it. Privacy measures primarily concern the regulation of social access, exposure, interruption, and communication within the workplace (Kim & de Dear, 2013). These processes are directly relevant to the territorial-boundary component of perceived deterritorialization, but they do not encompass the loss of spatial identity or the instability of workspace availability and configuration. Privacy is therefore treated as a neighbouring construct rather than as a synonym for deterritorialization.

These distinctions define the proposed conceptual domain of perceived deterritorialization; they do not establish its empirical distinctiveness. No neighbouring construct was administered in either study, and the boundaries advanced here should therefore be understood as theoretical propositions requiring direct examination in subsequent validation research.

2.3. Theoretical derivation of the three proposed dimensions

The proposed dimensional structure was derived deductively from the three territorial functions identified in Section 2.1. Each dimension represents the subjective experience expected when one of these functions is weakened: personalization and identity expression, continued or predictable occupation and environmental control, and boundary regulation through control over access. These dimensions were specified before item generation as theoretically proposed components of perceived deterritorialization, rather than as empirically established factors. Table 1 summarizes this derivation.

Spatial identity loss

The first proposed dimension derives from the identity function of territory. Personalization and relatively stable occupation allow physical settings to express aspects of their occupants' identities and provide continuity between the individual, their activities, and the surrounding environment. Proshansky (1978) conceptualized place identity as the component of self-identity formed through an individual's relationship with the physical environment.

In organizational settings, territorial markers can similarly communicate occupancy, professional role, and the association between a person and a particular spatial domain (Altman, 1975; Brown et al., 2005).

When opportunities for personalization or recognizable occupation are weakened, the workspace may cease to function as a material support for professional self-expression. Spatial identity loss therefore refers to the perceived weakening of this identity-supporting function. It does not designate low organizational identification or weak workplace attachment, but the more specific perception that the workspace no longer expresses or supports one's professional identity.

Environmental instability

The second proposed dimension derives from the instrumental functions of predictable occupation and environmental control. A stable or sufficiently predictable workspace allows employees to anticipate where they will work, whether an appropriate space will be available, how it will be configured, and where necessary resources can be found. Perceived freedom and control in built environments concern the availability of environmental choices and the capacity to anticipate the consequences of those choices (Barnes, 1981). Vischer's (2007) account of the workplace as a source of functional and psychological support likewise emphasizes the relationship between environmental conditions and occupants' ability to perform their activities.

When workspace availability, configuration, or use cannot be anticipated, this instrumental function may be weakened even if the physical environment remains comfortable or adequately equipped. Environmental instability therefore refers to perceived unpredictability in the spatial conditions of work. It concerns neither objective organizational change alone nor a general personal intolerance of uncertainty, but employees' appraisal that their immediate working environment cannot be experienced as sufficiently stable or predictable.

Territorial intrusion

The third proposed dimension derives from the boundary-regulation function of territory.

Territorial boundaries allow occupants to regulate access to themselves, their workspace, and the resources associated with it. Such boundaries need not be permanent or architectural; they may also be communicated through spatial markers, patterns of use, and shared expectations concerning occupancy. Altman's (1975) privacy-regulation framework treats privacy as a process through which individuals regulate social access, while Sundstrom et al. (1980) applied this logic to privacy and enclosure in organizational environments. When access can no longer be adequately regulated, other people's presence, use of the workspace, or interference with spatial resources may be experienced as encroachment. Territorial intrusion captures this perceived loss of boundary control. It is narrower than general privacy dissatisfaction because it specifically concerns perceived access to or use of a territorial domain, rather than all forms of exposure, interruption, or communication.

The three dimensions were expected to be distinguishable but related. They correspond to different territorial functions, yet the same workplace arrangement may weaken several functions simultaneously. A non-assigned workstation, for example, may limit personalization, reduce predictability, and alter control over access without making these experiences interchangeable.

The proposed three-dimensional structure was therefore treated as a theoretical specification to be evaluated empirically through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, rather than as a structure assumed to have been established before item development.

4.2. Item generation and expert review

An initial pool of 56 French-language candidate items was generated from the construct definition and the theoretical derivation presented in Section 2.3. Expert review of a candidate item pool prior to first administration is a standard component of scale development (Haynes et al., 1995; Hinkin, 1998). The complete pool was submitted as a single undifferentiated list to four doctoral-level researchers in environmental or work psychology, who reviewed it independently. They were asked to assess the relevance and clarity of the formulations and their suitability for measuring employees' subjective workplace experience.

The expert reviews converged on a 26-item selection. The 30 excluded formulations were primarily factual or descriptive statements concerning workplace equipment, amenities, habits, or physical features and were judged unsuitable for measuring a subjective psychological experience. The retained formulations were written in the first person, were relatively homogeneous in structure, and covered the three proposed domains of spatial identity loss, environmental instability, and territorial intrusion. The expert review therefore reduced the candidate pool from 56 to the 26 items subsequently administered without further wording changes. The expert review concerned the resulting item pool; the dimensional framework itself was not submitted for a separate expert evaluation before item generation. No quantitative content validity index was computed.

Of the 26 administered items, 19 were target items intended to represent the proposed dimensions. Seven were retained as content-boundary items because, although formulated as subjective workplace evaluations, they did not theoretically define perceived deterritorialization.

These items represented three types of neighbouring content: spatial features not defining territoriality, namely furniture functionality and parking organization; general work evaluations, namely meeting frequency, relations with colleagues, and managerial feedback; and broader environmental or work-related conditions, namely temperature and commuting. Their inclusion allowed the exploratory analysis to test whether the empirical domain expanded beyond territorial experience. Absorption of these items into the retained structure would have indicated an overly broad operational domain, whereas their non-retention would be consistent with the proposed content boundary.

No cognitive interviews or qualitative pilot study were conducted with members of the target population. The expert review addressed content relevance and wording clarity from the perspective of researchers, but it did not establish how employees understood the questions. This omitted development step is represented in Figure 1 and considered in the limitations of the study.

All 26 items were rated on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). They were administered in a fixed but interspersed order, identical for all participants, so that items associated with the same proposed dimension were distributed throughout the questionnaire. The item labels used in the analyses reflect their analytic organization rather than their order of presentation. The analytic sample contained no missing responses on the 26 items.

4.3. Data analysis

All analyses reported in the article were conducted from the source workbook in R 4.6.0.

Exploratory analyses used psych 2.6.5. Confirmatory models used lavaan 0.6-21 with robust maximum-likelihood estimation; composite reliability, average variance extracted, and HTMT were obtained with semTools 0.5-8. Figures were produced using ggplot2 4.0.3 and semPlot 1.1.8. The complete session information and fixed random seeds are recorded in the reproducible analysis script.

Sampling adequacy was examined using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin statistic and Bartlett's test of sphericity. The number of factors was determined from parallel analysis, the scree pattern, and theoretical interpretability. Parallel analysis (Horn, 1965) used principal-axis factor eigenvalues and the 95th percentile of the corresponding values obtained from 1,000 simulated correlation matrices. Raw correlation-matrix eigenvalues and the Kaiser criterion were reported descriptively but were not used as the primary retention rule because this criterion frequently overextracts factors (Fabrigar et al., 1999). The parallel analyses of the 26-and 15-item sets used fixed seeds of 20260830 and 20260831, respectively. Because the fourth factor in the initial pool was the closest comparison, its simulated reference value was additionally examined across ten fixed seeds, 20260830-20260839.

Principal-axis factoring with Oblimin rotation was used because the proposed dimensions were expected to correlate. Item-retention criteria were applied once to the complete three-factor solution for the 26-item pool. An item was retained when its absolute primary pattern loading was at least .40 and every secondary loading was below .30. The procedure was not implemented as sequential item-by-item deletion. The sensitivity of the retained composition to this operational choice was examined in a supplementary analysis reported in Section 5.5. Appendix B reports the pattern coefficients, communalities, and decision for every item in the administered 26-item pool.

5. Study 2: Confirmatory structural evidence

>Study 2: Confirmatory structural evidence

5.1. Participants and procedure

Study 2 comprised an independent sample of 225 French employees working in office settings, including 128 women (56.9%) and 97 men (43.1%). Participants were aged between 22 and 62 years (M = 36.65, SD = 9.19), and organizational tenure ranged from 0.5 to 30 years (M = 5.51, SD = 5.85). Recruitment, eligibility, consent, and ethical procedures followed the same general approach as in Study 1.

Because recruitment did not use a closed sampling frame, a response rate could not be calculated. Office configuration was not recorded in Study 2. Participants completed the 15 French-language items retained in Study 1 using the same five-point response scale. This sample had no role in item generation or retention, and the 11 items excluded in Study 1 were not administered.

5.2. Confirmatory factor analysis

Four measurement models were estimated using robust maximum likelihood. Model 1 specified a single general factor. Model 2 retained spatial identity loss as one factor and combined environmental instability and territorial intrusion into a second factor. Model 3 specified three mutually orthogonal factors. Model 4 specified the theoretically proposed three correlated factors. No post hoc correlated residuals, cross-loadings, or item deletions were introduced.

Model fit was evaluated using robust χ², CFI, TLI, RMSEA with its 90% confidence interval, and SRMR. The correlated three-factor model provided the best fit, robust χ²(87) = 109.39, p = .053, CFI = .982, TLI = .978, RMSEA = .034, 90% CI [.000, .052], SRMR = .044.

5.3. Internal consistency and internal factor distinctiveness

Cronbach's alpha was .840 for spatial identity loss, .823 for environmental instability, and .818 for territorial intrusion. Composite reliability was .843, .824, and .820, respectively. Average variance extracted was .522 for spatial identity loss, .484 for environmental instability, and .481 for territorial intrusion. The latter two values were slightly below the conventional .50 reference value, indicating that evidence based on AVE was mixed rather than uniformly strong.

Composite reliability nevertheless exceeded .80 for every factor. HTMT values were .475 for spatial identity loss-environmental instability, .343 for spatial identity loss-territorial intrusion, and .404 for environmental instability-territorial intrusion. These values, together with the moderate latent correlations and the superior fit of the correlated three-factor model, support the internal separation of the three proposed dimensions.

They do not establish discriminant validity relative to external constructs because no neighbouring measure was administered.

5.4. Supplementary measurement-invariance analysis

To examine whether measurement parameters were comparable across office configurations, multigroup CFA was conducted using Study 1, the only sample in which office type was recorded: individual offices (n = 99), open-plan offices with assigned workstations (n = 113), and flex-office arrangements without assigned workstations (n = 128). Configural, metric, and scalar models were estimated using MLR. Changes in robust CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR were interpreted jointly as descriptive guidelines because their performance depends on sample size, group balance, and the pattern of non-invariance (Chen, 2007). Satorra-Bentler corrected χ²difference tests (Satorra & Bentler, 2001) were also reported.

The configural model showed acceptable fit, CFI = .974, RMSEA = .040, SRMR = .058.

Imposing equality constraints on all loadings produced limited changes in approximate fit, ΔCFI = -.006, ΔRMSEA = .003, and ΔSRMR = .014. However, the corrected difference test was significant, Δχ²(24) = 37.31, p = .041. This combination suggests statistically detectable differences in loadings whose overall magnitude was limited according to the approximate indices.

Ordinary score tests were examined as diagnostic localization tools because robust score tests were not implemented for MLR in the software used. For PDS_5, the tests indicated a departure of the individual-office loading from the equality imposed on the open-plan and flexoffice groups, χ²(1) = 8.80, p = .003. For PDS_12, the flex-office loading departed from the equality imposed on the open-plan and individual-office groups, χ²(1) = 10.34, p = .001. A weaker signal concerned PDS_14, χ²(1) = 3.53, p = .060, which did not reach the conventional significance threshold. These tests concern unstandardized loadings and are conditional on the remaining equality constraints; they should not be interpreted as independent pairwise comparisons.

For descriptive purposes, standardized loadings from the configural model were .635, .770, and .783 for PDS_5 in the individual-office, open-plan, and flex-office groups, respectively. Corresponding loadings for PDS_12 were .712, .731, and .905, and those for PDS_14 were .728, .662, and .627. These standardized estimates describe the group-specific loading patterns but are not numerically equivalent to the unstandardized constraints evaluated by the score tests. No separate score test directly contrasted the flex-office and individual-office loadings.

A partial metric model allowing the loadings of PDS_5 and PDS_12 to vary across groups did not differ from the configural model, Δχ²(20) = 14.82, p = .787, and fitted better than the fully constrained metric model, Δχ²(4) = 21.70, p < .001. Because full metric invariance was not supported by the corrected difference test, scalar invariance was subsequently evaluated from the partial metric specification. Adding equality constraints on the item intercepts produced a partial scalar model with CFI = .978, RMSEA = .034, and SRMR = .067. This model did not differ from the partial metric model, Δχ²(24) = 22.51, p = .549.

As a sensitivity check, the loading of PDS_14 was additionally allowed to vary across groups. This less constrained model did not improve fit relative to the partial metric model that released only PDS_5 and PDS_12, Δχ²(2) = 1.58, p = .454. PDS_14 was therefore retained under equality constraints in the primary partial-invariance models. Note. The results provisionally support partial scalar invariance rather than complete scalar invariance. The non-invariance was localized post hoc in the Study 1 sample, and the group sizes were modest relative to the complexity of the model. Consequently, comparisons of latent means across office configurations would remain conditional on the partially invariant specification. An independent replication should prespecify the released PDS_5 and PDS_12 loading constraints rather than repeat an exploratory search for non-invariant parameters. The observed loading differences may be contextually meaningful, but the multigroup analysis alone does not establish differences in how respondents interpreted the items.

5.5. Item-selection stability and procedural sensitivity

The independent CFA confirms the internal structure of the selected 15-item model but cannot establish the reproducibility of item selection because the 11 excluded candidates were not administered in Study 2. Two supplementary analyses therefore examined the sensitivity of the retained composition to sampling variation and to the operational application of the retention criteria.

Repeated split-sample stability

The Study 1 sample was randomly divided 1,000 times using a fixed seed of 20260829. All partitions were generated before any factor analyses were conducted. In each repetition, the complete retention rule was reapplied to a random half-sample of 170 participants. Because these half-samples overlapped and were drawn from the same dataset, the procedure is described as a repeated split-sample stability analysis rather than as cross-validation or independent replication. This sequential specification retained 16 items: the published 15-item configuration plus PDS_23. The result demonstrates that the decision concerning this item depends partly on how the retention criteria are operationalized. The sequential rule was one post hoc specification among several possible implementations; it does not reconstruct the historical procedure and does not establish that a 16-item version would be preferable. Taken together, the independent CFA and the supplementary sensitivity analyses support the 15-item PDS as a defensible and structurally confirmed configuration, but not as a uniquely determined or demonstrably optimal item set.

6. General discussion

>General discussion

6.1. Summary and theoretical contribution

The present research developed and examined the internal structure of a French-language measure of perceived deterritorialization. An initial set of 56 candidate formulations was reduced through independent qualitative review by four doctoral-level researchers to a 26-item pool suitable for administration. Exploratory analysis of this pool supported three correlated dimensions derived from territorial functions: spatial identity loss, environmental instability, and territorial intrusion. Fifteen items satisfied the prespecified loading criteria. In an independent sample, the correlated three-factor model fitted substantially better than the unidimensional, twofactor, and orthogonal alternatives, and all retained items loaded significantly on their proposed factors.

The principal theoretical contribution is the operational distinction between three ways in which territorial conditions may be experienced as weakened. Spatial identity loss concerns the perception that the workspace no longer supports professional self-expression or continuity.

Environmental instability concerns the perceived unpredictability of workspace availability, configuration, and spatial landmarks. Territorial intrusion concerns weakened control over access to or encroachment on the workspace. The moderate correlations among these dimensions are consistent with related territorial experiences rather than mutually independent phenomena. At the same time, the poor fit of the one-factor model indicates that the three dimensions should not be treated as interchangeable manifestations of an undifferentiated experience.

The results also qualify the status of the retained item composition. The independent CFA supports the internal structure of the 15 administered items, but it cannot validate their selection because the 11 excluded candidates were not administered in Study 2. The repeated split-sample analysis indicated a stable retained core but a less stable exclusion boundary, while a post hoc sequential implementation of the same loading thresholds retained PDS_23 in addition to the 15item configuration. The present results therefore support the 15-item form as a defensible and structurally confirmed configuration, not as a uniquely determined or demonstrably optimal item set.

The multigroup analyses provided provisional evidence that the measurement structure was broadly comparable across workplace configurations, while also identifying two localized loading differences. For PDS_5, the individual-office group departed from the equality imposed on the open-plan and flex-office groups. For PDS_12, the flex-office group departed from the equality imposed on the open-plan and individual-office groups. These post hoc findings indicate that the relations between these items and their respective dimensions were not entirely uniform across configurations. They do not, however, establish why these differences occurred or whether respondents interpreted the items differently. Confirmatory replication in an independent sample is therefore required.

6.2. Scope of use

At its present stage, the PDS is appropriate for methodological research and continued psychometric evaluation of perceived deterritorialization in French-speaking samples. The available evidence supports interpretation of three dimension scores for research on internal structure. It does not support organizational diagnosis, individual assessment, diagnostic thresholds, or evidence-based recommendations concerning workplace design.

The poor fit of the unidimensional model does not support the use of a single undifferentiated total score. Until a higher-order or bifactor structure is explicitly evaluated, spatial identity loss, environmental instability, and territorial intrusion should be scored and reported separately. No normative values or interpretive cutoffs were established.

The PDS should not yet be used to estimate the psychological consequences of a spatial reorganization, evaluate change before and after a workplace transition, guide an organizational intervention, or draw conclusions about stress, burnout, turnover, or other employee outcomes.

Such applications require external construct validation, criterion evidence, temporal-stability data, and demonstrated responsiveness to environmental change.

Partial scalar invariance may permit comparisons of latent means when the non-invariant parameters are explicitly modelled and sufficient invariant indicators remain to anchor each factor. No latent-mean comparisons were conducted in the present research. Because PDS_5 and PDS_12 were identified post hoc and the office-configuration groups were modest in size, substantive comparisons across configurations should await independent confirmation of the partial-invariance specification.

Finally, all psychometric evidence concerns the French items. The English wording presented in Appendix A serves only to describe item content and should not be treated as an English-language version of the PDS.

6.3. Limitations

First, although the qualitative expert review addressed item relevance and wording clarity, no quantitative content-validity index was computed and no cognitive interviews or qualitative pilot study was conducted with members of the target population. Expert appraisal and cognitive pretesting serve different purposes: the former evaluates content from a disciplinary perspective, whereas the latter examines how intended respondents understand and answer the questions.

Consequently, the present study does not establish that employees interpreted every item as intended. Future development should include cognitive interviewing before further psychometric evaluation.

Second, although the initial qualitative pool was broad, the administered pool contained only 19 target items, four more than the final 15-item configuration. The quantitative stage therefore offered limited surplus for selection within the proposed dimensions. The equal fiveitem subscales reflect the composition of the administered pool and the loading criteria rather than evidence that five items is the optimal length for every dimension. The repeated splitsample results and sequential sensitivity analysis further showed that decisions concerning marginal target items were affected by sampling variation and by the operational application of the retention criteria.

Third, neither study included measures of neighbouring constructs. The present evidence concerning HTMT, AVE, factor correlations, and competing models addresses separation among the three internal dimensions only. It does not establish external convergent or discriminant validity relative to workplace attachment, environmental satisfaction, perceived privacy, or functional comfort.

Fourth, no external criterion was measured. The studies therefore provide no evidence that PDS scores are concurrently or prospectively associated with perceived stress, occupational exhaustion, turnover intention, job satisfaction, organizational identification, or other employee outcomes. The practical relevance of the construct remains a theoretical proposition rather than an empirical finding of the present research.

Fifth, no longitudinal or repeated-measures data were collected. Perceived deterritorialization is conceptualized as a perception tied to relatively durable spatial conditions rather than as a purely momentary state. This conceptualization implies moderate-to-high stability while relevant workplace conditions remain unchanged and responsiveness when those conditions change. Neither temporal stability nor responsiveness to environmental change was tested.

Sixth, recruitment was non-probabilistic, the invitation denominator was unavailable, and response rates could not be calculated. Self-selection may therefore have affected the composition of both samples; employees who had experienced more pronounced spatial change may have been more likely to respond. The measurement-invariance analysis was conducted in the Study 1 development sample, with group sizes ranging from 99 to 128, and its non-invariant parameters were identified post hoc. Office configuration was not recorded in Study 2, preventing independent confirmation of the invariance results. All participants were recruited in France, which also limits generalization to cultural contexts in which workspace norms and territorial practices may differ.

6.4. Future validation

The next stage should begin with cognitive interviews involving employees from individual-office, open-plan, and flex-office settings. These interviews should examine comprehension, retrieval, judgment, and response processes for each item, with particular attention to PDS_5 and PDS_12 and to employees who have never occupied an individually assigned workstation. The findings could support wording refinement before further quantitative validation.

External construct validation should then administer the PDS jointly with established French-language measures of workplace attachment, satisfaction with the physical work environment, and perceived control over and privacy within the workspace. This study should test whether the PDS is related to these neighbouring constructs while remaining empirically distinguishable from them. It should also determine whether the three dimensions explain unique variance beyond these neighbouring assessments of workplace experience.

Criterion validation should distinguish concurrent association from prediction. A crosssectional study could examine concurrent relations with validated French-language measures of perceived stress, work-related exhaustion, turnover intention, and organizational identification.

The theoretical derivation supports three dimension-specific hypotheses: environmental instability should be most strongly associated with stress-related outcomes because it concerns reduced predictability; territorial intrusion should be most strongly associated with privacyrelated outcomes because it concerns boundary regulation; and spatial identity loss should be most strongly associated with organizational identification because it concerns the workspace's support for professional self-expression. These propositions are hypotheses for future testing, not findings of the present studies.

Predictive validity would require a prospective design in which outcomes are measured after PDS scores and their baseline levels are controlled. Cross-sectional correlations should not be interpreted as prediction. A test-retest study should separately assess temporal stability over a two-to four-week interval among employees whose spatial conditions are verified as unchanged.

A longitudinal study surrounding an actual workplace reorganization should examine responsiveness to environmental change using measurements before and after the transition.

Intensive repeated-measures research could additionally determine whether daily fluctuations coexist with the more durable component proposed here.

An independent replication should administer the complete 26-item pool rather than only the retained items. It should test the present 15-item configuration as a prespecified model and compare it with a prespecified alternative that includes PDS_23. This would distinguish confirmation of the proposed configuration from renewed data-driven selection. Measurement invariance should also be retested with larger groups, directly covering all office-configuration contrasts and prespecifying the released PDS_5 and PDS_12 loadings.

If an English-language adaptation is developed, it should undergo independent forward translation, back-translation, reconciliation by a bilingual committee, cognitive pretesting in the target language, and psychometric evaluation in an anglophone sample. Cross-language measurement invariance would then be required before scores from the French and English versions could be compared.

6.5. Conclusion

The present studies provide initial structural support for a French-language measure of perceived deterritorialization comprising spatial identity loss, environmental instability, and territorial intrusion. The correlated three-factor structure was supported in exploratory analysis and fitted well in an independent confirmatory sample, while each dimension demonstrated acceptable internal consistency. Supplementary analyses identified both a stable retained core and limits to the uniqueness of the selected item composition, together with provisional partial invariance across office configurations.

The 15-item PDS should therefore be regarded as a theoretically derived candidate configuration for continued validation. Its external distinctiveness, criterion relevance, temporal stability, responsiveness to environmental change, and generalizability beyond the present samples remain to be established. Procedural sensitivity

A separate post hoc analysis examined whether applying the same loading thresholds sequentially altered the retained composition. The three-factor principal-axis solution with Oblimin rotation was re-estimated after each removal. At each iteration, one item violating either the .40 primary-loading threshold or the .30 cross-loading threshold was removed, using its minimum margin from the two thresholds to identify the weakest item. The process stopped when every remaining item satisfied both criteria.

Figure1maps the procedures actually conducted onto this framework and distinguishes them from recommended procedures that were not undertaken in the present research.During item development, 56 French-language candidate formulations were generated and independently reviewed by four doctoral-level researchers in environmental or work psychology. The qualitative review produced a 26-item pool judged suitable for assessing subjective workplace experience; 30 primarily factual or descriptive formulations were excluded before data collection. The administered pool comprised 19 target items and seven contentboundary items. The theoretical specification of the construct domain and its three proposed dimensions, presented in Sections 2.1 and 2.3, preceded item writing. The expert review concerned the constituted pool; the three proposed dimensions were not evaluated separately by experts before item generation.Scale development was conducted in Study 1 with 340 French employees. The pool was administered in a single fixed, interspersed order. Exploratory factor analysis was used to examine the proposed structure and apply prespecified primary-loading and cross-loading criteria, detailed in Section 4.3, to the complete 26-item solution. This process resulted in a 15item set representing three correlated dimensions. No cognitive pretest or qualitative pilot study with members of the target population was conducted before administration. This omitted step is shown explicitly in Figure1and is acknowledged as a limitation of the development procedure.Scale evaluation was continued in Study 2 using an independent sample of 225 French employees. Confirmatory factor analysis evaluated the retained three-factor correlated structure against three alternative measurement models. Internal consistency, composite reliability, average variance extracted, and the internal separation of the three proposed dimensions were also examined. Supplementary analyses assessed measurement invariance across office configurations in the Study 1 sample, the stability of item-retention results across repeated split samples, and the sensitivity of the retained composition to the operational application of the retention criteria.The present research therefore addresses dimensionality and internal consistency while providing limited evidence concerning the internal separation of the proposed dimensions. It does not include cognitive pretesting, test-retest reliability, or external convergent, discriminant, criterion, and predictive validation. Figure1represents these procedures as components of the broader development framework that were not conducted rather than as completed stages of the present research.

Participants were 340 French employees working in office settings. The sample comprised 197 women (57.9%) and 143 men (42.1%), aged between 22 and 62 years (M = 36.55, SD = 9.24). Organizational tenure ranged from 0.5 to 30 years (M = 5.73, SD = 5.26). Ninety-nine participants worked in individual offices (29.1%), defined as enclosed workspaces assigned to one person; 113 worked in open-plan offices with assigned workstations (33.2%); and 128 worked in flex-office arrangements without assigned workstations (37.6%). Participants were recruited through professional networks, organizational contacts, and online channels. Eligibility required being at least 18 years old, currently employed in an officebased role, and working primarily from the organization's premises. Because the survey link circulated through open networks rather than a closed sampling frame, the number of people who received the invitation is unknown and a response rate cannot be calculated. Recruitment was therefore non-probabilistic and potentially subject to self-selection. The survey was anonymous and non-interventional. Participants received information concerning the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and their right to discontinue at any time. They provided informed consent before beginning the survey. No formal research-ethics committee approval was sought because the anonymous, non-interventional survey did not fall within the scope of French legislation requiring approval by a Comité de Protection des Personnes. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the ethical guidance of the French Psychological Society, and applicable dataprotection requirements.
