Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Across different countries and contexts, parental socioeconomic status (SES) has been consistently associated with childhood cognitive performance. Differences in cognitive ability predict a variety of meaningful, later-life outcomes, making it paramount to understand cognition's etiology and potential malleability. Despite this, prior longitudinal work examining the influence of SES on later adolescent cognitive trajectories is mixed -with support for 1) widening differences (whereby children with low and high SES diverge), 2) stable/parallel trends, and 3) lessening differences whereby the gap between low and high SES children decreases. Here, we test these competing developmental dynamics in a large cohort of adolescents (N = 9,760, 4 waves) using cutting-edge nonlinear growth curve modeling. Doing so, we find widening cognitive inequality in the United States and identify early adolescence as particularly salient. Given the wide-ranging impact of interindividual differences in cognitive performance, it is paramount to reduce socioeconomic disparities in childhood.

[div-01]

Interindividual differences in cognitive ability are one of the most predictive and replicable psychological traits 1, 2 . Put succinctly, children who do well on one cognitive task are more than likely to do well on all other cognitive tasks (conversely, for those who perform poorly) 3 . Cognitive performance differences demonstrate robust associations with a variety of meaningful life outcomes, such as health outcomes, life expectancy, educational attainment, life and job satisfaction 1, 4, 5 . This wide-ranging impact of cognitive performance makes it paramount to understand its etiology and potential malleability 6, 7.

Cognitive malleability has been a heated topic since its discovery -yet the prevailing view is that both genetic and environmental factors shape it 6, 8 . Attempts to increase children's cognitive (or even longer-term academic) outcomes have been much more challenging and commonly result in small effect sizes [9] [10] [11] [12] . Yet there are many well-established negative influences causing decreases in cognitive performance (e.g., environmental toxins 13 , childhood neglect 14 , and traumatic brain injury 15 ) with lifelong developmental consequences.

Another body of evidence examines cognitive malleability through large environmental changes rather than by isolating specific causes, such as family environments (adoption studies 16 ), cohort differences (Flynn effect 17 ), and proxies of environmental exposure differences (socioeconomic status 18, 19 ). Parental socioeconomic status (SES) -an index of parental income, education, and neighborhood quality -is consistently positively associated with childhood cognitive performance and academic achievement 20, 21 . Suggesting that unequal access to resources leads to inequality in children's cognitive outcomes.

Unfortunately, most prior work is cross-sectional, limiting insight into when and how this divide develops. For instance, early life environmental or genetic influences could be the predominant, or sole, driver of these effects 22 . In addition, any translational public policy implications rely on understanding when (and how) socioeconomics contributes to a widening or narrowing gap over childhood development. Longitudinal data is necessary to understand three possible developmental dynamics: 1) widening differences (whereby children with low and high SES diverge), 2) stable trends (they stay the same, developing in parallel -consistent yet not indicative of the hypothesis of cognition as a stable trait), or 3) lessening differences whereby the gap between low and high SES children decreases (Figure 1a).

Prior evidence on school-age socioeconomic divide dynamics is largely inconclusive, finding positive 23, 24 , null [25] [26] [27] , or negative 28, 29 associations of parental SES on cognitive development. Three major challenges contribute to this lack of clarity; 1) limited longitudinal datasets with adequate cognitive tasks, 2) statistical measurement challenges 21 (outlined in Sup. Methods) and 3) contextual differences between samples (e.g., the consequences of childhood inequality will differ across countries and cohorts). For instance, notable work in a large 1990's UK longitudinal cohort found the size of parental SES predicted cognition gaps to triple between the ages of two and sixteen 23 . Despite addressing limitations in prior work 21 , the large age range necessitated the use of different tasks; therefore, it is possible that measurement artifacts (e.g., test reliability increasing with age) contributed to the positive association found. Moreover, generational and national differences preclude direct applicability to policymakers in the United States.

Here, we fill this gap by examining how differences in parental SES are associated with trajectories of cognitive development in a large cohort of adolescents in the United States (N = 9,760, ages 10 -16, born 2007/8) 30 . Crucially, we use the same tasks measured four times longitudinally from 10 to 16 years of age and employ advanced modeling techniques to lessen the impact of prior statistical concerns. Allowing us -in a contemporary sample of US adolescents -map how early parental SES predicts later adolescent cognitive trajectories.

First, we first combine four cognitive tasks per time point and ensure it is the same concept throughout time (i.e., measurement invariance; Sup. Table 1). As expected, this cognitive performance factor demonstrates high stability over time and correlated positively with a variety of other tasks (Sup. Figure 5 & 8). In addition, we find the latent cognitive variance to increase over adolescence (by 30% from 10 -16 years of age), possibly indicating growing interindividual differences over time.

Having established a longitudinal cognitive performance factor, we use a multi-indicator nonlinear growth curve modeling framework to measure cognitive development (outline in Sup. Methods Section 2). Our modeling framework was explicitly chosen to circumvent statistical critics on prior, influential work relating children's academic achievement with parental SES 21.

Over early adolescence, we find an average of two standard deviations in cognitive improvement (p < .001), which slows notably as children enter high school (Figure 1b; Sup. Table 3). Children's rate of change over early adolescence (i.e., their within-person slopes) was only weakly correlated (r = 0.18, p < .001) with their initial levels of cognitive performance at ten years of age (T1; intercept). This gives an early indication that the factors driving interindividual differences in change over adolescence are distinct from those associated with initial cognitive differences.

Having successfully isolated cognitive performance trajectories over early adolescence, we proceed to test the extent to which parental SES predicts their children's initial cognitive performance levels at 10 years of age (intercept) and the subsequent development over early adolescence (slopes). To do so, we enter SES and age at baseline as covariates in a strong measurement-invariant latent basis growth curve model predicting the intercept and the slope over adolescence (Sup. Figure 7). Notably, for slope effects, we report results with two standardization approaches: the default, standardized by slope variance (β), and another standardizing by the variance of the intercept (βT1). These complementary approaches provide a richer understanding of SES's magnitude on cognitive development, either relative to the amount of change during early adolescence or relative to baseline interindividual differences.

As expected, we find a large relationship between parental SES and initial levels of cognitive performance at 10 years of age (βT1 = 0.58, p < .001) -the association is positive; initial levels of cognitive performance are higher in children from higher SES backgrounds. Having replicated prior cross-sectional findings, we move on to test, in a sample of contemporary adolescents from the United States: To what extent does a parent's socioeconomic background predict the development of their children's cognition over adolescence?

We find a moderate effect of parental socioeconomic status on the cognitive trajectories (β = .27, p < .001; Figure 1c; Sup. Table 3). This effect is positive, indicating widening cognitive differences over adolescence -children from the most well-off families showing accelerated cognitive growth compared to children with less well-off parents (i.e., rich-get-richer; Matthew's effect). We conduct a variety of secondary tests to ensure robustness. A similar pattern emerges across alternative specifications: 1) examining each SES component separately (Figure 1c; Sup. Table 3), 2) controlling for site differences (Sup. Table 6), 3) examining each task separately (one had ceiling effects; see Sup. Methods 3.3 & Sup. Table 5), 4) using a numerical cognition test administered at different time points (Sup. Figure 1, Sup. Table 7). The addition of a numerical cognition test was particularly informative. Despite being administered at different ages (13, 15, 17 years of age; Sup. Figure 1 & 3), this task showed a strikingly similar pattern to cognitive performance; 1) a relatively low correlation between initial levels and growth (i.e., intercept-slope; r = .13), 2) non-linear growth, with most (66%) happening in middle school, and 3) a large effect of parental SES on initial levels (i.e., intercept; β = .42, p < .001) and, importantly, a similarly sized effect of parental SES on adolescent change (i.e., slope; β = .32, p < .001). Lastly, we find intra-individual change over adolescence to be modestly correlated between numerical cognition and the cognitive tests (i.e., slope-slope correlation; r = .37, p < .001). This series of robustness tests helps solidify a pattern of parental SES predicting widening cognitive differences within this contemporary cohort of US adolescents. 3). These coefficients are positive, indicative of diverging cognitive development (a rich-get-richer or Matthew's effect). We report slope effects with two standardization methods [see Sup. Table 4 for variance standardization by initial cognitive differences (i.e., yellow cloud in panel b)].

Next, we focus on the size of this slope effect in reference to our cross-sectional (intercept) findings -as this reflects the cumulative genetic and early life influences up until the first time point (roughly 10 years of age). As expected, this standardization yields a smaller effect size (βT1 = .11, p < .001; Sup. Table 4) with 80% of this effect contained within the middle school years (ages 10 to 14). Yielding a per-year trajectory divergence of one-third the yearly intercept effect size during this period. This yearly heuristic is underestimated for two reasons; 1) we assume the intercept effect is linear from gestation until 10 years of age, and 2) adolescent effects focus on within-subject change -in turn, removing stable time-invariant causes/confounders 31 . These effects may be substantial, as fetal influences, static genetic effects, and early-childhood factors have established large effects on cognitive performance and differentially affect individuals across socioeconomic gradients.

To gauge the magnitude of the correlative effect of parental SES on adolescent cognitive performance at 16 years old, we combine the initial observed association with the growing gaps we find over adolescence. The current standardized effect performance gap we find in the United States between children from differing SES backgrounds is distressingly large -a 16-year-old with parents earning on average over $100,000 (+1SD SES) performs 1.4SDs (or 20 IQ points) above a child whose parents on average earn less than $35,000 (-1SD SES).

In summary, we find parental SES to be a major predictor of interindividual differences in their children's cognitive growth over adolescence. Crucially, these effects are present in a contemporary cohort of Gen Z adolescents from the United States (at the time of writing, they are 18/19 years old). While our results align with prior longitudinal work in different contexts 21, 23 , we identify middle school as a meaningful period of cognitive malleability. Given the wideranging impact of interindividual differences in cognitive performance, it is paramount to lessen these gaps.

Our findings suggest that, despite past challenges [9] [10] [11] [12] , interventions to raise cognitive performance during middle school can still be effective, and genetically informed interventions (twin, polygenic scores, etc.) 22, 32, 33 offer promise for understanding heterogeneous response profiles. Difficulties remain in isolating and identifying the specific, causal environmental exposures that parental socioeconomic status indexes by proxy. Neighborhood-level factors are most likely meaningful contributors to diverging cognitive trajectories, and future work with quasi-experimental designs is essential 34, 35 . Lastly, while cognitive performance is predictive of a wide range of meaningful life outcomes, it is not the be-all-and-end-all of childhood development, and there are a variety of other adaptive skills which low-SES individuals possess over their wealthier peers 36.

Supplementary methods

Cognitive and socioeconomic data were acquired from the ABCD study (v. 7) -the largest, most comprehensive longitudinal resource on child health and brain development. The ABCD consortium is an ongoing multi-center study that recruited 9 -11-year-old children, who are longitudinally followed up in two-year increments for ten years. Our analysis focuses on the first six years with four approximately equally spaced 2-year waves (ABCD v7.0). Children had four cognitive tasks over four measurement occasions at 10 (ses-00A), 12 (ses-02A), 14 (ses-04A) and 16 (ses-06A) years of age. We include an additional robustness analysis using a numerical fluency test (SMARTE) which was administered three times in yearly staggered fashion at 13 (ses-03A), 15 (ses-05A), 17 (ses-07A) years of age.

As the ABCD consortium includes twins and siblings, we sampled the individual with the most follow-up attendance (if equal random sampling was used), resulting in a sample of 9,760 children with wave 1 data (starting N = 11,868). Ethics was reviewed and approved by the central institutional review board at the University of California, San Diego, and granted by the Swedish Ethical Authority. Written parental informed consent, along with child assent, was obtained for all participants 1 . All analyses were completed in R² , and scripts are available online (https://github.com/njudd/msd).

We use a latent growth curve modeling approach to minimize potential measurement artifacts and have dedicated a section addressing limitations of prior similar work. All models were built in a nested fashion -taking a measurement approach, whereby we confirmed model fit and adequate measurement before testing the associational effect of parental socioeconomics' on cognitive development.

1. Measurement

>Measurement

1.1 Cognitive Performance

Prior to fitting inferential models, we took a 'measurement approach' using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to combine four cognitive tests (Picture Vocabulary, Picture Sequence Memory, Oral Reading Recognition, and Working Memory 2-back) 3, 4 into a latent cognitive performance factor for each of the four time points (see Sup. Figure 4 & 5). We chose these tasks because they were administered across the four time points and balanced fluid and crystallized domains. Briefly, three tasks are from the NIH behavioral toolbox (https://nihtoolbox.org/domain/cognition/). Picture vocabulary measures receptive vocabulary (crystallized skill) in a computerized adaptive format in which participants hear an audio recording of a word and select the best matching picture (out of four). Picture sequence assesses episodic memory (fluid skill) by having the participant recall an increasingly lengthy series of illustrated objects presented in a particular order. In oral reading recognition (crystallized skill) the child is asked to read and pronounce letters and words as accurately as possible, while a test administrator scores them. Lastly, we included a Working Memory n-back task not from the NIH toolbox; we administered it in an fMRI scanner and recorded the total number of correct responses. As this task is only a 2-back visuospatial working memory task, it has significant ceiling artifacts for older and higher ability children (we therefore run robustness checks without it & address this below).

We combined tasks in a longitudinal CFA for three primary reasons: 1) conceptually, it allowed us to measure general cognitive performance; 2) it allowed explicit tests of measurement invariance, making sure it is the same concept through time; and 3) lastly, benefits from increased power by removing measurement error (Sup. Figure 5). In line with best practice 5 , all models were specified with correlated error variance within tasks over time. The lavaan R package (v. 0.7-2) 6 was used with full-information maximum-likelihood estimation and a robust maximum-likelihood estimator with a Yuan-Bentler-scaled test statistic. To account for missing or incomplete test data were dealt with using Full Information Maximum Likelihood estimation 7 . This approach leverages all available data, increases power, and decreases bias compared to simply omitting individuals with partial data 8 . We determined adequate (CFI > .9; RMSEA < .08) and good (CFI > .95; RMSEA < .05) fit using the CFI and RMSEA cutoffs 9.

We then tested for longitudinal measurement invariance to ensure the latent factors represent the same concept throughout time -a prerequisite for any change-based analysis using summary measures. Briefly, a series of model comparisons, in ascending order of strictness, is run to determine longitudinal measurement invariance (see 10 ). Due to the large sample size of ABCD, we tested invariance via a change of ≥ -.010 in CFI, supplemented by a change of ≥ .015 in RMSEA 11 . We did not test strict measurement invariance (error variances fixed through time) as this was not necessary for our statistical inferences. A strong measurement invariance longitudinal CFA fit the data well (scaled χ2 = 2200, df =112, p <0.0001, RMSEA = .055, CFI = .962) and passed measurement invariance tests (Sup. Table 1).

To ensure the construct validity of our strong measurement invariant factor, we extracted latent intercept factor scores and correlated them to a variety of cognitive tasks administered at the first time point yet missing in later time points (precluding their inclusion). As expected (Sup. Figure 8), this demonstrated a broad positive manifold, whereby children performing well in one task also perform well in other cognitive tasks (conversely for those who do poorly).

1.2 Parental Socioeconomic Status

Parental socioeconomic status (SES) was defined as the first principal component from a probabilistic principal component analysis (PCA) of total household income, highest parental education, and neighborhood quality (reported at the first timepoint). Our neighborhood quality variable (area deprivation index when negatively coded) included 17 US Census variables outlined here 12 and relate specifically to census tract socioeconomic indicators (e.g., percent of population employed, educational attainment, median income, measures of wealth). We chose PCA over factor analysis for conceptual reasons related to the inherent epistemology of SES, namely, that it is a useful index variable (i.e., formed of its parts) rather than a latent, underlying causal construct 13 . This also frames our interpretation of SES on adolescent cognitive change: a useful index variable to capture many, correlated, time-varying causal factors (yet, not a causal entity itself). Using a probabilistic PCA allowed us to account for missing data. SES was standardized with a mean of zero and a standard deviation (SD) of one (for more details, see 14 ), with higher scores reflecting more parental income/education and less neighborhood deprivation.

To give a sense of what an additional standard deviation of SES means in a practical sense, we subsetted values 1SD above (>.95 & <1.05) and 1SD below the mean and computed median parental education and income scores. On average, parents around 1SD above the mean had an annual income over $100,000 and held an MSc degree or above; while parents around 1SD below the mean had an income below $35,000 and did not hold a bachelors degree.

2. Longitudinal Modeling (Latent Growth Curve)

Having established four cognitive tasks to fit well in a strong longitudinal CFA, we moved on to estimating the development (i.e., slope) of cognition during childhood and early adolescence (from 10 -16 years of age) using latent growth curve modeling (LGC). Briefly, this powerful longitudinal model strategy allows, in a single step, to 1) fit our longitudinal strong measurement invariant factor to the four cognitive tests, 2) estimate intercept and slope effects of intelligence over development, and 3) determine how socioeconomic status (i.e., non-causal X) is associated with both the intercept and slope of intelligence. We use the same methods described above for estimation, handling missing data, and model fit criteria.

As the development of cognition over adolescence is most likely non-linear, we compare a linear LGC model to a basis LGC model whereby the rate of change between the middle timepoints are freely estimated. We compare these two models (using a likelihood ratio test and AIC/BIC differences), prior to adding any covariates or independent variables. As expected, the non-linear basis LGC model demonstrated preferred model fit over a linear specification (χ2= 677.8, df =2, p <0.0001, ΔAIC = 1219, ΔBIC = 1204). This model (without any covariates or IVs) is used to report the amount of change over time, rates of change, and intercept-slope correlations. Lastly, we entered parental socioeconomic status (SES) as a predictor of the intercept and slope factors. Age at baseline (ses-00A) was included as a covariate to control for initial cross-sectional age differences.

3. Robustness Analyses

>Robustness Analyses

3.1 Parental Socioeconomic Status Components Analyses

In cases of significant SES composite findings, we also reported the results for each SES subcomponent separately. This was done for two reasons: 1) research showing specific SES measures relating to cognitive performance in distinct ways, and 2) calls in the literature to treat SES measures independently [15] [16] [17] . To accomplish this, we substitute each individual factor for SES in our strong measurement invariant basis latent growth curve model -the structure, estimation and modeling choice were otherwise identical. We then report model fit and extract the estimated effects of each component on the intercept and slope. All SES components showed adequate model fit with significant effects on baseline cognitive performance and the change (slope) over adolescence (Sup. Table 3) -confirming the results from our probabilistic PCA.

3.2 Site correction

The ABCD consortium consists of 21 sites spread throughout the contiguous United States. This site nesting comes with two major considerations 1) Non-independence, the clustering of individuals makes them more similar, leading to incorrect standard errors and resulting p-values, and 2) possible site confounding.

Controlling for site (either as a fixed or random effect) will remove true site-based SES differences while also controlling for (other) possible site-based confounding factors. In line with the goals of this paper -explicitly non-causal: aiming to see how much parental socioeconomics predicts their children's cognitive trajectories over early adolescence. We would ideally correct our standard errors for non-independence and, not add site as a fixed (or random) effect control. The modeling package we are using (Lavaan) allows us to compute cluster-robust (sandwich) standard errors which would solve non-independence inferential issues. Unfortunately, our models have more free parameter's (51 for the multi-indicator basis LGC) than clusters/sites (21) making this option untenable.

We therefore corrected for sites as fixed-effect controls on both our intercept and slope terms. Intriguingly, most of SES's variance is within-site (ICC = .23), so we see very little difference between standardized intercept or slope effects of cognitive performance with SES when fixedeffect site correcting (Sup. Table 6). Nevertheless, for the a priori conceptual reasons listed above, we do not add site fixed effects to our main model specification.

3.3 Individual Task Analyses

Our a priori goal was to determine parental SES's role in diverging cognitive performance trajectories -that is, the common variance across a group of diverse cognitive tasks. To do so, we fit a strong measurement invariant factor and estimated interindividual differences in interindividual change over early adolescence. This strategy has many benefits (outlined above & in section 4 below "Past methodological concerns"), yet it also obfuscates how parental SES impacts the trajectories of individual tasks. We therefore conducted a series of robustness analyses whereby we fit LGC model individually to each of the four tasks -the structure, estimation and modeling choice where otherwise identical to our multiple-indicator LGC model. Notably, we report the nonlinear basis model specification as it was preferred over the linear for all tasks (p's < .0001, ΔAIC 369-630, ΔBIC 355-616) yet sign and significance of SES's effect on the slope agreed across longitudinal modeling methods (e.g., linear mixed effects model, linear LGC and basis LGC).

Table 5). For, the working memory n-back task we found the opposite -those from lower SES backgrounds catching up over early adolescence (negative slope term). This was a working memory task where the subjects needed to respond if an item was presented two items previously. Unfortunately, the task was not adaptive and showed very poor reliability 18 -in an attempt to increase interindividual differences, we used the total number of correct responses. Unfortunately, this showed a large ceiling effect at the later ages as most children seem to be able to get almost every trial correct by the last timepoint (Sup. Figure 2). A ceiling effect will cause a spurious negative sign between SES and growth -as those with higher performance (and higher SES) will not be able to perform better. 3.4 Stanford Mental Arithmetic Response Time Evaluation Analyses

The Stanford Mental Arithmetic Response Time Evaluation (SMARTE) test was administered three times two years apart to measure enumeration, math fluency, and math recall (at 13 (ses-03A), 15 (ses-05A), and 17 (ses-07A) years of age) 19 . Crucially, this testing regime interleaved our cognitive tests administered at 10 (ses-00A), 12 (ses-02A), 14 (ses-04A), and 16 (ses-06A) years of age (Sup. Figure 1). This additional, SMARTE analysis offered three complementary benefits; 1) to see if our cognitive performance findings generalize to an age-appropriate standardized mathematical cognitive test, 2) task administration in separate sessions allowed us to remove test dependency effects, and 3) the time range and yearly spacing allowed us to determine if it is a general phenomenon over adolescence.

To do so, we added the three SMARTE (henceforth, numerical fluency) tasks specified as a latent growth curve (LGC) in our cognitive performance model incrementally, using the identical framework and estimation as outlined previously for cognitive performance (Sup. Figure 3).

We started with a bivariate LGC model whereby numerical fluency growth was modeled linearly next to the linear cognitive performance specification, which fit the data well (χ2 = 4536.6, df = 157, p <0.0001; CFI = 0.93; RMSEA = 0.053). Prior, cognitive performance preferred non-linear growth, with the two middle time points freely estimated (basis specification). We therefore wanted to see if freely estimating the middle loading across the three waves of math tasks led to improved model fit. That is, do numerical fluency tasks show a similar pattern whereby performance growth slows down as adolescences entered high school. To isolate numerical fluency model fit improvement, we compared to bivariate LGC models where cognition was specified with freed loadings in both (basis), and the only difference between models was the free estimation of the loading on numerical fluency's middle timepoint slope/growth factor (0, x 1) vs a linear model with fixed loadings on the slope (0, 1, 2).

As expected, numerical fluency tasks preferred a non-linear specification (χ2 = 454.79, Δdf =1, p <0.0001; ΔAIC = 632; ΔBIC = 624), with 66% (p <.001; Sup Table 7) of the change happening over middle school (ages 13 -15) vs high school (ages 15 -17). In addition, this dual basis bivariate LGC model fit the data better than a full linear specification (χ2 = 3154.1, df = 154, p <0.0001; CFI = 0.95; RMSEA = 0.045). Similarly to cognition, there was a minimal correlation between children's starting performance on numerical fluency at 13 and interindividual differences of change over time (r = .13, p = 0.016). Starting performance was highly correlated between our cognitive performance factor and numerical fluency (r = 0.75, p < .0001). Interindividual differences in intraindividual change over time between cognition and numerical fluency were moderate (i.e., slope-slope correlation; r = 0.37, p < .0001).

Having fit a dual basis bivariate LGC model, we next moved on to see if parental SES showed similar slope effects in the SMARTE task as with cognitive performance. To do so, we estimated the effect of parental SES on both the intercept and the slope of SMARTE while controlling for age at baseline. We replicated our finding for cognitive performance (Sup. Table 7), with large intercept (βT1 = .42, p < .001) and slope (β = .32, p < .001) effects of parental SES in the expected direction. This adds evidence to a general phenomenon of parental SES widening differences over early adolescences, agnostic to task, testing session or specific yearly patterns.

4. Past methodological concerns: advancements and limitations

Prior influential 20 and controversial 21, 22 work reported social class associations in the UK with widening academic achievement (test scores) throughout time 23 -finding crossover whereby initially low-performing high-SES children cross over their formerly higher-performing low-SES peers. While this literature base uses the term 'cognitive performance', academic achievement is a separable, yet highly correlated construct 24.

While we do not find crossover, the direction of the effect is similar to ours: a positive interaction between higher parental SES and greater academic achievement growth.

More than a decade later, the original author and prominent critics wrote a comment and debate piece 23 ; three main issues with the original analysis are highlighted: 1) random measurement error will cause regression to the mean, leading to a spurious negative sign when grouping children on baseline performance, 2) non-comparable tests across ages (essentially measuring changes in performance of possibly different concepts), 3) cutting a continuous variable such as SES into groups -and the sensitivity of the main findings to this decision. Almost all prior work has one of these three limitations present (discussed in depth below) -the first is a statistical artifact that will cause a spurious finding (we therefore only cite work use longitudinal change models; two-timepoint subtraction or alternative parametrizations, e.g., 25 , multi-timepoint linear mixed effects or latent growth curve models).

To date, we are unaware of any study measuring SES's effect on general cognitive development that is multi-timepoint longitudinal (robust to RTM) with the same tasks administered (there is notable two-timepoint null findings with working memory tasks [25] [26] [27] ). There is also notably multitimepoint longitudinal work using different cognitive tasks to examine general cognitive performance (e.g., 28, 29 ). Using different tasks is an inherent limitation/tradeoff when studying wide age ranges (as age-inappropriate tasks, will themselves cause statistical artifacts; as illustrated by our ceiling effects in the 2-back working memory task).

The first concern is a statistical artifact when grouping (or conditioning) children on baseline achievement values measured with error. Measurement error means children further from the mean (showing baseline high or low performance) will differentially 'regress to the mean'; that is, due to random noise, they are more likely to move toward the average value at the 2 nd time point. In two-timepoint change, this leads to a negative coefficient between baseline starting values and subsequent change. There is notable work 30, 31 whereby measurement error was minimized and then a residualization approach was taken -this changes the question to one of 'how does parental SES predict childhood cognitive growth, if we control for baseline cognitive differences'.

In our parameterization, we model growth using multiple waves, separating latent intercepts and slopes from occasion-specific residual variance, in turn, avoiding spurious negative baselinechange association that arises from shared measurement error in two-wave difference scores [32] [33] [34].

We do not condition on error-laden observed baseline scores, thereby avoiding spurious falsepositives of change [35] [36] [37] . Having minimized occasion-specific residual variance, our modeling approach goes a step further by including latent cognitive performance factors to minimize the role of task-specific variance.

The second criticism is that changing tasks themselves can impact comparison, reliability, and scaling 23, 38, 39 . This is particularly astute when comparing academic achievement. For instance, when measuring language development, how many 'points' do we assign when we compare an infant that has learned to speak for the first time versus a child that just learned how to read? As age ranges increase, this issue increases, as the tested knowledge (and questions) diverge, making comparison challenging. Our use of the same tasks (configural measurement invariance), in a narrow age range (10-16 years), on elementary cognitive abilities partially mitigates this issue. In addition to having identical tasks, we ensure our latent 'cognitive performance' concept remains comparable across the time points with weak (fixed loadings) and strong (fixed intercepts) measurement invariance.

While we avoid the major limitations regarding between-wave task equivalence, within-task scaling remains an inherent limitation of any longitudinal work. For instance, almost all studies on human behavior assume our scales are interval despite, in reality, measuring human behavior in an ordinal fashion 39 . While this issue is lessened with cognitive tasks, even the seemingly most linear/continuous metric task (with no measurement error) could potentially suffer this limitation. For instance, in our episodic memory task (Picture sequence) we assume that increasing your memory by 1 more word is equivalent/equal regardless of where on the ability scale the subjects lies. Yet is it equally difficult to remember one more item when you start low (e.g. going from 2 -> 3 times), to when you are already remembering many (e.g., going from 20 -> 21)? Convincing theoretical arguments to scale difficulty differently can be made in either direction (for instance, the well-replicated 'chucking' effect for memory). This assumption of within-task interval scaling remains an inherent limitation of our study (and all longitudinal work on human behavior) -our use of multiple tasks does lessen the plausibility of this issue as it would need to affect many separate cognitive domains/tasks (as does our additional numerical cognition task, administered at different times). Lastly, we address the last criticism by using a continuous measure of SES rather than binarizing groups, a practice widely criticized [40] [41] [42].

In summary, our use of advanced statistical methods (strong measurement invariant latent basis growth curve modeling) removes some prior limitations and minimizes others. The longitudinal aspect helps separate past causes associated with SES by removing stable time-invariant causes and confounders 43 . Our focus on cognitive performance rather than academic achievement, widens practical implications while also minimizing comparison issues (although we do assume interval scaling). To our knowledge, there is almost no longitudinal work using identical cognitive tasks (although see 25, [27] [28] [29] ), moreover none examining the development of a general factor. Lastly, by modeling slopes over multiple time points (>2), we ensure regression-to-the-mean artifacts have equal bias over time and are not a contributing factor to our results.

Sources of missing data are due to observable characteristics (MAR) or reasons unknown to us (MNAR). For instance, socioeconomic status (SES) is an observable characteristic that is known to impact the probability of 1) initial enrollment and 2) follow-up attendance in large consortium studies 45 . We employ a variety of approaches to minimize the impacts of differential missingness. First, we use a probabilistic PCA to estimate each child's SES; unlike standard principal components methods, this allows us to include subjects with partially missing data 14 . Due to this choice, for the initial sample of eligible children (N = 9806) none are missing baseline socioeconomic status data. Our modeling framework uses full information maximum likelihood (FIML), and will yield unbiased estimates if missingness varies as a function of characteristics also included in the model. We do not exclude any subjects based on missing data, relying on FIML.
