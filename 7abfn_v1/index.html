Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

The human embryo grows within a pulsatile maternal milieu structured by cardiac, respiratory, locomotor, vocal and circadian rhythms. Here we integrate evidence from embryology, foetal physiology, developmental cognitive neuroscience and chronobiology: we review how the foetal heart and placenta co-develop under maternal haemodynamic forcing, how maternal rhythms reach the foetus and how they may modulate foetal physiology. We propose that multimodal, semi-periodic input streams may provide a temporal reference frame scaffolding early timing abilities and autonomic selfregulation. In this view, we discuss that co-regulation may ground interoception and allostasis. Next, we formalize the mother-foetus and mother-infant dyads as multiscale systems of coupled oscillators spanning about seven orders of magnitude in frequency -from circadian cycles to neural oscillations.

Within this coordination-dynamics framework we propose that healthy co-regulation is marked by metastability: flexible and transient coordination, preserving the endogenous tendencies for systems to display endogenous behaviours and to couple with other systems in an adaptive manner. The framework yields testable predictions on the link between dyadic coordination dynamics and developmental outcome. We are, quite literally, born on rhythms.

Introduction

The brain does not only process the external world. It continuously receives, predicts and regulates signals from the body's own organs (Khalsa et al., 2018). In adults, cortical and subcortical activity is coupled to cardiac, respiratory and gastric rhythms (Park and Blanke, 2019; Rebollo et al., 2021; Banellis et al., 2025; see for reviews : Engelen et al., 2023; Criscuolo et al., 2022 Criscuolo et al., , 2025)), mirroring mechanisms of neural entrainment observed for exteroceptive input (Lakatos et al., 2008). Brain and body rhythms are, thus, proposed to form a nested hierarchy of coupled oscillators (Buzsáki, 2006; Klimesch, 2018; Kluger and Gross, 2020; Criscuolo et al., 2022 Criscuolo et al., , 2025 Criscuolo et al., , 2026) ) whereby cross-modal coupling shapes brain excitability states, perception, action and the sense of a bodily self (Craig, 2002; Park and Blanke, 2019). Disruptions in coupling mechanisms have been linked to cognitive and psychiatric dysfunction (see for reviews Kluger et al., 2024; Villringer et al., 2025; Criscuolo et al., 2025).

The ability to encode one's own sensory input from the body in relation to the environment is a key survival mechanism, as it provides predictable stability of incoming signals in a noisy milieu (Damasio, 2000). There is a rising awareness on the importance of measuring bodily signals and autonomic functions next to brain activity to understand cognition (Bambha et al. 2026). For example, previous work showed that healthy bodies self-regulate by following circadian rhythms geared to their environment (e.g. night/day) (Reppert and Weaver, 2002). The regularity of the physical world (e.g. circadian rhythms based on light exposure) coupled with the biomechanics of the human body movements (e.g. heartbeat, breathing, walking) generate distributions of highly probable signals that are internalized by the brain in the course of a lifetime (Betti et al., 2021). Bodily and circadian rhythms are thus key for tracking and regulating physiological and behavioural processes in mammals, operating typically on a 24-h cycle (Reppert and Weaver, 2002).

Taking up on this idea from a developmental angle, recent work suggests that the basic architecture of human cognition is structured by sensory inputs arising from visceral organ systems during development (Corcoran et al., 2025). This important research proposes a new account of how the mind "from within" is shaped by the body in early life and beyond (Monti and Aglioti, 2026). Several recent contributions provided remarkable new insights to understanding human early experiences in relation to the foetal brain and body development (reviewed in Dehaene-Lambertz and Spelke, 2015; Bayne et al., 2023; Southgate, 2024).

Yet, the fundamental connection between the foetal body and the other body (i.e. the pregnant person) remains a remarkable blind spot (Ciaunica, 2025; Ciaunica et al., 2021; Martínez Quintero and De Jaegher, 2020). This neglect is surprising given the substantial evidence indicating the vital role of maternal-foetal and infant interactions in early life, extensively discussed in both theoretical and empirical work (Murray and Trevarthen, 1985; Stern, 1985; Feldman, 2007; see Dunkel Schetter and Tanner, 2012; Servin-Barthet et al., 2025; Ballesteros et al., 2025). All humans are mammals and as such their bodies grow and develop within another human body for a given gestational period during which bidirectional signal processing is omnipresent (Ciaunica et al., 2022 (Ciaunica et al., , 2023)). Moreover, the pregnant maternal body is not an external objectual "environment", but rather a living dynamic system, with her own bodily rhythms (Ciaunica, 2024) which in turn depend on wider circadian and environmental rhythms and dynamic interactions.

When does body-brain coupling first emerge? Do maternal visceral and behavioural rhythms play a role in calibrating body-brain physiological coordination dynamics in the developing foetus? And more generally, does periodicity -in the form of multimodal, quasi-rhythmic sensory streams -influence neurocognitive development?

In this article, we address these questions in 5 steps. First, we discuss evidence for rhythmical sensory processing in adults and show that domain-general timing abilities emerge early. When and how does the brain learn to process temporal regularities in input streams? As we will see shortly, foetuses respond to sound from about 19 weeks of gestation (Shahidullah and Hepper, 1994), preterm infants track the envelope and metre of auditory sequences (Edalati et al., 2023), and newborns detect violations of the musical beat (Winkler et al., 2009) and recognize sounds heard in utero (DeCasper and Spence, 1986; Partanen et al., 2013).

In next sections, we trace the co-development of the foetal heart and the placenta, discussing that the earliest rhythmic signals of life are cardiovascular, generated in continuous haemodynamic exchange with the maternal body. The embryonic heart begins to contract around day 22 and peaks at about 170 beats per minute (bpm) at 9-10 weeks of gestation, well before any cortical circuit forms (van Heeswijk et al., 1990; Burton and Jauniaux, 2018; Gerri et al., 2020). From the 25th week of gestational age (wGA), the foetus' sensory systems process the omnipresent rhythms of the maternal heartbeat and respiration (Dunham, 1990; Cheour-Luhtanen et al., 1996; Candia-Rivera and Chavez, 2025), as well as footsteps, speech, and songs (Kisilevsky et al., 2004; Granier-Deferre et al., 2011; Santos-Rocha et al., 2022; Trevarthen, 1998). The semi-periodic stimulation provided by the intrauterine environment (Fig. 1) is thought to provide the foetus with early 'rhythmic' experiences, potentially influencing domain-general timing and rhythm abilities (Rocha et al., 2021), and future abilities for dyadic interactions and synchronization (e.g., vocal, gestural and gaze communication synchrony) (Ullal-Gupta et al., 2013; Phillips-Silver et al., 2010; Feldman, 2007).

Finally, we propose to frame dyadic interactions in a multiscale system of coupled oscillators displaying metastable coupling regimes (see FIGs 2 & 3). This approach aims to fill at least three gaps in the literature. First, accounts of early embodiment mostly model the foetus as a self-contained system whose visceral signals influence its own brain; the pregnant body enters, if at all, as an external "environment".

Yet the foetus develops inside a living system with its own endogenous rhythms, thus bi-directional influence cannot be neglected (Ciaunica et al., 2022; Ciaunica, 2025). Second, maternal-foetal and caregiver-infant coupling is usually summarized as an average degree of synchrony or via correlation metrics. This has hindered a more thorough understanding of if and how coupling evolves in time: namely, when it forms and breaks; in which direction it acts; and how it depends on behavioural and mental states. Third, rhythms at different timescales, from circadian cycles to neural oscillations, are studied in separate fields with separate methods, so there is no common language to ask how they interact across the transition from womb to world.

Here we suggest that the dynamics of coupled oscillators offer such a language. Specifically, coordination dynamics (Kelso, 1995; Kelso and Tognoli, 2007) and synchronization theory (Pikovsky et al., 2001) describe how weakly coupled, noisy oscillators lock, slip and switch between patterns, and how these regimes depend on intrinsic frequencies, coupling strength and noise. These tools have been applied successfully to cardiorespiratory interaction (Schäfer et al., 1998) and to networks of organ systems (Bashan et al., 2012), but only sparsely to the developing dyad (Van Leeuwen et al., 2009).

Dynamic systems theory has long framed motor and cognitive development as self-organization (Thelen and Smith, 1994), yet without modelling the coupling between two bodies. Applying these tools to the mother-foetus/infant system turns a descriptive notion, "co-regulation", into quantities that can be estimated.

Furthermore, the proposed approach provides with a set of predictions on the if and how coupling regimes in the dyad influence neurodevelopment. Our proposal lays the ground for a new, dynamic system modelling for mother -infant physiological interactions, with important implications for advancing the science of developing minds and bodies both in health and disease.

1 Keeping Life's Upbeat: Timing and Rhythm Perception Abilities

Early pioneering work on embodied cognition and phenomenology by Varela and colleagues (1991) and Merleau-Ponty (1945) highlighted the close relationship between environment and dynamic brain and body signal processing for self-experiences and ultimately cognition (see Gallagher, 2000; Damasio, 2000; O'Regan and Noë, 2001; Legrand, 2006; see also Qin et al., 2020 for a review).

Humans are remarkably equipped to track rhythms and detect temporal regularities coming from both inside and outside their bodies. As most extero-and interoceptive signals show gradients of temporal regularities, sensorimotor brain areas readily collaborate in processing multisensory rhythmic inputs in adults (Fleming et al., 2011; Brændholt et al., 2023; Engelen et al., 2023). Neural entrainment -the alignment of neural oscillations with (e.g., auditory/visual) rhythmic sensory stimuli -has been identified as a key mechanism in human sensory perception (Buzsáki, 2006). The core idea is that temporal alignment of neural activity with an external stimulus optimizes sensory processing (Buzsáki and Draguhn, 2004). Extending this notion from purely exteroceptive to interoceptive rhythms, it has been recently proposed that modulatory dynamics in body-brain interactions offer an integrative perspective to investigate cognition and mental health (Criscuolo et al., 2022 (Criscuolo et al., , 2025;; Alekseeva and Ciaunica, 2025). Brain, body and environmental rhythms are proposed to be deeply connected and to form a cross-frequency architecture governed by scaling laws (Klimesch, 2018; Criscuolo et al., 2026).

Consequently, timing abilities -the capacities to process temporal patterns in sensory sequences and generate temporal expectations about future events -have been proposed to be key for both basic homeostatic self-regulation, as well as for language development, music and social bonding in early life (Thomson and Goswami, 2008; Hove and Risen, 2009; Fairhurst et al., 2014; Cirelli et al., 2018; Atzil et al., 2019; Savage et al., 2021).

As early as infancy, children display a striking sensitivity to rhythmic structures (Provasi et al., 2014; Iorio et al., 2026). Neurophysiological evidence demonstrates that 7-month old infants' brains show synchronization to both instrumental music and nursery rhymes (Cantiani et al., 2022), as well as to maternal infant-directed singing (Nguyen et al., 2023a). Hence, at birth newborns' brains already display the capacity to track rhythmic regularities in auditory sequences, encoding the beat (Winkler et al., 2009), and slightly later (7-15 months of age) hierarchical metrical structures (Cirelli et al., 2016). Bianco et al. (2026) further demonstrated that newborns are capable to process statistic regularities in the temporal patterns in music, and can subsequently generate temporal predictions -i.e., expectations about when they will hear next sounds. Recent work by Borjon and colleagues (2024) show some early evidence of body-brain coupling, in the form of an influence of bodily rhythms in motor coordination.

Next to rhythm processing abilities, infants further exhibit early rhythm production capacities: from the 5th month of age, infants show a spontaneous mean tempo (i.e., an endogenous, preferred, movement rate; McAuley et al., 2006) of ~500ms; however, the ability to precisely phase-lock (i.e., synchronize) their movements with external stimuli develops slightly later (Rocha et al., 2021; de l'Etoile et al., 2020; Rocha and Addyman, 2022). In fact, from early school age onwards, children gradually approach adultlike motor timing and synchronization abilities (e.g., tempo flexibility, reduced variability) (Nave et al., 2023). Thus, while sensorimotor synchronization abilities may require a few years to develop, more general timing and rhythm processing capacities seem to be present since birth.

Further evidence shows that even premature newborns encode rhythmic information (Edalati et al., 2023; Mashhadi et al., 2025), and they do so by engaging an extended network encompassing auditory, sensorimotor and premotor regions (Mashhadi et al., 2025), similarly to what observed in adults. Premature infants' brain activity display precise phase-alignment to the envelope of auditory sequences (Edalati et al., 2023), a frequency representation of the beat across 27-35 weeks of gestational age, and later pre-terms further display a neural representation of hierarchical metrical structures (Saadatmehr et al., 2025).

These observations raise the question of whether timing and rhythmic abilities are innate, and consequently, a fundamental characteristic of living species. How and when do we develop rhythm processing capacities?

2 Getting in Tune with Each Other: Coupled Rhythms in the Womb

Within the intrauterine environment, the foetus is early on exposed to continuous, semi-periodic, multisensory stimulus streams, which stimulate the auditory, tactile, and vestibular systems (Provasi et al., 2021). Such sensory landscape is composed of a variety of internally generated sensations, as well as many sensory input originating from the mother and the external environment. Research shows that as early as 25 weeks gestational age (wGA), the auditory system allows the foetus to hear the omnipresent rhythms of the maternal heartbeat and respiration (Dunham, 1990; Cheour-Luhtanen et al., 1996).

While maternal respiratory, cardiovascular, intestinal activity, and body movements provide a background noise floor, maternal vocalizations and externally-generated sounds reach the foetus' brain through the auditory canal (although the outer and middle ear are fluid-filled), as well as via bone conduction. These stimulations are partially filtered: in fact, the womb acts as a low-pass frequency filter with high-frequency energy rejected at a rate of ~6dB/octave; energy above 1 kHz is attenuated by up to 40 to 50 dB (Gerhardt and Abrams, 1996, 2000; Querleu et al., 1988; Armitage et al., 1980; Gélat et al., 2019 Gélat et al., , 2025)).

Despite the filtering, as we saw earlier, the foetus brain hears. Auditory responsiveness begins about the 24th week (~6th month) of gestation and the hearing sensitivity range becomes comparable to adults (.3 to 3 kHz range) by 33-35 weeks gestation (Webb et al., 2015). Studies demonstrated that already in the prenatal stage, certain brain regions are active and can react to auditory stimuli and extract statistical regularities during the third trimester of gestation (Moser et al., 2021; Saffran and Kirkham, 2018). For example, prenatal experiences, such as hearing the mother's voice, can influence brain development and linguistic abilities (Holst et al., 2005). Intrauterine auditory perception is further demonstrated in studies where late-term foetuses show the ability to discriminate their native language, to which they were exposed in utero, from an unknown language (Kisilevsky et al., 2009). A milestone experiment demonstrated that 2-3 day-old preferred to hear the recording of female voice saying a specific text iteratively uttered by the pregnant mother to a novel, unheard text (DeCasper and Spence, 1986). In a similar vein, recent studies reported that newborns exhibit salient pitch-based elements in their own cry melodies and sensitivity to prosodic features (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2013; Prochnow et al., 2019; Wermke et al., 2021) having had ample opportunity to become familiar with their mother's language in the womb.

Thus, the foetus brain responds to both mothers' heartbeats as well as external auditory stimulation (Porcaro et al., 2006). Some authors suggest that prenatal exposure to maternal vocalizations paves the way for the development of neural pathways later engaged in auditory and language processing (Gómez et al., 2014). An observation partially supported by evidence showing that daily exposure to recorded maternal voice and heartbeat sounds (~3 h) was linked to auditory cortex plasticity in the first month of life in preterm infants (Webb et al., 2015).

The periodic stimulation provided by maternal heartbeat is suggested to provide the foetus with early 'rhythmic' experiences, influencing future abilities for dyadic interactions and synchronization (e.g., vocal, gestural and gaze communication synchrony) during mother-infant interface (Ullal-Gupta et al., 2013; Phillips-Silver et al., 2010; Feldman, 2007) which is important for infants' socioemotional development and self-regulation (Feldman et al., 1999). More generally, the periodic stimulation may influence timing and rhythm abilities (Rocha et al., 2021). In fact, the maternal heartbeat is a dynamic signal which fluctuates over time according to maternal activity and emotional states, ultimately offering the foetus the opportunity to experience a plethora of rhythmic patterns over time (Porcaro et al., 2006).

The maternal heartbeat further serves as a regulator of neonatal autonomic activity (Puddu et al., 2026).

In this view, the external heartbeat may serve as a temporal reference frame to pace the foetus' autonomic activity. Importantly, evidence shows that maternal-foetal heartbeat synchronization are modulated in function of the foetus and maternal states: synchronization is stronger when the foetus is quiet and the mother is more active (e.g., high respiratory rates; Samjeed et al., 2024). Although these observations demand for further empirical confirmation, results seem to align with known mechanisms of entrainment and Arnold tongue mapping, suggesting that mother-child physiological synchrony could potentially be modelled as a system of dynamic oscillators.

Endogenous cardiac activity develops during the first trimester of prenatal development. In this period, cardiac activity is myogenic, i.e. not modulated by the brain. Rather it is spontaneously and autonomously generating rhythmic patterns of sensory signals reaching the developing brain via visceral afferents. These pathways shape and gear the connectivity of brainstem and higher regions towards the final weeks of the first trimester (Cheng et al., 2008). Later, during the third trimester of pregnancy, the process of neurodevelopmental maturation undergoes a rapid structural and functional growth (Rakic, 1988; Kostović, 2024). Previous work showed that the synaptic connections are finessed not only by autonomic bursts of activity (Winnubst et al., 2015; Babola et al., 2018; Saadatmehr et al., 2022 Saadatmehr et al., , 2026) ) but also by sensory-driven neural activity (Minlebaev et al., 2007; Colonnese et al., 2010; Saadatmehr et al., 2022). This means that synaptic connections begin to unfold as flexible response to predating rhythmical foetal bodily signals (i.e. cardiac inputs) which gradually scaffold bodily self-representations (Corcoran et al., 2025). As gestation advances into the third trimester, the foetal heart displays accelerations (Amorim-Costa et al., 2016; Sorokin et al., 1982; Pillai and James, 1990; Hofmeyr et al., 2014). Cardiac activity is intimately connected with spontaneous foetal bodily movements (Lee et al., 1975) which is typically considered an important clinical indicator of foetal wellbeing in the third trimester (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2021; O'Neill and Thorp, 2012).

In the late phases of gestation, the coordination between foetal body movements and cardiac accelerations gradually increases within a narrower temporal window (DiPietro et al., 1996; Shuffrey et al., 2019; Hayat and Rutherford, 2018). The alteration of cardiac responses during the second trimester of gestation can be interpreted as the basic foundation of oriented responses towards salient events which in turn modulate physiological activity (Kisilevsky and Hains, 2011).

Taken together, these studies open a fascinating window into the first steps of human bodily self experiences. The vital link with the maternal visceral body may play a fundamental, yet overlooked role in addition to the foetal visceral afferents. Indeed, there is more than one heart involved in the delicate and vital process of keeping the foetal life's upbeats at the optimal pace for self-preservation and growth.

There is another pacing heart that influences the activity of the foetal cardiovascular system.

In the remainder of this paper, we discuss the implications of this observation in understanding the autonomic co-regulation of brain-body dynamics, co-embodiment and social homeostasis (Atzil et al., 2018; Fotopoulou and Tsakiris, 2017; Ciaunica et al., 2022). We turn to these considerations now.

3 At the Heart of Foetal Development: from Pulsing Cells to Moving Bodies

The development of the complex architecture of neural functionalities of a human brain does not suddenly emerge in a snapshot. Rather it is resulting from a hierarchical web of multicellular interactions occurring already at the onset of the embryogenesis and maturing across the lifespan. Human bodies are biological self-organising systems made of cells, which are driven by the imperative to survive, thrive and reproduce in dynamic and uncertain environments (Varela et al., 1991; Friston et al., 2010). Bodily survival crucially depends on self-regulation or homeostasis which refers to the organism's need to maintain sensed variables of the internal environment within ranges compatible with survival despite fluctuating external environment (Modell et al., 2015). It is well-established that this control involves automatic actions (e.g. motor reflexes, endocrine, immunological, and autonomic processes) that are driven by feedback inputs and the resulting "prediction error"-the discrepancy between an expected bodily state (a homeostatic setpoint) and its actual level as signalled by sensory inputs from the body (Sterling, 2014).

Human life and early bodily foetal development is thus intimately linked to cellular metabolism and homeostatic self-regulation (Damasio, 2000), with embryonic cells actively negotiating energetic resources via exchanges with maternal cells after fertilization (Shahbazi, 2020). A detailed and systematic description of the human embryogenesis and foetal development is beyond the scope of this paper (see Bhattacharya and Stubblefield, 2016; Rossant and Tam, 2017). In what follows we provide a bird eye view on its most important milestones, to emphasize the key role of bidirectional and dynamic exchanges and orchestration happening in between the two living systems from the outset, i.e. the development in tandem of the foetal cardiovascular system and maternal placenta.

After fertilization and during the first 5 to 6 days, the human zygote develops into a "blastocyst that comprises an outer layer of trophectoderm (TE) enclosing the blastocyst cavity in which a population of inner cell mass (ICM) cells is juxtaposed asymmetrically to the TE on one side of the blastocyst" (see Rossant and Tam, 2022, p. 152).

Healthy development of the blastocyst requires the organization of connectivity with the maternal uterine body for the provision of embryotrophic support. This task is assured by the implantation of the blastocyst via trophoblast-uterine tissue interaction and embedding of the blastocyst in the uterine stroma. This peri-implantation period is followed by "gastrulation, placentation, and postgastrulation organogenesis: critical phases of embryonic development during which the blueprint of development (the body plan) is established through the ordered generation of diverse cell types and the assembly of the precursor tissues for body parts and organs (Rossant and Tam, 2022, p. 152).

Once the blastocyst is embedded in the uterine tissue, the embryo becomes inaccessible for experimental study, due to major ethical considerations. However, recent advances in the methodology for the in vitro culture of developing blastocysts over the implantation period (Deglincerti et al., 2016; Shahbazi et al., 2016; Xiang et al., 2020) examine this crucial time window in human life journey. Importantly, research on human embryonic material is subject to strict ethical and regulatory constraints that limits the extent of experimental analysis in vitro. For example, the so-called 14-day rule limits the culture of intact human embryos to 14 days postfertilization and/or the initiation of gastrulation, is legally in place in many jurisdictions (Matthews and Moralí, 2020).

While the exact nature of bidirectional cellular exchanges between the blastocyst and the uterine tissue remains an open question, more research has been done on the relational organ supporting foetal development during pregnancy, namely the placenta (Turco and Moffett, 2019).

Importantly for our discussion here, the placenta and the foetal heart are two of the first organs to differentiate, and hence it is assumed that their development is interlinked. The placenta is the largest of the foetal organs, and at term receives about one third in mid-gestation and about one fifth after 32 weeks (Kiserud et al., 2006). It has been hypothesized that the resistance offered by the arterial and capillary network within the placental villous trees plays a key role at every stage of development and potential pathology. Indeed, as Burton and Jauniaux note, "because cardiac gene expression is highly sensitive to biomechanical cues, that resistance may determine the morphogenesis of the heart" (Burton and Jauniaux, 2018, p. 2). Hence, potential biomechanical cues offered by the extra-embryonic circulations might support in tandem human placental and cardiac development. During the first 2 months of gestation, the developing embryo and its placenta are protected from excessive oxygen exposure during the sensitive period of organogenesis (Jauniaux et al., 2000 (Jauniaux et al., , 2003)).

Research showed that the embryonic heart starts as a "primitive tube" (Burton and Jauniaux, 2018, p. 3) and the first contractions are seen at around 22 days (Männer 2022). By 10 weeks the foetal heart rate reaches its peak at around 170 beats/min (bpm) and then slows down to 120-160 bpm for the rest of pregnancy (van Heeswijk et al., 1990). In healthy pregnancies, the maternal arterial circulation to irrigate the human placenta is in place the end of the first trimester (Jauniaux et al., 2000). At the end of the first trimester of pregnancy, the placenta undergoes considerable remodelling, with an adaptive vasculature constantly responding to environmental conditions and to variations in the blood supply received from the mother.

Around the same time the foeto-placental circulation starts unfolding (Burton and Jauniaux, 2018, p. 3).

Crucially for our discussion here, this sharp rise in oxygenation is directly linked to the maternal blood flow, which at this stage is still pulsatile (Collins et al., 2012). It has been hypothesized that disruptions of the placental development impacts foetal cardiac (and other foetal organs) development (Barker, 1999; Barker et al., 2002; Eriksson et al., 2001). While the exact mechanisms underlying the heartplacenta axis remain unknown (Linask, 2013), animal models suggest that the haemodynamics within the umbilical circulations can have a major impact on heart (and other foetal organs) development (Kowalski et al., 2014; Midgett et al., 2017). Hence there is close haemodynamic link between the development of the placenta and the foetal heart.

So far, we saw that the focus on the bodily cells rather than the neural architectures in early development, allows us to establish three key facts. First, the cardiovascular system is the initial system to develop, and not the brain, due to the growing embryo's mounting metabolic demands. Second, the placenta, i.e. the largest foetal organ, is a key player in securing key metabolic exchanges necessary to healthy foetal development in relation to the maternal body. Third, these two biological systems are deeply interconnected and constantly exchanging signals with each other, laying the ground for incoming maternal-foetal co-regulatory dynamics. Indeed, living organisms constantly need to adapt to environmental changes, especially when the proximal interactions involve others' active and interactive bodies, i.e. a social presence (Rochat, 2009; Alekseeva and Ciaunica, 2025).

In the next section we dive deeper and examine these early signals in relation to timing and bodily rhythms processing. These timing and modulatory dynamics are key in leveraging body-brain interactions which in turn may offer an integrative perspective to investigate cognition in early life and beyond (Criscuolo et al., 2025 (Criscuolo et al., , 2026)).

4 Autonomic co-regulation and the development of interoceptive sensitivity in relation to the other

'Interoception' typically refers to the processing and integration of internal, autonomic, hormonal, and physiological signals (Tsakiris and Critchley, 2016; Critchley and Garfinkel, 2017; Craig, 2002).

Interoception does not merely refer to estimating the current state of the body but is thought to include the abilities to predict future visceral sensations, and to actively seek and integrate predictions with incoming sensory information via allostasis (Allen and Friston, 2018; Theriault et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).

Based on the observation that interoceptive signalling is a key element of self-related neural representations (see Monti and Aglioti, 2026 for a recent review), the experience of selfhood (and subsequently conscious experiences) may be thus fundamentally grounded in visceral signals. For example, alterations in interoceptive capacities -e.g., (hyper-/hypo-) sensitivity, or altered predictions mechanisms -are associated with a variety of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders (see Criscuolo et al. (2025) for a review).

Evidence shows interoceptive sensitivity already in 3-to-18-month-old infants, despite some interindividual variability (Tünte et al., 2025; Maister et al., 2017). Children showed early sensitivity to cardiac sensations, while respiratory interoceptive sensitivity emerged after the first year of life (Tünte et al., 2025). Next to autonomic regulation, interoceptive sensitivity is suggested to play a pivotal role in early development, especially for the maturation of the self and the development of early social abilities (Filippetti, 2021; Fotopoulou and Tsakiris, 2017; Musculus et al., 2021).

Importantly however, interoceptive sensitivity does not develop in a vacuum. Rather, the maternal body offers a continued regulatory support for self-preservation and metabolic processes (Ciaunica, 2024).

In fact, interoceptive processing abilities in offsprings are influenced by the caregiver's ability to process, interpret and respond to babies' physiological needs (Ciaunica and Fotopoulou, 2017; Fotopoulou and Tsakiris, 2017). Early in life, the regulation of infants' internal states can be seen as a dynamic process involving interoceptive sensitivity, learning, predictive allostasis and social interaction (Filippetti, 2021; Ciaunica and Crucianelli, 2019; Ciaunica et al., 2021). In fact, dyadic interactions represent the primary sources of learning about one's own body: over time, reiterations of experiences of (mis-)attunement led to infants' ability to interpret what the body really needs at any given point; build predictions about the causes of changes in bodily states; generate expectations about caregivers' behaviors (i.e., their consistency and timing); and more generally support the development of self-other distinction (Fotopoulou and Tsakiris, 2017). Indeed, infants need to learn that they are in charge of successfully recognizing changes in bodily states, and of conveying their needs to the caregiver. Some authors suggest that there is a close relationship between shared perceptual rhythms and interpersonal neural synchrony in early life (see Atzil and Gendron, 2017; Hoehl et al., 2025 for reviews). The key idea is that the synchronization of brain rhythms with perceptual rhythms may be a fundamental process subserving early social bonding. Caregivers spontaneously provide infants with rich rhythmic sensory stimulation (Markova et al., 2019). Studies revealed that during pregnancy, listening to music (Baltacı et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2008), singing (Wulff et al., 2021) and dancing (Arioli et al., 2026; Branson Dame et al., 2024) are used to decrease stress. Similarly, after birth maternal singing and music contribute to emotion regulation (Filippa et al., 2013; Lejeune et al., 2019) via mechanisms of neural tracking and motor synchronization to maternal singing (Nguyen et al., 2023b). Furthermore, infants can process maternal cardio-respiratory physiology (Maister et al., 2017; Tünte et al., 2025; Imafuku et al., 2023), as evidenced by studies showing an effect on newborns' heartrate variability (Van Puyvelde et al., 2015; Waters et al., 2017).

So far, we saw that next to autonomic regulation, interoceptive sensitivity is suggested to play a pivotal role in early development, especially for the maturation of the self and the development of early social abilities (Filippetti, 2021; Fotopoulou and Tsakiris, 2017; Musculus et al., 2021). At a much slower time scale, maternal physiology is also thought to co-regulate foetal and infant circadian periods.

5 The role of circadian rhythm

Most living organisms, ranging from bacteria, animals, and plants, follow circadian rhythms. A complex system enables to synchronize physiological activities with a circa-diem cycle of 24h. The circadian system coordinates homeostatic processes -including hormonal regulation, immune functions, sleepwake and feeding cycles -to the periodic alternation of sunlight and darkness (Patke et al., 2019). The central circadian timekeeping system in mammals is located in in the brain's suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN) (Dibner et al., 2010). The SCN serves as a central pacemaker, integrating and coordinating subsidiary clocks in nearly every cell in the body.

Endogenous rhythms regulating circulating cortisol, melatonin, and core body temperature emerge within 2-4 months after birth (Joseph et al., 2015; Ivars et al., 2015) while the sleep-wake cycle consolidate progressively through the first few years of life (Crowley et al., 2007), shift toward later wake and sleep times in adolescence before maturing into adults' robust rhythms (Crowley et al., 2007).

The (semi-)regularity of circadian cycles is fundamental: disruptions of the rhythmicity is associated with increased risk for mood disorders, subjective wellbeing, cognitive dysfunction, dementia and neuropsychiatric conditions in adults (Lyall et al., 2018). In fact, the inability to align endogenous to exogenous cycles impact not only sleep-wake cycles, but other periodic processes such as metabolism, hormone secretion, food intake, cortisol levels, energy, mood, immune system efficiency (see for reviews Finger and Kramer, 2021; Patke et al., 2019), ultimately leading to with adverse consequences for cardiovascular and general health.

Less is known about how the circadian system influences early life neurodevelopmental processes.

There are indications that sleep disruptions either in the mother or infant are associated with alterations of many physiological processes (McCarthy et al., 2019), which in turn may lead to neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), such as autism (Yang et al., 2016). Circadian rhythms are influenced by the environment within which child development takes place: even simply aberrant light-dark cycles may lead circadian alterations, and predispose to mental health and affective issues (Mendoza, 2024; Varcoe et al., 2018).

During gestation, foetal circadian rhythms are highly regulated by maternal influences (Sumová et al., 2012; Carver et al., 2026; Bates and Herzog, 2020; Reppert and Weaver, 2002; Reppert and Schwartz, 1983). The maternal circadian system is thought to entrain foetal biological clocks by modulating hormonal secretion (Bates and Herzog, 2020), circulating levels of melatonin, glucocorticoids and dopamine (Mark et al., 2017), and further regulates microglia-directed synaptic refinement (Routh et al., 2026). During the early developmental stages, the foetus' peripheral clocks are further influenced by maternal feeding (Ohta et al., 2008), and remain so until the light-darkness cycle becomes dominant postnatally.

The theoretical observations and empirical evidence discussed so far highlights the relevance of bodily signals in early social interactions (Atzil et al., 2018; Fotopoulou and Tsakiris, 2017; Ciaunica, 2016 Ciaunica, , 2025)). According to the Social Physiology framework (Atzil et al., 2018; Masalha et al., 2026), physiological function and development in social animals depend on conspecifics and do not operate properly in isolation. From this perspective, the developing foetus is strongly dependent on maternal physiology for homeostasis. Because maternal physiology is largely periodic, these rhythms actively shape the organism's developing physiology. Since these regulatory inputs are predictable, tracking them allows the foetus, and later the infant, to anticipate rather than merely react to physiological needs, lowering the metabolic cost of regulation and yielding social metabolic gains (Masalha et al., 2026).

Reinforced by these gains, such rhythms may be learned and integrated into the neural control of allostasis (Cohen and Atzil, 2026). Repeated coupling between social rhythms and their homeostatic consequences may therefore shape both endogenous rhythmicity and the developing brain's capacity to anticipate and regulate bodily needs.

In the remainder of the paper, we propose to investigate mother-foetus/infant bodily interactions as a multi-level system of oscillators at different frequencies, with the aim to characterize multi-and metastable coupling regimes. The core tenet is that neural oscillators proposed to scaffold timing abilities across modalities must be calibrated during development. During gestation, the foetus' brain is exposed to a rich multisensory landscape featuring his own, as well as maternal bodily and environmental semiperiodic sensory input. Periodic, predictable maternal rhythms (e.g., circadian, cardiac and speech) may provide a temporal reference frame for autonomic co-regulation and for the development of basic timing and rhythm abilities.

6 A dynamical system framing for metastable dyadic interactions

The periodic input discussed so far constitute a dynamic system of endogenous oscillators (Criscuolo et al., 2022) spanning ~7 orders of magnitude in frequency (from slow circadian (24h) and ultradian (e.g., feeding) cycles to respiratory (~.3Hz), cardiac (~1.4Hz for the mother, 2-2.7Hz for the foetus), prosodic (~2 Hz in language), and neural (~1-100Hz) rhythms. Multistability and metastability

The classical view of phase-locked coordination dynamics prescribes that each of the recruited elements would lose its intrinsic behaviour, obeying the dictates of the driver or of the assembly.

However, the body-brain dyadic system we are studying displays the subtle interplay of two competing forces: on the one hand, the components of the system want to preserve endogenous rhythms; on the other hand, they may show the tendency to couple together, adjusting their intrinsic dynamics to other components' phase evolution. In coordination dynamics, the co-existence of autonomous and synergistic tendencies is described by metastability.

Metastability is thought to arise as a result of the self-organizing nature of biological and behavioural systems, and is the result of broken symmetry in the relative phase equation governing the coordination of nonlinearly coupled oscillators (Kelso and Tognoli, 2007). In a metastable regime there are no stable phase and frequency synchronized states: systems are neither completely independent, nor are fully locked-in. In contrast, a system is called stable when attracting tendencies couple the components, making them interdependent. Similarly, a system may be called multistable when several stable patterns of coordination ('attractors') coexist; which pattern is expressed would depend on the system's history and on noise, which can push it from one attractor to another (Kelso, 2012). In-phase and anti-phase are just two out of many possible (multistable) phase states that can occur between systems of oscillators, depending on their respective intrinsic properties and complex mutual influence.

When the tendency to express intrinsic independent behaviours co-exists with the tendency to couple with other system components (attraction), a dynamical flow of phase trapping (i.e., dwell times) and phase scattering (i.e., escape time) is observed (Tognoli and Kelso, 2014; Fig. 2). In metastable regimes, the presence of multiple attractors for phase relationships is thought to provide increased opportunities for flexible coordination and information exchange. In this view, instability (as opposed to unique, stable or multi-stable phase relations) allows adaptive phase transitions (Kelso and Tognoli, 2007). This description yields quantities that average estimates of synchrony inevitably miss: e.g., how long the dyad dwells in each pattern, how often and how quickly it switches, whether switching depends on history (hysteresis), and whether variability rises before transitions. This approach further changes the normative question: a well-regulated dyad should be able to enter and leave coordinated states rather than maximise them, which keeps each partner's dynamics partly autonomous -possibly a prerequisite for self-other differentiation.

Available evidence is compatible with this view but remains indirect. Before birth, maternal and foetal heartbeats show brief (~15 s), nonstationary epochs of phase synchronisation at several n:m ratios (most often 3:2, followed by 5:3 and 4:3), which become more frequent at faster paced maternal breathing (Van Leeuwen et al., 2009) and depend on foetal behavioural state (Samjeed et al., 2024) -consistent with multiple coexisting locking modes tuned by a control parameter (Fig. 2B). After birth, face-to-face interaction alternates between matched and mismatched states, with mismatches repaired frequently rather than avoided (Gianino and Tronick, 1988; Tronick and Cohn, 1989). Maternal and infant heart rhythms couple selectively during episodes of behavioural synchrony (Feldman et al., 2011), and the strength and direction of affective and autonomic coupling vary from moment to moment, with infantto-parent influence often prominent (Chow et al., 2010; Wass et al., 2019).

Importantly, developmental outcomes seem to follow an optimum-midrange rather than a linear rule: midrange vocal rhythm coordination at 4 months predicted secure attachment at 12 months, whereas higher and lower coordination predicted insecure classifications (Jaffe et al., 2001; Beebe and Steele, 2013). More anxious mothers show higher (not lower!) physiological synchrony with their infants (Smith et al., 2022). Rigid parent-child interaction, quantified with state space grids, seems to be associated with externalising and internalising problems (Hollenstein et al., 2004; Fig. 3C), although the benefit of flexibility may differ between mother-and father-child dyads (Lunkenheimer et al., 2011).

Simple coupled-oscillator models reproduce this inverted U, with the temporal variability of coupling maximal near the threshold for locking (Fig. 2D).

To our knowledge, however, no study has directly estimated attractor landscapes, dwell-time distributions or transition signatures in mother-foetus or mother-infant physiological recordings, so the metastability account of the dyad remains a well-motivated but untested hypothesis. We encourage future studies to acquire foetus/neonatal and mother respiratory, cardiac and neural signals in parallel, and potentially in a longitudinal cohort, with the scope of characterizing co-modulatory dynamics in a system of endogenous oscillators and inter-individual variabilities.

The framing proposed goes beyond mere correlational evidence, and may advance our understanding of co-regulation mechanisms in the dyad, as well as more general abilities for phase-amplitude multi-scale coupling between neural activity and periodic sensory streams in the foetus and infant.

7 Discussion and Outlook

In this article, we discussed that the pregnant body provides the fetus with a rich multisensory landscape featuring a plethora of visceral signals, bodily movements, as well as acoustic and vibrotactile sensory input. Each signal represents a continuous, time-varying, yet temporally structured stimulation, scaffolding and shaping the foetus' neurocognitive developmental trajectory.

In turn, we showed that the human developing brain is equipped early on with the abilities to process and adapt to temporal regularities in the intero-and exteroceptive sensory environments. The evidence reviewed here supports such an account at four levels.

Developmentally, the first rhythmic signals of life are cardiovascular: the embryonic heart beats from about day 22, and its morphogenesis depends on haemodynamic loads set jointly by the foetal circulation and the placenta (Burton and Jauniaux, 2018). Second, perceptually, the foetal auditory system responds to sound from about 19 weeks of gestation (Shahidullah and Hepper, 1994) and is exposed to a lowpass-filtered mixture of maternal heartbeat, breathing, movement and voice, transmitted mainly by bone conduction (Abrams and Gerhardt, 2000). Prenatal exposure leaves measurable traces: newborns recognize sounds heard in utero (DeCasper and Spence, 1986; Partanen et al., 2013), their cry melodies follow the prosody of the native language (Mampe et al., 2009), and recorded maternal voice and heartbeat sounds modulate auditory cortex neuroplasticity in preterm infants (Webb et al., 2015). Third, physiologically, maternal and foetal hearts show transient epochs of n:m phase synchronization that depend on maternal breathing rate and foetal behavioural state (Van Leeuwen et al., 2009). Similarly, maternal circadian signals entrain foetal clocks (Reppert and Schwartz, 1983; Reppert and Weaver, 2002). Finally, after birth, maternal and infant heart rhythms couple during episodes of behavioural synchrony (Feldman et al., 2011), and the strength and direction of autonomic coupling fluctuate from moment to moment (Wass et al., 2019).

Taken together, these findings suggest a developmental continuity. Before birth, coupling is mainly maternal-to-foetal and mediated by haemodynamic, mechanical, acoustic and endocrine channels. After birth, coupling becomes bidirectional and increasingly mediated by behaviour, touch and voice. Much of this evidence, however, is indirect. Several findings rest on small samples, and the physiological basis of maternal-foetal cardiac synchronization is still debated (Riedl et al., 2009).

The dynamical-systems framework proposed in Section 6 may support advancements in the field in (at leas) three ways. First, it replaces the question "is the dyad synchronized, and how much?" with questions that the data can answer more precisely: e.g., does coupling act from mother to foetus, from infant to mother, or is there bidirectional influence? At which n:m ratios does coupling occur, and how often do the systems tune-in (dwell times) and -out (escape times) of coupling? Which parameters (e.g., maternal breathing rate, arousal or foetal behavioural state) move the dyad between regimes? Secondly, the proposed approach offers a mechanistic account of an otherwise puzzling result: developmental outcomes follow an optimum-midrange rather than a monotonic rule. Midrange vocal coordination at 4 months predicts secure attachment at 12 months (Jaffe et al., 2001), and more anxious mothers show higher, not lower, physiological synchrony with their infants (Smith et al., 2022). In simple coupled-oscillator models, the variability of coupling peaks near the locking threshold (Fig. 2D).

This places a well-regulated dyad in a metastable regime, able to enter and leave coordinated states, rather than at maximal synchrony.

Third, the framework treats development itself as a change in model parameters. Maturation of autonomic control, of the auditory pathway and of motor output alters intrinsic frequencies and coupling strengths, and the transition at birth can be modelled as a change in coupling channels and direction. This yields testable predictions: coupling should depend on behavioural state; dwell-time distributions should differ between flexible and rigid dyads; and rising variability should precede transitions between coordination patterns, as observed before critical transitions in other complex systems (Scheffer et al., 2009).

We suggest that future work on developmental approaches to cognition, consciousness and dyadic interactions may focus on the key role of bidirectional interactions between neuronal spiking and rhythmic electric field activity generated by the brain as well as the multitude of bodily waves. In the brain, intentional behaviour relies on top-down control to coordinate large neural populations into lowdimensional, task-oriented dynamics (Miller et al., 2026). One may consequently argue that similar mechanisms may underlie body-brain interactions: a communication subspace may orchestrate the brain mechanisms for processing, integrating and coordinating bodily sensation with external input. Brain waves can flexibly route and organize multimodal signals, enabling context-dependent computations via mesoscale influence on network excitability. In turn, subspace coding in systems neurophysiology and travelling waves may provide a well-suited mechanism to capture the complexity of maternal-foetal neurophysiological dynamics (Shervani-Tabar et al., 2026).

Limitations

At least two limitations constrain the framework at present. To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet estimated attractor landscapes, dwell-time distributions or transition signatures in simultaneous maternal and foetal recordings, so the metastability account remains a well-motivated hypothesis.

We encourage future studies to acquire foetus/neonatal and mother respiratory, cardiac and neural signals in parallel, and potentially in a longitudinal cohort, with the scope of characterizing comodulatory dynamics in a system of endogenous oscillators and inter-individual variabilities.

The framing proposed goes beyond mere correlational evidence, and may advance our understanding of co-regulation mechanisms in the dyad, as well as more general abilities for phase-amplitude multi-scale coupling between neural activity and periodic sensory streams in the foetus and infant.

Secondly, phase-oscillator models abstract away from the endocrine, metabolic and mechanical routes by which maternal signals reach the foetus. Testing the account therefore requires simultaneous, longitudinal recordings of maternal and foetal cardiac, respiratory and, where feasible, neural activity, for example by foetal magnetocardiography, abdominal electrocardiography and foetal magnetoencephalography (Moser et al., 2021), followed into infancy with dual recordings during caregiving.

Why does this matter? Clinically, preterm birth abruptly removes the maternal rhythmic milieu during a sensitive period of auditory and autonomic development. Interventions that partly restore it, such as maternal voice and heartbeat sounds or skin-to-skin contact, improve auditory and physiological outcomes (Webb et al., 2015; Feldman et al., 2014). The framework predicts which features of maternal rhythms should matter, including their regularity, variability and state dependence, and could guide the design of neonatal environments. It also provides candidate early markers of dysregulation in dyads exposed to maternal stress, anxiety or sleep and circadian disruption.

Conclusions

Here we tentatively suggested that if the neural oscillators that scaffold basic rhythm and timing abilities as well as interoception are calibrated against the rhythms of another body during development, then the self begins not as an isolated system but as one partner in a dynamic dyad. Hence the kickstart of understanding early minds and experiences is to examine the biophysical and neurophysiological processes happening in between the maternal and foetal bodies. We are literally born on pre-existing bodily rhythms, with the developing foetal body actively striving to "tune in" and to gear its own visceral pace into a preexisting maternal neurophysiological "melody".
