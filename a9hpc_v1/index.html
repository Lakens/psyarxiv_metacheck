Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. Abstract

This conceptual analysis argues that mindful leadership has not yet been established as a distinct leadership construct. Leader mindfulness is an individual-level psychological construct studied among people who lead; its connection to leadership conduct or collective outcomes requires separately specified mechanisms and evidence. Drawing on 58 purposively assembled full-text publications, the article examines construct content and location, empirical representation, research design, and inferential warrant at the level of individual claims. It uses mindfulnessrelated leadership enactment only as a provisional coding domain, a classificatory device for observable conduct involving attention, openness, and regulated responding, not a validated construct. Where item wording is reported, an item-level comparison shows that supervisorreferenced adaptations of an individual mindfulness scale largely preserve content concerning everyday attentional lapses. Communication-focused and purpose-built leadership measures introduce different behavioral and managerial content. The analysis organizes misalignment around four sites in claim construction: phenomenon specification, empirical representation, inference, and intervention decomposition. Worked audits illustrate how claims should enter synthesis through direct comparison, qualified comparison, or distinct treatment. The analysis also separates team mindfulness, mindful organizing, and organizational mindfulness because each requires different composition models and evidence. The resulting framework supports construct development, multisource measurement, temporal and multilevel analysis, intervention decomposition, and a reproducible scoping review. Its purpose is to preserve relevant findings while preventing shared terminology from creating an artificial cumulative literature.

Introduction

Research connecting mindfulness and leadership asks several different questions: whether mindful leaders behave differently; whether leaders communicate with greater attention, openness, or restraint; whether development programs combining meditation with other content are effective; and whether mindfulness describes shared team experiences, organizing processes, or organizational capacities. Reviews indicate both rapid growth and conceptual elasticity in workplace mindfulness research (Roche et al., 2020; Tewari et al., 2026). These literatures can inform one another, but they concern different objects and cannot be combined merely because each uses the term mindfulness (Good et al., 2016; Reb et al., 2020; Urrila, 2022).

The central distinction is between mindfulness in a leader and mindfulness in leadership.

When a general individual scale measures present-focused attention, awareness, or a nonjudgmental orientation, adding leader identifies the participant's organizational role but adds no leadership content. Leadership enters the analysis only when a study specifies and measures the conduct, influence, relationships, or collective processes through which mindfulness is expressed. Studies that preserve this separation measure leader mindfulness and leadership behavior or relational processes separately (Arendt et al., 2019; Lange et al., 2018; Reb et al., 2019; Walsh & Arnold, 2018). By contrast, adapting an individual scale for followers to rate a supervisor changes the observer and rated target, but the score has leadership meaning only if its items represent leading rather than general attentiveness or absentmindedness.

Prior contributions have identified this distinction from several perspectives. Some separate the person from the leadership process by treating mindfulness as a potential antecedent rather than a leadership style (Sauer & Kohls, 2011), questioning its direct translation into effectiveness (Lippincott, 2018), or identifying the conflation of leaders with leadership (Tobias Mortlock & Robinson, 2020). Others distinguish construct location from evidence: Ashkanasy and Kay (2023) separate five forms of mindfulness, while Urrila (2022) and Donald et al. (2026) distinguish personal from relational outcomes and observational from intervention evidence.

Mechanism-focused accounts examine metacognitive practice (Kudesia, 2019) or pathways whose effectiveness remains unestablished (Stedham & Skaar, 2019). Collective constructs have also been specified (Vogus & Sutcliffe, 2012; Yu & Zellmer-Bruhn, 2018). Together, these contributions establish much of the terrain but do not supply a claim-level procedure for determining what a claim concerns, what represents it, and whether differently labeled findings are comparable. This paper therefore integrates four judgments: the claimed phenomenon and its location; the conceptual account that gives it meaning; the evidence representing it; and the conclusion warranted by the design. A study may contain a well-aligned measure of leader mindfulness, an untested behavioral mechanism, a relationship-specific follower outcome, and an organizational implication. Classifying its claims rather than the article as a whole reveals which findings are directly comparable, require qualification, or concern distinct phenomena.

Applying this approach supports a substantive judgment: mindful leadership has not yet been established as a distinct leadership construct. Existing evidence addresses mindfulness among leaders, followers' perceptions, specific forms of attentive or nonreactive conduct, and development programs, but does not show that these phenomena form a coherent leadership style with clear boundaries. This judgment is provisional. Future construct development may establish distinctiveness, but a label, reliable scale, or favorable association is insufficient. Researchers must distinguish whether the construct has leadership-specific content, whether measures discriminate it from alternatives, whether it shows differentiated nomological relations, and whether it adds utility where an increment is theoretically expected (Banks et al., 2018; Hoch et al., 2018; Shaffer et al., 2016).

The argument proceeds in three steps. It first separates the phenomena under study from their conceptual accounts, empirical representations, designs, and interventions. It then applies this architecture to leader mindfulness and conduct, collective constructs, and supervisorreferenced measures. Finally, worked claim audits translate the distinctions into comparison decisions and implications for research.

Approach to Conceptual Analysis

The problem concerns construct meaning and evidential alignment rather than effect magnitude. Accordingly, the article uses conceptual analysis to reorganize existing theory and evidence (MacInnis, 2011). The analysis draws on two related source sets. A purposive focal corpus of 58 full-text conceptual, empirical, intervention, review, and measurement publications, assembled through exploratory reading, author and citation tracing, and targeted searches, was used to examine how mindfulness-related leadership phenomena have been labeled, located, represented, and interpreted. Sources were retained when they defined or measured a focal phenomenon, supplied a diagnostic or boundary case, or informed the claim-level analysis.

Auxiliary sources on mindfulness measurement, construct validity, levels of analysis, and neighboring leadership constructs supply analytical standards or benchmarks and are not members of the focal corpus. For each source, the analysis recorded the nominal construct, operational content, evidence source, analytical location, theoretical role, and inferential limits.

Item-level comparisons used wording reported in the original article or its cited instrument source; Table A2 identifies the provenance, language, availability, and quotation or paraphrase status of each item set. Table A1 documents each focal publication and its use in the article.

Because the corpus is purposive, it supports conceptual diagnosis rather than estimates of fieldwide prevalence.

The analysis examines each claim through four linked questions. First, what phenomenon is being asserted, what content defines it, and who or what is said to possess it? Second, which conceptual tradition or mechanism gives the phenomenon its meaning? Third, what evidence represents it, including the rater, item content, time frame, and level of analysis? Fourth, what conclusion is warranted by the design and analysis? For intervention claims, the fourth question also distinguishes among program exposure, package effects, component effects, mechanisms, and outcomes. The first two questions draw on principles of construct clarity and content validation (Colquitt et al., 2019; Podsakoff et al., 2016; Suddaby, 2010), whereas the third applies standards for measurement and multilevel composition (Chan, 1998; Klein et al., 1994; Kozlowski & Klein, 2000; MacKenzie et al., 2011). The fourth distinguishes common-source limitations from causal warrant (Antonakis et al., 2010; Podsakoff et al., 2012). Applying these questions together is essential because claims about mindfulness and leadership often cross several boundaries at once.

The analysis keeps conceptual judgment distinct from empirical findings. Reliability or factor structure indicates score consistency or internal structure, not proof that a scale captures its named construct. Temporal separation is not equated with causal identification, and follower reports are not treated as direct observations of a leader's private mental state. The aim is to identify the strongest interpretation warranted by each claim rather than assign one quality rating to an entire study. This approach separates the analytical layers grouped under the mindful leadership label.

The term mindful leadership currently spans several analytical layers, creating problems when categories from different layers are presented as alternative versions of the same construct.

A psychological capacity, a philosophical account, a follower rating, and a training program differ fundamentally in kind. The first proposes a phenomenon; the second supplies theoretical meaning or evaluative commitments; the third represents evidence from a particular perspective; and the fourth is an intervention intended to change one or more outcomes.

Phenomena and Analytical Location

At the individual level, mindfulness may refer either to a momentary state or to a relatively stable capacity.

These formulations require different research designs. Within-person fluctuations are best examined through repeated state assessments, whereas between-person differences require evidence that a score reliably distinguishes individuals over an appropriate period. A person's organizational role may change why the capacity matters, but it does not necessarily change the nature of the capacity itself. In this usage, leader mindfulness refers to individual mindfulness examined among people who hold leadership roles. Leadership conduct, by contrast, is located in episodes, relationships, or repeated patterns of influence. A leader may display attentive and regulated conduct in one interaction but not in another, and different followers may experience the same leader differently. Repeated behavioral evidence could support a claim about a leader-level tendency, but such a claim requires a composition rule explaining how episodes or relationships combine to form a stable pattern. A single global rating cannot establish that pattern by itself. Maintaining this distinction preserves a theoretically useful possibility: personal mindfulness may increase the likelihood of particular forms of conduct without determining them.

Collective phenomena require further distinctions. Team mindfulness, mindful organizing, and organizational mindfulness differ in stability, direction of emergence, empirical indicators, and the role assigned to formal leaders (see the Collective Mindfulness Phenomena section), so they should neither be compressed into one category of collective mindfulness nor arranged as an automatic progression from mindful individuals to mindful organizations.

Conceptual Accounts and Normative Commitments

In addition to occupying different analytical locations, mindfulness carries different theoretical meanings. Contemplative-informed psychological accounts locate mindfulness in the quality of attention to present experience, emphasizing present-focused attention and an open or nonjudgmental orientation. However, their operational definitions and measures vary in the roles assigned to intention, awareness, acceptance, and cultivation (Bishop et al., 2004; Van Dam et al., 2018). Sociocognitive accounts instead locate mindfulness in active engagement with the environment, emphasizing distinction-making, novelty, and sensitivity to context without requiring meditation. Kudesia's (2019) metacognitive-practice account bridges these traditions by treating mindfulness as the situated monitoring and adjustment of information processing, supported by enabling beliefs. These differences matter for leadership theory because each account implies a different mechanism and, consequently, different forms of leader conduct and evidence, for example, a calmer response to a follower, a more flexible interpretation of a situation, or better regulation of one's own processing. The accounts may inform one another, but theoretical integration does not make their measures or mechanisms interchangeable (Roche et al., 2020). Normative accounts extend the distinction further by incorporating values into the definition itself.

Buddhist, Daoist, and other philosophical accounts may treat compassion, nonattachment, wisdom, ethical discernment, authenticity, or relational responsibility as constitutive elements of mindful leadership. These formulations can be situated along a continuum according to how far they extend beyond attention. Applied accounts remain closest to attentional formulations by connecting mindfulness with leader focus, reflection, communication, and development (Chaskalson et al., 2020; Ehrlich, 2017; Wells, 2015). Integrative frameworks retain attention while adding further qualities. Doornich and Lynch (2024), for example, derive a review-derived three-pillar framework of attention, awareness, and authenticity, whereas King and Badham (2020) propose a second-generation framework designed to move beyond first-generation attentional accounts. Explicitly normative accounts extend furthest by incorporating ethical and relational ideals into the definition itself (Nilsson & Kazemi, 2023; Tan, 2023). Across this continuum, the content of mindful leadership shifts from a describable capacity toward a claim about what leadership ought to be. These accounts therefore advance definitional and evaluative claims, whose coherence is assessed through their premises and treatment of the traditions rather than through data. The recurrence of a valued quality across multiple accounts does not establish that it belongs to a single empirical construct; likewise, associations between questionnaire scores and desirable outcomes cannot establish the normative adequacy of a philosophical account.

Representations, Raters, and Designs

The source of evidence contributes to a score's meaning because different raters have access to different aspects of the phenomenon. Leaders have privileged access to their own attentional experiences and behavioral intentions but limited access to how others receive their conduct. Followers have the opposite vantage point: they can report observed conduct and their own experience of the relationship, but judgments about a leader's inner attributes require inferences from what they observe.

Self-other disagreement is therefore expected rather than anomalous. Fleenor et al. (2010) show that such disagreement is common in leadership assessment and that neither source should automatically be treated as providing the true score.

The reasons for disagreement follow from the same logic of differential access. Raters vary in their opportunities to observe, the relationships through which observation occurs, their goals and contextual information, and whether the measure functions equivalently across sources.

Differential access is not the only influence on follower reports; followers' expectations of leaders also shape their judgments. Implicit leadership theories organize prototypes of effective and ineffective leaders that influence perception, interpretation, and memory (Epitropaki & Martin, 2004). The extent to which these prototypes affect a rating depends partly on the information the measure asks followers to retrieve. Global retrospective ratings rely on semantic summaries and general evaluative impressions, leaving greater scope for prototypes to operate, whereas event-specific assessments draw more directly on episodic information about particular interactions (Shondrick et al., 2010). This does not mean that follower ratings are inherently invalid. Rather, their meaning and evidentiary strength depend on the design.

Behavioral specificity, temporal proximity, repeated occasions, and multiple sources can reduce reliance on general impressions and thereby strengthen the claims that follower ratings support.

These considerations become particularly important when studies move from measuring leader mindfulness to making claims about leadership conduct.

From Leader Mindfulness to Leadership Conduct

Leader Mindfulness as an Individual-Level Construct

Unless leadership-specific content is explicitly added and validated, leader mindfulness is best treated as an individual-level psychological construct studied among people in leadership roles, not as a form of leadership itself. Its theoretical role depends on the model: it may operate as a between-person antecedent, a within-person state, a moderator, a time-varying resource, an intervention target, or an outcome of training. Leaders' self-reported mindfulness has been related to separately measured transformational or destructive leadership behavior, mindful communication, leader-member exchange, interpersonal justice, employee stress, and follower well-being or performance (Arendt et al., 2019; Lange et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2021; Reb et al., 2014 Reb et al., , 2019)). Walsh and Arnold (2018) examine it as a resource that may alter leaders' behavioral responses to emotional exhaustion, whereas Zhang et al. (2020) position leader mindfulness as a moderator in a model centered on follower mindfulness and perceived authentic leadership. These models need not rename personal mindfulness as leadership. Instead, they test whether an individual capacity or state predicts, conditions, or changes alongside conduct, perceptions, and relational processes.

Recent studies extend these proposed pathways to resilience, support for change, knowledge hiding, and outcomes beyond the immediate workplace (Feng, 2025; Feng et al., 2026; He et al., 2026; Kurian, 2026). The focal capacity must therefore be kept distinct from its proposed mediators and consequences, and evidence should accumulate around equivalent operationalizations and relationships rather than around the desirability or novelty of the outcomes examined.

Whatever theoretical role leader mindfulness occupies, the meaning and distinctiveness of its score remain consequential. Reliability and a one-factor solution cannot by themselves determine what a score represents (Grossman, 2011; see the section What the Measures Actually Represent), and the same scrutiny applies to separation from neighboring individual attributes such as attentional control, self-control, psychological flexibility, emotional stability, decentering, and low absent-mindedness. A leadership theory cannot acquire specificity that its individual-level measure lacks merely by changing the rated target or organizational context.

Findings should therefore remain tied to the operationalization used instead of being generalized automatically to every definition of mindfulness.

Mindfulness-Related Leadership Enactment as a Provisional Domain

To distinguish observable conduct from personal mindfulness and follower attribution, this article uses mindfulness-related leadership enactment as a provisional coding domain, not a validated leadership style, for observable conduct in a leader-follower, task, or interactional setting that is plausibly connected to present-focused attention, openness before judgment, or regulation of impulsive responding. The term does not imply that these behaviors are unique to mindfulness. Across the accounts examined here, recurring candidate dimensions include attention to the follower, task, or unfolding interaction; openness to information and willingness to revise an interpretation; and regulation of impulsive or automatic responses. These dimensions are sensitizing categories for analysis, not necessary or sufficient attributes or proposed scale dimensions, and their boundaries from perspective taking, metacognitive monitoring, communication competence, and emotion regulation require empirical study. The domain is initially located in leadership episodes or relationships. Evidence that such conduct recurs across situations would be required before it could be described as a stable leader-level pattern.

A passage or measure is coded within the domain only when it describes conduct attributable to a leader in an interaction or work episode.

Leader self-reports of internal mindfulness alone, global follower evaluations without behaviorally anchored content, program participation, outcomes such as trust or well-being, and team-or organization-level phenomena are excluded. Compassion, ethical judgment, authenticity, nonattachment, and systemic awareness are not included automatically; a theory must specify whether each quality is constitutive of the domain, an antecedent, a consequence, or a normative commitment. Attentive listening and calm communication are plausible indicators, but they may also reflect communication competence, emotional regulation, interpersonal sensitivity, or established leadership behaviors. Arendt et al.'s (2019) measure of mindfulness in communication therefore samples relevant conduct without establishing the full domain. Similarly, Donaldson-Feilder et al.'s (2022) Delphi study identifies interpersonal capacities considered important for leader development, but expert agreement does not by itself establish a distinct construct. The domain therefore has a classificatory rather than ontological role. It prevents personal mindfulness from being treated as behavioral leadership by definition and marks the content that would need to be explained and tested if mindful leadership were later proposed as a distinct construct. It does not establish distinctiveness in advance: communication competence, active listening, perspective taking, emotion regulation, and ethical, authentic, and servant leadership remain comparator domains against which any future construct claim must be tested. The Burden of Construct Distinctiveness Mindfulness-related enactment overlaps with existing constructs that already represent attentive, ethical, relational, or regulated leadership. Authentic leadership includes selfawareness and balanced information processing; ethical leadership includes normatively appropriate conduct and reinforcement; servant leadership emphasizes follower development and care; and transformational leadership includes individualized consideration. Reviews of leaderbehavior measures indicate that construct proliferation and overlapping behavioral content already impede cumulative leadership research (Banks et al., 2018). Hoch et al. (2018) likewise demonstrate why theoretical differentiation alone is insufficient. In their analysis, authentic and ethical leadership correlated strongly with transformational leadership and generally contributed limited additional explanatory value. Servant leadership appeared more distinct, but the supporting literature was smaller and heterogeneous. This evidence does not establish that mindful leadership is redundant, because mindful leadership was not included in the comparison. Rupprecht et al. (2019) raise the same concern from within the mindful leadership literature, noting overlap with servant, authentic, humanoriented, and agile leadership and calling for tests of whether mindful leadership is conceptually distinct. It does, however, clarify several distinct evidentiary questions. Definitional distinctiveness concerns whether the construct has theoretically specified content not reducible to neighboring constructs. Empirical discriminant validity concerns whether measures recover that distinction, and nomological distinctiveness concerns whether the construct shows theoretically differentiated relationships. Incremental utility is a separate question: whether the construct improves explanation or prediction beyond existing constructs for outcomes where such improvement is theoretically expected. A purpose-built measure such as Baek's (2024) can provide initial evidence on some of these questions, but incremental prediction cannot by itself establish distinctiveness, and limited incremental prediction does not by itself establish redundancy.

Until these questions are answered and replicated, mindful leadership is best treated as a field label encompassing several constructs.

Collective Mindfulness Phenomena

Team Mindfulness

Among the collective phenomena considered here, team mindfulness is the most clearly specified shared-perception construct. Yu and Zellmer-Bruhn (2018) shifted the object of judgment from the individual to the team, developed team-referent items, and provided a composition model explaining how member reports form a team-level score. Before examining team-level relationships with conflict, they tested agreement within teams and differentiation between teams. This approach goes beyond administering individual mindfulness items to several members and calculating an average. It defines the proposed collective property and provides evidence that members report a sufficiently shared experience of team interaction.

Mindful Organizing

Mindful organizing represents a different type of collective phenomenon. It concerns coordinated attention and action in ongoing work, particularly the ways frontline actors detect weak signals, resist simplification, maintain sensitivity to operations, recover from disruption, and defer to relevant expertise. The construct originates in research on collective mindfulness in high-reliability organizing (Weick et al., 1999). Vogus and Sutcliffe (2012) subsequently distinguish the recurrent process of mindful organizing from a more enduring organizational capability. The empirical object is therefore collective enactment rather than a stable climate or the aggregated mindfulness of team members. Formal leaders may enable or constrain this process, but leadership is not the construct's sole analytical location.

Organizational Mindfulness

Organizational mindfulness, in turn, refers to a comparatively enduring capability embedded in strategic attention, structures, and practices. Ray et al. (2011) illustrate an organization-referent measurement approach by asking administrators to assess their college's practices across the five high-reliability dimensions. Their findings also reveal a measurement challenge: deans, associate deans, assistant deans, and department chairs reported systematically different levels of organizational mindfulness. Although the items explicitly named the organization, sparse sampling within schools and perspectives conditioned by organizational role limited the extent to which the scores could be interpreted as a verified shared property of the organization.

These distinctions change how multilevel relationships should be modeled. Team mindfulness requires evidence of shared perceptions of team interaction, mindful organizing requires recurrent collective action, and organizational mindfulness requires evidence of structures or practices that persist beyond individual actors. What the Measures Actually Represent: An Item-Level Analysis

The central measurement problem becomes apparent when items, rather than scale labels alone, are examined (Table 3). The MAAS is examined not as representative of mindfulness measures but because corpus studies adapted it for follower ratings of supervisors and labeled the scores as mindful leadership. The MAAS was developed to assess everyday lapses in attention and awareness (Brown & Ryan, 2003), including forgetting a newly heard name, acting on automatic pilot, breaking or spilling things through inattention, preoccupation with the past or future, and eating without awareness. Grossman (2011) questioned whether this content adequately represents intention, nonjudgment, openness, and cultivated awareness. Brown et al. (2011) replied that attention and meta-awareness are central and reviewed evidence supporting the MAAS. The argument need not resolve that debate: even if the MAAS validly measures an attentional form of personal mindfulness, its items contain no leadership role, relationship, influence process, or organizational task. Sites of Construct-Evidence Alignment

The item-level analysis clarifies why conceptual conflation has practical consequences.

Rather than introducing a second taxonomy, this section maps the five layers in Table 2 onto four sites at which a claim may become misaligned. The layers inventory the information a claim may require; the sites identify where misalignment can arise as the claim is constructed.

Phenomenon and location, together with conceptual account, inform specification; empirical representation corresponds to representation; design and inferential purpose correspond to inference; and intervention specification corresponds to intervention decomposition, which applies only when the evidence concerns a program. The sites draw on established concerns about levels and composition (Chan, 1998; Klein et al., 1994; Kozlowski & Klein, 2000), construct representation, content, and method (Colquitt et al., 2019; MacKenzie et al., 2011; Podsakoff et al., 2012 Podsakoff et al., , 2016)), and design-based causal inference (Antonakis et al., 2010). They are analytically distinguishable but not statistically or causally independent: specification sets the target for representation, representation and design jointly constrain inference, and decomposition is required when a treatment label bundles several components. The sequence is therefore a practical order of appraisal, not a claim that all errors originate upstream or a global judgment of study quality.

Specification: Definition and Analytical Location

Misalignment at specification occurs when an argument begins with one phenomenon but reaches a conclusion about another. One recurring shift in the examined corpus is from individual mindfulness to mindful leadership: the theory describes personal attention and awareness, a leader or follower completes an individual-mindfulness scale, and the resulting association is then presented as evidence of a leadership style. A related cross-level shift occurs when leader mindfulness is presumed to produce team or organizational mindfulness. A person's leadership role may make a personal capacity consequential for leadership, but it does not relocate that capacity into conduct, a relationship, or a collective phenomenon. Supervisoradapted MAAS studies place the supervisor in the item stem while retaining individual-attention content, leaving the score ambiguously located between the leader's capacity and the follower's attribution.

Representation: Evidential Correspondence and Access

A representation mismatch arises when the evidence either fails to cover the construct's content or cannot provide the type of access that the claim requires. The original MAAS underrepresents several elements included in broader contemplative definitions, whereas supervisor-adapted versions require followers to infer attentional causes that may be private.

At the opposite end of the spectrum, broad purpose-built measures may introduce content whose relationship to mindfulness remains uncertain. Several of Baek's items are relevant to leadership but may instead indicate conscientious management, emotional regulation, ethical leadership, or coaching. Arendt et al.'s behavioral specificity improves observability but narrows the represented domain to communication. Neither breadth nor specificity is automatically preferable. The measure must correspond to the stated definition and intended analytical object, and any important content excluded by that choice should be acknowledged. Inference: Conclusions Warranted by Design Inferential overreach occurs when the prose advances a stronger conclusion than the research design can support. Cross-sectional indirect effects do not demonstrate a temporal mechanism, and temporal separation alone does not eliminate stable omitted causes or commonsource bias. Zheng et al. (2022), for example, separated some measurements by three months, but employees reported mindful leadership and creative-process engagement at the same time, and all variables remained self-reports. Meng et al. (2026) estimated a complex sequence involving psychological safety, self-efficacy, and safety citizenship from a single-respondent survey. Such models test whether the observed covariance is consistent with the proposed pathways. Causal claims require stronger temporal ordering, source separation, experimental intervention, or another form of design-based identification.

Intervention Decomposition: Package, Component, Mechanism, and Outcome

Misalignment in intervention claims occurs when a multicomponent development program is treated as a test of mindfulness or mindful leadership without separating exposure and implementation, package effectiveness, active components, psychological mechanisms, leadership-behavior outcomes, and downstream follower or collective outcomes. Programs evaluated with medical specialists, organizational leaders, and public-administration executives combine meditation practice with psychoeducation, reflection, coaching, communication exercises, peer discussion, stress management, or established leadership content (Kersemaekers et al., 2020; Lange & Rowold, 2019; Rueda-Sánchez et al., 2026; Rupprecht et al., 2019). A pre-post change can support a claim about package effectiveness under the observed implementation conditions. It does not by itself identify meditation as the active component, establish personal mindfulness as the mechanism, or demonstrate change in leadership conduct. Component contrasts, credible active comparators, fidelity evidence, temporally ordered mediators, and measures at the claimed outcome level are required for stronger conclusions. Reviews accordingly find more consistent effects on personal well-being than on relational or leadership outcomes (Donald et al., 2026; Urrila, 2022). Discussion and Implications for the Future of Work

The principal implication is that mindful leadership cannot yet be treated as a single cumulative variable. The literature contains informative evidence about personal mindfulness among leaders, follower attributions, communication conduct, leadership-development packages, and collective mindfulness phenomena, but the value of this evidence depends on preserving these distinctions. Effect estimates should therefore remain linked to the operational object, rater, analytical location, and focal relationship from which they were derived. Evidence can then accumulate within clearly defined families while theory specifies how those families may be connected.

Several structural conditions may encourage boundary crossing. Individual-mindfulness instruments predate leadership-specific measures, making referent adaptation a low-cost option.

Role-based labeling can also redescribe a property of a person who leads as a property of leadership. The positive valence of mindfulness may reduce scrutiny of favorable associations, while "mindful leadership" can serve as an umbrella label for multicomponent development programs. These are hypotheses about field development rather than demonstrated prevalence or causes.

Mindfulness should not be assumed uniformly beneficial. Brief state inductions have reduced task motivation without reducing performance and attenuated guilt-driven reparative responses in specific contexts (Hafenbrack & Vohs, 2018; Hafenbrack et al., 2022). These findings do not establish adverse leadership effects, but they require theories to specify the form, context, mechanism, and outcome. Evaluative halo should therefore be treated as a rival substantive hypothesis, not only as a limitation. Adapted follower-rated mindfulness scales may load substantially on a general positive-leadership or relationship-evaluation factor, and their associations with follower outcomes may attenuate after controlling for general leader evaluation, relationship quality, or established positive-leadership constructs. Event-specific and behaviorally anchored ratings should differentiate more clearly than global retrospective judgments, while relationship-specific variance should exceed leader-level consensus when scores primarily reflect dyadic experience.

Multitrait-multimethod and multilevel designs can test whether convergence follows mindfulness-related content or rater source.

For a proposed mindful leadership construct, correlations with trust, satisfaction, commitment, or well-being provide weak evidence because established positive-leadership constructs predict similar outcomes (Hoch et al., 2018); the four questions set out in the section on the burden of construct distinctiveness require content-validity research, appropriate comparators, replication, and measurement invariance.

Research Agenda

The first research priority is definitional and operational, and later priorities depend on it.

Studies must distinguish personal mindfulness in a leader, mindfulness-related leadership behavior, follower perceptions of a leader, dyadic processes, and collective phenomena before scores can be compared or accumulated; stronger designs or larger samples cannot repair this ambiguity because they estimate relationships among differently constituted variables. Authors proposing mindful leadership should specify its defining and excluded content, who or what possesses it, its temporal scope, and its theoretical role. They should also state whether the term denotes a leadership style, a domain of behavior, a relational property, a developmental aim, or an umbrella field. These alternatives require different forms of evidence and cannot be distinguished through factor analysis alone.

Measure development should then begin by considering the access each source has to the proposed phenomenon. Follower experience requires reports from followers with adequate exposure, whereas relational and collective constructs require explicit composition models and evidence of agreement or coordinated process. Item generation and content-validity assessment should precede tests of internal structure (Colquitt et al., 2019). Subsequent validation should distinguish definitional distinctiveness, empirical discriminant validity, nomological distinctiveness, and incremental utility rather than treating them as one test. Self-other agreement, temporal stability, measurement invariance, and comparisons with adjacent constructs should be examined where appropriate (Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). Designs should also test evaluative halo as a rival explanation by comparing global follower ratings with general leader evaluation, relationship quality, established positive-leadership constructs, and eventspecific behavioral evidence.

Process research should directly test the transitions shown in Figure Table 5 summarizes the resulting program of research. A systematic scoping review is especially important because the present analysis cannot determine how frequently each form of misalignment occurs. The unit of coding should be the individual claim or operationalization rather than the publication as a whole; coding articles globally would reproduce the very conflation the review is intended to examine. Item wording, rater, target, time frame, analytical level, proposed mechanism, and stated conclusion should therefore be extracted separately.

Limitations

Because the corpus is purposive rather than systematic, it cannot establish how frequently particular practices occur or whether the selected examples represent the field, and recent operationalizations may modify the boundaries proposed here.

The item-level analysis is selective rather than a comprehensive psychometric review. Its conclusions about attentional-lapse content apply to the MAAS and the supervisor-referenced adaptations examined here; they do not automatically extend to instruments based on different mindfulness definitions or dimensions. Complete item wording was available for Meng et al.

Conclusion

Leader mindfulness and mindful leadership should not be treated as interchangeable.

Leader mindfulness locates a psychological capacity or state within an individual who leads.

Evidence described as mindful leadership may instead concern follower attributions, observable conduct, relational experience, a development program, or a collective process. These phenomena may be connected, but shared terminology does not establish common content, analytical location, mechanism, or measurement. Current research therefore supports several related families of evidence more clearly than it supports a single encompassing mindful leadership construct.

The central contribution of this analysis is a method for preserving those findings without overstating their comparability. A stronger construct claim would require bounded leadershipspecific content, adequate representation, empirical and nomological separation from plausible alternatives, and incremental utility where theory predicts it. Multilevel and intervention theories must then specify and test the transitions from personal mindfulness to conduct, relational interpretation, and collective phenomena. Shared labels do not warrant shared interpretation: whether findings can be compared depends on the content, bearer, rater, level, and design behind each claim, and conclusions should extend only as far as that evidence supports.

An informant interpretation requires correspondence with other indicators and an account of which internal qualities followers can reasonably observe. An attribution interpretation locates the score in the follower's judgment and treats disagreement among followers as potentially meaningful rather than merely erroneous. A conduct interpretation, by contrast, requires items that describe leadership-relevant actions rather than private experience or generic inattentiveness. Simply changing personal-mindfulness items from "I" to "my supervisor" does not resolve which of these objects the score represents. The grammatical change identifies the person being judged, but the item content determines whether the judgment concerns observable leadership conduct. The relevant principle is inferential distance: items describing what a leader did in an interaction can represent observed conduct, whereas items requiring an inference about an unobservable attentional cause represent an attributed capacity. In the absence of validation evidence supporting an accurate informant or conduct interpretation, a follower-rated adaptation of an individual-mindfulness scale should therefore be interpreted by default as a follower-level attribution of the supervisor's apparent attentional functioning. Stronger interpretations require evidence such as convergence across followers, agreement with leader self-report, behavioral observation, separation of relationship-specific from leader-general variance, and discriminant validity from general leader evaluation. Authors should specify the analytical location and intended meaning of the score before using it as a predictor, mediator, or indicator of mindful leadership.
