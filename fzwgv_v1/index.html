How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Measuring Cognitive Noise: Separating Signal Strength from Random Fluctuations in Evidence Accumulation

Evidence accumulation models are among the most successful formal models in cognitive psychology. The diffusion model (Ratcliff, 1978; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Voss et al., 2013) assumes that a decision between two response alternatives is based on the noisy accumulation of evidence until one of two boundaries is reached. The model predicts the joint distribution of choices and response times, and it has been applied to perception, memory, lexical decisions, individual differences, aging, and clinical questions (Ratcliff et al., 2016). Its parameters are interpreted as measures of distinct cognitive processes: The drift rate v reflects the speed of information uptake, the boundary separation a reflects response caution, the starting point z reflects a priori bias, and the nondecision time t0 reflects the duration of encoding and motor processes (Voss et al., 2004). The validity of this decomposition has been supported by experimental manipulations (Arnold et al., 2015; Lerche & Voss, 2018; Voss et al., 2004), and it was also confirmed by a large blinded collaborative inference study (Dutilh et al., 2019).

In addition to the four parameters of the basic diffusion model, an additional parameter (the diffusion coefficient s) describes the random variability of the accumulation process within a trial. This parameter is typically not estimated as a free parameter, but fixed to a constant value.

The reason is the scaling property of the diffusion model: The likelihood only depends on the ratios of a, v, z with s, and thus the predicted RT distributions do not change when these parameters are multiplied by a constant value. To identify the remaining parameters, s is fixed to a constant, at s = 0.1 in the tradition of Ratcliff (1978) and at s = 1 in most current software packages (Fengler et al., 2021; Fengler et al., 2026; Henrich et al., 2024; Voss et al., 2015). In the literature, s is therefore often described as an arbitrary scaling parameter without psychological meaning (Donkin et al., 2009; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008).

It can be argued, however, that this view is incomplete. The diffusion coefficient has a clear psychological interpretation: It quantifies the amount of random variability in the accumulation of evidence, that is, cognitive noise. Fixing s to a constant implicitly makes the assumption that cognitive noise is identical for all persons and all conditions. As a consequence, drift and boundary are measured in units of the individual noise level. When noise differs between persons or conditions, observed differences in drift or boundary separation become ambiguous. A lower drift rate in one group may reflect less signal or more noise, and a smaller boundary separation may reflect less caution or more noise. I argue that cognitive noise is a psychologically meaningful construct, and that its measurement can contribute to the understanding of cognitive processes.

In this article, two different evidence accumulation frameworks are presented that make it possible to separate signal strength and cognitive noise from behavioral data. Both approaches replace the conventional scaling assumption by a substantive assumption about the way information enters the decision process. The Bounded Drift approach assumes that the drift is a bounded function of stimulus information and that the maximum drift, which is reached for completely unambiguous stimuli, is the same for all persons. The Random Walk approach assumes that evidence arrives in discrete samples of constant size at an individual rate. In this model drift rate and noise are both consequences of the sampling process, and the unit of the evidence axis is anchored to a property of the information flow rather than to the noise, and the noise becomes a free parameter.

The article is organized as follows. First, the diffusion process and its scaling property are described, and consequences of fixing the diffusion coefficient are discussed. Then, I review previous attempts at identifying the diffusion coefficient and introduce the two new approaches, including their formal specification and the assumptions that provide identification. Because the Random Walk Model has no tractable likelihood function in the form used here, the models were fit with amortized Bayesian inference using neural networks (Radev et al., 2022; Radev et al., 2023). Simulation studies on parameter recovery and calibration are presented, followed by an empirical pilot study in which both models were fitted to the same data from five younger and five older adults. The paper closes with a discussion of cognitive noise as a construct and of the research program that is needed to validate its measurement.

The Scaling Property of the Diffusion Model

The first passage time distributions of the process in Equation 1depend on the parameters only through ratios. Multiplying v, a, za, and s by a common positive constant leaves the predicted choice probabilities and response time distributions unchanged. The model is therefore not identified unless one of these parameters is fixed. By convention, the diffusion coefficient s is set to a constant value for this purpose (Donkin et al., 2009). The remaining identified quantities are the signal-to-noise ratio v/s and the boundary-to-noise ratio a/s (the relative starting point z is unaffected by scaling).

This convention has a direct consequence for the meaning of the parameters. With s fixed to 1, the estimated drift rate v is numerically identical to the ratio v/s, and the estimated boundary separation a is numerically identical to the ratio a/s. In other words, the unit of the evidence axis is the amount of random movement that the process makes in one second. This is called an information unit in this paper. A drift rate of v = 1 means that the systematic movement of the process in one second equals the random movement; a boundary separation of a = 1 means that the amount of information that is required for a response equals the contribution of the noise in one second. Drift rates and boundary separations are thus relative quantities, scaled by the noise of the person whose data are analyzed.

Consequences of Fixing the Diffusion Coefficient

Using a constant diffusion coefficient amounts to the assumption that all persons and all conditions have the same amount of cognitive noise. This assumption is rarely made explicit, and there is no empirical evidence that supports it. On the contrary, there are theoretical reasons to expect that the amount of random variability in the accumulation process might differ between persons, states, and tasks. The neural noise hypothesis of cognitive aging, for instance, attributes age-related cognitive decline to an increase in the randomness of neural information processing (Cremer & Zeef, 1987; Li et al., 2001), and states like fatigue, stress, or sleep deprivation plausibly affect the fidelity of neural signaling. If such differences exist, they will be absorbed into the estimates of the other parameters in a standard diffusion model analysis.

Specifically, when a drift effect is observed, we know only that the signal-to-noise ratio differs between conditions or groups. We do not know whether the signal, the noise, or both differ. A decrease of drift in older adults in memory retrieval (Ratcliff et al., 2011; Spaniol et al., 2006; Theisen et al., 2021) or in general decision making in very high age (von Krause et al., 2022) is compatible with a reduced rate of information uptake, but it is equally compatible with an unchanged rate of information uptake and an increased level of noise. The same ambiguity holds for the relationship between drift rate and intelligence (Lerche et al., 2020; Schubert et al., 2015): More intelligent persons may extract information faster, or they may process information with less noise. Likewise, when a boundary effect is observed, we know only that the boundaryto-noise ratio differs. A smaller boundary separation can reflect a lower response criterion, but it can also reflect an increase of noise with an unchanged criterion.

A further consequence concerns the structure of individual differences. If noise varies across persons, all estimated drift rates and boundary separations of a person carry a common factor 1/s. This factor induces positive correlations between estimated drift rates and boundary separations that do not reflect a relation between information uptake and caution, and it attenuates relations between the parameters and external criteria. Interindividual differences in noise may therefore blur effects on the other parameters. Finally, the scaling convention hides a quantity that may be of substantive interest in its own right. Within the model, noise is a determinant of accuracy and speed that is logically distinct from the quality of the stimulus information and from the amount of information required for a response, and it is a natural candidate for a mechanism that is affected by age, clinical conditions, and states such as intoxication or sleep loss.

Previous Approaches to Identifying the Diffusion Coefficient

Two strategies have been discussed for freeing the diffusion coefficient. The first is to fix a different parameter instead of s, for example a = 1 or v = 1 (Donkin et al., 2009). Donkin et al.

showed that the choice of the fixed parameter is immaterial for model fit, but that it changes the interpretation of parameter differences between conditions. Fixing a or v, however, is only a reparameterization. The resulting estimate of s is again a ratio, s/a or s/v, and the identification problem is relocated rather than solved. No new information about the noise is extracted from the data.

The second strategy is to use external covariates.

Nunez et al. (2025) proposed diffusion models in which drift rate, boundary separation, or within-trial noise are linked to observed covariates such as single-trial electroencephalographic measures or to person-level variables. If the linking assumption is correct, the covariate anchors the scale of the evidence axis and all three parameters become identifiable. Nunez et al. demonstrated good parameter recovery in simulations, including some robustness to misspecification of the link. The limitation of this strategy is practical: Covariates with a high and known validity for one specific model parameter are hard to obtain, and the identification rests entirely on the assumed link between the covariate and the parameter. Any identification of s requires an additional assumption, because the behavioral data alone are invariant to the scaling. The important question is which assumption makes it possible to extract independent information about noise, drift, and boundaries from the data, is psychologically plausible, and can be tested. In this paper I propose two such assumptions. Both anchor the unit of the evidence axis to a property of the information that enters the decision process. The first anchors it to the maximum rate of information uptake; the second anchors it to the size of a single piece of information.

The Bounded Drift Approach

Rationale

The drift rate in the diffusion model depends on the quality of the stimulus information. In many tasks, stimulus information can be varied parametrically. Examples are the proportion of white pixels in a black-and-white array (Ratcliff & Rouder, 1998; Ratcliff et al., 2003), the coherence of random-dot motion (Palmer et al., 2005), or the numerosity of displayed items (Ratcliff et al., 2015). For such tasks, the drift rate is a monotone function of the stimulus information, and this function must be bounded. There is a maximum rate (𝐿) at which a person can accumulate evidence, and this maximum is reached when the stimulus is completely unambiguous, that is, when every piece of information points in the same direction.

The Bounded Drift approach assumes that this theoretical maximum is the same for every person. Here I fix it to an arbitrary value of L = 1. For a stimulus that contains only evidence for one response, there is no ambiguity to resolve, and the accumulation proceeds at the ceiling that the task permits. Individual differences in the quality of information uptake are then expressed by the slope of the psychophysical function, that is, by how quickly the drift rate approaches its maximum as the stimulus information increases. With L fixed, the unit of the evidence axis is the amount of evidence that an unambiguous stimulus delivers per second, and the diffusion coefficient s becomes a free parameter that quantifies the random variability in units of this maximum drift.

Importantly, fixing the maximum possible drift is not the same as fixing the actually observed drift. The bounded drift approach still allows for interindividual differences in all observed drift rates, as persons may vary in how close they come to the assumed maximum. For a highly perceptive person, the drift rate for a stimulus with 40% white and 60% black squares might be close to the maximum, whereas for a less perceptive person it is notably lower.

The identification of s can be understood as follows. For each stimulus type, the data identify the ratios 𝑣(𝑥)/𝑠 and 𝑎/𝑠, as in the standard model. This argument also reveals the importance of the chosen levels of difficulty. In this case, k and s trade off against each other and both are estimated with reduced precision. On the other hand, when the task is too easy (even for the difficult stimulus type), k becomes very high and cannot be identified precisely.

The Random Walk Approach Rationale

The second approach to estimate cognitive noise rests on a different assumption. Instead of anchoring the evidence axis to the maximum drift, it anchors the axis to the size of a single piece of information. Historically, the diffusion model developed from discrete Random Walk Models of choice (Laming, 1968; Link & Heath, 1975; Stone, 1960). In a random walk, evidence is accumulated in discrete steps, and the diffusion model is the limit of such a process when steps become infinitely small and infinitely frequent (see Ratcliff & Smith, 2004, for a comparison of discrete and continuous sequential sampling models). In the limit, the mean and the variance of the increments become independent parameters, which is the source of the scaling problem. In a discrete random walk, in contrast, the mean and the variance of the increments are both determined by the step size and the step probabilities. If the (mean) step size is fixed, drift and noise are no longer free to trade off against each other and thus can be estimated from data.

The Random Walk Model assumes that pieces of information with a constant expected value arrive at a rate of r samples per second. Each sample is a noisy percept of the stimulus, and it moves the process up or down by one unit, plus some random variation. The unit of the evidence axis is thus one average step, and the boundary separation a is measured in samples, that is, as the number of pieces of information that are required for a response. The rate r takes over the role of the speed of information uptake, and the noise is determined by the variability of the steps. This yields separate estimates for rate, threshold, and noise.

Amortized Bayesian Inference

The Bounded Drift model is a diffusion model with a stimulus-dependent drift rate, for which the likelihood is in principle available, but the Random Walk Model has no closed-form likelihood.

To estimate both models in a way that is robust to contaminant responses, simulation-based inference is used here (Cranmer et al., 2020) with BayesFlow (Radev et al., 2022; Radev et al., 2023), a framework for amortized Bayesian inference with neural networks that has previously been applied to diffusion models (von Krause et al., 2022). In this approach, a neural network is trained on data simulated from the model with parameters drawn from the prior. After training, the network returns the full posterior distribution for any new data set within milliseconds, so that the computational cost is paid once and inference is amortized over all later applications.

Training Data

For each model, data from 100,000 synthetic participants with 320 trials each were simulated.

Trials were distributed randomly over the six stimulus types. Parameters were drawn from independent prior distributions (Table 2). Response times were simulated from the respective process (Equation 2for the Bounded Drift model, Equation 4for the Random Walk Model) with the nondecision time added.

Network Architecture and Training

The summary network was a Set Transformer, which is invariant to the order of trials and compresses a data set of arbitrary size to a vector of 32 learned summary statistics. The inference network was a coupling flow with 24 layers, which transforms the summary statistics into samples from the approximate posterior. To make the networks robust to the properties of real data, two forms of data augmentation were used during training. First, for each training batch, a random number of trials between 260 and 320 was retained, so that the networks tolerate varying numbers of trials per participant. Second, 1% of trials in each data set were replaced by a random response and a random response time (rt~Gamma(1.5, scale 1)), which mimics fast guesses and other contaminants that are typical for empirical data (Ratcliff & Tuerlinckx, 2002). Training was performed offline on the precomputed training set for 100 epochs with a batch size of 64.

Training took approximately 12 hours on a consumer-grade GPU. Table 3 summarizes the configuration.

Validation of the Networks

Before applying the networks to empirical data, the networks are validated in two ways. First, parameter recovery is assessed on a new set of 300 simulated participants that were not used during training. For each simulated participant, posterior means and a central posterior interval of each parameter are computed and compared with the true values. Recovery is summarized by the correlation between posterior means and true values. Second, posterior distributions are assessed with simulation-based calibration (Talts et al., 2020). If the posterior is correct, the rank of the true parameter value among the posterior draws is uniformly distributed. The distribution of fractional rank statistics is assessed with the empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) difference plots proposed by Säilynoja et al. (2022). These plots show the deviation of the empirical rank distribution from uniformity together with 95% simultaneous confidence bands. A curve that stays within the band indicates calibrated posteriors; a curve that leaves the band indicates that the posteriors are biased or have the wrong width.

Empirical Demonstration

The aim of the pilot study was to apply both models to the same empirical data and to examine whether the noise estimates of the two approaches converge. Because the two approaches rest on different identifying assumptions, agreement between their estimates would be a first indication that the estimates reflect a property of the person rather than an artifact of a particular assumption. In addition, the study included younger and older adults to explore whether the noise parameters are sensitive to age. Given the small sample, this second aim is exploratory.

Method

Participants

Five younger adults (age mean: 23.6; range: 23-24) and five older adults (age mean: 73.6; range: 71-76) were recruited through Prolific (Palan & Schitter, 2018) and received a fixed compensation of £5.50 for a session of 15 to 20 minutes. The pilot study was not designed to test hypotheses about age differences. Its purpose was to demonstrate that both models can be applied to empirical data, to examine whether the noise estimates of the two approaches converge, and to obtain information about plausible parameter ranges for the specification of prior distributions in future studies. For these purposes, five participants per age group were considered sufficient.

In Germany, there is no general legal requirement for an ethics committee approval of behavioral studies with healthy adults. Following the criteria of the German Research Foundation (DFG), approval is required when participants are exposed to considerable emotional or physical strain, are not fully informed about the purpose of the study, are patients, or undergo invasive or neurostimulation procedures. None of these criteria applied to the present study, which consisted of a brief, anonymous online perceptual task. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the German Psychological Society (DGPs) and the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants gave informed consent before participation.

Materials and Procedure

The experiment was programmed in lab.js (Henninger et al., 2022) and run online in the participants' web browsers in full-screen mode. After giving informed consent, participants reported their age, gender, and highest level of education, and indicated whether they could see the screen clearly (with glasses or contact lenses if necessary). They could optionally note a known visual impairment.

On each trial, a square pattern of 100 × 100 small black and white squares was presented in the center of a mid-gray screen. Participants decided whether black or white squares were in the majority by pressing the A key (more black) or the L key (more white). Six stimulus types with 40, 44, 48, 52, 56, or 60% white squares were used (Figure 2A). On every trial, a new pattern was generated by placing the exact number of white squares for the respective stimulus type at random positions. The realized proportion matched the nominal level exactly, and only the spatial arrangement varied between trials. Because the study was run online, the physical size of the stimuli depended on the participant's screen. Throughout the task, the response labels "more black [A]" and "more white [L]" were shown in dark gray in the lower left and lower right corners of the display.

Each trial started with a fixation cross presented for 250 ms. The stimulus followed and remained on screen until a response was given. After the response, a blank gray screen (with the response labels) was shown for 750 ms before the next trial began. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly as possible while keeping errors rare, and to decide spontaneously when they could not tell. Each block was preceded by a countdown.

The session began with 30 practice trials, followed by five experimental blocks of 62 trials each.

In all blocks, the stimulus type on each trial was drawn at random with equal probability from the six types, so the number of trials per stimulus type varied slightly across blocks and participants. No trial-by-trial feedback was given. Between blocks, participants could take a selfpaced break and started the next block by pressing the space bar. The first two trials of each experimental block served as warm-up trials and were excluded from the analysis, so that a maximum of 300 trials per participant entered the analysisfoot_1.

General Discussion

The diffusion coefficient of the diffusion model is conventionally fixed and treated as a meaningless scaling constant. I have argued that this convention hides a substantive assumption, namely that all persons and conditions share the same amount of cognitive noise, and that it renders the interpretation of drift and boundary effects ambiguous. I proposed two approaches that identify the diffusion coefficient from behavioral data. The Bounded Drift approach assumes that the drift rate is a bounded function of stimulus information with a common maximum for all persons; the Random Walk approach assumes that evidence arrives in discrete samples of constant size at an individual rate. Both models were implemented with amortized Bayesian inference. Simulations showed that the noise parameters can be recovered with calibrated posteriors, and in a pilot study the noise estimates of the two approaches converged across persons. In the following, I discuss the interpretation of the noise parameter, the relation between the two approaches, the limitations of the present work, and the research program that is required to validate the measurement of cognitive noise.

What Does the Diffusion Coefficient Measure?

Within the diffusion model, the diffusion coefficient quantifies the random variability of the evidence accumulation process within a trial. This variability has several possible sources. Part of it is external: The stimulus itself may provide inconsistent evidence from moment to moment, as is the case for dynamic random-dot displays, or the sampling of a static stimulus by eye movements and attention may yield variable evidence. Part of it is internal: Neural representations of the evidence are noisy, and the transmission and integration of neural signals adds variability at every stage (Gold & Shadlen, 2007; Smith & Ratcliff, 2004). Ratcliff et al. (2018) have shown how internal and external sources of variability can be separated experimentally by manipulating the variability of the stimulus. In the present pilot study, the stimuli were static, so that the external source of within-trial variability was limited to the sampling of the pattern, and the estimated noise mainly reflects internal variability.

The Random Walk Model makes this decomposition more explicit. It distinguishes the perceptual noise σ, which determines how reliably a single sample points in the correct direction, from the step noise η, which determines how much the evidence increment varies in size. The effective diffusion coefficient of Equation 8 combines both sources with the sampling rate. The two models therefore offer complementary descriptions: The Bounded Drift model treats noise as a single parameter that is scaled by the maximum drift, whereas the Random Walk Model decomposes it into components of the sampling process. The question which decomposition is more useful is an empirical one that depends on which components show selective effects of experimental manipulations and consistent individual differences.

It is important to distinguish the within-trial noise discussed here from the across-trial variabilities of the full diffusion model (Ratcliff, 2013; Ratcliff & Tuerlinckx, 2002). Across-trial variability of drift, sv, describes variation of the mean rate of evidence accumulation between trials and accounts for slow errors; within-trial noise s describes variation of the increments within a trial and accounts for the fact that errors occur at all. The two quantities are conceptually distinct and, with the present approaches, both can be estimated in principle. In the present implementation, I omitted the across-trial variabilities to keep the models simple, following the evidence that their inclusion often has limited impact on results in studies from individual differences research (Lerche & Voss, 2016). Whether the identification of s remains robust when across-trial variabilities are included is a question for future simulation work.

Relation Between the Two Approaches

The two approaches rest on different and mutually independent assumptions. The Bounded Drift approach assumes that the maximum drift rate is the same for all persons. Obviously, the present data does not make it possible to test if this assumption is correct. If a person's maximum rate of information uptake is lower than that of other persons, for example because of slower sensory encoding, the Bounded Drift model will absorb this difference into a higher noise estimate or a lower sensitivity. The Random Walk approach assumes that every sample contributes a constant average amount of evidence. Again, this assumption could be wrong; if the size of the evidence increment differs between persons, the Random Walk Model will absorb this difference into the sampling rate and the noise parameters. Because the two assumptions concern different aspects of the process, errors in one assumption are not expected to produce the same distortions as errors in the other. The convergence of the noise estimates in the pilot study (r = .74) is therefore informative: If either assumption were grossly wrong, the noise estimates of the two models should diverge.

The comparison also revealed systematic differences. The Random Walk Model yielded lower boundary separations in noise units and slightly higher noise estimates than the Bounded Drift model. These differences do not indicate disagreement between the models but follow from the way noise is defined. In the Random Walk Model, noise depends on the stimulus through the term 1 -𝑚 2 in Equation 8, so that noise is higher for ambiguous stimuli than for easy stimuli.

For the comparison, I used the noise at m = 0, which is the maximum of this function. The Bounded Drift model assumes a constant noise across stimulus types, so its estimate corresponds to an average over stimuli. A reference point at maximum noise necessarily produces higher noise estimates and lower boundary separations in noise units. This is a testable difference between the models. The standard diffusion model and the Bounded Drift model predict that the shape of the response time distributions is invariant across stimulus types up to the change in drift, whereas the Random Walk Model predicts an additional change in the variability of accumulation. Experiments with a wide range of stimulus information could discriminate between these predictions.

More generally, the two approaches can be viewed as members of a family of identification strategies that anchor the evidence scale to a property of the information flow.

Other anchors are conceivable, for example the assumption that the drift rate is a linear function of a physical stimulus property with a slope that is known from psychophysics, or the assumption that an experimental manipulation affects only the noise. The approach of Nunez et al. (2025), which anchors the scale to a neural covariate, belongs to the same family. The strength of a research program on cognitive noise lies in the convergence of estimates across such anchors rather than in any single assumption.

Measurement Precision and Design

The simulation results show that the noise parameters can be recovered, but also that their posterior uncertainty is substantial with 300 trials. For the Bounded Drift model, the posterior intervals of s were wide for large noise values, and the sensitivity k can be expected to trade off with s when the drift function did not saturate within the range of stimuli. For the Random Walk Model, the two noise parameters σ and η were partly interchangeable. These results point to the importance of the experimental design. In the Bounded Drift model, s is identified by the asymptote of the drift function in noise units, so that stimuli close to the extremes of the information range are the most informative. The present stimulus set, which ranged from 40 to 60% white squares, was chosen to keep accuracy below ceiling, but it may not be optimal for the identification of s. In the Random Walk Model, σ is identified by the stimulus dependence of the step probability, so that intermediate stimuli are informative for σ, and η is identified by the shape of the response time distributions. A systematic design optimization, in which the expected posterior precision of the noise parameters is computed for different stimulus sets and trial numbers, is the next step. Amortized inference is well suited for this purpose, because the trained networks can evaluate thousands of candidate designs at negligible cost. Next to the question of task difficulty and number of stimulus types, also the number of trials per person is important.

Plausibly, the required trial number for a stable estimate of s might be larger than the number required for the conventional parameters (Lerche & Voss, 2017), and studies on individual differences in cognitive noise should plan accordingly.

Limitations of the Inference Procedure

Amortized inference with neural networks has two properties that require attention. First, the posteriors are only as good as the networks, and their validity must be established by simulation.

The calibration analyses showed well-calibrated posteriors for all parameters except the nondecision time, for which the posteriors were slightly biased relative to their very small width.

For the present purpose, which focuses on the noise parameters, the consequences are negligible, because t0 was recovered with correlations above .99.

Second, amortized inference assumes that the empirical data are generated by the model that was used for training. If the data lie outside the range of the training simulations, the posteriors can be arbitrarily wrong without any indication in the posteriors themselves. Methods for detecting such model misspecification in the summary space have been developed (Schmitt et al., 2024) and should be applied routinely, together with posterior predictive checks of the response time distributions.

A Validation Program for Cognitive Noise

The present work establishes that the diffusion coefficient can be identified and estimated. It does not yet establish that the resulting estimates are valid measures of the intended cognitive constructs.

The validation of a new model parameter requires the same program that has been carried out for the conventional parameters of the diffusion model (Arnold et al., 2015; Lerche & Voss, 2019; Voss et al., 2004). Different lines of research are important to ensure the validity of the approaches presented here.

First, design optimization, as discussed above, should establish which stimulus sets and trial numbers yield the most precise estimates of the noise parameters, and simulation studies should examine the robustness of the estimates against violations of the identifying assumptions and against the omission of across-trial variabilities.

Second, experimental validation should test the selective influence of manipulations on the noise parameters (Voss et al., 2004). A manipulation that is expected to affect cognitive noise, such as a concurrent attentional load, sleep restriction, or a pharmacological intervention, should change s but not the sensitivity k or the boundary separation. Conversely, a manipulation of speed-accuracy instructions should affect the boundary separation but not s, and a degradation of the stimulus, for example by reducing contrast, should affect the sensitivity but not s. The models also make the prediction that the maximum drift (in the Bounded Drift model) and the step size (in the Random Walk Model) are invariant across persons; experimental conditions that are designed to push the drift rate to its ceiling can test this prediction directly.

Third, individual differences research should establish the psychometric properties of the noise estimates. The retest reliability of s and its consistency across tasks are the minimum requirements for interpreting it as a trait. If cognitive noise is a general property of a person's information processing, estimates from different tasks should correlate; if it is task specific, the construct needs to be refined. The relation of s to measures of intelligence and to the conventional drift rate is of particular interest, because it would show whether the association between drift and intelligence (Lerche et al., 2020; Schubert et al., 2015) reflects faster information uptake, lower noise, or both.

Fourth, substantive research can use the noise parameter to address questions that the standard model cannot answer. Age differences are the most obvious example. The neural noise hypothesis of cognitive aging (Cremer & Zeef, 1987; Li et al., 2001) and the processing speed theory (Salthouse, 1996) make different predictions about the locus of age effects, and the separation of signal and noise in the decision process offers a direct test. A large-scale analysis of the conventional diffusion model has shown that mental speed, measured as drift rate, remains high until the age of 60 (von Krause et al., 2022); whether this holds for both signal and noise is an open question. Other candidate applications are clinical groups (White et al., 2010), the effects of alcohol (van Ravenzwaaij et al., 2012), and sleep deprivation (Ratcliff & Van Dongen, 2009), where an increase of noise is a plausible mechanism that has so far been indistinguishable from a decrease of drift.

Relation to Other Evidence Accumulation Models

The identification problem discussed here is specific to models with within-trial noise. The linear ballistic accumulator (Brown & Heathcote, 2008), for example, has no within-trial noise and anchors its scale by fixing the variability of the drift rates across trials. Within-trial noise is, however, the feature that makes the diffusion model falsifiable in the sense discussed by Smith et al. (2014), because it constrains the shape of the response time distributions. The present approaches keep this feature and add a second constraint that anchors the scale of the evidence axis. In this sense, the proposed models are more constrained than the standard diffusion model, not less, and their additional assumptions can be tested.

Conclusion

Cognitive noise is a quantity that evidence accumulation models have always contained but rarely measured. I have presented two approaches that make the diffusion coefficient of the diffusion model identifiable from behavioral data, one based on a bounded psychophysical function with a common maximum drift and one based on a discrete random walk with samples of constant size. Both were implemented with amortized Bayesian inference, both recovered the noise parameters in simulations, and both yielded converging noise estimates for the same empirical data. I propose here that the amount of noise in the decision process should be treated as a measurable individual difference variable, and I have outlined the validation program that this proposal requires.

Because tanh(u/2) = 2Λ(u) -1, where Λ is the logistic function, Equation 3 is a logistic function of x rescaled to the interval [-L, L]. Figure2Bshows the function for three values of k. For a sensitive person (k = 0.90), the drift rate reaches its maximum already for stimuli with 56% white squares; for a less sensitive person (k = 0.15), even the most extreme stimuli produce drift rates far below the maximum. A positive value of c shifts the function to the right, so that stimuli with slightly more than 50% white squares are perceived as balanced.
