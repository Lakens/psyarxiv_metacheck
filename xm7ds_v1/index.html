Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Full text

Abstract

Unitization is the process of integrating items into one entity providing the possibility to subsequently remembering these associations via familiarity. According to cortical reinstatement accounts, successful retrieval depends on the degree to which encoding-related neural activity is reactivated when the memory is later accessed. The goal of the current study was to investigate whether action relationships as a bottom-up unitization approach alter the correspondence between neural representations formed during encoding and those reinstated during associative retrieval. Younger and older adults (N = 47) studied semantically unrelated object pairs that were either arranged in a way that an action could be imagined (unitized, e.g., a milk bottle above a shoe) or not (non-unitized, e.g., a shoe above a milk bottle). Afterwards, they had to distinguish between old, recombined and new object pairs. Encoding-Retrieval Similarity (ERS) was used to assess item-specific similarity of EEG-based neural activation at encoding and retrieval. Across both age groups, greater similarity was observed for same object pairs (i.e., item specific pairs) than for different object pairs. Critically, however, in the time window traditionally associated with familiarity (300-500ms post-stimulus), larger ERS was observed for actionrelated than action-unrelated object pairs, but only when same object pairs were presented at encoding and retrieval. These findings suggest that action relationships increase the reinstatement of pair-specific encoding representations, even though the direct functional link between this enhanced reinstatement and associative memory benefit remains open.

Introduction

Remembering associations between separate items is typically thought to depend on recollection -the retrieval of the specific episode in which those items were previously encountered together. In contrast, familiarity provides a more general sense that an item has been encountered before, without recovery of contextual details. According to dual-process theories of recognition memory, familiarity is generally sufficient for recognising individual items, whereas associative recognition ordinarily requires recollection because the relation between the items must be recovered (Yonelinas et al., 2010). An important exception to this distinction is provided by unitization, whereby multiple items are encoded as a single integrated representation. Under these circumstances, later recognition of the association can be supported by familiarity, reducing the reliance on recollection (Graf & Schacter, 1989; Parks & Yonelinas, 2015).

Over the past two decades, evidence for unitization has accumulated across a broad range of paradigms and stimulus types. We have previously proposed that unitization can be achieved through both top-down and bottom-up encoding processes (Tibon et al., 2014). Topdown approaches involve intentional strategies applied by the individual to promote the integration of multiple items into a single representation, such as assigning compound definitions or generating interactive mental imagery for otherwise arbitrary associations (Bader et al., 2010; Bastin et al., 2013; Haskins et al., 2008; Khoo & Tibon, 2025; Rhodes & Donaldson, 2008a; Tibon et al., 2018). In contrast, bottom-up unitization arises from properties inherent to the stimuli themselves. Examples include pre-existing semantic relations, compound words, plausible spatial arrangements, and action relationships between objects (Ahmad & Hockley, 2014; Bridger et al., 2017; Rhodes & Donaldson, 2007; Tibon et al., 2014). Despite these diverse manipulations, they share a common underlying principle: by integrating multiple elements into a unified representation during encoding, unitization changes the nature of subsequent associative retrieval, enabling familiarity to support successful associative recognition and reducing reliance on recollection.

By definition, however, unitization is an encoding phenomenon. The integrated representation that characterises unitized memories is established while the episode is initially experienced, yet its consequences are typically inferred from behaviour or neural activity measured only at retrieval. Indeed, much of the existing electrophysiological evidence for unitization comes from event-related potentials (ERPs), demonstrating that retrieving unitized associations is accompanied by enhanced familiarity-related activity and, in some circumstances, reduced reliance on recollection (e.g., Bader et al., 2010b; Bridger et al., 2017b; Bridger & Mecklinger, 2012; Delhaye et al., 2018; Huffer et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Meßmer et al., 2024; Rhodes & Donaldson, 2007b , 2008b; Zheng, Li, Xiao, Broster, Jiang, et al., 2015). These findings have substantially advanced our understanding of how unitization changes recognition memory, but they leave largely unanswered a more fundamental question: how closely do the neural representations established during encoding correspond to those reinstated during successful retrieval?

This question aligns with broader theoretical accounts of episodic memory, which view remembering as a process of reinstating neural representations formed during encoding.

According to cortical reinstatement and transfer-appropriate processing accounts, successful retrieval depends on the degree to which encoding-related neural activity is reactivated when the memory is later accessed (Kent & Lamberts, 2008; Rugg et al., 2008). One approach for quantifying this relationship is Encoding-Retrieval Similarity (ERS), which measures the similarity between neural activity during encoding and subsequent retrieval of the same event. ERS has been used extensively in functional neuroimaging to demonstrate that successful remembering is associated with greater reinstatement of encoding-related activity than unsuccessful remembering (e.g., Favila et al., 2018; Ritchey et al., 2013; Xiao et al., 2017). Although the application of ERS analyses to EEG data has been comparatively limited, the superior temporal resolution of EEG enables the investigation of the temporal dynamics of neural reinstatement in ways that are not possible with other neuroimaging techniques. For example, temporal-pattern similarity analyses have shown that reinstating the temporal context present during encoding can either facilitate or impair subsequent memory, depending on whether the reinstated context matches the retrieval demands (Staudigl et al., 2015). Most directly relevant to the present study, Staudigl and Hanslmayr (2019) demonstrated in a MEG study that successful recognition memory is characterised by the reinstatement of encoding-related neural patterns and that the degree of reinstatement predicts recognition performance in accordance with the principle of encoding specificity. These findings highlight the utility of EEG-based similarity analyses for investigating not only whether memory representations are reinstated, but also when reinstatement occurs during retrieval. Thus, although the majority of ERS studies have focused on spatial activation patterns measured with fMRI, EEG and MEG provide a complementary approach for characterising the temporal evolution of encoding-retrieval correspondence with millisecond precision.

Surprisingly, despite the central role of encoding in unitization, ERS has rarely been used to investigate how unitized representations formed during encoding are reinstated during retrieval. This gap is particularly important because unitization may alter not only the behavioural expression of memory, but also the nature and temporal dynamics of the underlying neural representations. In the present study, we focus on action relationships as a form of bottom-up unitization. Action-related object pairs are arranged such that they afford a plausible interaction between the objects -for example, a teapot positioned above a cup as though pouring tea into it.

Importantly, these action relationships do not depend on pre-existing semantic associations between the objects. Rather, they arise from the affordances created by their spatial arrangement, such that even semantically unrelated objects -for example, a fire extinguisher positioned above a cup as though pouring its contents into it -can form a coherent action representation (Humphreys et al., 2006) . We have previously shown that action affordances improve associative memory and increase familiarity-based retrieval, particularly in older adults, suggesting that they promote unitization (Huffer et al., 2022). However, this conclusion was based on retrieval-related measures alone. Whether action relationships influence the formation of memory representations during encoding, and whether these representations are subsequently reinstated during retrieval, remains unknown. If action relationships promote the formation of integrated memory representations during encoding, then successful retrieval should be accompanied by greater reinstatement of those representations than for actionunrelated associations. Conversely, if action relationships primarily activate general semantic or perceptual knowledge without strengthening memory for the specific study episode, then increased similarity should also be observed between action-related pairs that have not been previously associated at encoding, indicating that any similarity reflects shared stimulus properties rather than episodic reinstatement.

In the present study, we addressed this question by reanalysing EEG data from our previous investigation of associative memory for action-related and action-unrelated object pairs in young and older adults (Huffer et al., 2022). The inclusion of older adults is particularly relevant given their greater reliance on familiarity due to age-related declines in recollection (e.g., Old & Naveh-Benjamin, 2008). We previously argued that unitization through action relationships may reduce reliance on recollection, and found ERP evidence consistent with familiarity-based associative retrieval specifically in older adults (Huffer et al., 2022). These findings suggest that action relationships may particularly benefit older adults by promoting the reinstatement of integrated representations, potentially resulting in stronger ERS effects during the familiarityrelated time window. Whereas the original study focused on neural activity elicited during retrieval, the present analyses directly examine the correspondence between encoding and retrieval using Encoding-Retrieval Similarity (ERS). Specifically, we quantified the similarity between EEG activity recorded during study and retrieval across time windows associated with early perceptual processing, familiarity, and recollection, and compared both corresponding ("same-pairing") and non-corresponding ("different-pairing") study-test events. This approach allowed us to determine whether action relationships modulate the correspondence between encoding and retrieval, whether these effects differ between younger and older adults, and whether any observed increase in similarity is specific to corresponding study-test events or instead reflects more general similarities arising from action affordances or other stimulus properties.

Methods

Sample

Twenty-four younger adults participated in the study. To obtain a comparable sample size for the older adult group, twenty-nine older adults were invited to the first session, which involved neuropsychological screening. Following the exclusion of participants with severe cognitive deficits (see the Neuropsychological Screening section below), 24 older adults took part in the second session, which comprised the EEG experiment. Younger adults were recruited from the student population of Saarland University, whereas older adults were recruited through internal participant databases and an advertisement in the local daily newspaper.

Data from one younger adult were excluded from the analyses due to a technical error during the experiment. The final sample therefore consisted of 23 younger adults (17 females; M age = 21.0 years, SD = 2.0, range = 18-25 years) and 24 older adults (18 females; M age = 72.75 years, SD = 4.2, range = 66-81 years). For one of the ERS analyses, the older adult sample was reduced to 21 participants due to insufficient relevant trials (see below for further details when the analysis is introduced). The two age groups did not differ significantly in years of education (younger adults: M = 15.08 years, SD = 1.97; older adults: M = 14.29 years, SD = 2.62), t(45) = 1.17, p = .25, or in gender distribution, χ²(1) = 0.00, p = .93.

All participants were native German speakers, right-handed as indicated by positive scores on the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield, 1971) , had no known neurological disorders, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no colour vision deficiencies.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Younger adults received either course credit or financial compensation of €8 per hour, whereas older adults received €8 per hour plus reimbursement of parking expenses. All participants were debriefed following the experiment. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Human and Business Sciences at Saarland University.

Neuropsychological Screening Session

Following a telephone screening to assess general eligibility criteria (e.g., age, native language, neurological and psychiatric history, and visual impairments), older adults were invited to a first session that included a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment. This session lasted approximately 45 minutes and began with the CERAD-Plus battery (Monsch et al., 1997) (Wechsler, 2009), consisting of nine digit-symbol pairings and a total of 93 digits. The session concluded with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield, 1971).

A total of 29 older adults completed the neuropsychological screening. Only those who showed no evidence of severe cognitive impairment across all CERAD subtests (i.e., scores no lower than 1.5 standard deviations below age-adjusted norms) were invited to participate in the second session, during which the EEG experiment was conducted (n = 24). To assess participants' ability in the domains most relevant to the current investigation, we focused on the Wechsler Digit Symbol Substitution Test and the MMSE, which are widely used indices of perceptual processing speed and global cognitive functioning, respectively. The older adults' performance on the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (M = 44.54, SD = 9.35) was within the expected normative range for this age group (e.g., Ferdinand & Kray, 2013; Kray et al., 2008). Likewise, MMSE scores indicated intact global cognitive functioning (M = 29.0, SD = 0.78, range = 28-30), with all participants scoring within the range typically associated with healthy ageing (Folstein et al., 1975).

Experimental Session

Materials and Procedure. The present study reports a secondary analysis of data previously published by Huffer et al. (2022). Below, we provide a brief summary of the stimuli and procedures used during the EEG session; for a full description, please refer to Huffer et al. (2022).

The stimulus set consisted of 320 pictures of semantically unrelated object pairs. Half of these were action-related pairs, in which the depicted objects afforded a plausible action when viewed from a right-handed perspective, whereas the remaining half were action-unrelated pairs, created by swapping the vertical positions of the two objects (see Figure 1 for examples). These stimuli were used across the study and test phases of the experiment as follows: the study phase consisted of 240 object pairs, including 120 action-related and 120 action-unrelated pairs. The test phase also comprised 240 object pairs, equally divided between the two action relationship conditions. Within each condition, the pairs were further subdivided into 40 intact pairs (i.e., pairs presented during the study phase), 40 recombined pairs (i.e., pairs consisting of two previously studied objects that had originally been paired with different associates), and 40 new pairs (i.e., pairs consisting of two previously unseen objects). Importantly, the relatedness status of the recombined pairs was preserved from the study phase, such that action-related pairs were recombined into action-related pairs and action-unrelated pairs were recombined into actionunrelated pairs. Assignment of object pairs to the different experimental conditions was counterbalanced across participants. The experimental session lasted approximately 3 hours. Participants first signed an informed consent and completed additional questionnaires, followed by EEG preparation, familiarisation with the task materials (see Huffer et al., 2022, for full details), introduction to the task, and completion of a study-test practice block. The main experiment (see Figure 2) comprised four study-test blocks, each consisting of four practice trials, 60 study trials (30 action-related and 30 action-unrelated object pairs), 60 test trials (30 action-related and 30 action-unrelated object pairs).

During the study phase, participants rated how appropriate the arrangement of the two presented objects was for performing an action (0 = not at all appropriate,5 = absolutely appropriate) using a six-button response box. They were also instructed to memorise the object pairs for the subsequent memory test. Each study trial began with a jittered fixation cross presented for 1000 ms, followed by the object pair presented for 2500 ms. Once a response was made, a blank screen was presented for 700 ms, concluding the trial. If no response was given during object presentation, a blank screen remained on the screen for an additional 2000 ms, during which participants could still provide their response. A self-paced break was provided halfway through each study block, during which participants reread the encoding instructions.

Following each study phase, participants completed an approximately 3-minute filler task before beginning the test phase. During the test phase, participants judged whether each presented object pair was old, recombined, or new by pressing one of three response buttons.

Each test trial started with a jittered fixation cross presented for 500 ms, followed by the presentation of the object pair for 4000 ms. Participants were instructed to respond as accurately as possible while the object pair remained on the screen. Once a response was made, the trial ended with a blank screen presented for 1250 ms. EEG Recording and Pre-processing. EEG data were recorded during both the study and the test phase using 28 scalp electrodes (BrainVision Recorder; Version 1.02; Brain Products) at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. The pre-processing pipeline, implemented with BrainVision Analyzer (Version 2.1; Brain Products GmbH), was identical for both age groups and included a 0.1-30 Hz band-pass filter, a 50 Hz notch filter, and correction of eye blinks and horizontal eye movements using the semi-automatic Ocular Correction ICA algorithm. The data were then re-referenced to the average of the left and right mastoid electrodes and segmented into epochs extending from 200 ms before stimulus onset to 2000 ms after stimulus onset. Baseline correction was applied using the 200 ms pre-stimulus interval. The segmented EEG data were subsequently exported to MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., 2022) as individual 2200 ms epochs, each consisting of 1100 data points, for further analysis.

Data Analysis

Data analyses were conducted in MATLAB using the FieldTrip toolbox (Oostenveld et al., 2011; http://fieldtriptoolbox.org) together with custom MATLAB functions, and in R (version 4.4.1) using RStudio (RStudio Team, 2019) and the ez (Lawrence, 2016), stats (R Core Team, 2019), and BayesFactor (Morey & Rouder, 2018) packages. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared (ηp²), and Hedges' g was calculated for both between-subjects (denoted by gs) and withinsubjects (denoted by gav) comparisons using the formulas described by Lakens (2013). The significance threshold was set at α = .05, and Bonferroni correction was applied where multiple comparisons were performed.

Behavioural Analyses.

The behavioural data were previously reported in Huffer et al. (2022). The younger adult sample is identical to that of the original publication, whereas the present analyses include five additional older adults who were previously excluded due to insufficient trials for ERP averaging. As the present ERS analyses focus only on intact pairs, these participants could be retained. Behavioural analyses were therefore repeated using the expanded sample, with results from all experimental conditions reported for completeness.

For accuracy, the proportion of correct responses (i.e., correctly recognised intact, recombined, and new object pairs) was calculated separately for each action relationship condition. Reaction times (RTs) for correct responses were also extracted. Accuracy and RT data were analysed using separate three-way mixed-model ANOVAs, with age group (young, old) as a between-subjects factor and action relationship (action-related, action-unrelated) and pair status (intact, recombined, new) as within-subjects factors. In addition, we calculated associative memory and item memory indices separately for action-related and action-unrelated object pairs by subtracting the false alarm rate from the hit rate (see Huffer et al., 2022 for details), and analysed these scores using a three-way mixed-model ANOVA with age group (young, old) as a between-subjects factor and action relationship (action-related, action-unrelated) and memory type (item, associative) as within-subjects factors.

Encoding-Retrieval Similarity (ERS).

In the current study, we focused on the neural similarity between encoding and retrieval as our key measure of interest, captured by the computation of Encoding-Retrieval Similarity (ERS). To this end, Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were computed between the EEG signal recorded during the study phase and the corresponding signal recorded for that trial during the test phase. These ERS values, computed for each intact test trial and its corresponding study-phase trial, were calculated separately for each electrode, and at pre-defined time windows within each EEG epoch, corresponding to three distinct stages of memory processing: an early visual processing window (TW1: 0-300 ms poststimulus onset), a familiarity-related window (TW2: 300-500 ms; Mecklinger, 2000), and a recollection-related window (TW3: 500-800 ms; for reviews, see Friedman & Johnson, 2000; Mecklinger & Bader, 2020). Additional time-windows included the pre-stimulus baseline period (BL: -200 to 0 ms) and across the entire active processing period (0-800 ms). The resulting correlation coefficients were then averaged separately for each participant, action relationship (action-related and action-unrelated), and time window, and subsequently exported for statistical analysis.

In addition to the similarity between corresponding study-test pairs, we also computed a baseline measure based on non-corresponding pairs. This was designed to account for similarity arising from the general intrinsic characteristics of the stimuli rather than the reinstatement of a specific instance. For example, pairs of objects that share an action relationship may elicit more similar neural activity than action-unrelated pairs, irrespective of whether the retrieval trial is matched to its original encoding episode. To isolate similarity attributable to the reinstatement of the same instance, in addition to the calculation of ERS for same-pairing trials (as described above), we calculated similarity between different-pairing trials within the three time windows of interest. Whereas same-pairing ERS was computed by correlating each intact test trial with its corresponding study-phase trial, different-pairing ERS was computed by pairing each intact study-phase trial with a different intact trial that appeared within the same study-test block and was also successfully recognised at test. This baseline provides an estimate of similarity unrelated to the reinstatement of a specific study episode, allowing us to distinguish memoryspecific reinstatement from more general stimulus-related similarity. Note that because different-pairing ERS was calculated by pairing trials within the same study-test block and condition, this analysis required at least two correctly recognised intact trials per block and condition. Three older adults were therefore excluded from analyses involving different-pairing ERS because they had insufficient trials to generate these comparisons.

Our primary analyses were designed to establish whether ERS for specific instantiations of associated pairs differed between action-related and action-unrelated object pairs, and whether these effects varied as a function of age. To this end, nine representative electrodes were selected to capture the frontal (F3, Fz, F4), central (C3, Cz, C4), and parietal (P3, Pz, P4) scalp distribution of the effects (see Bridger et al., 2017; Bridger & Mecklinger, 2012; Huffer et al., 2022, for similar electrode configurations). Separate factorial ANOVAs were conducted for each time window of interest (TW1-TW3), with Action Relationship (action-related, action-unrelated), Trial Type (same pairing, different pairing), Laterality (left, midline, right), and scalp Location (frontal, central, parietal) as within-subjects factors and with Age Group (young, old) as a betweensubjects factor. Significant interactions were followed up using simple-effects analyses and pairwise t-tests, as appropriate. Critically, evidence for specific re-instantiation would be reflected in a significant interaction involving trial type, indicating that ERS differed between same-and different-pairing trials. Of particular interest was the action relationship X trial type interaction (and its potential modulation by age), which would demonstrate that any differences in ERS between action-related and action-unrelated pairs were specific to the reinstatement of the initial presentation, rather than reflecting general stimulus-related similarity.

In addition to these primary analyses, for time windows in which significant effects were observed, we examined whether these effects were directly related to successful memory retrieval by comparing ERS as a function of participants' responses to intact trials. Correctly endorsing an intact pair as intact (a hit) reflects successful retrieval of the associative link between the two items. In contrast, when an intact pair was incorrectly classified as recombined, the association was not successfully retrieved. Likewise, when an intact pair was incorrectly classified as new, neither the association nor the individual items were successfully recognised (both classified as misses). We conducted a factorial ANOVA with Response Type (hit, miss), Action Relationship (action-related, action-unrelated), Laterality (left, midline, right), and Location (frontal, central, parietal) as within-subjects factors, and Age Group (young, old) as a between-subjects factor. We reasoned that if ERS is greater for correctly endorsed than incorrectly rejected intact pairs -and if this effect is further modulated by action relationshipsit would provide evidence that ERS reflects mnemonic retrieval processes rather than perceptual similarity alone.

We acknowledge, however, that the number of correctly endorsed intact trials (Actionrelated: mean = 32.04, SD = 5.22; Action-unrelated: mean = 26.45, SD = 6.71) substantially exceeded the number of incorrectly rejected intact trials (Action-related: mean = 3.34, SD = 2.78; Action-unrelated: mean = 5.94, SD = 4.27), resulting in a highly unbalanced distribution of observations across conditions. We therefore repeated the analysis using linear mixed-effects models, which accommodate both within-and between-subject variability and are particularly well suited to unbalanced data (i.e., an unequal number of trials across conditions; Tibon & Levy, 2015; see also Tibon et al., 2019 for a similar application). Unlike the ANOVA, which was based on participant-level averages, the mixed-effects analysis was conducted on trial-level ERS estimates. These trial-level estimates were submitted to a linear mixed-effects model, with fixed effects corresponding to the factorial ANOVA described above (Response Type, Action Relationship, Laterality, Location, and Age Group), and with participant-specific slopes for each within-subject factor (see Barr et al., 2013, and Bates et al., 2015, for discussion of estimation and convergence issues associated with maximal random-effects models) and a participantspecific intercept specified as random effects. The model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) with the lme4 package in R ( Bates et al., 2015 As detailed in the Results section (see below), the factorial ANOVA and the linear mixedeffects model analyses did not reveal an interaction between Response Type and Action Relationship, and therefore did not provide direct support for the hypothesis that the modulation of ERS by action relations is driven by mnemonic processes. However, within the frequentist framework, the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Therefore, to quantify evidence in favour of the null hypothesis, i.e., that the modulation of ERS by action relations is not driven by mnemonic processes, we conducted a Bayesian model comparison using the anovaBF() function from the BayesFactor package in R. Bayes factors (BFs) were calculated for all hierarchical models containing the main effects and interactions of Action Relationship, Response Type, and Group, with participant included as a random effect. Evidence for the Action Relationship x Response Type interaction was evaluated by comparing the Bayes factor of the model including the interaction with that of the otherwise identical model containing only the corresponding main effects. The resulting Bayes factor ratio (BF₁₀) quantified the evidence for including the interaction, with values greater than 3 and 6 interpreted as moderate and strong evidence, respectively.

Finally, to verify that increased ERS emerges following the presentation and processing of the associated pair, rather than reflecting a general increase in synchronisation, ERS values during the baseline period (-200 to 0 ms pre-stimulus) and the active period (0-800 ms poststimulus) were compared. To this end, separate three-way mixed-model ANOVAs were conducted for each of the nine representative electrodes (F3, Fz, F4; C3, Cz, C4; P3, Pz, P4), with Age Group (young, old) as a between-subjects factor and Action Relationship (action-related, action-unrelated) and Time Period (baseline, active) as within-subjects factors. Results were Bonferroni-corrected for multiple comparisons.

Taken together, these analyses suggest that repeated presentation of the same object pair increases neural similarity throughout the presentation time course, relative to a baseline condition involving different object pairs. However, only during the second time window does the presence of action relations produce an additional increase in similarity across re-instantiations of the same object-pair.

This analysis was restricted to intact trials, for which successful associative retrieval could be assessed by comparing hits (intact trials classified as "intact") and misses (intact trials classified as "recombined" or "new"). If the observed similarity effects reflect successful associative retrieval, we would expect greater ERS for hits than misses, particularly for action-related pairs. Separate three-way ANOVAs for each of the nine representative electrodes, with age group (young, old) as a between-subjects factor and action relationship (action-related, action-unrelated) and time period (baseline, active) as withinsubjects factors, revealed a significant main effect of time period at all electrodes, reflecting greater ERS during the active period than during baseline (all ps < .005). No other significant effects or interactions emerged.

In summary, ERS was significantly increased for same-pairing trials relative to differentpairing trials in all three time windows. This enhanced ERS for same-pairings was further modulated by action relationships in the familiarity-related time window (300-500ms), i.e., greater ERS for action-related than for action-unrelated object pairs when the same object pairs were presented (vs. different object pairs). This effect was observed across both age groups and was not evident during the earlier visual-processing window (0-300 ms) or the later recollectionrelated window (500-800 ms). However, evidence directly linking this effect to mnemonic processing were not obtained with the current data.

Discussion

The present study examined whether action relationships alter the correspondence between neural representations formed during encoding and those reinstated during associative retrieval. Using encoding-retrieval similarity analyses of EEG data, we found that repeated presentation of the same object pair was associated with greater neural similarity than comparison with a different pair across all three predefined processing windows. Critically, however, the contribution of action relationships was temporally specific. During the 300-500 ms interval, same-pairing ERS was greater than different-pairing ERS for action-related pairs, whereas no corresponding same-pairing advantage was observed for action-unrelated pairs. This pattern suggests that action relationships increase the reinstatement of pair-specific encoding representations during a time window commonly associated with familiarity-based retrieval. By contrast, the analyses comparing successful and unsuccessful retrieval did not establish that this action-related ERS effect was directly associated with retrieval success. Together, the findings provide evidence that action relationships shape encoding-retrieval correspondence, while leaving open the precise mnemonic function of this correspondence.

Increased ERS for 'same' object pairs throughout the time-course

The greater ERS for same-than different-pairing trials across the three time windows is consistent with neural reinstatement accounts of episodic memory. Although both trial types involved object pairs drawn from the same experimental conditions, only the same-pairing comparison linked a test event with its corresponding encoding episode. The same-pairing advantage therefore indicates that retrieval elicited neural activity that contained information specific to the original study event, over and above similarity attributable to general properties of the stimuli or task. The presence of this effect from the early visual-processing interval through the later recollection-related interval further suggests that reinstatement was not restricted to a single discrete stage of retrieval. Instead, information associated with the original episode appears to have influenced a temporally extended sequence of processing, encompassing early perceptual analysis as well as later memory-related activity. This finding complements previous EEG evidence (Staudigl & Hanslmayr, 2019) that successful recognition involves the reinstatement of encoding-related neural patterns and illustrates the value of ERS for tracing the temporal development of encoding-retrieval correspondence.

Increased ERS for action-related object pairs during a familiarity-related time window

The central finding was the interaction between Trial Type and Action Relationship during the 300-500 ms interval. In this window, action-related pairs showed greater similarity between their corresponding study and test presentations than between non-corresponding presentations, whereas action-unrelated pairs did not. Importantly, this result cannot readily be explained by action-related pairs simply eliciting more similar perceptual or semantic processing in general. If the effect were driven solely by shared action affordances or by the general configuration of action-related stimuli, increased similarity should also have been evident between different action-related pairs.

Instead, the additional similarity was specific to the reinstatement of the same pair. Action relationships therefore appear to promote a representation that preserves information about the particular conjunction of objects encountered during encoding, rather than merely activating a generic representation of object interaction.

The timing of this effect is theoretically important. The 300-500 ms interval overlaps with the mid-frontal ERP old/new effect commonly associated with familiarity-based recognition (Mecklinger, 2000; Kwon et al., 2023). Previous work has suggested that unitization allows associations that would ordinarily require recollection to be recognised through familiarity, because the constituent items are encoded as a single integrated representation (e.g., Bader et al., 2010; Bridger & Mecklinger, 2012; Bridger et al., 2017; Delhaye et al., 2018; Han et al., 2022; Huffer et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019; Rhodes & Donaldson, 2007 , 2008; Zheng et al., 2015). The temporally confined effect observed in the current study extends this account by demonstrating a correspondence between encoding and retrieval within the same familiarity-related period. In other words, the action-related advantage was not expressed only as a retrieval-stage difference, as in much of the previous electrophysiological literature. Rather, it was evident in the degree to which the neural representation elicited during retrieval resembled that established when the pair was initially encoded. This provides support for the proposal that bottom-up action affordances influence the structure of the encoded representation and that this integrated representation is subsequently reinstated during retrieval.

The findings therefore offer a potential neural mechanism for the behavioural memory benefit associated with action relationships. Participants recognised intact action-related pairs more accurately and more quickly than action-unrelated pairs, and action relationships improved both item and associative memory indices. Arranging two otherwise semantically unrelated objects so that they afford a plausible action may encourage them to be processed as a coherent event rather than as two independent items. Such integration could strengthen the representation of the pair as a whole, making the encoded configuration more readily accessible when the same pair is encountered again. The enhanced pair-specific ERS observed during the familiarity-related interval may reflect this reinstatement of an integrated representation.

Nevertheless, the present data do not establish a direct trial-by-trial relationship between the ERS enhancement and the behavioural advantage, and this interpretation should therefore remain cautious.

In particular, the comparison between hits and misses did not reveal the predicted Action Relationship x Response Type interaction. The factorial ANOVA identified an Age Group x

Response Type x Location interaction, but follow-up analyses did not detect a reliable difference between hits and misses within any age-group and scalp-location combination. The trial-level mixed-effects analysis produced a small main effect of Action Relationship but no interaction with Response Type. Bayesian model comparison was similarly inconclusive: the model including the Action Relationship x Response Type interaction was supported to approximately the same degree as the model containing only the corresponding main effects, BF₁₀ = 1.03. Thus, the Bayesian analysis provided neither evidence for the predicted interaction nor meaningful evidence in favour of its absence. The current findings consequently cannot determine whether the action-related increase in pair-specific ERS contributes directly to successful retrieval.

Several considerations may explain this inconclusive result. First, the hit-miss analysis was based on substantially unequal numbers of observations. Accuracy for intact pairs was relatively high, especially in the action-related condition, leaving comparatively few miss trials from which to derive stable ERS estimates. Although the mixed-effects analysis retained triallevel observations and was better suited to this imbalance, a small number of misses within some participant-by-condition combinations may still have limited sensitivity to the interaction.

Second, collapsing intact pairs judged recombined and intact pairs judged new into a single miss category increased the available number of trials but combined qualitatively different retrieval outcomes.

An intact pair judged recombined may involve successful item recognition accompanied by failure to retrieve the association, whereas an intact pair judged new reflects failure to recognise even the constituent items. The neural processes underlying these responses may therefore differ. A larger study with enough trials to analyse these outcomes separately would provide a more diagnostic test of whether action-related ERS is associated specifically with associative retrieval.

A further, more conceptual possibility, is that the increased ERS reflects the reinstatement of an integrated action representation that is not itself sufficient to determine the overt memory decision. Reinstatement may support retrieval while operating alongside other processes, including decision criteria, monitoring, recollection of contextual details, and interference from competing associations. Under this account, action relationships could reliably influence the neural representation activated at retrieval without producing a simple correspondence between the magnitude of ERS and whether an individual trial is classified correctly. It is also possible that the relationship between reinstatement and memory performance is nonlinear or varies across participants. Analyses using continuous measures of memory confidence, response time, or participant-level action-related memory benefits may be more sensitive than a binary hit-miss comparison.

The absence of an Action Relationship effect during the later 500-800 ms interval is also informative. Same-pairing ERS remained greater than different-pairing ERS during this period, indicating continued reinstatement of episode-specific information, but this reinstatement was not further enhanced by the presence of an action relationship. The action-related benefit therefore appears to be temporally selective rather than reflecting a general increase in neural similarity throughout retrieval. This pattern is compatible with the suggestion that unitization primarily increases the contribution of familiarity, without necessarily enhancing later recollection-related processing (e.g. Bader et al., 2010; Kriukova et al., 2013). It would nevertheless be premature to conclude that action relationships have no influence on recollection. The present ERS analysis measured similarity within broad predefined time windows and across a restricted set of scalp electrodes. Action-related differences in the timing, frequency composition, or spatial distribution of recollection-related activity may not be captured by this approach.

Memory benefit by action relations and increased ERS for action-related object pairs in younger and older adults

The results concerning age require similarly careful interpretation. Behaviourally, older adults showed the expected disproportionate reduction in associative relative to item memory (Naveh-Benjamin, 2000). At the same time, the action-related benefit was evident across the sample, and the critical Trial Type x Action Relationship interaction in the 300-500 ms window was not reliably moderated by Age Group. This suggests that the capacity of action relationships to promote pair-specific reinstatement during the familiarity-related interval remains available in healthy older age. Importantly, this finding is consistent with the ERP results reported by Huffer et al. (2022), which provided evidence for familiarity-based associative retrieval of action-related pairs specifically in older adults. Together, the ERP and ERS findings suggest that action relationships may facilitate associative recognition through processes operating within the familiarity-related time window, although the present ERS effects were observed across both age groups. Such preservation may be important because unitization has been proposed as a means of reducing older adults' reliance on recollection (e.g., Huffer et al., 2022; Ahmad et al., 2015; Zheng et al., 2015), which is particularly vulnerable to ageing (Friedman, 2013), by allowing associative recognition to draw more strongly on relatively preserved familiarity processes.

Nevertheless, although enhanced reinstatement was observed across both age groups, the agespecific ERP findings reported by Huffer et al. (2022) suggest that its contribution to associative retrieval may differ between younger and older adults.

Any apparent age differences during the earlier interval (0 -300 ms) should be interpreted in light of their specificity and consistency across analyses. Early ERS may reflect several partially overlapping influences, including perceptual processing, attentional allocation, processing speed, and the rapid extraction of action affordances. Older adults may derive greater benefit from the coherent configuration supplied by an action relationship, or they may engage with action-related arrangements differently during initial perceptual analysis. However, because the theoretically critical evidence for pair-specific action-related reinstatement occurred in the 300-500 ms interval and was not moderated by age, the strongest conclusion is that action relationships affected encoding-retrieval correspondence in both groups. Future studies with larger samples and measures of individual differences in associative memory, familiarity, and processing speed could determine whether the magnitude or functional significance of this effect changes across adulthood.

Stimulus-and task-related neural activity during the active period

The comparison between the prestimulus baseline and the 0-800 ms active period provides some reassurance that the observed ERS effects reflected stimulus-and task-related neural activity rather than a general tendency toward correlated EEG signals. ERS was greater during the active period than during baseline at all nine representative electrodes. At the same time, this analysis cannot fully rule out contributions from low-level perceptual similarity. The same-pairing and different-pairing comparison was therefore essential: it demonstrated that the relevant 300-500 ms action-related effect depended on whether the neural similarity is driven by the reinstatement of the same object pair. Nevertheless, because the same images appeared in the same-pairing condition, pair-specific ERS may include reinstatement of both perceptual and mnemonic information. In the present study, we attempted to further isolate the mnemonic contribution by comparing ERS for successful and unsuccessful retrieval. Although this analysis did not provide conclusive evidence linking the action-related ERS effect to retrieval success, future studies with a greater number of error trials, or alternative measures of memory such as confidence ratings, may offer a more sensitive test. Likewise, perceptual contributions could be examined more directly by varying object exemplars, viewpoints, spatial positions, or visual format between encoding and retrieval while preserving the underlying association.

Additional considerations and potential limitations

Before concluding, several additional aspects should be considered. First, ERS were calculated within predefined time windows and across individual electrodes, providing an interpretable measure of similarity but potentially obscuring more transient or spatially broader distributed representational effects. Moreover, scalp-level EEG similarity cannot identify the neural generators underlying reinstatement. Approaches combining time-resolved representational similarity analyses with source localisation could provide a more direct link between the temporal dynamics of reinstatement and their underlying neural generators. Finally, it remains to be established whether similar effects like those found for action-related pairs would be observed for other forms of bottom-up unitization such as semantic relations, compound words, or spatially coherent scenes. However, to our knowledge, the current study is the first one to show unitization effects on ERS applied to EEG data. The present findings thus highlight the value of examining the temporal correspondence between encoding and retrieval in the study of unitization. Although unitization is, by definition, an encoding phenomenon, the vast majority of previous studies have inferred its effects solely from behavioural or neural measures obtained at retrieval. The present work demonstrates the utility of our approach for investigating how integrated representations formed during encoding are subsequently reinstated during retrieval, while capturing the temporal dynamic of this correspondence. With these, the current approach opens new avenues for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying unitization across different paradigms and stimulus domains.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that action relationships influence the correspondence between neural activity at encoding and retrieval. Although reinstatement of the same object pair was evident across the whole analysed time course, action relationships produced an additional pair-specific increase in ERS selectively during the 300-500 ms familiarity-related interval. This finding extends previous retrieval-focused evidence for unitization by showing that action-related associations are characterised by enhanced similarity between their encoding and retrieval representations. However, the absence of a reliable interaction between action relationships and retrieval success, together with an inconclusive Bayes factor, means that the functional relationship between this reinstatement and successful memory remains unresolved. Action affordances therefore appear to shape the neural representation reinstated at retrieval, but further evidence is required to determine whether this enhanced reinstatement is the mechanism through which action relationships improve associative memory.
