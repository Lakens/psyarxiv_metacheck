Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

research_questions RQ1: How does students’ motivation change in college courses in their first academic year at college?

Numerous studies have shown a consistent decline in students’ expectancies for success and subjective task values across various subjects, both over the long term from 1st to 12th grade (e.g., Jacobs et al., 2002; Watt, 2004; see Scherrer & Preckel, 2019 for a meta-analysis), and in shorter periods such as a single college semester (e.g., Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Perez et al., 2014; Kosovich et al., Sutter et al., 2021; Zusho et al., 2003). This trend is particularly pronounced following transitions, like entering new (and challenging) courses and learning environments (Becker & Neumann, 2016; Becker et al., 2014; Coelho et al., 2017; Gniewosz et al., 2012; Wouters et al., 2012). Different reasons for such declines have been discussed, including social comparisons with a new and high achieving peer group after the transition (Becker & Neumann, 2016; Wouters et al., 2012), re-calibration of ability beliefs based on performance feedback in a more challenging curriculum (Benden & Lauermann, 2022), or more differentiated views about students’ own strengths and weaknesses that develop over time (Gaspard et al., 2020). Based on these findings, we expect a decline in motivational beliefs during the first year of college, especially at the beginning of the quarter and academic year. Our specific hypotheses regarding RQ1 are:
H1.1: Students’ expectancy beliefs will decline over the course of one academic quarter.
H1.2: Students’ subjective task values will decline over the course of one academic quarter.
H1.3: Students’ cost values will increase over the course of one academic quarter.
H1.4: The change in students’ motivational beliefs will be larger in the first half of the academic quarter than in the second half.
H1.5: The change in students’ motivational beliefs will be larger in courses in the first quarter than in courses in the second and third quarter of the academic year.

RQ2: Do the change patterns in students’ motivation differ as a function of (perceived) challenges and difficulty of a course?

The Situated Expectancy Value Theory (Eccles & Wigfield., 2020) emphasizes that students’ motivation underlies several contextual and situation-specific influences. Hence, even when students take courses within one major (e.g., biology), their expectancy-value beliefs might differ across their courses (e.g., “cell biology” and “introduction to chemistry”). We expect that students adjust their expectancy beliefs based on available information about course difficulty and hence, we hypothesize that students report lower expectancy beliefs and higher cost values in courses they personally perceive as very difficult, and in courses that have on average lower final grades and higher failure rates. Furthermore, we expect that students report higher subjective task values in courses they personally perceive as very important for them. Benden and Lauermann (2022) showed that most substantial changes in students’ expectancy-value beliefs occurred after their first performance feedback in a course. This suggests that students used the feedback to recalibrate their expectancy-value beliefs in the course. We therefore expect that students adjust their expectancy-value beliefs based on their grades of their midterm exams. Our specific hypotheses regarding RQ2 are:
H2.1: Students’ initial expectancy beliefs and subjective task values will be lower and students’ cost values will be higher in the course students perceive as very difficult, than in the course they perceive as very important.
H2.2: Students’ initial expectancy beliefs and subjective task values will be lower and students’ cost values will be higher in courses that have lower average grades.
H2.3: Students who obtained lower grades in their midterm exams have a steeper decline in expectancy beliefs and subjective task values.
We have no specific hypotheses about differential rates of change in difficult and important courses. While empirical research consistently shows a general decrease in motivation in student populations, more differentiated analysis showed that some students experienced increases in their expectancy value believes in college courses (Benden & Lauermann, 2022) and in some high school domains that were relevant for students’ future plans (Gaspard et al., 2017). Robinson et al., (2019) have shown that students’ subjective task values were subject to more positive change in large introductory courses with a supportive climate, compared to challenging introductory courses with lower average course grades and higher failure rates (e.g., “weeding-out” courses). Based on these findings we would expect a steeper decrease in expectancies and subjective task values and a higher increase in cost values in courses with higher average failure rates. On the other hand, students might have more realistic initial judgements about their expectancy-value beliefs in very difficult courses, whereas students might be overly optimistic about their initial expectancy-value beliefs in courses they perceive as personally important. In this case, we would expect stability in motivation or higher rates of change in motivation in important courses than in difficult courses. Therefore, our analyses regarding differences in change patterns of motivation in the difficult and important course have an exploratory character.
We will add gender and first-generation college student background as demographic predictors, and high school GPA as a measure of academic preparedness as predictor motivational change. We have no directed hypotheses regarding the associations of these background variables and change. We add information about cohort membership as a control variable in all models.

RQ3: Is change in motivation related to students’ performance and drop-out (intentions)?

High motivation is related to positive educational outcomes, whereas decreases in motivation in college are associated with lower performance, lower satisfaction with the study program, and higher drop out intentions (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Robinson et al., 2019; Schnettler et al., 2020). Our third research question focuses on associations between motivation and motivational change in the first college quarter and academic outcomes, including students’ final course grades, reported drop-out intentions at the end of each quarter, and dropout of the study program in the first or second college year. Our specific hypotheses regarding RQ3 are:
H3.1: Students with higher expectancy beliefs and higher subjective task values obtain better course grades, report lower drop-out intentions, and are less likely to drop out of the study program.
H3.2: Students with higher cost values obtain lower course grades, report higher drop-out intentions, and are more likely to drop out of the study program.
H3.3: Steeper declines in expectancy beliefs and subjective task values predict lower course grades, higher drop-out intentions, and higher likelihood to drop out of the study program.
H3.4: Steeper increases in cost values predict lower course grades, higher drop-out intentions, and higher likelihood to drop out of the study program.

statistical_tests Data cleaning is being conducted in the statistical software SPSS. All statistical analysis will be conducted in the statistical software Mplus 8.6. In a first step, we will carefully examine missing data and study dropout in the longitudinal data set. There are 2 potential analytical approaches to address our research questions. We start describing approach 1. If approach 1 is not feasible (e.g., in case of non-convergence in the model in Mplus, etc.), we will switch to approach 2.
Approach 1 - Second order latent change score models. All motivational variables (intercepts) and change scores (slopes) will be modeled as latent factors in the model.
First, we will use confirmatory factor analysis to specify latent measurement models of the motivational constructs (expectancies, subjective task values, cost values). When modeling these motivational constructs as latent factors, several problems may arise and we cannot anticipate all of them. The items used in the study are based on the SEVT framework and on survey questions used in prior studies. The items were adjusted to the specific study contexts and the wording is not identical to prior SEVT questionnaires. Therefore, there may be single items that show a substantially low(er) loading on the latent factors than others and model fits might not be in line with traditional recommendations (e.g., Hu & Bentler, 1999). In such instances, we will carefully check both statistical indicators (e.g., lower loading, worse model fit) and content-related indicators and adapt our models to achieve adequate fit.
Second, we will test for measurement invariance in motivational variables across timepoints to assure that the measurement instruments worked equally well at each timepoint. We will conclude that the models are invariant if the fit of the models with metric invariance (i.e., same factor loadings at T1 and T2) and the models with scalar invariance (i.e., same factor loadings and intercepts) does not differ by more than |.01| on the CFI or .015 on the RMSEA (Chen, 2007).
When measurement invariance is given, we will proceed with second order latent change score models (Geiser et al., 2013) to address our research questions. For RQ1, we will estimate latent neighbor change score models to examine motivational change from T1 (week 1 of the term) to T2 (middle of the term), and from T2 (middle of the term) to T3 (end of the term) in three consecutive academic terms (fall term, winter term, spring term). This approach allows us to examine whether the magnitude of change from week 1 until the first performance feedback through midterm exams (T1-T2) differs from the magnitude of change from a time point after the first midterm to the end of the course (T2-T3). It also allows us to compare the change of motivation in students’ first quarter at the university (fall quarter), compared to the second and third quarter (winter and spring quarter). Because we anticipate substantial correlations among students’ expectations of success, their subjective task values, and their cost values, we will run separate latent neighbor-change score analysis per each motivational construct to avoid problems with multicollinearity.
To address RQ2, we will add person-specific predictors (gender, first-generation college background, received midterm exam grade) and context-specific predictors (average grade in the past three years of the course; achievement related peer composition in the course) on intercepts and change in motivation to the models. Finally, we will add outcome variables (course performance, drop-out intentions, and major change) to the models (RQ3).
Approach 2 - First order latent change score models. All motivational variables (intercepts) will be introduced as manifest variables, and change scores (slopes) will be modeled as latent factors in the model (see e.g., Klopack & Wickrama, 2020). The approach of using parcels as manifest scores instead of latent factors in SEMs is controversially discussed in the literature (see e.g., Little et al., 2020; Rhemtulla, 2016). However, clear advantages of this approach are that it reduces the number of parameters in complex models which leads to better convergence and possibly better model fits, particularly in smaller samples (Rhemtualla, 2016). Thus, we will switch to approach 2, if models don’t converge in approach 1, or other difficulties, such as a nonpositive latent covariance matrix occur in any of the estimated models. We will conduct the same models as described in approach 1 to examine our research questions with neighbor-change score models to address RQ1, added predictors of intercepts and change in motivation to address RQ2, and added outcome variables of intercepts and changes in motivation to address RQ3.
inference_criteria We will rely on the criteria for good model fit described by Bentler and Hu (1999) for confirmatory factor analysis and latent change score models (CFI, TLI ≥ .95, RMSEA ≤ .06, SRMR ≤ .08).
We will use the standard p < .05 criteria for determining if change scores and regression coefficients in latent change score models are statistically significant from zero. We will use two tailed tests in our analyses.
data_exclusion_criteria We will include all students in the analysis, who completed at least two surveys with items on course-specific motivation.
missing_data_handling Attrition of study participants is a relevant concern in longitudinal research studies (Enders, 2022). We will conduct a careful examination of missing data in our sample. When missing at random can be assumed for the data, we will use the full maximum likelihood approach (FIML) to deal with missing data in our statistical analysis (Enders, 2022). Based on findings of missing examinations, we will add auxiliary variables to improve the model estimations.
exploratory_analyses We expect that certain demographic traits may be related to initial levels and change in motivation. Therefore, we will look for relationships between demographic variables (gender, first-generation college background) and intercepts and change in motivation.

We will also explore differences in change patterns in students self-identified most difficult course and their most important course. We have no specific hypotheses about differential rates of change in difficult and important courses. While empirical research consistently shows a general decrease in motivation in student populations, more differentiated analysis showed that some students experienced increases in their expectancy value believes in college courses (Benden & Lauermann, 2022) and in some high school domains that were relevant for students’ future plans (Gaspard et al., 2017). Robinson et al., (2019) have shown that students’ subjective task values were subject to more positive change in large introductory courses with a supportive climate, compared to challenging introductory courses with higher failure rates (e.g., “weeding-out” courses). Based on these findings we would expect a steeper decrease in expectancies and subjective task values and a higher increase in cost values in courses with higher average failure rates. On the other hand, students might have more realistic initial judgements about their expectancy-value beliefs in very difficult courses, whereas students might be overly optimistic about their initial expectancy-value beliefs in courses they perceive as personally important. In this case, we would expect stability in motivation or higher rates of change in motivation in important courses than in difficult courses.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. Abstract

Educational Impact and Implication Statement:

Results from a longitudinal study with 942 first-year college students showed early declines in course-specific expectancies of success and perceived course utility. These declines occurred repeatedly across subsequent courses in the first college year and may reflect calibration to course demands and performance feedback. Lower motivational starting points in later quarters and associations with weaker intentions to remain in the major suggest that repeated declines gradually undermine students' sense of academic fit. Instructors can support productive calibration by providing early, actionable feedback, explicitly connecting foundational coursework to students' goals, and identifying students whose motivation remains low across multiple courses.

Is Change in Motivation in College a Normative Calibration Process or an Early

Warning Sign?

Students' academic motivation is central to successful learning and persistence in higher education. Students who are confident that they can succeed and who perceive their coursework as valuable have better performance outcomes, show higher effort investment and persistence when courses and tasks are difficult, enroll in more challenging courses, and are less likely to drop out of study programs (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Dietrich et al., 2017; Lauermann et al., 2017; Trautwein & Lüdtke, 2007; von Keyserlingk et al., 2019; Wigfield & Eccles, 2020). It is therefore of substantial theoretical and practical interest to understand how students' motivation develops after the transition to college. This transition is a particularly important period because students encounter new academic demands, new social comparison contexts, and new information about their own performance and fit within a chosen field of study.

Prior research consistently finds that students' motivation often declines during college, particularly at the beginning. Across single courses, academic terms, and longer periods of study, students tend to report decreases in expectancies of success and subjective task values and increases in perceived cost (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Beymer & Rosenzweig, 2023; Robinson et al., 2019; Schnettler et al., 2020; Totonchi et al., 2021).

However, the meaning of these declines is not yet fully understood. On the one hand, decreases in expectancies and subjective task value and increases in cost may reflect an adaptive calibration process: As students enter a new educational context, they may adjust initially optimistic beliefs in response to more demanding coursework, higher-achieving peers, and performance feedback. On the other hand, continued declines across courses and academic terms may indicate an emerging motivational risk process that undermines performance, well-being, and persistence in the major.

The overarching goal of the present study is to better understand these two possible interpretations of motivational change in the first college year. Specifically, we examine whether changes in motivation are concentrated early in students' first year and early within courses, which would be consistent with a normative calibration process, or whether declines continue across later quarters and courses, which would suggest a more persistent and potentially harmful developmental trend. To address this question, we draw on situated expectancy-value theory (SEVT: Eccles, 1983; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020), which conceptualizes expectancies of success and subjective task values as central drivers of achievement-related behavior and performance. We focus on three motivational constructs that are especially relevant for first-year students' academic decision-making: expectancies of success, utility value, and perceived cost.

We use longitudinal data from students' first college year at a public university in California, U.S., including repeated course-specific assessments of motivation in two personally salient courses per quarter across the fall, winter, and spring quarters. In each quarter, students reported their motivation for one course they selected as their most difficult and one they selected as their most important (i.e., a total of six courses per student). This design allows us to examine motivational change within courses, across quarters, and across courses that are likely to be especially relevant for students' judgments about academic success, fit, and persistence in their major. In addition, we examine whether course characteristics and performance feedback predict motivational trajectories and whether motivational levels and motivational changes are associated with final course grades, intentions to remain in the major, and subsequent major change.

Theoretical Background

The Situated Expectancy Value Theory (SEVT)

The situated expectancy value theory (SEVT) provides a broad theoretical framework that describes expectancies of success and subjective task values as central drivers of achievement-related behavior and performance (Eccles, 1983; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).

Expectancies of success refer to students' subjective beliefs about their skills and competencies to succeed in a given task or course. Subjective task values comprise four subcomponents and describe the subjective value students attach to being successful in a given task or course. Intrinsic value refers to the enjoyment experienced when engaging in the task; attainment value refers to the personally perceived importance of being successful in the task; and utility value refers to the perceived usefulness of the task for short-and long-term future goals. Cost values, on the other hand, describe the anticipated or perceived effort and time students need to invest in a task, the resulting stress when engaging in it, and the perceived cost of giving up other valued activities to have enough time to focus on a given task.

A central assumption of SEVT is that motivational beliefs are situated. Students' expectancies and values are shaped by socialization processes, prior academic experiences, and performance feedback (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). They are therefore not static characteristics of students but can change as students encounter new academic demands and receive new information about their abilities, interests, and goals. Developmental changes in expectancies and values may play an important role in helping students identify their strengths, weaknesses, and preferences and in supporting informed decisions about courses, majors, and career pathways (Eccles, 2009; Wigfield et al., 2020).

We focus on expectancies of success, utility value, and cost, because these constructs are particularly relevant during the first college year, when students are evaluating whether they can succeed in their coursework, whether their courses are useful for their academic and career goals, and whether continued investment in a major is worth the required effort. These beliefs are also closely aligned with the academic outcomes considered in the present study, including course performance and major change (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Schnettler et al., 2020). Prior intervention research further suggests that utility value and cost are malleable and can be targeted to support students' academic motivation, academic outcomes, and to buffer against motivational declines (Rosenzweig et al., 2020 (Rosenzweig et al., , 2022)).

Change in Motivation at College

Despite the positive associations between expectancies and subjective task values with desired academic outcomes, empirical studies consistently show declining trends in students' expectancies and subjective task values and increases in cost values over time. This pattern has been shown across different higher education contexts (e.g, the US and Germany), and across different time frames, including single courses in one semester, one academic year, or longer time periods of two and for academic years (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Beymer & Rosenzweig, 2023; Brahm et al., 2017; Kosovich et al., 2017; Robinson et al., 2019 Robinson et al., , 2022;; Schnettler et al., 2020; Sutter et al., 2024; Totonchi et al., 2021). The majority of studies focus on STEM (science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) students, but similar declines in expectancies and subjective task values, along with increases in cost, are observed across other major groups, including economics and management, law, psychology, and non-medical students more broadly (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Brahm et al., 2017; Dresel & Grassinger, 2013; Schnettler et al., 2020; Sutter et al., 2024). Hence, these changes in motivation are not characteristic of certain fields of study, but a phenomenon that emerges among students across very different disciplines and grade distributions.

Yet prior research leaves open an important question: Are early declines in college students' motivation best understood as a normative adjustment to a new educational context, as an early warning sign, or a more complex process combining adaptive calibration with accumulating academic risk? This distinction matters because the implications for theory and practice differ. If motivational change mainly reflects calibration, then declines should be strongest early in the transition or early in a course, when students first encounter new academic demands and feedback and stabilize with increasing familiarity to the educational contexts. Such changes may help students develop more realistic beliefs about their abilities, goals, and the costs of continued engagement. If motivational declines continue across later courses and academic terms, however, they may indicate an undesired motivational development that can undermine academic success and persistence.

Motivation in Educational Transitions: Underlying Mechanisms

Most studies examined changes in students' motivation in the first college year (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Brahm et al., 2017; Dresel & Grassinger, 2013) or in introductory courses (Beymer & Allen, 2024; Beymer & Rosenzweig, 2023; Lee et al., 2024; Sutter et al., 2024), hence, in environments and topics new to the students. A change in motivation in new contexts could be an expression of an adjustment process to new information and experiences in the courses. Motivation research revealed that information about individual performance and contextual factors are highly relevant for the development of ability beliefs, which are closely related to students' expectancies of success (Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Social comparisons with the achievement of peers (Marsh, 1987 (Marsh, , 2005)), dimensional comparisons of one's own achievement levels in different domains (Möller & Marsh, 2013), and performance feedback and grades (Marsh & O'Mara, 2008; Sievers et al., 2026; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000) all matter for students' ability beliefs and expectancies of success. For example, when students transition into a course with higher-achieving peers, they begin comparing their abilities with those of these peers, which can lead to subsequent decreases in their ability beliefs (Becker & Neumann, 2016; von Keyserlingk et al., 2019).

These comparison processes and the performance feedback are not only relevant to students' ability beliefs, but also to their interests and value beliefs (Cambria et al., 2017; von Keyserlingk et al., 2021; Wigfield et al., 2020).

Let's imagine a university freshman who enrolled in chemistry at the university. In high school, this student might have been one of the few high-achieving students in chemistry, and mastering the high school course content did not require much effort. After transitioning to university, the student is in a new academic context and may be surrounded by many highly achieving peers, and mastering the course content may now require considerable effort. This would be a drastic change compared to high school. Comparing with other high-achieving peers and experiencing academic struggles might lead to a decrease in students' expectancy beliefs and an increase in perceived costs. If the course covers the fundamentals of chemistry without applied components, the student might also begin to question the usefulness of this course for their later career. Such changes in motivation could result from recalibrating motivational beliefs in a new environment. Indeed, past research has shown that students' ability beliefs, interests, and task values change shortly after educational transitions (Gniewosz et al., 2012; Kyndt et al., 2015; von Keyserlingk et al., 2019).

A Closer Look at Motivational Change in Single College Courses

Studies that examine motivation during one college course with at least three measurement points at the beginning, middle, and end of the semester revealed that students' motivation changed mostly in the first few weeks of a course and remained more stable in the second half (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Lee et al., 2024; Sutter et al., 2024). Benden and Lauermann (2022) described the change pattern as a "motivational shock" in math lectures with steep declines in students' expectancies and subjective task values and increases in cost values in the first weeks of the course. The change in motivation was most pronounced after students received their first performance feedback in the course. Sutter et al. (2024) found a similar pattern of change among students of an introductory statistics class. Students showed high initial levels of expectancies, intrinsic value, and utility value that declined in the first weeks of the course, while cost values showed lower initial levels that increased over the first weeks. The authors referred to this change pattern as a "honeymoon phase" at the beginning of the term. Lee et al. (2024) reported highly similar trends of early decreases in students' self-efficacy, intrinsic, attainment, and utility value, and increases in cost values among Chemistry and Computer Sciences students. Across all three studies, smaller decreases in motivation were associated with better performance and persistence in the course. It is important to note here that the reported patterns show average trends across all students in the courses. While the majority of students in the courses experienced decreases in motivation, some students reported stable or even increasing motivation over time (see e.g. Benden & Lauermann, 2022).

These findings align with the idea that students recalibrate their motivation when they encounter a course's actual demands. However, single-course studies cannot determine whether early motivational shifts are primarily course-specific adjustments or whether similar declines continue across later courses and terms. In other words, prior course-level studies show when motivation changes within a course, but they do not show whether motivation stabilizes after an initial adjustment to college or whether motivational decline accumulates across the academic terms.

Patterns of Long-Term Motivational Changes Across College Years

Other studies investigated students' motivation over longer time periods, such as one or more college years (Brahm et al., 2017; Robinson et al., 2019 Robinson et al., , 2022;; Totonchi et al., 2021).

Overall, these studies showed that students reported higher expectancy and value beliefs at the beginning of a college year, which decreased over time, and lower cost values that increased over time. Robinson et al. (2019 Robinson et al. ( , 2022) ) and Totonchi et al. (2021) examined associations of motivational change patterns with performance and retention in STEM courses and consistently showed that declines in expectancies and subjective task values and steeper increases in cost values predicted lower term grade point averages (GPAs) and lower retention rates over multiple semesters. These longer-term studies indicate that motivational decline can be harmful for students' academic thriving in their majors. However, because they typically include fewer measurement occasions and assess motivation at broader intervals, they cannot identify whether motivational changes occur mainly early in the first college year, early within courses, or gradually across multiple terms. What is still needed to better understand motivational changes after transitions is a design that combines repeated within-course assessments with longitudinal follow-up across the first college year.

The Present Study

The present study addresses this gap by examining course-specific motivational changes across students' first college year. We use data from the larger UCI-MUST project (Arum et al., 2021). Students completed repeated surveys about their motivation in two courses per quarter across the fall, winter, and spring quarters of their first year. In each quarter, students selected one course they perceived as their most difficult course and one course they perceived as their most important course. If students selected the same course as their most difficult and most important one, we asked them to select their second most important course for this study. Students reported their expectancies of success, utility value, and cost in the two selected courses three times during each quarter.

This design allows us to examine whether motivational changes are concentrated early in students' first quarter and early within courses, which would be consistent with a calibration process, or whether motivational declines continue across later quarters, which would suggest a more persistent developmental process with potential negative consequences.

The design is also uniquely suited to examine course-level and student-level predictors of motivational change: Specifically, we investigate whether motivation differs between courses selected as most difficult versus most important, and whether course-level indicators of difficulty and individual performance feedback predict motivational change. Furthermore, we examine whether motivational levels and changes in motivation are associated with final course grades, intentions to remain in the major, and actual major change within the first two college years.

Because students selected courses that were personally difficult or important, these course experiences may be especially relevant for broader judgments about success, fit, and persistence in the major. At the same time, our claims are specific to highly salient course experiences rather than to all courses students take during the first year. We therefore conceptualize these courses as meaningful academic contexts in which students may receive important information about their competence, goals, and the costs of continued engagement.

Three research questions guided the present study:

RQ1: What are the patterns of change in student motivation in college courses during the first academic year?

Prior research suggests that students' expectancies and values tend to decline and their perceived costs tend to increase during college courses and across the first year. Based on single-course studies, we further expect that motivational change will be strongest early in a course (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Lee et al., 2024). If motivational change reflects calibration to the new college environment, then changes should be larger in the first quarter than in later quarters. We therefore propose the following hypotheses (H): H1.1: Students' expectancy beliefs and utility values will decline, and cost values will increase over the course of one academic quarter. H1.2: The change in students' motivational beliefs will be larger in the first half of the academic quarter than in the second half.

H1.3: The change in students' motivational beliefs will be larger in courses in the first quarter than in courses in the second and third quarters of the academic year.

RQ2: Do the change trajectories in student motivation differ as a function of (perceived) challenges and difficulty of a course?

SEVT (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) emphasizes that students' motivation is subject to several contextual and situation-specific influences. Even within the same major, students may therefore hold different expectancy-value beliefs across courses. In the present study, we examine whether motivational beliefs and motivational change is related to three different aspects of course difficulty: subjective perception of course difficulty, average performance in the course of past student cohorts, and individual grades students received in the respective courses. We expected that students would report lower expectancy beliefs and higher costs in courses selected as most difficult than in courses selected as most important. Because courses selected as most important may be more strongly connected to students' goals, we expect students to report higher utility value in these courses. We also examine whether course-level difficulty is associated with students' motivation. Courses with higher average grades in prior cohorts may represent less challenging academic contexts, whereas courses with lower average grades may signal greater academic difficulty. We expect higher expectancies and utility values, and lower cost values in courses with higher past average grades.

SEVT describes the relevance of prior academic experience for expectancies and subjective task values (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). In line with this, Benden and Lauermann (2022) showed that the most substantial changes in students' expectancy-value beliefs occurred after their first performance feedback in a course. This suggests that students used feedback to recalibrate their expectancy-value beliefs regarding the course. We therefore expect that students adjust their expectancy-value beliefs based on their midterm exam grades.

Our specific hypotheses regarding RQ2 are: H2.1: Students' initial expectancy beliefs and utility values will be lower, and students' cost values will be higher in the course students perceive as very difficult than in the course they perceive as very important.

H2.2: Students' initial expectancy beliefs and utility values will be higher, and students' cost values will be lower in courses that had better average grades in past cohorts.

H2.3: Students who obtained lower grades in their exams have a steeper decline in expectancy beliefs and utility values.

We also included gender and first-generation college student background as demographic predictors of motivation and change for exploratory analysis. Previous studies have shown some gender differences in STEM fields (see e.g., Benden & Lauermann, 2022), however, students in our sample were enrolled in a wide range of fields of study. From a theoretical and conceptual perspective, one could expect that students from non-academic families had a less academically oriented socialization or experienced more academic challenges in the past, which could shape their initial levels and changes in motivation at college. However, empirical findings in this area are mixed, with some results showing higher initial expectancies and subjective task values among first-generation college students, while other studies revealed no differences in initial levels or changes in motivation across students from different academic backgrounds (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Robinson et al., 2019).

Therefore, we had no specific hypotheses regarding the associations between gender, academic background, and motivation or motivational change.

RQ3: Is a change in motivation related to students' final course grades, major change intentions, and major change?

Expectancy-value theory and prior empirical research suggest that students with higher expectancies, higher values, and lower cost experience more positive academic outcomes (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Robinson et al., 2019; Schnettler et al., 2020; Wigfield & Eccles, 2020). In the present study, we examine whether students' course-specific motivation is associated with final course grades, intentions to remain in the major, and major change within the first two college years. We distinguish between motivational levels and motivational change because both may be informative: Students who begin a course with more positive motivational beliefs may perform better and be more likely to persist, and students whose motivation declines more steeply may be at greater academic risk.

For course performance, we examine whether initial motivational levels and early changes are associated with final course grades. For persistence-related outcomes, we examine whether initial motivational levels and changes predict students' intentions to remain in their major and actual major change within the first two college years. We use the term major change to refer to students leaving or changing their initial major rather than leaving college altogether.

Our specific hypotheses regarding RQ3 are: H3.1: Students with higher initial expectancy beliefs, utility values, and lower cost values obtain better course grades, report higher intentions to stay in their major, and are less likely to change their major within the first two college years.

H3.2: Steeper declines in expectancy beliefs and utility values, and higher increases in cost values predict lower final course grades, lower intentions to stay in their major, and a higher likelihood of changing the major within the first two college years.

Taken together, the present study bridges intensive single-course research and longerterm longitudinal research on motivation in college. By assessing motivation repeatedly within multiple personally salient courses across the first academic year, we examine whether early motivational declines are best understood as short-term calibration to new academic contexts or as part of a continuing developmental process with consequences for performance and persistence.

Pre-registration and deviations of the pre-registration in the final analysis

This study's design and hypotheses were preregistered at OSF after data had been collected but before analyses were undertaken; see https://osf.io/2xf4h/overview?view_only=895e4bcc29eb4e6b9ac742fff2cf2a8d. We summarize the deviations from the pre-registration below. A detailed description and explanations of how analyses deviated from the pre-registration are provided in the supplemental material C. First, we changed the wording in some research questions and hypotheses for better readability. These modifications did not change the content of the questions or expected effects. Second, we used a larger sample of all students who completed at least one survey on motivational variables in the study, instead of including only students who completed at least two surveys. We estimated complex models with latent change trajectories in three quarters, including several predictor and outcome variables, and our models required high statistical power. A careful analysis of missing data revealed that missingness in surveys was not related to midterm grades and major change, and only weakly related to final course grades and intentions to stay in the major. Therefore, we decided to be more inclusive in our sample, and we used the full information maximum likelihood approach (FIML) to account for missing data in our models. Third, we initially planned to include high school GPA as a measure of academic preparedness and a variable with the achievement level of peers in each course in our analysis. However, this led to five performance indicators in total (i.e, high school GPA, achievement level of peers, achievement level of prior cohorts, midterm grade, final grade) and resulted in overly complex models that did not converge. Therefore, we decided to keep only achievement measures most relevant to our research questions: students' midterm and final grades, and the average grade of students' courses in the past three decades as a proxy for course difficulty. Fourth, instead of using one composite latent subjective task value factor, we decided to keep only items on utility value and excluded items on intrinsic and attainment value. We did so for the following two reasons: A) Model fit statistics in confirmatory factor analysis and baseline change models were substantially higher when only utility value items were included compared to models with a general subjective task value factor based on intrinsic, attainment, and utility value items. Most likely because of unexpected positive correlations of some items on intrinsic and cost value components (see Supplemental Material C for further details). Furthermore, models reached scalar invariance when only utility value items were used, but only partial strong invariance when a general subjective task value factor with all value items was used. B) From a theoretical perspective and for practical implications, we perceive it as more informative to focus on utility value as one specific subjective task value facet that is central to major commitment and can be targeted in utility value interventions, rather than using a combined factor with utility value and attainment value items. This would lead to a theoretically incomplete composite score of subjective task values.

Sample and Procedure

We used data from a longitudinal multi-cohort study at a public university in California (UCI-MUST project). All undergraduate students in the academic years 2020/21 to 2022/23 in their freshman or junior year were invited to participate as part of a large longitudinal project on undergraduate success trajectories. The UCI-MUST project was approved by the institutional review board (#HS: 2018-4646). We used data from a subsample of freshmen who consented to participate in an intensive element of the study with weekly short surveys throughout their first year in college (N = 942). 62% of the sample were firstgeneration college-going students, 29% were male students, and the sample was ethnically diverse (48% Asian/ Asian American; 34% Hispanic; 10% White/ European American; 5% other; 3% missing information). The ethnic composition of the sample represents the student composition at the university, whereas female and first-generation students were overrepresented in the sample (ca. 50% female students and 50% first-generation students at the university). Students were enrolled in different fields of study. 23% of the students were enrolled in biology and health sciences, 17% in physics, chemistry, engineering, math, and computer sciences, 30% in social sciences, 8% in arts and humanities, and 14% of the students had not declared a major yet. Students completed weekly surveys about their course experiences in two courses per term: a course they selected as their most difficult one and a course they selected as their most important one. If students selected the same course as their most difficult and important course, we asked them to select their second most important course for this study. Students completed online questionnaires on motivational beliefs in the first week of the course term (Time 1), after receiving the grade of their first midterm exam (Time 2), and after receiving their final grades (Time 3). Time 2 surveys were administered during weeks 5 to 8, depending on when students' midterm exams were scheduled. Students reported their midterm grades at Time 2 and their intentions to stay in or leave the major at Time 3. Students received course credit as compensation for participating in the study. In addition to survey data, we used administrative data on demographic information, course achievement levels of prior cohorts, and final course grades. The sample had some attrition within each academic quarter and across academic quarters (fall: N = 942, winter: N = 798, spring: N = 687; for further information on the missing rates, see the section on statistical analysis).

Measurement Instruments

Motivation.

All items to assess students' course-specific motivation were assessed in online surveys at three time-points. All items on students' motivation were developed by the research team based on the SEVT literature (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020; Wigfield & Eccles, 2000). Students responded to the items on a 7-point Likert scale. Three items were used to assess course-specific expectancies at each time point. Reliability of the scale was high (difficult course: ω = .85; important course: ω = .87). An example item from T1 is: "How good do you think you will be at learning the new material in your most difficult course [course name]?". We used two items to assess course-specific utility value at each time point.

Reliability of the scale was acceptable (difficult course: Spearman-Brown-Coefficient = .70; important course: Spearman-Brown-Coefficient = .70) An example item for T1 is: "On a scale from 1-7, how much do you expect that your most difficult course [course name] will be useful in terms of your long term goals?". We used two items to assess course-specific cost values at each time point. Reliability of the scale was satisfactory (difficult course: Spearman-Brown-Coefficient = .79; important course: Spearman-Brown-Coefficient = .78). An example item from T1 is: "On a scale from 1-7, how much do you expect that your most difficult course [course name] will be stressful?". For an overview of all survey items that were included in this study, see Table S1 in the supplemental material.

Midterm Grade.

Students reported the letter grade (A+ to F) of their midterm exams in their most difficult and most important course. We transformed letter grades into a numeric variable (A+ = 13 to F =1) for statistical analysis.

Major Intentions.

We used one item that measured students' intentions to remain or drop out of the major at T3: "Based on the experiences in this course, is it more or less likely that you will continue studying in this field?" Students responded to this item on a 7-point Likert scale from1 = much less likely to7 = much more likely.

Student Background Information.

All information on students' backgrounds was obtained from the university's administrative data. We used a binary indicator of students' biological gender (0 = female,1 = male) and a binary indicator of students' first-generation college-going status (0 = continuing-generation college student,1 = first-generation college student).

Course Difficulty.

We generated a variable indicating a course's overall difficulty by averaging the grades students received in that course over the past three academic years. This variable is treated as an indicator of the difficulty of courses and was generated from the college record data.

Final Course Grade.

The final course grade was obtained from the college record data. We recoded the letter grades from A+ to F into a numeric variable for our analysis (A+ = 13, F = 1).

Major Change.

We used a binary variable indicating whether students changed their major after their first year of college (0 = no major change,1 = major change). This variable was generated based on college record data.

Statistical Analysis

We used the statistical software Mplus 8.6 (Muthen & Muthen, 2017) for our analyses.

First, we used confirmatory factor analysis to specify latent measurement models of the motivational constructs (expectancies, utility values, cost values). Second, we tested for measurement invariance in motivational variables across time points to ensure that the measurement instruments worked equally well at each time point. We concluded that the models were invariant if the fit statistics of models with metric invariance (i.e., same factor loadings at T1, T2, and T3) and with scalar invariance (i.e., same factor loadings and intercepts across time) did not differ by more than .01 on the CFI or .015 on the RMSEA (Chen, 2007). The analyses revealed scalar invariance in all quarters for difficult and important courses (see Table S2 in the supplemental material).

Following these preliminary analyses, we used latent change score models (Geiser et al., 2013) to address our research questions. The model fit of latent neighborhood change score models was evaluated based on the model fit criteria by Kline (2016) with RMSEA = .05-.08, CFI/TLI ≥ .90, and SRMR ≤ .08. For RQ1, we estimated latent neighbor change score models to examine motivational change from T1 (week 1 of the term) to T2 (middle of the term), and from T2 (middle of the term) to T3 (end of the term) in three consecutive academic terms (fall, winter, spring). This approach allowed us to examine whether the magnitude of change from week 1 to the first performance feedback after the midterm exams (T1-T2) differed from that from a time point after the first midterm to after the course end (T2-T3). It also allowed us to compare the rate of motivational change in students' first quarter at the university (fall quarter) with changes in the second and third quarters (winter and spring quarters). We ran separate latent neighbor change score analyses for each motivational construct to avoid overly complex models and problems with multicollinearity.

To address RQ2, we added person-specific predictors (midterm and final exam grade, gender, and first-generation college background) and context-specific predictors (average course grade from the past three cohorts) on intercepts and change in motivation to the models. We added cohort membership as a control variable to all models. Finally, we added outcome variables (drop-out intentions and major change) to the models (RQ3). Figure 1 shows the conceptual model for the analysis of RQ2 and 3.

The sample was affected by attrition with N = 942 participating students in the fall quarter, N = 798 in the winter quarter, and N = 687 in the spring quarter. Within the academic quarters, missing rates in motivational variables (expectancies, utility value, cost value) increased over time (fall/winter/spring: T1 4%/10%/9% missing, T2 18%/19%/22% missing, T3 22%/15%/48% missing). Missing data in these motivational variables was not related to midterm exam grades but showed small negative correlations with final course grades (r = -.08 to -.19) and small correlations with intentions to stay in the major in the difficult course in winter (r = .08) and in the important course in spring (r = .11). Missing data was not related to changing the major within the first two college years. Given the correlations of missing data with some of the performance measures and intentions to stay in the major were small, we assume that missing was at random (Enders, 2022). We included all these achievement measures and major commitment variables in our models and used the full information maximum likelihood (FIML) approach to account for missing data in our analyses.

Change in Motivation in Particularly Difficult Courses

In particularly difficult courses, students had medium levels of expectations to succeed at the beginning of the quarter (T1 expectancies ranging from M = 4.56 in fall to M = 4.31 in spring). The analysis showed statistically significant declines in expectancies from T1 to T2 and an increase in expectancies from T2 to T3. Hence, across quarters, expectancies declined toward the middle of each quarter and recovered toward the end, after students received their final course grades (H1.1). As hypothesized in H1.2, the decline in expectancies was greater in the first half of the quarter, until students received their midterm exam scores. Furthermore, Wald tests showed that the rate of change did not differ in the fall, winter, and spring quarters (p > .05; H1.3). However, initial expectancies at T1 were higher in the fall quarter compared to the winter quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 20.08, p < .001) and spring quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 23.78, p < .001). This means that the change pattern was similar in the three academic quarters of students' first college year, but the starting point of students' expectancies of success was lower in the second and third quarters than in the first quarter.

Students reported medium levels of utility value at the beginning of particularly difficult courses (T1 ranging from M = 4.45 in fall to M = 3.80 in spring). As expected, students' utility values decreased from T1 to T2 after the midterms and remained stable in the second half of the courses in fall and winter (H1.1). In the spring quarter, students' utility values recovered in the second half of the course. The decrease in students' utility values was larger in the first half of the quarter than in the second half (H1.2). Furthermore, Wald tests showed that the decrease in utility values from T1 -T2 was larger in the fall quarter compared to the winter quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 4.07, p < .001) and the spring quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 4.69, p = .030; H1.3). The initial utility values were higher in fall compared to the winter quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 32.12, p < .001), and higher in winter, compared to the spring quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 13.15, p = .002). Put differently, students experienced a larger decline in their utility values in their very first college quarter than in the second and third quarters. Furthermore, they started with lower utility values in the second and third quarters.

Regarding cost values, analysis revealed unexpected findings. Students reported high perceived costs at the beginning of particularly difficult courses (T1 cost value ranging from M = 5.90 in fall to M = 5.80 in spring). Results of latent neighbor change score models showed declines in cost values from T1 to T2 and no change in cost in the latter course halves in fall, winter, and spring quarters (H1.1, H1.2). Wald tests showed that the rate of change from T1-T2 was comparable in all three quarters of the first academic year (H1.3). Initial perceived costs were lower in the winter quarter compared to the fall quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 5.63, p < .018), but not statistically different in fall and spring quarters.

Change in Motivation in Particularly Important Courses

In courses students selected as particularly important to them, they reported high expectancies at the beginning of the quarters (T1 expectancies ranging from M = 5.56 in fall to M = 5.11 in spring). Similar to the findings regarding difficult courses, students' expectancies declined from T1 to T2 and partially recovered from T2 to T3 (H1.1). The change in expectancies was larger in the first half of the courses than in the second half (H1.2). The magnitude of the change from T1 -T2 was comparable in fall and winter, but larger in fall than in spring (Wald χ²(1) = 9.27, p = .002; H1.3). Furthermore, Wald tests showed that the initial expectancies were higher in courses in the fall quarter than in the winter quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 30.80, p < .001), and higher in the winter quarter than in the spring quarter (Wald χ²(1) = 87.56, p < .001). Hence, in each subsequent quarter of the first college year, students reported lower expectancies at the beginning of their important courses.

Students reported medium to high utility values at the beginning of their courses, ranging from M = 5.31 in the fall quarter to M = 4.52 in the spring quarter. As expected, the analysis showed declines in students' utility values from T1 to T2 (H1.1). Utility values remained stable in the latter half of the courses in the fall quarter and slightly recovered in the winter and spring quarters. Hence again, the decline was more pronounced in the first half of the course than in the second half (H1.2). Furthermore, Wald tests showed that the decline in utility values was larger in fall compared to spring (Wald χ²(1) = 5.59, p = .018, H1.3). Regarding cost values, the analysis showed medium initial cost values in the fall, winter, and spring quarters (T1 cost values ranging from M =5.00 in fall, to M = 4.90 in winter). Like in particularly difficult courses, cost values decreased from T1 to T2 and remained stable from T2 to T3 (H1.1, H1.2). The amount of change did not differ between the quarters (H1.3). Initial costs differed between fall and winter, with slightly lower initial costs in the winter term (Wald χ²(1) = 3.86, p = .049). Initial costs in the spring quarter were not statistically different from initial costs in the fall and winter quarters.

Discussion

In the present study, we asked whether motivational change during the first college year reflects a normative calibration process or an early warning sign of undesirable motivational decline and academic risk. Our findings suggest that this distinction is too simple. Motivational change in the first year appears to involve repeated, situated calibration rather than a one-time adjustment after the transition to college. Students' expectancies of success and utility values declined most strongly in the first half of each quarter and often stabilized or recovered later in the course, suggesting that students adjusted their motivational beliefs based on course demands and performance feedback. At the same time, initial expectancies and utility values were lower in later quarters than in the fall quarter, indicating that these repeated course-specific calibrations occurred at lower motivational baselines over time. Thus, motivational decline in the first year should not be interpreted as either purely adaptive or uniformly harmful. Instead, it appears to be a regular part of students' adjustment to new academic contexts that may become concerning when it accumulates across courses and is accompanied by weakening commitment to the major.

This interpretation extends prior work on motivational change in college in two ways.

First, it bridges intensive single-course studies, which show that motivation often shifts during the early weeks of a course, with longer-term empirical studies, which show that motivational decline can be associated with academic risk (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Robinson et al., 2019; Schnettler et al., 2020; Sutter et al., 2024). Second, it adds a more contextualized account of when motivational change may be adaptive and when it may be concerning. The same downward shift in motivation can have different meanings depending on its timing, recurrence, and consequences. A decline in expectancy beliefs after students receive more realistic information about course demands may reflect meaningful calibration (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). However, repeated declines across multiple salient courses, especially when accompanied by lower starting levels in later quarters, may gradually erode students' motivational resources and their intentions to stay in the major.

Repeated Situated Calibration Across the First College Year

Consistent with our expectations and with prior studies of college courses, students' expectancies of success and utility values declined from the beginning to the middle of the quarter. These declines were generally larger in the first half of the quarter than in the second half, when expectancies often recovered, and utility values tended to stabilize or partially recover. This pattern supports the idea that early motivational change reflects calibration to the actual demands of a course. At the beginning of a course, students may rely on incomplete information, prior experiences, course reputations, and expectations about their own performance. By the middle of the quarter, they have encountered the workload, instruction, peer context, and first performance feedback, all of which provide new information for revising their motivational beliefs (see also Benden & Lauermann, 2022).

At the same time, the findings do not support the view that motivational change is limited to a single adjustment at the beginning of college. Students showed similar earlycourse declines not only in the fall quarter but also in the winter and spring quarters. In addition, their initial expectancies and utility values tended to be lower in later quarters than in the fall quarter. This pattern suggests that students do not simply recalibrate once they enter college; instead, a declining motivational trend appears to continue throughout the first college year. Each new course may function as a new situated context in which students evaluate their competence, the usefulness of the course, and the effort required to succeed.

This finding is consistent with the situatedness of the expectancy-value theory (SEVT; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) and shows that motivation is not only course-specific at a single point in time; it is dynamically recalibrated within and across course contexts during the first college year.

The results, therefore, suggest two simultaneous adjustment processes. One process occurs within courses: students often begin with relatively optimistic expectancy and utility beliefs and then revise these beliefs downward as they encounter course demands (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Lee et al., 2024; Sutter et al., 2024). A second process unfolds across the academic year: students begin later quarters with lower expectancies and utility values than they reported at the beginning of the fall quarter. The first process is consistent with shortterm calibration. The second process may suggest a stepwise declining trend in motivation.

While each course-specific decline may be understandable and potentially adaptive in isolation, the repeated pattern may lead students to enter subsequent courses with less confidence and lower perceived usefulness.

The unexpected findings for cost values further support the interpretation that students are calibrating their beliefs. Contrary to our hypotheses and to prior research (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Beymer & Rosenzweig, 2023; Lee et al., 2024), perceived cost declined from the beginning to the middle of the quarter and remained stable thereafter. One interpretation is that initial cost ratings in the course captured anticipated cost under uncertainty, whereas later ratings reflected experienced cost after students had more concrete information about workload, grading demands, and their own coping capacity. Because students rated courses they perceived as particularly difficult or important, they may have initially expected these courses to be highly stressful and time-consuming. As the quarter progressed, students may have realized that the courses were more manageable than expected.

Thus, decreasing cost may represent a form of cost calibration in which students adjust initially high expectations of cost downward after gaining experience in the course.

What Matters for Calibration: Course Difficulty and Performance Feedback

Findings for course difficulty and performance feedback further support the interpretation of motivational change as situated calibration. Students reported lower initial expectancies and utility values and higher initial cost values in courses they selected as particularly difficult than in courses they selected as particularly important. Hence, their motivation varied meaningfully across two personally salient courses within the same academic term. This course specificity is central to SEVT's assumption that expectancy-value beliefs are situated in particular contexts (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020).

Analyses using past cohort grades as an indicator of course difficulty also showed that students' initial expectancies and cost values aligned with course-level difficulty. Students reported higher expectancies and lower perceived costs in courses with higher average grades in prior cohorts. This pattern may reflect students' awareness of course reputations, advising information, or peer communication about course demands. This explanation remains on a speculative level, because we did not directly assess whether students knew prior grade distributions. However, our findings show that students' initial motivational beliefs were meaningfully aligned with course contexts that were objectively more or less difficult, as indexed by past course performance. Interestingly, students showed steeper declines in expectancies in courses with higher average grades in prior cohorts. One possible explanation is that students entered these courses with overly optimistic expectations and invested less effort than was ideally needed. When their own experiences in the course and the midterm exams were less positive than expected, they downward-adjusted their expectancies. Another possibility is that courses with high average grades in past cohorts provide a course context with high-performing peer groups. Social comparisons with peers are one important source of information for students' self-concepts (Marsh & Hau, 2003). In courses with high average grades, students may revise their expectancy beliefs downward when they compare themselves with high-performing peers during the course (see big-fish-little-pond effect; Marsh, 1987; Marsh & Hau, 2003; von Keyserlingk et al., 2019). Future research should test this explanation more directly by examining the roles of individual and course-level achievement for expectancies of success in longitudinal multilevel models.

A strong indicator for calibration processes is the effect of performance feedback on motivational changes. Students who obtained better midterm grades showed more positive change in expectancies and utility values and a steeper decline in cost. These findings are consistent with SEVT's assumption that academic experiences and performance feedback shape motivational beliefs (Eccles & Wigfield, 2020) and with prior research showing that feedback can be followed by substantial changes in expectancy-value beliefs (Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Kosovich et al., 2017). They also suggest that early performance feedback may be a critical moment in first-year students' motivational development. Midterm grades or even earlier performance feedback (e.g., via weekly quizzes) may help students update their beliefs about whether they could succeed, whether the course was useful, and how costly continued engagement would be.

The results also suggest that students may calibrate different motivational beliefs using different kinds of information. Expectancies and cost values were more closely tied to course difficulty and performance feedback than utility values. In courses students selected as particularly important, midterm grades predicted changes in expectancies and cost, but were less consistently related to changes in utility value. Utility value was also less consistently related to past cohort grades. This pattern suggests that expectancy and cost may be especially responsive to information about performance and demands, whereas utility value may be more closely tied to students' goals, identity, and perceived connection between coursework and future pathways (Eccles, 2009). If students perceive a course as central to their academic or career goals, they may maintain its perceived utility even when performance feedback is disappointing.

Exploratory analyses revealed that male students and first-generation college students reported higher initial expectancies and lower cost values, particularly in perceived difficult courses. This suggests that males and first-generation students begin their courses with more optimistic motivational beliefs, which is consistent with prior empirical findings (Robinson et al., 2019). However, demographic background variables were not related to utility values or changes in any of the SEVT components. According to SEVT, socialization processes and motivational beliefs of role models and teachers play an important role in the formation of motivational beliefs (Eccles, 2009; Eccles & Wigfield, 2020). Therefore, we could hypothesize that students from different social, cultural, and ethnic backgrounds start college with different expectancies, utility, and cost values. However, the empirical evidence on this topic remains mixed (see also Robinson et al., 2019; Totonchi et al., 2021), and further research is needed to understand when and why differences in motivational beliefs by demographic backgrounds occur.

Motivational Change, Major Commitment, Major Change, and Course Performance

A central goal of this study was to examine whether motivational change should be considered an early warning sign for academic challenges and leaving the major. The answer depends on the outcome considered. The findings provide stronger evidence for commitmentrelated risk than for immediate performance-related risk or actual major change. Students with higher expectancies and utility values, as well as more positive changes in these beliefs, reported stronger intentions to remain in their major. These associations were consistent across difficult and important courses and across quarters. Thus, motivational beliefs are closely tied to students' subjective commitment to their chosen academic pathway (see also Benden & Lauermann, 2022; Lee et al., 2024; Rosenzweig et al., 2025; Schnettler et al., 2020).

However, motivational beliefs and motivational changes were not consistently related to actual major change within the first two college years, with only one exception: Higher T1 utility values predicted a lower likelihood of changing a major in difficult courses in the fall quarter. This is consistent with recent findings by Rosenzweig et al. (2025) that perceived utility is a central driver of decisions on major changes.

This discrepancy between intentions and actual major change is interesting. Intentions to remain in the major capture students' commitment and willingness to continue. Actual major change, by contrast, is likely shaped by additional constraints and opportunities, including university policies, GPA requirements, access to alternative majors, and financial considerations. Prior studies revealed that motivational beliefs are particularly relevant for educational choices when individuals have certain degrees of freedom in their choice (Watt et al., 2012). In the sample studied, about 30% of students reported a change in their major (including adding or dropping a major or minor). However, at the study site, policies for changing majors were relatively strict (e.g., students need to meet a certain GPA benchmark to be eligible for major changes), which may have limited the extent to which motivational beliefs translated into behavioral change. Thus, motivation may be highly relevant for students' subjective commitment while being less predictive of actual major change when institutional constraints are strong.

It is also important not to conceptualize every major change as a negative outcome.

From the perspective of persistence in an initially chosen major, motivational declines and lower intentions to remain may appear undesirable. From a developmental perspective, however, changing majors can be adaptive when students move toward a field that better fits their strengths, values, and long-term goals. Expectancies and subjective task values are theorized to support students in identifying their competencies, interests, and preferred pathways (Eccles, 2009; Wigfield et al., 2020). Therefore, motivational decline may sometimes indicate a problematic loss of confidence or value, but it may also reflect useful information that helps students revise their academic plans. Future research should distinguish major changes that represent academic risk from those that represent adaptive reorientation toward a better-fitting pathway (see e.g., Rosenzweig et al., 2025).

Findings regarding the role of initial motivation and motivational changes in final course grades differed from our hypothesis. Although bivariate correlations between motivation and course grades were generally in the expected direction, motivational levels and early motivational changes (from T1 to T2) did not consistently predict final grades in the latent change score models once midterm grades were included. This pattern suggests that early course performance was the strongest predictor of final performance and that motivational beliefs added no explanatory power beyond this performance information. One interpretation is that motivation may influence performance partly through earlier engagement and achievement, which were already reflected in midterm grades. Another possibility is that final course grades in the present models were too proximal to midterm performance to reveal additional effects of motivational change.

In sum, our results do not suggest that early declines in expectancy or utility necessarily lead directly to poor course performance or major change. Instead, motivational change may be an early indicator of students' changing academic self-understanding and commitment to their major. Declines in expectancy and utility appear to matter most for how students interpret their fit within the major and whether they want to continue in the field. In this sense, motivational change may signal commitment-related risk before it becomes visible in behavioral outcomes such as major change or in performance outcomes beyond early grades.

Second, our analyses focused on average patterns of intraindividual change. Although the average trends showed declines in expectancy and utility values, some students likely experienced stable or increasing motivation. Identifying subgroups of students with distinct motivational trajectories would be an important next step. Future analyses could clarify whether some students show adaptive calibration followed by stabilization, whereas others show cumulative motivational decline. Third, the courses examined in this study were personally salient courses selected by students as their most difficult and most important courses. This focus was intended because these courses are likely to be consequential for students' judgments of competence, value, cost, and fit. However, the findings should not be generalized to all courses students take during the first year. Motivation may develop differently in elective courses, or courses students perceive as less challenging and important. Future studies could compare salient and non-salient courses to examine whether repeated calibration is strongest in courses students perceive as especially relevant in their academic pathway. Finally, the sample experienced attrition across the first year. Although missingness was not or only weakly associated with performance and intentions to remain in the major, the sample became more selective over time. Students who experienced the strongest motivational declines or academic difficulties may have been less likely to participate in later surveys. We used the full information maximum likelihood approach to retain all available data when estimating our models, but future studies with intensive retention efforts would strengthen confidence in the generalizability of the identified trajectories.

Conclusion

Taken together, the findings suggest that motivational change during the first college year is best understood as repeated situated calibration with potentially accumulating consequences. Early declines in expectancy and utility value appear to be a regular part of students' adjustment to new course contexts, especially as they encounter course demands and performance feedback. Yet students' lower expectancy and utility beliefs in later quarters suggest that these adjustments may gradually reduce motivational resources across the first year. The clearest consequence was not actual major change or final course performance, but students' subjective commitment to remaining in the major. Thus, motivational change in the first college year is not necessarily a warning sign of immediate academic failure, but it may be an early indicator of students' developing sense of academic fit. The challenge for instructors and institutions is therefore not to eliminate all motivational decline, but to help students use early course experiences as constructive information, while maintaining confidence, perceived relevance, and a manageable sense of cost in their chosen academic pathway.

Implications for Supporting First-Year Students

The findings have implications for how instructors and institutions should interpret motivational declines during the first college year. Because some motivational change appears to reflect calibration, the goal should not be to prevent all declines in expectancies or utility values. Students need accurate information about course demands, their own performance, and the fit between their goals and academic pathways. Inflated confidence or unrealistic expectations may not serve students well in the long run because they lead to wasted effort and failure. However, calibration becomes concerning when repeated negative experiences reduce students' confidence, undermine perceived utility, or weaken their commitment to the major across multiple courses. Supporting first-year students, therefore, requires helping them interpret early difficulty in adaptive ways. Early performance feedback may be especially important. If midterm grades are a central moment for recalibrating motivation, then students may benefit from feedback that is not only evaluative but also diagnostic and actionable. Instructors can help students understand what a grade means, which strategies are likely to improve future performance, and how early difficulty fits within normal learning processes. Such feedback may prevent students from interpreting early struggles as evidence that they cannot succeed in the field.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although the longitudinal design with repeated assessments across three quarters is a major strength, the study followed students only through the first year of college. Future research should examine whether repeated within-course calibration continues in the second year or whether motivational beliefs stabilize after students gain more experience with college-level work and their major.
