How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Trans people experience elevated levels of mental health problems compared to the general population. Trans-specific psychosocial stressors and resilience factors have been shown to account for part of the variance in mental health. However, most studies have included only a limited subset of relevant stressors and resilience factors, leaving unclear both the amount of variance that can be explained by a comprehensive predictor set and which individual stressors and resilience factors are most informative for mental health outcomes.

Methods: A total of 447 adult trans individuals participated in a cross-sectional online study and completed measures assessing trans-specific stressors and resilience factors, as well as well-being, social functioning, and overall mental health problems. Prediction models were estimated using random forests within a nested cross-validation framework. Shapley additive explanations were applied to identify the most influential predictors for each mental health outcome.

Results: Prediction models based on psychosocial stressors and resilience factors, and demographic data achieved R² = 26% for well-being, R² = 32% for social functioning, and R² = 35% for overall mental health problems. Gender dysphoria, rumination about others' perception, and internalized transnegativity consistently ranked among the five most important predictors across all models.

Discussion: Trans-specific stressors and resilience factors explain a substantial proportion of variance in mental health outcomes. Nevertheless, incorporating additional predictors, including general risk and protective factors for mental health, may further enhance predictive accuracy and thereby improve mental health prediction in this population.

1. Introduction

Reviews and empirical studies consistently show that trans people experience higher rates of mental health problems compared to the general population and cisgender people (Hanna et al., 2019; Pinna et al., 2022). Given these disparities, developing accurate prediction models of mental health outcomes in trans people may be an important step toward more personalized mental health care in this population. To develop these models, it is first necessary to identify variables that are associated with mental health outcomes and may therefore serve as informative predictors.

One group of variables that may be particularly relevant for predicting mental health in trans populations are trans-specific psychosocial stressors and resilience factors. These transspecific factors are most often embedded in the gender minority stress model (Hendricks & Testa, 2012), which posits that trans people's mental health is influenced by the interplay of external stressors (e.g., discrimination), internal stressors (e.g., internalized transnegativity), and available resilience factors (e.g., community connectedness). Empirical work adopting this perspective has demonstrated that these factors account for a meaningful proportion of the variance in mental health outcomes, suggesting that they may also represent promising candidates for mental health prediction models.

For instance, Bariola et al. (2015) investigated the impact of LGBTQ+ contact, social support, and experiences of victimization on psychological distress, which together explained 19% of the variance. Other studies have focused more explicitly on minority stressors, such as negative expectations, internalized transnegativity, community connectedness, and factors such as gender identity appearance congruence, with binary outcomes of fulfilling criteria for depression or generalized anxiety disorder (Chodzen et al., 2019). Chodzen et al. (2019) reported that these predictors explained 29% of the variance in depression, correctly classifying 75% of participants, and 13% of the variance in generalized anxiety disorder, with 65% correct classification. Similarly, Trujillo et al. (2017) found that experiences of harassment, rejection, and discrimination explained 26% of the variance in suicidal ideation and 19% in affective and anxiety symptoms. In another study, Hunter et al. (2021) showed that gender dysphoria, proximal and distal stressors, and resilience factors captured by the Gender Minority Stress and Resilience Measure (GMSR; Testa et al., 2015), as well as heteronormative attitudes and beliefs explained 30% of the variance in depression, 25% in anxiety, and 27% in well-being.

However, while the regression analyses reported in these studies provide insight into associations between risk and resilience factors and mental health outcomes, the proportion of explained variance by these factors is always estimated and evaluated on the full sample. This full-sample estimation reflects the model fit on the training data and leaves open the question whether comparable performance of the calculated model would be achieved for new unseen people (James et al., 2021). Possibly, the full sample estimation of variance explained may display an overestimation of the variance explained of these factors for new people. To obtain a more realistic estimate of the proportion of variance in mental health explained by risk and resilience factors, approaches that evaluate out-of-sample performance such as crossvalidation may be useful (James et al., 2021). Cross-validation repeatedly partitions the data training sets, in which the model is estimated, and test sets, in which model performance is evaluated separately, thereby providing an estimate of predictive performance in unseen data (James et al., 2021). Taken together, these considerations indicate that estimates of explained variance calculated on the full sample should be interpreted with caution, particularly when the primary interest lies in the predictive performance of models for new individuals, and should, where possible, be evaluated using out-of-sample approaches.

Despite these methodological considerations, the existing literature consistently demonstrates that psychosocial stressors account for a substantial proportion of variance in mental health outcomes. While Hunter et al. (2021) included a broad range of both stressors and resilience factors, most studies to date have focused on a relatively narrow set. Replicating and extending the estimation of explained variance using more comprehensive sets of predictors may therefore provide deeper insight to which extent the specific stressors shape mental health, simultaneously taking other relevant stressors into account. Furthermore, based on the existing literature, it remains unclear which specific stressors and resilience factors exert the strongest influence on mental health outcomes.

A thorough investigation of multiple risk-/resilience-factors at once requires openness to the possibility that these predictors may exhibit non-linear relationships with mental health outcomes and may interact with each other in complex ways. Because we do not yet know the exact form of these relationships, statistical approaches are needed that can capture complex interactions and non-linear effects without requiring pre-specification. Machine learning methods such as random forests are well-suited to this task, as they can model complex interactions and nonlinear associations without pre-specification (Strobl et al., 2009).

Using this analytical machine learning approach, this study examines how much variance psychosocial stressors and resilience factors specific to trans people explain in mental health outcomes and which variables exert the strongest influence on mental health outcomes. We reanalyzed data from the validation study of the A-JOURNEY questionnaire (Strakeljahn et al., 2025), constructing prediction models based on these stressors and resilience factors, as well as on demographic data collected in the study. First, we assessed the variance explained (A) by demographic variables alone, (B) by the A-JOURNEY questionnaire alone, and (C) by the combined set of variables. We hypothesized that the combined set of predictors increases the explained variance. We then explored variable importance within the prediction models, i.e. which individual variables contributed the most to accurate predictions of mental health outcomes within the previously derived optimal prediction model. Additionally, to facilitate comparability with prior research that has predominantly relied on regression approaches and to examine whether full-sample regression models yield different estimates of explained variance than cross-validated models, we estimated regression models using the same predictors as in the machine learning analyses and report these alongside the cross-validated results.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Design and Procedure

This online study recruited participants via social media (e.g., Reddit and Instagram) and via leaflets handed out in universities and LGBTQ+ associations in Germany. Prior to participation, participants provided informed consent. Then participants filled out a set of questionnaires assessing demographic information, mental well-being, stress, depression, anxiety, social functioning, and trans-related stressors/resilience factors. At the end of the study participants could decide if they would be willing to fill out an additional, optional, questionnaire assessing suicidality. The local ethics committee of the University of Wuppertal approved this study.

2.2 Sample

To be included participants in this study needed to be at least 18 years of age and experience a discrepancy between their assigned sex at birth and gender identity. Of 661 participants who provided informed consent 448 participants completed the study and 417 participants additionally the optional part about suicidality. One participant who identified as cisgender was excluded. One participant who identified as cisgender but had previously detransitioned was retained in the sample, resulting in 447 participants included in our analytical sample. Table 1 depicts the demographic data of the whole sample (n=447).

2.3 Materials

2.3.1 Outcome Measures

We utilized either one of the following measures of mental health as the outcome variable for our prediction models:

We assessed current well-being (over the last two weeks) with the 14-item Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (WEMWBS; Tennant et al., 2007). Items were rated on fivepoint Likert scales (1 = none of the time to5 = all of the time). We calculated a sum score for our analyses.

We utilized the Social Functioning Questionnaire (SFQ; Tyrer et al., 2005) to assess social functioning during the last two weeks across eight items on item-specific four-point Likert scales. We calculated a sum score for our analyses. Lower values in this scale indicate higher social functioning.

As an indicator of overall mental health problems, participants answered the German version of the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scales (DASS-21, Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995; Nilges & Essau, 2015). The DASS-21 assesses levels of depression, anxiety, and stress during the last two weeks with 7 items, respectively, on a four-point Likert scale (0 = never to3 = always).

We calculated the DASS-21 total sum score as our primary measure of mental health problems and depression-, anxiety-, and stress-subscale scores for supplementary analyses.

2.3.2 Predictors

To assess psychosocial stressors and resilience factors, we utilized the A-JOURNEY questionnaire (Strakeljahn et al., 2025). The A-JOURNEY questionnaire assesses the following nine stressors and four resilience factors: violence (e.g., "I was physically attacked because I am trans/non-binary."), non-affirmative experiences (e.g., "People address me with the wrong The following demographic variables were included in some of our prediction models: age, sex assigned at birth, gender identity, highest educational qualification, highest professional qualification, and current employment status. Additionally, we included whether participants already received gender affirming medical care with participants indicating what type of medical care they received (e.g., hormone therapy). Further, we included whether participants planned to receive additional gender-affirming medical care in the future, or, if they had not received any such care, whether they would like to do so.

2.4 Strategy of Data Analysis

We used R 4.4.2 for descriptive analyses and data preprocessing and Python 3.13 for the machine learning analyses. We used random forests for our machine learning analyses to predict either well-being, social functioning, or overall mental health problems. Additionally, we calculated prediction models for the subscales depression, anxiety, and stress of the DASS-21 and appended the results in the supplement. As predictors, we utilized either solely the A-JOURNEY questionnaire subscales, solely demographic data and information on gender affirming medical treatment, or utilized both. Missing data were imputed using K-Nearest Neighbors (non-weighted mean of the three nearest neighbors). Categorical variables were one-hot encoded for our machine learning analyses.

Mirroring this approach, we additionally calculated multiple regression models on the full dataset (without cross-validation) using the same predictor and outcome variables as in the machine learning analyses. For the regression analyses, categorical variables were dummy coded. Due to singularities in the design matrix, some categories of the categorical variables had to be excluded. For a complete list of variables used in each prediction model and the amount of missing data per variable, see Table S1.

For the machine learning analyses, hyperparameter tuning and model evaluation were conducted using nested cross-validation with five outer and five inner folds. We always calculated 200 decision trees but varied the maximum depth of decision trees (2-30 in increments of one), and the maximum number of variables used per split (5-80% in increments of five percent). We tested all hyperparameter configurations across our hyperparameter space (grid search). Model performance was evaluated on the outer test folds using R².

During nested cross-validation, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP; Lundberg & Lee, 2017) to the random forest models trained on each outer training fold using the treeshap function (Lundberg et al., 2020). The SHAP explainer model was then applied to the corresponding outer fold test sets to estimate their SHAP values. For the SHAP analyses, the random forest prediction models with the full predictor set (A-JOURNEY questionnaire and demographic data) were used.

All R² values reported in this study are unadjusted.

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Variance explained of random forests and regression analyses

Using only demographic data for our random forest yielded an explained variance (R²) of 5%-10%, with mental health problems achieving the highest R² of 10%. Using only the A-JOURNEY questionnaire yielded an R² of 25%-33%, again with mental health problems achieving the highest R² of 33%. Utilizing both the A-JOURNEY questionnaire and demographic data, random forests yielded R² = 26% for the prediction of well-being, R² = 32% for social functioning, and R² = 35% for mental health problems. Table 2 depicts the nested crossvalidation results of each calculated prediction model. Table S2 depicts the nested crossvalidation results for the subscales of the DASS-21.

The regression analyses calculated on the whole sample achieved higher R² values across all predictor sets and mental health outcomes compared to the random forest analyses.

Using only demographic data resulted in R² values of 17%-21%. Using only the A-JOURNEY questionnaire as a predictor set yielded R² values of 34%-40%. Utilizing both predictor sets (A-JOURNEY questionnaire and demographic data) resulted in R² values of 43%-49%. The highest explained variance across all models was achieved for the prediction of mental health problems using both predictor sets, with an R² of 49%. Table 2 summarizes the results of the regression models, and Table S2 provides the corresponding results for the DASS-21 subscales.

3.2 Variable importance

SHAP-based variable importance analyses, on the random forest analyses with the full predictor set, consistently identified internalized transnegativity, gender dysphoria, and rumination about others' perception among the five most influential predictors across models predicting well-being, social functioning, and overall mental health problems. Moreover, most models highlighted body dysphoria related to body hair (except for overall mental health problems) and anticipated discrimination (except for well-being) as top predictors. 4. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the variance explained by trans-specific psychosocial stressors and resilience factors for mental health outcomes in a cross-sectional online study, and to identify which of these factors exert the strongest influence on mental health outcomes.

We saw that utilizing random forests within nested cross-validation demographic data and the psychosocial stressors and resilience factors assessed in the A-JOURNEY questionnaire yielded up to 35% explained variance for mental health problems, 32% for social functioning, and 26% for mental well-being. While adding demographic variables to trans-specific psychosocial stressors and resilience factors resulted in only a small increase in explained variance (ΔR² = 1-2%), adding psychosocial stressors and resilience factors to demographic variables yielded substantial gains in explained variance (ΔR² = 21-25%), highlighting the predictive value of factors assessed in the A-JOURNEY questionnaire.

A key difference between the present study and much of the existing literature concerns the analytical approach used to estimate explained variance. Many previous studies relied on regression models and reported R² values calculated on the same sample used to fit the model (e.g., Brennan et al., 2017; Hunter et al., 2021; Jäggi et al., 2018). To facilitate comparison with previous research, we additionally estimated regression models using the same predictors as in the machine learning analyses. These analyses yielded up to 49% explained variance for mental health problems, 43% for social functioning, 43% for mental well-being, and up to 48% for depressive symptoms. Consequently, the full sample estimation of variance explained using regression analyses yielded an increased R² of up to ΔR² = 11-17%. These findings illustrate that regression analyses can yield higher explained variance estimates when evaluated on the same sample used for model fitting, whereas calculating cross-validated random forest models in the current study resulted in lower explained variance, reflecting a more conservative estimation of model performance (for a general introduction to cross-validation, see James et al., 2021).

Despite the reduction in variance explained for the nested cross-validation estimation using random forests, the random forest models retained considerable explanatory power, supporting the relevance of trans-specific stressors and resilience factors in understanding mental health outcomes.

When comparing the results of our prediction models predicting overall mental health with those using only a limited selection of stressors or resilience factors, our prediction models, which included a more comprehensive array of predictors, achieved higher explained variance (random forest: R² = 35%; regression analysis: R² = 49%). For instance, Trujillo et al. (2017) included experiences of harassment, rejection, and discrimination as predictors, explaining 19% of the variance in affective and anxiety symptoms, while Bariola et al. (2015) used LGBTQ+ contact, social support, and experiences of victimization as predictors, explaining 19% of the variance in psychological distress.

While the above studies relied on isolated psychosocial predictors, other work has used more comprehensive theoretical frameworks to increase predictive performance. Specifically, studies based on the full GMSR have reported higher explained variance. Brennan et al. (2017) included age, hormone replacement therapy status, and the higher-order GMSR factors (proximal stressors, distal stressors, and resilience) in their regression model predicting depression, achieving R² = 30%. Similarly, Hunter et al. (2021) reported R² = 30% when predicting depression using the higher-order GMSR factors (proximal stressors, distal stressors, resilience), along with additional predictors such as age, gender dysphoria, sexuality, sex assigned at birth, and heteronormative attitudes and beliefs.

Extending beyond studies that used only higher-order GMSR factors, research incorporating lower-order GMSR components has achieved even higher explained variance.

When predicting overall mental health problems, Helsen et al. (2022) achieved R² = 38% using selected GMSR dimensions (internalized transnegativity, expectations of rejection, concealment, community connectedness) and gender nonconformity. The highest explained variance was reported by Jäggi et al. (2018), who achieved R² = 51% in predicting depressive symptoms by including all lower-order GMSR factors alongside demographic variables (age, gender identity, and occupational status). Taken together, these findings suggest that models including the full spectrum of lower-order GMSR factors outperform those restricted to higherorder factors or small subsets of predictors.

Comparing the present findings of this study with those of Jäggi et al. (2018), whose study reported the highest proportion of explained variance to date using all lower-order GMSR factors, provides additional context. Using a comparable regression-based analytical approach, models in the present study yielded R² = 48% for depressive symptoms, closely matching the level of explained variance reported by Jäggi et al. (2018). However, when predictive performance was evaluated out of sample using random forests, explained variance decreased substantially by 15 percentage points (R² = 33%). This discrepancy illustrates that similar analytical approaches can indeed produce comparable levels of explained variance when a comprehensive set of predictors is used but these estimates may reflect overfitted performance that relies on full-sample training data. The substantially lower out-of-sample performance observed in the random forest models highlights the extent to which full-sample regression estimates may overstate predictive performance.

At the same time, these findings raise an important issue for efforts to model and track mental health using trans-related risk and resilience factors. Although the currently identified factors appear to be meaningfully associated with mental health outcomes in cross-sectional analyses, a considerable proportion of variance remains unexplained. As a result, current models may still fall short of providing sufficiently precise estimates of individual mental health outcomes.

One possible strategy to enhance predictive performance in future work is to incorporate additional predictors beyond trans-specific stressors and resilience factors. In particular, models could include transdiagnostic factors of mental health, such as socioeconomic status (Peverill et al., 2021), or overall adverse life events irrespective of gender identity (Abate et al., 2024). Including a broader set of non-trans-specific stressors and resilience factors may help to clarify the incremental predictive value of these factors for mental health, as well as whether general factors or trans-specific stressors and resilience factors exert greater influence on trans people's mental health.

Another possible direction is to revisit and further develop theoretical frameworks of trans-specific stressors and resilience factors. Existing theoretical models, such as the gender minority stress model (Hendricks & Testa, 2012), have already been subject to proposed refinements. For example, some researchers have suggested incorporating gender dysphoria as a proximal stressor within the framework (Galupo et al., 2020; Lindley & Galupo, 2020) or conceptualizing gender dysphoria into intra-and interpersonal dimensions (Brecht et al., 2024; Galupo et al., 2020; Toomey, 2021). Additionally, the framework lacks resilience factors such as gender euphoria. Re-examining trans-specific stressors and resilience factors and expanding current theoretical models to account for additional factors may therefore help identify further influences on mental health outcomes in this population and ultimately improve predictive models.

Beyond overall predictive performance, the present study also aimed to examine the relative importance of trans-specific stressors and resilience factors for mental health outcomes. Across all calculated random forests in our main analyses, gender dysphoria, rumination about others' perception, and internalized transnegativity consistently emerged among the five most influential predictors. The strong influence of internalized transnegativity aligns with findings from a recent meta-analysis on gender minority stressors and mental health, which reported that internalized transnegativity showed descriptively the strongest associations with depression levels (Wilson et al., 2023). This suggests that internalized transnegativity may exert a strong influence on mental health, underscoring its relevance as a target for prevention and psychological intervention. Encouragingly, initial efforts to develop interventions addressing these internal stressors are underway. For example, a recent randomized controlled trial introduced an online psychological intervention specifically designed to reduce internalized transnegativity (Israel et al., 2021).

An important limitation of the present study is that we examined mental health outcomes cross-sectionally. Yet, perhaps of greatest relevance is understanding how changes in stressors and resilience factors affect subsequent mental health outcomes. Addressing this question requires longitudinal research that investigates how these relationships unfold over time and at which time scales changes in stressors and resilience factors exert effects on mental health.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that trans-specific stressors and resilience factors account for a substantial proportion of variance in mental health. In particular, gender dysphoria, internalized transnegativity, and rumination about others' perceptions appear to heavily influence cross-sectional mental health predictions. Still, prediction models for tracking mental health in trans people need to be further improved before they can be usefully implemented in precision mental health settings.
