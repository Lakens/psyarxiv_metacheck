Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Abstract

Broadly defined, polysubstance use refers to the consumption of two or more psychoactive substances within a short period of time. In addition to the desired effects, such as intensifying or prolonging the high or counteracting unpleasant effects, polysubstance use also entails risks. However, much of the existing research on psychoactive substance use continues to focus on single-substance use, leaving the heterogeneity of polysubstance use comparatively less understood. A differentiated understanding of these patterns is therefore needed to inform user-centered approaches to health promotion and safer use. In an online cross-sectional study, people who consume psychoactive substances in recreational settings (N= 649; Mage= 32.09) provided information on sociodemographic characteristics and substance use patterns, with a particular focus on polysubstance use. Hierarchical cluster analysis identified distinct patterns of polysubstance use, followed by chi-square tests to examine group differences. Three distinct clusters (Cluster 1: n= 182; Cluster 2: n= 129; Cluster 3: n= 137) were identified, differing in polysubstance use patterns, underlying motivations, and engagement in substance use management practices. The findings highlight that people who engage in polysubstance use constitute a heterogeneous population with distinct patterns of use and related behaviors. Recognizing this heterogeneity should help to support the development of more targeted, user-centered approaches for safer use and health promotion.

1 Introduction

So-called psychoactive substance use in recreational settings, defined as the controlled and planned consumption of legal and illegal psychoactive substances during leisure time and in specific social situations (Pol and Hawks, 1992), does not necessarily imply problematic use or a substance use disorder. Evidence suggests that a substantial proportion of substance use remains episodic or non-problematic and occurs alongside otherwise socially integrated lifestyles, highlighting the importance of distinguishing recreational patterns of use from clinical populations with substance use disorders (Elmer et al., 2024; Grabenhofer et al., 2018; Karden et al., 2025; Schlag, 2020; Stetina et al., 2008). At the same time, recreational patterns of substance use remain relevant from a public health and clinical perspective, particularly in the context of polysubstance use. The risks associated with the consumption of psychoactive substances in leisure settings are often due to the predominantly illegal status of psychoactive substances. Possession and use may lead to stigmatization, pathologization, and legal consequences, prohibition creates uncertainty about substance origin and composition, increasing the risk of unintended overdoses, unexpected substances, and other health-related harms (EMCDDA, 2021).

A widely observed practice of substance use is polysubstance use (PSU), which is increasingly considered a public health issue, as the simultaneous use of different substances may be associated with an increased risk of overdose, substance use disorder, and adverse drug interactions (Boileau-Falardeau et al., 2022; Crummy, 2020; EMCDDA, 2021; Fleury et al., 2026; Grov et al., 2009; LeComte et al., 2025; Reichenberger et al., 2026; Rodzlan Hasani et al., 2023; Rough and Nader, 2026). Importantly, the specific substances combined may be associated with distinct health risks and pharmacological interactions. For example, combining opioids and benzodiazepines increases the risk of respiratory depression (Sun et al., 2017), while the combined use of cocaine and alcohol is associated with increased cardiotoxicity compared to either substance alone (Crummy et al., 2020). Furthermore, the combined use of alcohol, cannabis, and tobacco has been associated with poorer sleep outcomes (Reichenberger et al., 2026), whereas combining stimulants with other substances may exacerbate cardiovascular, neurological, and mental health risks (Rough and Nader, 2026). Non-medical use of prescription medications is also relevant in the context of PSU, with previous studies identifying distinct consumption patterns involving combinations with cannabis, alcohol, and other prescription medications (Rockhill et al., 2025; Steinhoff et al., 2022). Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering the diversity of substance combinations and their associated risks when examining PSU and developing targeted harm reduction strategies.

Various studies of people who use psychoactive substances in recreational settings have reported PSU prevalence rates of 70-94%, depending on the study design, the precise definition of PSU, and substance groups examined (Schauer et al., 2015; Schlienz and Lee, 2018; Stetina et al., 2008; Uhl and Stetina, 2025). Young adulthood represents a particularly relevant developmental period for substance use and PSU. This stage of life is often characterized by increased autonomy, identity exploration, and experimentation with different behaviors, including substance use (Arnett, 2000). Substance use also tends to peak during young adulthood, although peak ages vary across substance classes (Schulenberg et al., 2020; Stevens et al., 2022). Previous research has shown that PSU increases from adolescence into early adulthood (Steinhoff et al., 2022), with a recent study by Brennan et al. (2025) reporting that nearly one in four 20-year-olds in Ireland engaged in PSU.

In broad terms, PSU refers to the use of two or more psychoactive substances.

However, PSU is not uniformly defined, as reflected by the variety of terms used in the literature, including "polydrug use", "co-use", "simultaneous use", "concurrent use", or "mixed use". Studies assessing the use of different psychoactive substances over broader reference periods, such as the past 12 months, do not necessarily capture whether these substances were consumed simultaneously or whether their effects overlapped. The different concepts used to describe PSU primarily differ in terms of the timing of substance use (Bunting et al., 2024; EMCDDA, 2021; Grov et al., 2009). Current research commonly distinguishes between simultaneous and concurrent use. Simultaneous use refers to the use of two or more psychoactive substances at the same time or within a short period during the same occasion, such that their effects overlap (Baggio et al., 2014; Foxx et al., 2022; Parthasarathy et al., 2025; Quek et al., 2013). In contrast, concurrent use refers to the use of multiple substances within a broader specified reference period (Fleury et al., 2026; Parthasarathy et al., 2025), such as the past 30 days (Foxx et al., 2022) or 12 months (Baggio et al., 2014; Quek et al., 2013), without necessarily involving overlapping effects. The timing and sequence of PSU may be closely related to the intended effects, illustrating the close connection between PSU (what is taken and when) and the underlying motives (why it is taken). The variety of terms and inconsistent definitions of PSU have important implications for empirical research, as they limit comparability across studies. Moreover, conceptual ambiguity may complicate the development and implementation of support strategies and health promotion measures in general by making it less clear "what to target" (e.g., Bunting et al., 2024). In the present study, PSU is defined as simultaneous use, referring to the use of two or more psychoactive substances at the same time or within a short period during a particular occasion such that their effects overlap. This definition is also consistent with Rough and Nader (2026), who argue that PSU should capture temporally proximal patterns of substance use rather than the use of different substances across periods of several weeks or months.

Previous research in the field of PSU has identified different underlying motives, such as enhancing desired effects, counteracting unwanted effects of other substances, prolonging the high, or combining different substance effects (Boileau-Falardeau et al., 2022; EMCDDA, 2009; Grov et al., 2009; Kataja et al., 2017). These motives may be closely related to the specific substances combined and their intended effects (Grov et al., 2009), as well as to whether substances are used simultaneously or sequentially (Boileau-Falardeau et al., 2022). Furthermore, Greń et al. (2024) demonstrated that individuals with different patterns and intensities of PSU also differed in their consumption motives, consumption settings, and substance-related outcomes. While the literature describes common motives, the specific combination and importance of these motives remain highly individual. Consequently, a holistic understanding of PSU requires consideration not only of the substances used in combination and their temporal sequence, but also the motivations underlying the combined use (Bunting et al., 2024; Fleury et al., 2026; Grov et al., 2009).

Although research on psychoactive substance use has expanded considerably, many studies continue to focus on single-substance use, with PSU often treated as an exclusion criterion (Crummy et al., 2020; Fleury et al., 2026; Rodzlan Hasani et al., 2023). Even when PSU is explicitly examined, research has predominantly focused on individuals with substance use disorders, people who co-use opioids, or people who inject drugs (Crummy et al., 2020; Ellis et al., 2023; Grov et al., 2009). In addition, studies have considered a range of factors associated with PSU, with gender, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, age, stress, peer or parental substance use, risky behaviors, and experiences of physical or sexual assault among the most frequently examined covariates (Skandan et al., 2025). Previous studies have employed person-centered approaches to identify distinct patterns of PSU. Several of these studies have concentrated on substance use patterns during adolescence and their associations with developmental trajectories, risk factors, delinquency, or adverse health outcomes (Björklund et al., 2025; Kruckow et al., 2026; Steinhoff et al., 2022). Other studies have examined substance use patterns in relation to substance use disorders and prescription drug use (Rockhill et al., 2025), or investigated differences in consumption motives, selfregulation, protective behavioral strategies, and personality traits across substance use profiles (Greń et al., 2024). Furthermore, research specifically examining the combined use of substances has frequently focused on particular substance combinations, such as alcohol and cannabis (Lee et al., 2022; Tucker et al., 2023), alcohol, cannabis, and tobacco (Reichenberger et al., 2026; Schlienz and Lee, 2018), psychedelics combined with other substances (Lake and Lucas, 2024), or 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) combined with psilocybin or lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD; Zeifman et al., 2023).

Given the potential risks associated with PSU (Boileau-Falardeau et al., 2022; Crummy, 2020; EMCDDA, 2021; Fleury et al., 2026; LeComte et al., 2025; Reichenberger et al., 2026; Rodzlan Hasani et al., 2023; Rough and Nader, 2026), individuals may seek to balance the desired effects of substance use against its potential risks. Previous research suggests that people who use drugs employ different strategies to manage their consumption, depending on factors such as the substances used, frequency of use, and individual attitudes toward substance use (Kataja et al., 2017). Consumption-related behaviors such as drugchecking, self-defined consumption rules, and the use of professional support services may therefore provide an additional perspective on how individuals manage their PSU. For example, drug-checking services provide information about substance composition and may influence consumption decisions, particularly when unexpected substances are detected (Betzler et al., 2021; EMCDDA, 2021). Self-defined consumption rules may represent another form of consumption management, as previous research has emphasized the role of individual and social rules in regulating substance use (Zinberg, 1984; Schippers and Cramer, 2002). Professional support services may additionally offer counselling and advice on safer substance use (EMCDDA, 2021).

However, to our knowledge, comparatively less attention has been paid to identifying heterogeneous patterns of PSU in recreational settings by jointly considering substance combinations, motives for mixed use, and substance use management practices, such as drugchecking, professional support service use, and adherence to self-defined consumption rules.

Considering these different dimensions together may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of PSU. Such an approach may help inform tailored harm reduction strategies and drug literacy initiatives (Gerlach, 2004). This study therefore examines patterns of PSU among people who use psychoactive substances in recreational settings to identify distinct consumption types and contribute to a more nuanced understanding of this heterogeneous phenomenon.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Research design and implementation of the survey

The study was launched online via SoSci-Survey (Leiner, 2019) in April 2025 and is currently still ongoing. The data presented here reflect an initial interim dataset collected in November 2025. Ethics approval was provided by the Ethics Commission by the Faculties of Psychotherapy Science, of Psychology, of Law of the Sigmund Freud University Vienna (RDBAM8PQCA@L7O91286) on December 17, 2024.

In an exploratory open online cross-sectional study, people who use psychoactive substances in leisure settings were asked about their use of both legal and illegal psychoactive substances. Individuals who use psychoactive substances in recreational settings are often considered a hard-to-reach population in research. Owing to the predominantly prohibitive legal and social context surrounding the use of illicit substances, many individuals prefer to remain anonymous and avoid disclosing their substance use, contributing to their characterization as a hidden population. Current studies in this context rely on online surveys, as this form of questioning offers a high degree of anonymity and is therefore well suited to reaching this hidden population (Miller and Sønderlund, 2018; Stetina et al., 2008; Stetina and Kryspin-Exner, 2009). The survey was distributed in collaboration with partners from the fields of drug-checking, music and techno collectives, and scene-specific internet and social media platforms, who shared the study link and flyers (supplemental figure 1) with the community.

An online questionnaire specifically developed for the present study was used to collect data, which, in addition to sociodemographic data such as age, gender, or educational status, collected aspects of general consumption behavior like the 1-year prevalence or the use of drug-checking, as well as characteristics of PSU (for example, combined substances, frequency, motives). All items were developed and formulated by the research team, with their content informed by a broad review of relevant empirical studies and epidemiological prevalence reports (Infodrog Swiss Coordination and Expert Center for Addiction, 2023; European Union Drugs Agency, 2025; EMCDDA, 2021; Boileau-Falardeau et al., 2022; Greń et al., 2024; Hughes et al., 2019; Gabrovec et al., 2023; Kruger et al., 2023; National Harm Reduction Coalition, 2020). No items were directly adopted or adapted from existing standardized instruments. Following questionnaire development, a pretest was conducted to assess item clarity and technical functionality, including questionnaire display across different devices and the functioning of survey filters. Supplemental Table 2 provides an overview of the data collected, including the respective items and response options used in the survey.

Prior to participation, all participants were informed about the study aims and expected duration, as well as the procedures for data processing and storage. No personally identifiable information was collected as part of the survey. Data were stored anonymously in SoSci Survey (Leiner, 2019), processed in SPSS (IBM Corp., 2022, Armonk, NY, USA), and securely stored in encrypted form on an external storage device accessible only to authorized members of the research team. After the required retention period, all data will be securely deleted.

2.2 Research questions and hypotheses

The aim of the study was to explore patterns of PSU and identify distinct types of consumption within this heterogeneous practice. Building on the research gap outlined in the Introduction, particularly the limited evidence on PSU patterns jointly considering substance combinations, motives for mixed use, and substance use management practices in recreational settings, an exploratory approach was adopted. Accordingly, no specific a priori hypotheses regarding the number or characteristics of the resulting clusters were formulated.

Research question: What distinct consumption types can be identified within polysubstance use?

2.3 Inclusion and exclusion criteria of the test subjects

Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) who reported previous or current use of psychoactive substances in recreational settings. To facilitate international participation, the questionnaire was provided in both German and English. No financial or other incentives were offered for participation. Before taking part in the study, all participants had to actively consent to participate, to data protection, and to the use of their anonymized data for scientific research and publication purposes.

2.4 Methods of statistical data analysis

Statistical calculations were performed using the SPSS 29 statistics program (IBM Corp., 2022, Armonk, NY, USA). All participants with valid data for the respective variables were included in the analyses whenever possible. Missing responses were coded as missing values in SPSS (Leiner, 2019) and reported alongside the corresponding results. No imputation of missing data was performed. Frequency analyses for descriptive sample descriptions were presented based on mean values (M) and standard deviations (SD). Frequency tables and histograms were used for graphical illustration. Since rounding may occur during descriptive calculations using the statistics program, the percentages may deviate slightly from 100% (Bortz and Schuster, 2010).

To reflect the diversity of PSU, questions were asked not only about the existence of this consumption practice and the combined substances, but also about its frequency. As Fleury et al. (2026), like Bunting and colleagues (2024), emphasize, assessing the three dimensions of (1) the number and type of substances combined, (2) the timing of substance use, and (3) the intention underlying their use is essential for a comprehensive assessment of PSU. Frequency was assessed using a scale of nine frequency categories (ranging from once a year to several times a day). To represent the frequency of PSU, the predefined response categories were grouped into three frequency levels: (1) low-frequency PSU (ranging from once a year to several times within six months), (2) moderate-frequency PSU (ranging from once a month to once a week), and (3) high-frequency PSU (ranging from several times a week to several times a day) (supplemental table 2).

Because substance combinations and underlying motives exhibit a high degree of individuality, both were assessed using open-ended questions. Open-ended responses allowed participants to report their individual substance combinations and subjective motivations without being restricted to predefined response categories. These questions provide particularly informative added value, especially when it comes to exploratory topics or the determination of cognitions and attitudes and were therefore considered the most appropriate approach (Hansen and Świderska, 2024; Möhring and Schlütz, 2019).

To analyze substance combinations, the reported substances were grouped into broader categories, as the large number of individual combinations (N = 556) resulted in frequencies that were too small for statistical analysis. First, each reported substance was classified according to its predominant effects as a stimulant (upper; e.g., amphetamine, cocaine), depressant (downer; e.g., benzodiazepines, cannabidiol [CBD]), or hallucinogen/dissociative (e.g., tetrahydrocannabinol [THC], ketamine). The complete classification of all reported substances into these three categories is provided in Supplemental Table 3. However, the complexity of such a classification must be acknowledged, as the predominant effects of individual substances may vary depending on factors such as dosage (e.g., gamma-hydroxybutyrate [GHB] may produce euphoric effects at lower doses and predominantly depressant effects at higher doses). Nevertheless, substances were classified according to their predominant pharmacological effects to provide a consistent framework for grouping the large variety of reported combinations and enable subsequent statistical analyses. Based on these classifications, the reported substance combinations were subsequently assigned to one of six combination groups (upper + upper, downer + downer, hallucinogens/dissociatives + hallucinogens/dissociatives, upper + downer, upper + hallucinogens/dissociative, downer + hallucinogens/dissociatives).

To analyse the motives behind PSU, the motivation categories were developed based on participants' open-ended responses and subsequently grouped into broader conceptual categories. Although previous studies have proposed more differentiated motivational frameworks (Boileau-Falardeau, 2022; EMCDDA, 2009; Fleury et al., 2026; Grov et al., 2009; Kataja et al., 2017; Reichenberger et al., 2026), Rough and Nader (2026) describe positive and negative reinforcement as key mechanisms underlying PSU, whereby substances may be combined either to enhance desired effects or to reduce unpleasant effects associated with another substance. Furthermore, the present study used broader motivational categories to enable systematic classification of open-ended responses and ensure sufficient frequencies for subsequent statistical analyses. Two motive classes were formed based on current research studies and theoretical considerations: (1) counteract effects (e.g., to promote sleep; to mitigate undesirable/unpleasant effects of other substances), (2) enhance (e.g., to intensify effect profiles or achieve new effects; to prolong the high).

Analysis of the responses revealed two notable features. Firstly, some participants reported "unintentional" or "inevitable" PSU. This was the case when the person was either receiving pharmacological treatment (e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder [ADHD], opioid painkillers), experiencing substance dependence (mostly nicotine), or consuming substances as part of the same administration practice (such as smoking cannabis together with tobacco). Given that PSU involving nicotine, alcohol, or cannabis is often overlooked or excluded from research despite its high prevalence and potentially enhancing effects, this motive warrants particular consideration (Bunting et al., 2024; Connor et al., 2023; Fleury et al., 2026; Goodhines et al., 2025; Graupensperger et al., 2024; Reichenberger et al., 2026).

Since mixed consumption was "inevitable" in these cases, the category of structurally induced PSU was introduced. In contrast, the analysis of consumption motives showed that many participants stated they engaged in PSU "without any particular reason" and "without thinking". From this, the consumption motivation of situational PSU without an explicit motive was derived.

To identify distinct PSU groups, we performed a hierarchical cluster analysis. This exploratory approach was chosen because it groups individuals with similar patterns across multiple characteristics without requiring the number or structure of clusters to be specified a priori. Ward's method was applied to obtain relatively homogeneous clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance, using squared Euclidean distance as the distance measure (Fromm, 2010; Hair et al., 2019). Variables used to form clusters included polysubstance use (yes/no), the substance groups combined, motives for mixed use, and behavioral factors, including drug-checking (yes/no), use of professional support services (yes/no), and adherence to selfdefined consumption rules. To identify group differences, the resulting clusters were tested for statistical significance (p< .05) using chi-square tests, including post-hoc comparisons (Bortz and Schuster, 2010).

3 Results

3.1 Sample description and descriptive results

At the first data cut-off in November 2025, the study included 649 participants, with a mean age of 32.09 years (SD= 10.34; range= 18-74). Among participants with available age data (n = 582), 49.0% were aged 18-29 years, 37.5% were aged 30-44 years, and 13.6% 45 years and older. Cis women represented the largest gender group (52.5%), followed by cis men (38.2%).

The sample covered 22 countries, although participation was concentrated in Austria (45.5%), Germany (22%), and Switzerland (6.8%). Heterosexual orientation was most frequently reported (63%), while bisexual (13.7%) and pansexual (10%) orientations represented the next largest groups. Regarding marital status, 42.8% of participants were single, 35.6% were in a relationship, and 13.7% were married. Approximately one in five participants reported having children (19.3%). Living arrangements were diverse, with living with a partner (27.9%) and living alone (27.6%) being the most common. More than half of the participants held a university or college degree (51.8%), while 20.3% reported a high school diploma as their highest educational qualification. A complete description of the sample is provided in table 1.

Past-year substance use covered a broad range of psychoactive substances. Alcohol (85.4%), tobacco (61.6%), and THC (56.4%) were reported most frequently, followed by CBD (37.8%), MDMA (34.2%), cocaine (31%), and amphetamine (24%). Several other substances were reported at lower prevalence rates, illustrating the diversity of substances used within the sample. Prevalence rates for all assessed substances are presented in figure 1.

Participants were asked about 13 personal consumption rules or rituals, which were grouped into three broader categories due to low frequencies of individual rules: (1) drugrelated, (2) set-related, and (3) setting-related rules (supplementary table 2). Overall, adherence was high across all three categories, with set-related rules reported most frequently (60.6%), followed by setting-related (57.3%) and drug-related rules (54.2%).

The use of drug-checking services was reported by 38.5% of participants (30.2% missing), whereas 10.2% reported having used professional support services related to their substance use (22.3% missing) (table 2).

3.1.1 Analysis of polysubstance use

A total of 404 participants (62.2%) reported having used two or more substances simultaneously or in quick succession. Among participants with available data on PSU frequency, 59.5% reported low-frequency PSU, 28.8% moderate-frequency PSU, and 11.7%

high-frequency PSU (table 2).

To characterize patterns of PSU, open-ended responses on substance combinations were assigned to broader substance classes. Across 556 reported combinations, upper + upper (n= 230) and upper + hallucinogen/dissociative (n= 222) were most frequently reported, followed by hallucinogen/dissociative + hallucinogen/dissociative combinations (n= 67).

Other combinations occurred considerably less frequently (table 2).

To examine motives for PSU, open-ended responses were assigned to four motive categories as described in section 2.5. Across 446 responses, enhancement motives were most frequently reported (n= 191), followed by situational PSU without an explicit motive (n= 128), counteracting effects (n= 90), and structurally induced PSU (n= 37) (table 2).

3.2 Inferential statistical analyses

After performing a hierarchical cluster analysis, a 3-cluster solution proved to be the most plausible. The substance combination groups downer + downer, downer + hallucinogens/dissociatives, and upper + downer were excluded from the chi-square tests due to expected cell frequencies (n≤ 5).

3.2.1 Cluster 1 (n= 182)

Cluster 1 was the largest cluster with 182 participants, an average age of 32.74 years (SD= 10.31, min.= 18, max.= 68) and with slightly more cis men (n= 86; 47.3%) than cis women (n= 78;42.9 %). Then followed by 5.5% (n= 10) non-binary persons, 1.6% (n= 3) genderfluid persons, 1.1% (n= 2) trans*women, and 1.1% (n= 2) other without further specification (missing answers: n= 1; 0.5%). All participants from cluster 1 (n= 182; 58.3%) reported PSU, in which all substance combinations [upper + upper (n= 97; 49.5%), hallucinogens/dissociatives + hallucinogens/dissociatives (n= 50; 89.3%), uppers + hallucinogens/dissociatives (n= 131; 68.6%)] were consumed significantly more frequently than expected. The motives counteract effects (n= 78; 94%) and enhance (n= 148; 87.6%) were mentioned significantly more often. Situational PSU without an explicit motive (n= 17; 16.2%) occurred significantly less often. People in cluster 1 made above-average use of professional support services (n= 35; 63.6%) and drug-checking (n= 139; 56.5%).

Compliance with consumption rules regarding drug (n= 152; 44.3%) and set (n= 171; 44.6%) was significantly more frequent (table 3).

3.2.2 Cluster 2 (n= 129)

Cluster 2 was the smallest cluster with n= 129. The participants were 31.10 years old on average (SD= 8.54, min.= 18, max.= 58), with the most cis women (n= 74; 57.4%), followed by 32.6% (n= 42) cis men, 8.5% (n= 11) non-binary individuals, and 0.8% (n= 1) other without further details (missing answers: n= 1; 0.8%). In cluster 2, all participants except one reported PSU (n= 128; 41%). The combination of the substance groups upper + upper (n= 99; 50.5%) occurred at a significantly higher frequency than expected. In contrast, the group hallucinogens/dissociatives + hallucinogens/dissociatives (n= 6; 10.7%) was consumed significantly less frequently. PSU was found more frequently than average in situational contexts, without any explicit motive (n= 88; 83.8%), and for structural reasons (n= 16; 50%).

The motives of counteracting effects (n= 5; 6%) and enhancement (n= 21; 12.4%) played a significantly lesser role. People from cluster 2 showed no significant deviations regarding the use of professional support services (n= 14; 25.5%) and drug-checking (n= 67; 27.2%), nor about consumption rules in the areas of drug (n= 94; 27.4%) and set (n= 108; 28.2%) (table 3).

3.2.3 Cluster 3 (n= 137)

The average age in Cluster 3 was 33.86 years (SD= 12.05, min.= 18, max. = 74). The cluster comprised predominantly cis women (n= 81; 59.1%), followed by cis men (n= 52; 38.0%), non-binary individuals (n= 2; 1.5%), genderfluid individuals (n= 1; 0.7%), and individuals reporting other gender identities (n= 1; 0.7%). Only two people (0.6%) reported PSU, while 135 people (99.3%) denied this. Analogous to the virtually non-existent PSU, the other variables examined also showed very low or no case numbers. None of the participants reported any of the three substance combination classes (n= 0; 0%), and the same applied to the different motives for PSU (n= 0; 0%). At the same time, behavioral factors such as the use of professional support services (n= 6; 10.9%), drug-checking (n= 40; 16.3%), and adherence to consumption rules regarding drug (n= 97; 28.3%) and set (n= 104; 27.2%) were reported significantly less (table 3).

4 Discussion

The identified heterogeneity in polysubstance use has important implications for prevention and health promotion.

Cluster 1: intentional PSU with support engagement

In cluster 1, the second-oldest cluster, all participants reported PSU, characterized by a broad range of substance combinations and predominantly targeted motivations such as minimizing unpleasant effects, enhancing pleasant effects, or creating new effects. This pattern is consistent with previous research showing that PSU may involve intentional substance combinations aimed at enhancing, modifying, or counteracting specific effects (Boileau-Falardeau et al., 2022; Grov et al., 2009; Lake and Lucas, 2024; Rodzlan Hasani et al., 2023).

Cluster 1 also constituted the largest polysubstance group in the present study, paralleling findings by Parthasarathy et al. (2025), who identified simultaneous PSU as the largest of three distinct PSU-groups.

Importantly, participants in cluster 1 also reported greater use of drug-checking, professional support services, and self-defined consumption rules. Taken together, these characteristics suggest that frequent and targeted PSU may coexist with greater engagement in self-regulatory practices and available support services. A similar pattern was observed by Greń et al. (2024), whose profile 5 combined a broad range of substances with contextspecific intentions, while showing the lowest health risks and greater use of protective behavioral strategies compared with other profiles. The authors suggested that these strategies may partly explain the relatively limited negative outcomes despite intensive PSU. Drugchecking services and related support measures, including counselling and educational interventions, represent important components of health promotion for people who use psychoactive substances in recreational settings and have been shown to be well accepted (Barsch, 2004; Betzler et al., 2021; Grace Rose et al., 2023; Hirschfeld et al., 2024; Karden et al., 2025). However, barriers such as inconvenient locations or limited opening hours, long waiting times, concerns about anonymity, and fear of police involvement may limit their regular use (Aronson et al., 2024; Grace Rose et al., 2023; Karden et al., 2025; Sande and Šabić, 2018). The characteristics of cluster 1 therefore suggest that increasing the availability and accessibility of drug-checking, low-threshold educational, and counselling services may be particularly relevant for individuals showing this pattern of PSU.

4.2 Cluster 2: situational stimulant-oriented PSU

Cluster 2 was the youngest and smallest cluster. Although all but one participant reported pronounced PSU, this cluster differed from cluster 1 in terms of substance combinations, underlying motivations, and substance use management practices. In addition to the predominant consumption of stimulant combinations, PSU was more often spontaneous or opportunistic rather than motivated by specific intended effects. In this context, Fleury et al. (2026) emphasize the importance of considering the intention underlying PSU when developing interventions and health promotion measures, as unintentional or opportunistic PSU, for example due to availability, social factors, habitual use, or accidental co-use, may require different preventive approaches than intentional PSU aimed at enhancing desired effects. Furthermore, cluster 2 was characterized by a significantly lower use of self-defined consumption rules, professional support services, and drug-checking. Given the more spontaneous and opportunistic pattern observed in this cluster, services that require prior planning or involve delays may be less compatible with the circumstances in which PSU occurs. This interpretation is supported by recent research identifying limited availability and relatively long waiting times for test results as key barriers to the uptake of counselling and drug-checking services (Eide et al., 2024; Karden et al., 2025; Maghsoudi et al., 2020). Low-threshold and readily accessible approaches to providing evidence-based information may therefore be particularly relevant for reaching individuals with this pattern of PSU and supporting informed decision-making. However, the reasons for the lower use of substance use management practices cannot be determined from the present data. Whether this reflects limited awareness or accessibility of such services, lower perceived relevance, or problematic assumptions about the substances being used (e.g., the belief that one is in possession of particularly "pure" or "harmless" substances) requires further investigation. A better understanding of these factors could help tailor information and support services to this subgroup's specific characteristics.

4.3 Cluster 3: predominantly mono-substance use

Only two participants in cluster 3, which was characterized by the highest mean age and the largest proportion of cis women, reported PSU. This finding is broadly consistent with epidemiological research indicating associations of PSU with younger age and male gender (De Jonge et al., 2022; Rodzlan Hasani et al., 2023; Stevens et al., 2022; Tucker et al., 2021).

Cluster 3 was also characterized by significantly lower engagement in substance use management practices, including drug-checking, professional support services, and selfdefined consumption rules. However, because of the cross-sectional design, it remains unclear whether this lower engagement reflects the absence of PSU or whether the identified clusters represent different patterns along a broader PSU continuum. Moreover, the absence of current PSU does not necessarily imply that such patterns remain stable over time. As reflected in cluster 2, PSU may occur spontaneously or situationally. In addition, unexpected psychoactive substances or adulterants may result in unintentional or unrecognized PSU (Palamar et al., 2021; Peck et al., 2019; Sevigny et al., 2024). From a harm-reduction perspective, which is preventive rather than reactive, early education and providing accessible information before such patterns emerge may therefore support informed decision-making.

To sum up, this study identified three distinct patterns of substance use, highlighting the heterogeneity of PSU. Previous research has also reported a three-group structure, commonly distinguishing between limited-range, moderate-range, and extended-range PSU based on the breadth of substances involved (Stevens et al., 2022). This complexity among people who use psychoactive substances and their consumption practices underscores the need to tailor measures promoting safer-use initiatives and health promotion to different subgroups. In this context, the understanding of harm reduction should be broadened, as Fleury et al. (2026) emphasize that harms associated with PSU extend beyond overdose and may also include social exclusion, school disciplinary consequences, and criminalization. By providing accessible, evidence-based information and support, such approaches may strengthen drug literacy and facilitate informed decision-making.

5 Limitations

The use of psychoactive substances in recreational settings repeatedly poses numerous obstacles to empirical research. The pathologizing and stigmatizing public discourse surrounding this topic marginalizes individuals who use such substances, which contributes significantly to the difficulty of reaching this hidden population (Stetina et al., 2008).

Although online studies allow direct access to the sample under investigation (Kothgassner et al., 2011; Miller and Sønderlund, 2018; Stetina et al., 2008; Stetina and Kryspin-Exner, 2009), limitations in representativeness must be noted. The convenience sample collected in this study may therefore be representative of people with a use of psychoactive substances in leisure settings but cannot claim to be representative of the general population.

To account for individual characteristics, participants reported the substances combined in PSU, as well as the underlying motivation, using an open-ended response format. This resulted in around 190 different substance combinations and over 640 motivations, some of which were mentioned only very rarely. Because small sample groups can distort effects (Bortz and Schuster, 2010; Möhring and Schlütz, 2019), this data were not suitable for statistical calculations. To make statistical statements, nonetheless, the individual mentions had to be assigned to higher-level substance categories. This enabled statistical calculations but came with a certain loss of information.

In addition, open-ended questions present several challenges during analysis. Potential sources of error include a lack of clarity and contextual information in written responses, researcher bias as well as translation and coding errors (Scholz et al., 2021). This limitation should be considered when interpreting the resulting categories.

Furthermore, participants were recruited from 22 countries, with the majority residing in Austria, Germany, and Switzerland. Differences in national legal and drug policy frameworks may influence substance use patterns, including the specific substances combined, as changes in the legal status of psychoactive substances can affect substance availability and consumption patterns (Boileau-Falardeau et al., 2022). Such differences may also affect access to harm reduction services and participants' willingness to disclose substance use. As these country-specific contextual factors were not specifically accounted for in the present analyses, their potential influence on the findings remains unclear.

The reliance on self-reported data collected through an online survey represents a further limitation. Self-reported substance use may be affected by recall bias and social desirability bias (Althubaiti, 2016; Schwarz and Oyserman, 2001). Previous research comparing self-reported substance use with hair toxicology has also demonstrated underreporting for certain substances, raising concerns about the validity of self-reported consumption patterns (Steinhoff et al., 2023; Janousch et al., 2025). As no repeated assessment was conducted, the test-retest reliability of the responses could not be evaluated.

Another limitation concerns the cross-sectional design of the present study. Crosssectional analyses provide a snapshot but cannot capture changes over time (Möhring and Schlütz, 2019; Ullrich, 2018). Although three distinct patterns of PSU were identified, it remained unclear whether these patterns represent different stages in the development of substance use over time. For example, it remains unclear whether individuals may transition from predominantly single-substance use (cluster 3) to more spontaneous PSU (cluster 2) and subsequently to more strategic PSU (cluster 1) or whether these clusters represent stable but distinct patterns among different individuals. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether transitions between clusters occur over time (Möhring and Schlütz, 2019; Ullrich, 2018).
