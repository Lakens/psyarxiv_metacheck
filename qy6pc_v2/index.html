Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Voluntary and involuntary external attention can differentially modulate perceptual and semantic information, but previous working memory (WM) studies found no corresponding dissociation.

Across four experiments (n=140), we tested whether increasing voluntary retro-cue reliability and making involuntary cues fully non-predictive would reveal this dissociation, and whether late visual masking promotes retrospective attention by protecting representations from interference.

Peripheral and central retro-cues were used, and performance was analyzed using hierarchical drift-diffusion modeling. No differential prioritization of competing perceptual and semantic WM contents emerged. Beyond this shared pattern, the results revealed distinct influences of cue format, cue reliability, and visual interference on voluntary and involuntary attention. First, increasing central cue reliability enabled voluntary benefits in representational quality that depended on protection from visual interference. Second, peripheral involuntary effects persisted when cue predictiveness was eliminated, whereas evidence for central cues remained ambiguous. Third, removing masking generally did not reduce voluntary retro-cueing effects.

Fourth, masking reduced peripheral involuntary benefits in representational quality but enabled retrieval-related benefits, increasing the difference between voluntary and involuntary attention.

Finally, more frequent self-reported voluntary cue use was associated with larger attentional effects in the voluntary task, while effects persisted among participants reporting no intentional cue use in the involuntary task. These findings suggest that voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention differ in their sensitivity to cue format, reliability, and visual interference while exerting similar relative effects on competing perceptual and semantic WM representations.

1. INTRODUCTION

Selective attention comprises processes that prioritize information relevant to ongoing behavior in a brain with limited processing capacity (Carrasco, 2011). This selection can be applied to information derived from the sensory stream (external attention) and from internal memory contents (internal attention) (Nobre & Gresch, 2025). As an instance of the latter, retrospective attention serves this purpose, as a type of internal attention it allows us to select, maintain, and transform varying types of working memory (WM) contents in an online fashion (Myers et al., 2017; Souza & Oberauer, 2016; van Ede & Nobre, 2023).

Either at the behavioral or neural level, attentional selection can be based on different stimulus properties and directed by different mechanisms. First, attentional selection can operate at different levels of visual processing, some low such as space (e.g., Muller & Rabbit, 1989) and orientation (e.g., Liu & Hou, 2011), but also at higher levels such as motion (e.g., Sàenz et al., 2002), and emotional valence (e.g., Cipriani et al., 2025b). Second, either at the internal (van Ede & Nobre, 2023) or external (Chica et al., 2013) levels of attention, this selection can operate by two mechanisms, one automatic and in a bottom-up fashion (i.e., involuntary or exogenous attention), and one that is controlled in a top-down way (i.e., voluntary or endogenous attention). The former is rapid and stimulus-driven, whereas the latter is goal-directed and sustained. Regarding retrospective spatial attention, most studies have investigated voluntary attention using highly predictive and attended retro-cues (Souza & Oberauer, 2016), whereas involuntary attention using non-or low-predictive retro-cues has received considerably less attention. However, recent studies have analyzed this type of attention too (e.g., Berryhill et al., 2012; Fuentes-Guerra et al., 2025a; Han et al., 2022; van Ede et al., 2020).

Most research on voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention has focused on low-level visual stimuli (e.g., colored squares, gratings, oriented bars; Berryhill et al., 2012; Han et al., 2022; van Ede et al., 2020). More recently, studies have examined how these forms of attention operate on real-world visual WM representations (Cipriani et al., 2024 (Cipriani et al., , 2025a;; Fuentes-Guerra et al., 2025a , 2025b; Loaiza et al., 2024; Shimi & Scerif, 2015), which integrate several features and are semantically rich (Brady et al., 2024). In this regard, in an examination of whether voluntary attention benefits from stimulus semantics or meaningfulness (high-level properties), Shimi and Scerif (2015) found larger retro-cueing effects in Cowan's K for familiar objects than for abstract shapes, suggesting an influence of long-term memory. However, Loaiza et al. (2024) reported no comparable benefit for recall error in a delayed estimation task. This pattern of mixed findings for visual WM is similar to what has been found for verbal WM (e.g., Camos et al., 2018b , Higgins & Johnson, 2013; Labaronne et al., 2023; Loaiza et al., 2015). Moreover, the study of how meaningfulness of WM contents conditions involuntary attentional selection, and how it differs from voluntary attention has been unexplored.

Furthermore, these studies have compared meaningful versus non-meaningful stimuli, where the first are considered to preserve the low-level physical properties of the second (Brady et al., 2024), but the comparison of how attention operates on low and high visual levels of WM representations has been unexplored till recently.

Across two complementary studies, the selection of competing perceptual and semantic WM contents by voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention was examined using real-world visual stimuli and both central arrows (Cipriani et al., 2024) and peripheral dashed circles as retro-cues (Cipriani et al., 2025a). To pursue this goal, hierarchical drift-diffusion modeling (HDDM) was employed, a method particularly useful for the study of retrospective attention that offers several advantages over traditional analyses by integrating reaction time and accuracy data while distinguishing decisional from non-decisional processes (Boag et al., 2023; Fuentes-Guerra et al., 2025c; Voss et al., 2013). The hypothesis was based on behavioral and neural findings from external attention research (Cervantes et al., 2023; Esterman et al., 2008; Fairhall & Macaluso, 2009; Fernández & Carrasco, 2020 , 2023; Landau et al., 2007; Loria et al., 2020; Prinzmetal et al., 2009; Tang et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024) that generally show larger effects of voluntary attention at high levels of visual processing-with weaker involuntary effects-and the reverse pattern for low-level processing. Testing this hypothesis at the internal level of attention, in the first study (Cipriani et al., 2024) with central arrows as retro-cues, the results showed a robust prioritization of perceptual over semantic information in WM, indexed by larger RCEs in drift rates, regardless of whether attention was voluntary or involuntary. By contrast, only voluntary attention facilitated pre-decisional retrieval of WM contents, reflected in RCEs in non-decision time. Critically, no interaction on the RCE emerged between attentional voluntariness and content type, suggesting that internal attention differs from external attention in this regard.

The follow-up study (Cipriani et al., 2025a) extended this research by replacing central cues with peripheral retro-cues to more strongly engage involuntary attention (Doallo et al., 2004; Giordano et al., 2009; Gong et al., 2023). The core pattern was replicated: voluntary and involuntary attention did not differentially modulate perceptual versus semantic contents.

However, peripheral retro-cues now successfully elicited involuntary RCEs in non-decision time, suggesting that peripheral-but not central-retro-cues automatically trigger involuntary attentional selection of WM representations. Regarding the main hypothesis, the two studies concluded that when perceptual and semantic WM contents are in competition, there is no evidence for differential effects of voluntary and involuntary attention depending on the type of WM content. One hypothetical explanation for this differential pattern between external and internal attention concerns the additional processing that follows attentional selection in WM.

Unlike external attention, retrospective attention involves the reformatting of selected WM representations into an action-oriented state in preparation for upcoming behavior (Myers et al., 2017; Nobre & Gresch, 2025). Importantly, once initiated, this transformation may not require voluntary attention to be sustained throughout the entire retention interval (Wallis et al., 2015).

Consequently, initial differences in how voluntary and involuntary attention prioritize low-and high-level WM contents may become attenuated as the interval between retro-cue and probe onset increases.

In general, Cipriani et al. findings are consistent across the two studies regarding the null triple interaction between voluntariness, content type and retro-cue validity. However, two main questions remain open. First, the role of retro-cue reliability in these studies warrants further consideration. Although participants in the involuntary condition were instructed to ignore the retro-cues-and Cipriani et al. (2024) reported selective voluntary RCEs in non-decision time, with no corresponding effect for involuntary attention-the involuntary retro-cues were low in predictiveness but not fully non-predictive. This approach, consistent with Shimi et al. (2014) but differing from other studies of involuntary attention (e.g., Berryhill et al., 2012; van Ede et al., 2020), enabled equal proportions of valid, neutral, and invalid trials, thereby controlling for selection-history effects arising from unequal trial frequencies (Awh et al., 2012). However, equalizing trial frequencies does not eliminate the statistical regularity conveyed by above-chance cue predictiveness. Participants could still become sensitive to the cue-probe contingency, potentially allowing learned predictiveness to bias attention toward the cued location, consistent with evidence that learned spatial regularities can guide attentional allocation (Theeuwes et al., 2022). Although this possibility has not been directly demonstrated in retro-cueing paradigms, rendering involuntary retro-cues fully non-predictive provides a clearer test of whether involuntary RCEs reflect automatic orienting independently of learned cue predictiveness. In parallel, voluntary retro-cues in prior studies were highly predictive (75%), yet increasing cue reliability has been shown to enhance voluntary attentional deployment (Gunseli et al., 2015 (Gunseli et al., , 2019)), at least up to approximately 80% (Liu et al., 2024). Jointly manipulating cue reliability in opposite directions-reducing it to chance for involuntary cues while increasing it for voluntary cues-provides a principled way to amplify the distinction between these two modes of attentional selection. This manipulation may also help clarify the absence of the predicted three-way interaction in previous studies, insofar as differences between voluntary and involuntary attention could have been attenuated under the original reliability conditions.

A second unresolved issue in Cipriani et al.'s paradigm concerns the presentation of visual masks before the test array. These masks were originally included to minimize potential contamination from residual sensory representations of the memory array or the peripheral retro-cues onto the test array. However, they may also have increased the contribution of protection from visual interference to the RCE. According to this account, retrospective attention progressively increases the resistance of the cued WM representation to perceptual interference (Makovski & Jiang, 2007; Makovski et al., 2008; Makovski & Pertzov, 2015; Pinto et al., 2013; Souza et al., 2016; van Moorselaar et al., 2015). Consistent with this view, voluntary retro-cues reduce interference from irrelevant visual displays presented during retention, although the degree of protection depends on the properties of the interfering display (Makovski & Jiang, 2007; Pinto et al., 2013; van Moorselaar et al., 2015), and they can also reduce interference from the memory probe itself (Makovski et al., 2008). This protection develops gradually, continuing to build over the first several hundred milliseconds and becoming largely established by approximately 500-600 ms after cue onset (Pinto et al., 2013; van Moorselaar et al., 2015).

Moreover, attentional protection may be particularly important for preserving feature-location bindings (Makovski & Pertzov, 2015). Nevertheless, protection from perceptual interference cannot fully explain the voluntary RCE. Although retro-cues produce larger benefits when interfering visual input is present, reliable cueing benefits persist when visual interference is minimized or held constant (Souza et al., 2016), indicating that additional mechanisms also contribute. Importantly, the evidence supporting the protection from interference account comes almost exclusively from comparisons between cued and uncued or neutrally cued representations. Once sufficient time has elapsed for protection to develop, visual masks should have relatively little additional effect on the validly cued representation, whereas representations maintained without focal prioritization should remain more vulnerable. Accordingly, any increase in retro-cue benefits under masking would be expected to arise primarily from poorer performance on neutral trials rather than from further enhancement of valid trials. The literature provides no direct evidence concerning invalidly cued representations, but insofar as both neutral and invalidly probed representations remain outside the attentional focus at mask onset, they may be similarly susceptible to interference. Under this interpretation, masking would lower performance in neutral and invalid trials to a comparable extent, increasing benefits while leaving costs relatively unchanged. Given the 700-ms interval between retro-cue and mask onset in Cipriani et al.'s paradigm, attentional protection should have had sufficient time to develop before the masks appeared. Eliminating the masks therefore provides a direct test of whether the previously observed voluntary RCE was partly driven by interference-related reductions in neutral and invalid performance. It also allows assessment of whether the same pattern extends to involuntary retrospective attention, for which there is currently no a priori basis to assume a different susceptibility to visual interference.

The present study aims to clarify the mechanisms underlying voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention to competing low-and high-level WM representations by addressing two questions that remain open following Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a)). First, Experiments 1A (peripheral cues) and 1B (central cues) will manipulate retro-cue reliability to further dissociate attentional modes, rendering involuntary retro-cues fully non-predictive (from 50% to 33%) while increasing voluntary retro-cue reliability to a very high level (from 75% to 88%). This manipulation is designed to sharpen the distinction between voluntary and involuntary internal selective attention by disentangling cue-driven prioritization from selection history effects, thereby allowing a clearer characterization of the relative contribution of automatic and goal-directed orienting mechanisms. Experiments 2A (peripheral) and 2B (central) will then assess the contribution of visual masking to the RCE by removing masks before test array onset, thereby testing whether previously observed effects reflect protection-from-interference mechanisms or instead emerge from alternative attentional processes operating during retro-cueing.

2. EXPERIMENTS 1A AND 1B

These pre-registered experiments examined the effects of voluntary and involuntary internal attention on competing perceptual and semantic WM representations of real-world stimuli.

Specifically, Experiments A (peripheral cues) and 1B (central cues) were designed to further dissociate these two modes of attentional selection by manipulating retro-cue reliability, building on the paradigms of Cipriani et al. (2025a) and (2024), respectively. By rendering involuntary retro-cues fully non-predictive (from 50% to 33%) and increasing voluntary retro-cue reliability (from 75% to 88%), these experiments aimed to characterize, on the one hand, the extent to which involuntary cueing effects may be guided by statistical learning of cue predictiveness, and on the other hand, whether increasing predictiveness potentiates the deployment of voluntary attentional mechanism.

For voluntary attention, two alternative hypotheses were also formulated. Under H1a, increasing retro-cue reliability would enhance RCE magnitude overall, without differentially affecting perceptual and semantic WM contents, thereby replicating the absence of a three-way interaction between cue type, task, and cue validity. Under H1b, increased reliability would not only amplify RCEs (Gunseli et al., 2015 (Gunseli et al., , 2019;; Liu et al., 2024) but would do so preferentially for semantic relative to perceptual representations, consistent with the prediction derived from external attention research of a stronger engagement of voluntary attention at higher levels of processing (as outlined in Cipriani et al., 2024 Cipriani et al., , 2025a)). Critically, this pattern would be reflected in the emergence of a significant three-way interaction between cue type, task, and cue validity.

For involuntary attention, two alternative hypotheses were considered. Under H2a, RCEs would remain comparable to those observed in previous studies, indicating that they primarily reflect automatic orienting and are not modulated by cue reliability, supporting the notion that instructions to ignore the retro-cue prevent learning of low retro-cue reliability. Under H2b, RCEs would be reduced when retro-cues are fully non-predictive, suggesting that prior effects partly reflect sensitivity to probabilistic regularities acquired through statistical learning of retro-cue reliability similarly to results obtained in visual search tasks (Theeuwes et al., 2022). Importantly, both hypotheses predict no change in the previously observed absence of a three-way interaction between cue type, task, and cue validity (Cipriani et al., 2024 (Cipriani et al., , 2025a)).

2.1. Method

>Method

2.1.1. Sample

Fifty-one participants volunteered for Experiment 1A while the final sample was fifty (38 cis-women and 12 cis-men) aged 18-33 (M=23.4 years old). For Experiment 1B, thirty-eight students volunteered while the final sample was thirty-five participants (30 cis-women and 5 cis-men) aged 18-27 (M=20.6 years old). All participants provided informed consent and received fourteen euros as monetary compensation. The experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee (code: 1928/CEIH/2020) of the Universidad de Granada and met the criteria established in the Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2025). As in Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a)), and as stated in the pre-registration, the sample size was determined following a sequential Bayes Factor (BF) with maximal n approach (Schönbrodt et al., 2017; Schönbrodt & Wagenmakers, 2018). After the first 15 participants, every new 5 participants a Bayesian ANOVA was calculated on accuracy rates and median reaction times introducing the four factors manipulated in the experiment (Suppl. Table 1). Data collection continued until each BF for each main effect or interaction reached substantial evidence for H1 (BF10 >3) or H0 (BF10 <1/3). Data collection for Experiment 1B finished at n=35, while in Experiment 1A given some interactions did not reach substantial evidence data collection was stopped at the maximal n (=50).

2.1.2. Procedure

Experiments 1A and 1B follow the ones of Cipriani et al. (2024; 2025a). In the same session, participants completed two computerized local recognition retro-cueing tasks with real-world stimuli in a counterbalanced order across participants. One task assessed involuntary attention, while the other assessed voluntary attention (Figure 1). Participants completed at least 15 practice trials with feedback until they responded correctly on at least 4 consecutive trials.

Between task blocks of approximately 4 minutes, participants had seven self-paced breaks, and between both tasks, participants had a 5-10 minutes break.

2.1.3. Retro-cueing tasks

This experimental design of Experiment 1A replicates the one of Cipriani et al. (2025a) with peripheral circles as retro-cues, while Experiment 1B follows the one of Cipriani et al. (2024) with central arrows as retro-cues. Participants had to memorize the color (gray vs. sepia) and category (natural vs. artificial) of items in a triangular (three items) memory array. In the voluntary task, a very high-reliability retro-cue instructed participants to direct attention to the item indicated by the relevant colored circle (the color participants were instructed to attend to was counterbalanced across participants). In the involuntary task, the retro-cue was non-predictive, and participants were instructed to ignore it and retain all items. The key difference between Cipriani et al. (2024; 2025a) designs and the current ones is that for the voluntary task, retro-cue reliability was increased from 75% to 88%, and for the involuntary tasks, reliability decreased from 50% to 33.3% becoming non-reliable. At the end of the trial, one question was presented at one of the item locations depending on the experimental condition, and participants were asked to either report the color or the category of the item presented at the question location: GRI? (gray?) or SEP? (sepia?) for color, and NAT? (natural?) or ART? (artificial?) for the category. Participants were informed about it and instructed to answer yes or no as fast and accurately as possible, with the corresponding keys.

Participants completed 320 and 288 trials in the voluntary and involuntary tasks, respectively.

The tasks were programmed with PsychoPy (v2021.2.3; Peirce, 2007 Peirce, , 2009)).

2.1.4. Images

The present study used the same dataset as Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a)). Artificial images depicted a wide range of human-made objects, including musical instruments, household and public-space items, kitchen utensils, appliances, clothing, accessories, tools, and weapons. Natural images were equally divided between the animal and plant kingdoms. Animal images represented unique species-including insects, amphibians, fish, whales, birds, and domesticated animals (e.g., dogs and cats from different breeds)-with humans excluded. Plant images depicted non-repeated species such as flowers, fruits, vegetables, plants, trees, and leaves.

2.1.5. Data analysis

We adopted almost the same analytic procedure described in Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a)).

Trials with misses or RTs below 200 ms were treated as errors (0.1%). One participant from Experiment 1A and three from 1B were excluded due to overall accuracy below 60% in one or both retro-cueing tasks. Experimental data were analyzed using the hierarchical drift-diffusion model (HDDM) toolbox (Wiecki et al., 2013). Outliers were managed with the "p_outlier" function, which implements a mixture model assuming a uniform distribution with a fixed probability of 0.05, meaning that 5% of trials were treated as outliers. Parameter estimation was conducted via Markov-chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling (Gamerman & Lopes, 2006). Each chain consisted of 5000 samples; the first 500 were discarded as burn-in, and every second sample was removed to reduce autocorrelation.

To quantify RCEs in the present experiments, a single model was fitted separately for each experiment. Prior research has demonstrated that drift rate (v) and non-decision time (t₀) are sensitive to cue type, task demands, and cue validity (Cipriani et al., 2024 (Cipriani et al., , 2025a;; Shepherdson, 2020; Shepherdson et al., 2018; Souza & Frischkorn, 2023). In line with the approach of Shepherdson et al. (2018), the decision threshold (a) was assumed to capture systematic differences between tasks. Accordingly, a was allowed to vary only as a function of cue type, which was blocked and counterbalanced across participants. Because participants were unaware of trial validity and task type (perceptual vs. semantic) prior to probe onset, these factors were not modeled as sources of variation in a. In contrast, cue type, task, and validity were allowed to modulate both v and t₀.

Model fit was evaluated using several complementary methods: (a) posterior predictive checks based on 500 simulated datasets, assessing mean accuracy, mean reaction time (RT), and RT quantiles (.1, .3, .5, .7, .9) for correct and error responses; (b) calculation of mean squared error (MSE) between observed and predicted values; and (c) qualitative inspection of RT distributions generated from 5,000 simulated trials per condition using posterior mean parameter estimates. Overall, these diagnostics suggested that the models provided a good fit to the empirical data.

To examine the experimental effects, Bayesian ANOVAs were conducted for each parameter (a, v, and t0). Analyses were performed in R using the anovaBF function from the BayesFactor package, with the option whichModels = top and 500,000 iterations. The resulting JZS Bayes Factors (BFs) indicate the strength of evidence for excluding effects or interactions relative to the full model. Default prior width values were used for all effect sizes; tests with wider and narrower priors yielded comparable results. BFs greater than 3 were considered to provide substantial evidence for the alternative hypothesis (H₁; presence of a main effect or interaction), whereas BFs below 1/3 indicated substantial evidence for the null hypothesis (H₀; absence of an effect). For significant main effects or interactions, follow-up Bayesian paired t-tests were performed using the ttestBF function. For the purposes of this study, only main effects and interactions involving validity were of theoretical interest and are discussed in detail below; other effects are reported but not further analyzed.

To further evaluate the effect of reducing the predictivity of involuntary retro-cues while increasing that of voluntary retro-cues, exploratory (non-preregistered) analyses compared the magnitude of the RCE, as well as retro-cueing benefits and costs, in Experiment 1A with those reported in Cipriani et al. (2025a) and in Experiment 1B with those reported in Cipriani et al. (2024). All comparisons were conducted using Bayesian independent-samples t-tests implemented via the ttestBF function. These between-experiment contrasts held all procedural aspects constant, except for cue reliability. The only further difference relative to Cipriani et al. (2024) and Cipriani et al. (2025a) concerns memory load: the previous studies included arrays of three and four items, whereas the present experiments employed only three items. This choice was motivated by prior HDDM model selection results indicating that memory load should be collapsed across levels.

2.3. Discussion

Experiments 1A and 1B tested whether manipulating retro-cue reliability modulates voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention, and whether these effects interact with WM content type.

Overall, the results support H1a and H2a and provide no evidence for a three-way interaction between cue type, task, and validity.

For voluntary attention, substantial RCEs and subeffects were observed across both peripheral and central retro-cues and across drift rate and non-decision time, with the exception of ambiguous evidence for drift rate benefits with central cues. Importantly, voluntary attention was clearly modulated by cue reliability. As predicted by H1a and H1b, increasing predictiveness from 75% to 88% enhanced RCE magnitude, particularly for non-decision times and more consistently with central cues. This enhancement was primarily driven by increased costs, with less consistent evidence for benefits. These findings suggest that higher cue reliability strengthens goal-directed attentional deployment.

For involuntary attention, peripheral retro-cues yielded substantial RCEs in both drift rates and non-decision times, whereas central retro-cues did not elicit reliable RCEs, with some subeffects remaining ambiguous. Critically, rendering retro-cues fully non-predictive (33%) did not substantially reduce RCE magnitude relative to prior low-predictive conditions (50%), as predicted by H2a. This pattern was supported by substantial evidence and was consistent across peripheral and central cues and across both drift rate and non-decision time, with the exception of ambiguous evidence for central cues in drift rates. These findings suggest that involuntary attention operates largely independently of the low reliability of retro-cues when cues are to be ignored, supporting an automatic, stimulus-driven account.

Critically, there was no evidence supporting a three-way interaction between cue type, task, and validity. Evidence against this interaction was substantial for non-decision times and remained ambiguous for drift rates. Thus, as predicted by H1a and H2a, even under strengthened voluntary attentional deployment and automatic involuntary orienting, voluntary and involuntary attention did not differentially modulate perceptual versus semantic WM contents. 3. EXPERIMENTS 2A AND 2B

The aim of pre-registered Experiments 2A and 2B was to determine whether the visual masks used in Cipriani et al.'s paradigms increased the RCEs through protection from visual interference. Therefore, the procedures used in these experiments were identical to those of Experiment 1, except that the masks presented at the end of the WM maintenance period, immediately before the test array, were now removed. Experiment 2A employed peripheral retro-cues, whereas Experiment 2B employed central retro-cues. This manipulation allowed us to assess whether the magnitude and components of the RCE depended on visual interference introduced late in the maintenance interval.

Based on Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a)), the most parsimonious prediction was that removing the masks would not alter the relative pattern of voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention. Because those studies found no cue x task x validity interaction under masked conditions, there was no a priori reason to expect mask removal to affect the two tasks differently. Accordingly, we predicted (H3) that the three-way interaction would remain absent.

Previous studies have shown that voluntary retro-cues protect WM representations from visual interference (Makovski & Jiang, 2007; Makovski & Pertzov, 2015; Pinto et al., 2013; Souza et al., 2016; van Moorselaar et al., 2015). Given the 700-ms interval between retro-cue and mask onset in the present paradigm, validly cued representations should have had sufficient time to become substantially protected before the masks appeared. By contrast, representations maintained under neutral cues should have remained more vulnerable. We therefore predicted that removing the masks would improve performance more on neutral than on valid trials, thereby reducing retro-cueing benefits (H4). Because prior research provides no direct evidence about the effects of masking on invalidly cued representations, we adopted the parsimonious logic of H4, and extended it to the costs, so invalidly probed representations would be more susceptible than the ones in neutral trials, as neither occupied the attentional focus at mask onset. On this basis, we expected mask removal to improve performance more on invalid than on neutral trials, thereby reducing retro-cueing costs (H5). Although protection from visual interference has not been directly demonstrated for involuntary retrospective attention, there was no theoretical basis for predicting a different masking pattern. We therefore expected the same reduction in benefits and stability of costs for involuntary retro-cues, preserving the absence of a cue x task x validity interaction.

Experiments 2A and 2B examined whether eliminating the visual masks used in Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. Overall, the results supported H3: removing the visual masks did not reveal a cue type x task x validity interaction. This pattern closely replicated that observed in Experiments 1A and 1B, indicating that removing the visual masks did not alter the relative effects of voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention on perceptual and semantic WM representations.

For voluntary attention, substantial RCEs were again observed across both peripheral and central retro-cues for both drift rate and non-decision time. Based on the protection-from-interference account, we expected mask removal to selectively reduce the RCE by decreasing retro-cueing benefits (H4) and costs (H5). For voluntary attention, H4 was only partially supported. Across both peripheral and central retro-cues, overall RCEs and benefits remained largely comparable between masked and unmasked conditions, with evidence either favouring equivalence or remaining ambiguous, except for drift-rate benefits following central retro-cues. Regarding H5, there was no substantial evidence that costs differed following mask removal. Thus, eliminating the visual masks did not provide evidence that masking selectively enhanced voluntary retro-cueing benefits, nor did it provide substantial evidence that masking influenced retro-cueing costs.

For involuntary attention, the results partially replicated the pattern observed in Experiments 1A and 1B. Peripheral retro-cues again elicited substantial RCEs in drift rate, although these RCEs were comparable in magnitude to those observed under voluntary attention, and evidence for involuntary RCEs in non-decision time remained ambiguous. 4. RETRO-CUEING EFFECTS ACROSS SELF-REPORTED LEVELS OF VOLUNTARY ATTENTION

The distinction between voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention relies partly on whether participants intentionally use the retro-cue to prioritize information in WM. Previous studies indicate that adults' self-reports of attention covary with trial-to-trial fluctuations in WM performance (e.g., Adam & Vogel, 2017; Arnicane et al., 2021; Krimsky et al., 2017).

Specifically, in retro-cueing tasks, Arnicane and Souza (2022) found that participants reported higher attention on retro-cue than no-cue trials when answering the question "How attentive were you on this trial?" on a continuous scale, both before and after the memory test. Here, we extended this approach by assessing how frequently participants reported voluntarily using the retro-cue, thereby focusing on voluntary selective attention rather than sustained attention. 5. GENERAL DISCUSSION

The present study investigated whether voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention differentially prioritize competing low-level perceptual and high-level semantic representations in working memory (WM). In contrast to findings from external attention research, previous studies employing both central and peripheral retro-cues found no interaction between attentional voluntariness and representational level (Cipriani et al., 2024 (Cipriani et al., , 2025a)). The first aim of the present study (Experiments 1A and 1B) was to strengthen voluntary attentional deployment by increasing retro-cue reliability while isolating involuntary attention by rendering retro-cues fully non-predictive. The second aim (Experiments 2A and 2B) was to assess whether late visual masking enhances retrospective attention through a protection-from-visual-interference mechanism. Specifically, Experiments 1A and 1B tested whether strengthening the distinction between voluntary and involuntary orienting would reveal an interaction between attentional voluntariness and representational level, whereas Experiments 2A and 2B examined whether late visual masking generally enhances the effects of retrospective attention.

The results showed that the absence of a cue x task x validity interaction was preserved despite stronger voluntary cue predictiveness, fully non-predictive involuntary cueing, and the removal of late visual masking. Furthermore, five findings emerged. First, increasing retro-cue reliability enhanced voluntary RCEs primarily through larger retro-cueing costs, while also enabling retro-cueing benefits with central retro-cues, the latter operating through a protection-from-visual-interference mechanism. Second, involuntary RCEs elicited by peripheral retro-cues remained robust despite reducing retro-cue predictiveness from low to non-predictive, whereas evidence for involuntary RCEs elicited by central retro-cues remained ambiguous.

Third, eliminating visual masking generally did not reduce voluntary RCEs, indicating a limited impact of late visual interference to voluntary retrospective attention. Fourth, visual masking selectively influenced involuntary attention elicited by peripheral retro-cues, reducing benefits in representational quality while inducing benefits in retrieval speed; critically, this modulation also increased the difference between voluntary and involuntary attentional deployment under masking, favouring voluntary attention. Fifth, exploratory self-report analyses linked more frequent voluntary retro-cue use to larger effects on WM retrieval in the voluntary task, while revealing substantial attentional effects among participants reporting no voluntary cue use in the involuntary task. The theoretical implications of these findings are discussed in the following sections.

The impact of attention on low-and high-level competing WM contents

The primary goal of this study was to determine whether strengthening the distinction between voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention would reveal the three-way interaction between attentional mode, WM content type, and retro-cue validity predicted from the external attention literature. Across the four experiments, and as Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a)), evidence pointed against this interaction or it was ambiguous. Thus, despite relying on central or peripheral retro-cues, substantially increasing voluntary cue predictiveness, eliminating residual predictiveness from involuntary cues, and removing a potential source of visual interference, none of these manipulations systematically altered the relationship between attentional voluntariness and content type. Taken together with our previous studies, these findings provide converging evidence that retrospective attention, whether voluntary or involuntary, does not differentially prioritize competing perceptual and semantic WM contents.

The original hypothesis advanced by Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a) ) was that voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention would differentially prioritize WM representations according to their representational level, with involuntary attention preferentially prioritizing low-level perceptual information and voluntary attention preferentially prioritizing high-level semantic information. This prediction extended evidence from external attention showing distinct effects of voluntary and involuntary orienting across the visual hierarchy (Carrasco, 2011 (Carrasco, , 2018;; Fernández & Carrasco, 2020 , 2023). The prediction was grounded, first, in evidence that perceptual and retrospective attention rely on partially overlapping neural and functional mechanisms, raising the possibility that principles governing external attentional selection also apply to the selection of internally maintained representations (Myers et al., 2017; Nobre & Gresch, 2025). Second, it followed from the convergence between the hierarchical organization of visual processing-from low-level features encoded in early visual cortex to increasingly abstract and semantic representations- (Clarke & Tyler, 2015) and the distinct temporal profiles of attentional orienting, whereby involuntary attention is rapidly deployed and transient, whereas voluntary attention develops more slowly and can be sustained (Carrasco, 2018; Liao et al., 2025). Finally, it was based on behavioral and neural external attention research, which generally evidences larger attentional effects of voluntary attention at high levels of visual processing-with weaker involuntary effects-and the reverse pattern for low-level processing (e.g., Fernández et al., 2023; Fernández & Carrasco, 2020; Prinzmetal et al. 2009; Wu et al., 2024). Across studies, three hypotheses were outlined by Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a Cipriani et al. ( , 2026) ) to explain the lack of interaction between attentional voluntariness and the type of WM representation.

One is addressed by this and previous studies by Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a) ) and refers to a potential lack of differentiation between involuntary and voluntary orienting. Across studies, no triple interaction between cue type, WM content, and validity has been found. Therefore, evidence does not support this hypothesis. A second explanation refers to a distinctive function of internal attention related to the reformatting and transformation of WM contents into an action-oriented prospective state (Myers et al., 2017; Nobre & Gresch, 2025). Following the initial allocation of attention, this transformation during maintenance may integrate perceptual and semantic features or attenuate their differences, thereby masking potential interactions in how attention initially operated on each content type. Thus, processes distinctive to internal attention may prevent the content-specific effects observed in external attention from emerging during retrospective selection.

While this hypothesis can not be discarded, recent research (Cipriani et al., 2026) points to an alternative explanation already outlined by Cipriani et al. (2024) which is related to the current mixed design of presenting perceptual and semantic trials. Given that perceptual and semantic WM contents are manipulated within the very same object, and given participants do not know till probe onset which feature is task relevant, participants may have been induced to orient their attention in an object-based fashion which may have attenuated the differences between both types of WM contents. Alternatively, presenting these trials in separate blocks as Prinzmetal et al. (2009) may induce participants to orient their attention independently to perceptual and semantic contents similarly to feature-based attention (Niklaus et al., 2017; Wheeler & Treisman, 2002), which would allow content-specific differences of attention to them to be detected. A recent retro-cueing study by Cipriani et al. (2026) may provide indirect evidence in this direction. In order to dissociate the high-level semantic dimension from low-level visual features, meaningfulness of real-world stimuli (semantics) was eliminated by creating a scrambled version of such stimuli. The evidence indicated that voluntary-but not involuntary-attention retrieval is facilitated by meaningfulness, while attentional prioritization does not differentially impact the representational quality. This study may suggest that it may be necessary either through separate blocks or through different memoranda to separate the type of contents before attention operates on them. Otherwise attention may be allocated in an object-based fashion obscuring differences between representations. However, future studies are required to directly test this interpretation.

Finally, Experiment 1A revealed a task x validity interaction in drift rate, replicating the pattern originally reported by Cipriani et al. (2024). In both studies, perceptual RCEs were larger than semantic RCEs and were primarily driven by retro-cueing costs. Cipriani et al. (2024) interpreted this finding as evidence that, when perceptual and semantic representations compete simultaneously for WM storage, retrospective attention more effectively modulates the quality of perceptual than semantic representations. They proposed that perceptual features may occupy a more accessible representational state than semantic information because of their earlier position in the visual hierarchy (Cichy et al., 2014; Clarke et al., 2013; Clarke & Tyler, 2015), making them more susceptible to attentional prioritization. An additional, non-mutually exclusive explanation concerns the relatively brief encoding duration used in the present paradigm. The 500-ms memory-array presentation is typical of retro-cueing studies employing simple low-level stimuli (e.g., Berryhill et al., 2012; Gunseli et al., 2019; Shimi et al., 2014), but may provide insufficient time for semantic representations of real-world objects to become fully established before retrospective selection operates. Consistent with this possibility, studies using substantially longer encoding durations (>1 s) have reported semantic advantages during both long-term memory retrieval (Linde-Domingo et al., 2019) and WM retrieval (Kerrén et al., 2023). Likewise, retro-cueing studies employing real-world objects have typically adopted longer encoding intervals (Loaiza et al., 2024; Thibeault et al., 2024). More generally, evidence indicates that conditions favouring sustained item-specific processing, including sequential presentation and longer encoding durations, enhance memory for real-world objects relative to simple visual stimuli (Brady & Störmer, 2022; Brady et al., 2016). Notably, this interpretation is also consistent with the robust main effect of content type observed across the Cipriani studies, whereby perceptual WM contents consistently exhibited higher drift rates than semantic contents, irrespective of retro-cue validity or attentional voluntariness. Together, these findings suggest that the relatively brief encoding interval may preferentially preserve the quality of perceptual representations, thereby increasing their susceptibility to retrospective attentional modulation. However, because evidence for the task x validity interaction was ambiguous in Experiment 2B and Cipriani et al. (2025a), and substantial evidence against it was obtained in the remaining experiments, this interpretation should be considered tentative until directly tested by manipulating encoding duration.

In short, the present findings provide strong evidence that voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention do not differentially prioritize competing perceptual and semantic WM representations. By systematically manipulating retro-cue reliability, retro-cue type, and late visual masking, the present study substantially reduced the plausibility that the previously reported null interaction resulted from insufficient differentiation between voluntary and involuntary orienting. Instead, the findings are more consistent with the view that retrospective attention either operates according to principles that differ from those of external attention or is deployed in an object-based manner when perceptual and semantic contents are integrated within the same representation. At the same time, the recurring tendency for perceptual WM representations to exhibit stronger attentional modulation than semantic contents suggests that representational level may still influence the efficacy of retrospective attention under some conditions. Determining whether this effect reflects differences in encoding time, attentional selection, or representational accessibility remains an important challenge for future research.

Voluntary vs involuntary retrospective attention

Secondary aims of this study were to strengthen the deployment of voluntary attention via increasing retro-cue reliability, to assess pure involuntary attention by turning retro-cues fully non-predictive, and to investigate whether late visual masking induced an attentional benefit of protection from visual interference. Results indicate that increasing retro-cue reliability potentiated voluntary attentional deployment, while in general, turning retro-cues from low-to-non-predictive did not impact involuntary orienting. Regarding visual interference, late masking generally had little influence on voluntary RCEs, although a benefit emerged under masking with voluntary central retro-cues. In contrast, masking selectively influenced involuntary orienting elicited by peripheral retro-cues, reducing benefits in drift rate while inducing benefits in non-decision time. Consistent with this selective modulation, voluntary RCEs in drift rate were larger than involuntary RCEs under masking, whereas this difference was no longer evident when masks were removed. Figure 7 summarizes these effects across the present experiments and the previous studies by Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a) ) regarding the interaction between cue type and validity.

First, increasing retro-cue reliability strengthened voluntary RCEs, particularly with central retro-cues, and this increase was primarily-but not exclusively-driven by larger retro-cueing costs. This finding extends previous evidence that voluntary retrospective attention is sensitive to cue reliability (Gunseli et al., 2015 (Gunseli et al., , 2019;; Liu et al., 2024). Importantly, the predominance of increased costs suggests that greater reliability does not simply strengthen the prioritization of the cued representation, but primarily increases the consequences of deprioritizing uncued information according to its expected future relevance. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that cue reliability regulates the fate of non-cued WM representations: when retro-cues are highly reliable, maintaining uncued information becomes less useful, promoting stronger deprioritization or removal, whereas lower-reliability cues favour its continued maintenance (Gunseli et al., 2015 (Gunseli et al., , 2019)). In the present study, these increased costs extended across both drift rate and non-decision time, suggesting that increased reliability strengthens the consequences of voluntary deprioritization for both representational quality and subsequent retrieval processes. At the same time, increasing reliability also enabled a drift-rate benefit with central retro-cues, indicating that sufficiently predictive cues can improve the quality of the prioritized representation in addition to increasing the consequences of invalid prioritization. Critically, this benefit was absent when visual masks were removed, suggesting that, unlike the reliability-related increase in costs, this specific benefit reflected protection of the prioritized representation from subsequent visual interference (e.g., Makovski & Jiang, 2007; van Moorselaar et al., 2015). Thus, the effect of increasing cue reliability on voluntary retrospective attention appears to operate primarily through greater costs of deprioritizing uncued information, rather than through enhanced benefits for prioritized information; nevertheless, when subsequent visual interference is present, higher reliability can additionally benefit the prioritized representation by protecting it from interference.

Second, reducing involuntary retro-cue predictiveness from low to completely non-predictive generally produced little change in RCE magnitude. For both drift rate and non-decision time, RCEs elicited by peripheral retro-cues were comparable between 50% and 33% predictiveness, while comparisons involving central retro-cues were equivalent for non-decision times, but ambiguous in drift rate. Although the present results cannot establish whether participants learned the cue-target contingencies, they demonstrate that above-chance predictiveness was not necessary for the involuntary effects elicited by peripheral retro-cues. This conclusion converges with previous demonstrations that spatially-or-feature corresponding, non-predictive signals can automatically prioritize information maintained in WM even when they provide no strategic information about the upcoming probe (e.g., Fuentes-Guerra et al., 2025a , 2025b , 2026; Han & Ku, 2022; Han et al., 2023; van Ede et al., 2020). Furthermore, this result highlights the importance of employing an explicit instruction in involuntary conditions for ignoring the retro-cue, and it opens the question of whether studies which do not explicitly state this instruction when measuring exogenous attention may unintentionally induce the partial deployment of voluntary attention until participants realize there is no advantage in orienting their attention to the pointed retro-cue direction. At the same time, the contrast between cue formats remains theoretically informative: peripheral retro-cues consistently produced involuntary RCEs, whereas evidence for corresponding RCEs with central retro-cues remained ambiguous. Together with the previous Cipriani studies, and coherently with external attention research (Doallo et al., 2004; Giordano et al., 2009; Gong et al., 2023), this pattern suggests that purely stimulus-driven retrospective orienting is more robustly triggered by peripheral cues spatially corresponding to WM representations than by symbolic overlearned central signals.

Thus, the persistence of peripheral RCEs with completely non-predictive retro-cues supports robust involuntary internal selection through peripheral cues, whereas evidence for central cues remains uncertain, while highlighting the methodological importance of combining non-predictive cues with explicit instructions to ignore them to minimize potential contributions of strategic voluntary orienting.

Third, regarding late visual masking, contrary to what was predicted, eliminating late visual masking generally did not reduce voluntary RCEs, except for ambiguous evidence regarding drift rates with peripheral retro-cues. This limited influence of masking should not, however, be interpreted as evidence against protection from visual interference as a mechanism of retrospective attention. Previous studies indicate that voluntary retro-cues progressively protect prioritized WM representations against subsequent perceptual interference, with this protection becoming largely established within approximately 500-600 ms following cue onset (Makovski & Jiang, 2007; Pinto et al., 2013; van Moorselaar et al., 2015). In the present paradigm, masks appeared approximately 700 ms after retro-cue onset. Consequently, the prioritized representation had sufficient time to reach a protected state before interference occurred, which may explain why removing such late interference had little impact on the overall RCE. Moreover, reliable retro-cueing benefits can persist when visual interference is minimized, indicating that protection from interference contributes to, but does not fully account for, retrospective attentional benefits (Souza et al., 2016). An exception was the drift-rate benefit following voluntary central retro-cues, which was absent when the masks were removed. This suggests that late visual interference can contribute to a specific component of voluntary retro-cueing, even though its effect on the overall voluntary RCE was limited. The restriction of this masking-related benefit to central retro-cues may be related to the contrasting pattern observed with involuntary peripheral retro-cues, whose drift-rate benefits were eliminated under masking. These findings point to effects of late masking that depend on cue format and attentional mode, rather than to a uniform increase in retro-cueing benefits. The effects of late masking on retro-cueing costs warrant separate consideration, as costs have received considerably less attention in prior research on protection from visual interference. Their relative stability across masking conditions suggests that the impaired quality or retrieval of invalidly prioritized representations is not primarily produced by their greater susceptibility to late visual interference. Instead, considered together with the reliability findings, costs may be more closely related to voluntary deprioritization of uncued representations, which is likely due to their removal from working memory (e.g., Gunseli et al., 2019; Oberauer et al., 2001; Williams et al., 2013). Fourth, late visual masking had a more selective influence on involuntary attention elicited by peripheral retro-cues. Although evidence for differences in overall involuntary RCE magnitude between masked and unmasked conditions was inconclusive, decomposition of the RCE revealed opposite effects across diffusion parameters. In drift rate, removing the masks produced a substantial involuntary benefit that was larger than under masking, indicating that late visual interference disrupted the representational-quality advantage generated by peripheral involuntary orienting. This finding is consistent with evidence that non-predictive peripheral events can automatically reactivate or prioritize spatially-or-feature corresponding WM representations and their associated contents (e.g., Fuentes-Guerra et al., 2025a; Han & Ku, 2022; van Ede et al., 2020). The present results further suggest that such a benefit remains susceptible to subsequent perceptual interference. In contrast, benefits in non-decision time were observed under masking but disappeared when masks were removed, consistent with the possibility that prioritization protected the cued representation from interference in a manner that subsequently facilitated its retrieval. Although the precise mechanism underlying this dissociation requires further investigation, the opposing effects across drift rate and non-decision time indicate that visual interference does not simply strengthen or weaken the effects of involuntary retrospective attention as a whole. Rather, it can differentially affect the expression of involuntary prioritization in representational quality and retrieval-related processes.

Finally, self-reports provide complementary evidence concerning voluntary retro-cue use.

In the voluntary task, participants reporting use most of the time or always showed larger non-decision-time RCEs than those reporting use a few times, whereas drift-rate RCEs showed no corresponding association. This suggests that subjective assessments capture differences in voluntary cue use expressed in retrieval-related processes. Whereas Arnicane and Souza (2022) found retro-cueing benefits generally independent of self-rated sustained attention, our results suggest closer associations with reports targeting voluntary selective attention. Future studies could test this possibility with trial-level reports collected before or after the memory probe, as in their study, to assess whether performance evaluation influences this relationship.

Examining whether reported voluntary use increases with cue predictiveness could also clarify whether the enhanced voluntary RCEs relative to the lower-reliability conditions of Cipriani et al. (2024 Cipriani et al. ( , 2025a) ) reflect more frequent voluntary cue use. In the involuntary task, drift-rate RCEs were evident even among participants reporting never intentionally using the retro-cue, while non-decision-time RCEs were evident among those reporting voluntary use a few times.

Importantly, comparisons among participants reporting never, a few times, or half the time provided substantial evidence against differences in RCE magnitude for both parameters. RCEs among participants reporting no intentional use are consistent with evidence that stimulus-driven signals influence WM selection, including effects on spatially associated contents and novel bindings (Fuentes-Guerra et al., 2025a , 2025b , 2026), gaze biases toward stimulus-matching items (van Ede et al., 2020), and electrophysiological signatures of competition with goal-directed selection (Ding et al., 2024; Ester & Nouri, 2023). These findings support an involuntary contribution to the observed effects, rather than their being attributable to voluntary cue use despite instructions. Nevertheless, retrospective reports cannot exclude occasional voluntary orienting or unrecognized cue use. Pooling participants across experiments and comparing unequal response groups also prevents these exploratory analyses from establishing whether associations with reported use are independent of cue format, reliability, or masking. Combining trial-level reports with manipulations of intentional cue use could clarify this relationship.

In sum, the present findings indicate that voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention are differentially sensitive to the conditions under which attentional selection operates.

Voluntary orienting was strengthened by increasing retro-cue reliability, particularly through greater consequences of deprioritizing uncued information, whereas involuntary orienting elicited by peripheral retro-cues remained robust when cue predictiveness was completely eliminated. Late visual masking contributed little to the overall magnitude of retrospective attention, but was associated with a drift-rate benefit following voluntary central retro-cues and altered how involuntary prioritization elicited by peripheral retro-cues was expressed across representational quality and retrieval-related processes. Self-reports additionally linked voluntary cue use to retrieval-related attentional effects and supported involuntary prioritization among participants reporting no voluntary cue use. Together, these findings suggest that cue reliability, cue format, and subsequent visual interference constrain voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention in partly distinct ways rather than simply scaling a common attentional effect.

Conclusions

Across four experiments systematically manipulating retro-cue reliability, cue type, and late visual interference, the present study provides converging evidence that voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention do not differentially prioritize competing perceptual and semantic WM contents. This finding constrains the extent to which principles derived from voluntary and involuntary external attention generalize to the selection of internally maintained representations and suggests that content-specific differences may be attenuated when perceptual and semantic information is integrated within the same object. Beyond this primary finding, the present study identifies distinct conditions governing voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention. Increasing retro-cue reliability strengthened voluntary orienting primarily by increasing the consequences of deprioritizing uncued information, whereas involuntary orienting elicited by peripheral cues remained robust when cue predictiveness was completely eliminated, with corresponding evidence for central cues remaining uncertain. Late visual interference, in turn, contributed little to voluntary RCEs overall, although it enabled a quality benefit following voluntary central retro-cues; it also selectively altered the expression of peripheral involuntary prioritization across representational quality and retrieval-related processes. Self-report analyses further associated more frequent voluntary cue use with larger retrieval-related effects in the voluntary task and indicated automatic attentional effects among participants reporting no intentional cue use in the involuntary task. Together, these findings indicate that voluntary and involuntary retrospective attention can similarly operate across competing representational levels while differing in their sensitivity to predictive relevance, cue format, and late visual interference. Thus, their distinction within WM appears to depend less on the representational level of the selected content than on the conditions under which internal selection is triggered and on the processes through which its consequences are expressed.
