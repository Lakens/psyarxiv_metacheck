Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Leveraging Consensus-Based Ratings to Assess Dimensions of Early Life Adversity: Links to Emotion Regulation and Psychopathology

Dimensional models of early life adversity (ELA) have become increasingly influential in developmental and clinical science. As a theoretically grounded alternative to the cumulative risk approach, dimensional models emphasize specific experiential elements of adversity exposure and their distinct consequences for neurodevelopment and psychopathology (Belsky et al., 2012; Ellis et al., 2022; McLaughlin et al., 2014; McLaughlin & Sheridan, 2016). Commonly studied dimensions of ELA include threat, deprivation, unpredictability, and uncontrollability. Whereas threat refers to experiences involving potential harm (e.g., community violence, physical abuse), deprivation refers to the absence of expected environmental inputs, such as cognitive stimulation (e.g., lower language exposure) or emotional support (e.g., neglect; McLaughlin & Sheridan, 2016). Unpredictability captures irregularity or inconsistency in environmental inputs, such as frequent changes in home environment (Ellis et al., 2022). Uncontrollability reflects experiences where one's actions have little or no impact on outcomes, such as familial conflicts (Cohodes, Odriozola, et al., 2023).

These dimensions are hypothesized to exert unique influences on development. For example, threat may bias emotion processing systems, whereas deprivation may constrain cognitive control and language development (Lambert et al., 2017; Milojevich et al., 2019; Schäfer et al., 2023; Sheridan et al., 2017). Unpredictability may disrupt children's ability to form stable expectations about their environment, leading to heightened vigilance and altered reward learning (Ellis et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). Uncontrollability, in contrast, may impair the development of perceived agency and stress regulation, sensitizing neural and physiological systems to future stressors (Cohodes, Odriozola, et al., 2023; Maier & Seligman, 2016). At the level of broad psychopathology, these pathways converge. Depressive, anxious, and posttraumatic stress symptoms are common sequelae of all four features, consistent with equifinality, and dimensional specificity is expected in mechanism rather than in which syndrome eventually appears (McLaughlin & Lambert, 2017; McLaughlin et al., 2021). We therefore expected each dimension to be associated with depressive, anxious, and posttraumatic stress symptoms, and we did not expect any one dimension to be uniquely associated with any one type of symptoms.

Dimensional models do not hold that threat and deprivation are experientially separable in every sample. They "assume that these features of the environment will be associated with some pathways that are shared" and "some that are unique," and they argue "that some developmental pathways, but not others, are uniquely influenced" (McLaughlin et al., 2021 (McLaughlin et al., , p. 1468)). In community samples, adversities co-occur, so events that load on one dimension are typically accompanied by events that load on others, and separating dimension-specific from shared variance at the person level becomes difficult. Whether adversity dimensions can be distinguished in unselected samples, as opposed to samples specifically chosen for a particular kind of adversity exposure, is an open question in the field (Pollak & Smith, 2026). We therefore expected the four dimensions to be highly correlated when scored at the person level even though there might be specificity at an event level.

Most studies assess whether an event occurred, not how threatening, depriving, uncontrollable, or unpredictable that event was. Some studies have assigned labels of a particular dimension to an event (Lambert et al., 2017; Milojevich et al., 2019; Schaal et al., 2009), ignoring that an event may be characterized by multiple dimensions simultaneously. For example, community violence and physical abuse are routinely categorized as exposure to threat, while poverty and neglect are categorized as deprivation. This approach overlooks the fact that many adverse experiences likely involve multiple experiential dimensions (Smith & Pollak, 2020). For example, emotional abuse is often conceptualized as a form of threat (Schäfer et al., 2023; Sheridan et al., 2017), but it may also reflect significant deprivation in the form of unmet emotional needs or the absence of supportive caregiving. Even when dimensional information is collected, we often rely on self-report (Cohodes, Mandell, et al., 2023), which may conflate dimensional exposure with appraisal and current psychopathology symptoms. Consequently, associations between ELA dimensions and outcomes may be partly driven by variations in how individuals interpret their experiences rather than by the exposures themselves (Danese & Widom, 2020). This is particularly consequential when it comes to understanding how adversity is linked to emotion regulation, as studies have shown that increased perceived controllability may be a product of emotion regulation (Willroth et al., 2025). Research has demonstrated the transdiagnostic role of emotion regulation (Aldao et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2025), potentially serving a developmental pathway from ELA to later mental health outcomes (Herringa et al., 2016; Jenness et al., 2021; Miu et al., 2022; Weissman et al., 2019). However, findings on ELA dimensions and emotion regulation have been mixed, which may reflect these measurement limitations. More specifically, some studies have shown associations between threat and decreased inhibitory control over responses to emotional stimuli (Lambert et al., 2017), increased bias towards negative stimuli (Schäfer et al., 2023), increased use of expressive suppression and rumination (Weissman et al., 2019), and increased use of other avoidance-based emotion regulation strategies (Milojevich et al., 2019). However, deprivation-related exposures have also been linked to regulatory disruptions, including impaired inhibitory control over emotional stimuli and increased amygdala reactivity to emotional faces (Tottenham et al., 2011), as well as greater reliance on avoidance-based strategies (O'Mahen et al., 2015). These overlapping findings likely reflect methodological issues. Studies often differ in how many and which indicators are used to represent each dimension-for example, one study used four multi-item measures for threat but only one for deprivation (Milojevich et al., 2019), and another used twelve items for threat versus six items for deprivation (Schäfer et al., 2023)-potentially inflating the effect of threat. Second, research commonly relies on participants' self-appraisals of adversity features (Cohodes, Mandell, et al., 2023), which can be influenced by current emotion regulation tendencies.

Consensus-based ratings address this by supplying the dimensional weight from independent raters other than the person being assessed, preventing the potential conflation of exposure and appraisal. We refer to these values as consensus-based ratings. They are averaged subjective appraisals contributed by independent raters other than the person whose adversity history is being assessed, not objective properties of the events themselves. Use of consensusbased ratings is intended to reduce the dependency between a person's adversity history and their own appraisal. However, the resulting values remain judgments that reflect shared beliefs about childhood adversity. In one demonstration of this logic, the controllability of participants' recent stressors was assessed by independent raters other than the participants themselves, and cognitive reappraisal ability was associated with lower depressive symptoms among people facing stressors judged by independent raters to be uncontrollable, but not among those facing controllable stressors (Troy et al., 2013). There are at least two benefits of this approach. First, because the weights are attached to events rather than to people, they can be applied to any dataset that records item-level endorsement of the same adversities, including most existing datasets that never collected dimensional appraisals. Second, because the same events can be scored with self-ratings and with consensus ratings in a sample where both are available, the two can be compared directly.

The Present Study

The present preregistered research addresses both limitations through consensus-based dimensional scoring, applied across two different samples to test feasibility and transportability of this approach.

First (Aim 1), we sought to establish consensus-based ratings for four experiential dimensions of ELA-threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability-by applying item-level, sample-averaged ratings to a comprehensive set of indicators drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Brieant et al., 2023) and the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ; Bernstein et al., 1994). Aim 1 was descriptive in nature, and we expected that ELA events would show dissociable dimensional profiles at the event level, reflecting that the four dimensions are conceptually distinct features of adversity.

Second (Aim 2), we examined whether individuals who reported experiencing at least one event (experiencers) within a given adversity category differed in their dimensional ratings compared to those who reported no events (non-experiencers). Drawing on prior work demonstrating the feasibility of using consensus-based ratings to characterize features of stressful experiences (Troy et al., 2013), we hypothesized that for a majority of ELA categories (>50%), sample-averaged ratings would not significantly differ between experiencers and nonexperiencers.

Third (Aim 3), we evaluated associations between dimension scores and emotion regulation and psychopathology outcomes through two complementary tests. The feasibility test, conducted in Sample 1 (Prolific sample; N = 193) and fully preregistered, compared dimensionoutcome associations using participants' self-ratings versus consensus-based ratings. The transportability test, conducted in Sample 2 (Community sample; N = 291), applied consensus values derived from Sample 1 to an independent sample. We preregistered dimension-specific predictions for the emotion regulation outcomes, and non-specific predictions for the psychopathology outcomes. Dimensional models locate specificity in the developmental pathways engaged by adversities rather than in which symptoms eventually emerge, so we expected each dimension to be associated with depressive, anxious, and posttraumatic stress symptoms without predicting which dimension would be uniquely associated with a particular type of symptoms.

Sample 1 aims, hypotheses, and analyses were preregistered (https://osf.io/vybf8/); deviations from preregistration were described in the Methods. Sample 2 was added during revision following feedback from a colleague and thus not preregistered but applied Sample 1derived consensus values without modification. This research contributes to the dimensional ELA literature in two ways: (a) by providing a scalable, reproducible method for deriving dimensional adversity scores in datasets lacking direct dimensional assessments (e.g., ABCD); and (b) by empirically testing whether dimensional specificity is recoverable at the person level in naturalistic samples.

Methods

Overview of Studies

Across two samples of young adults, we developed item-level consensus-based ratings for four experiential dimensions of early life adversity (Sample 1) and tested whether dimension-outcome associations established in Sample 1 transported when applied to a different sample (Sample 2).

Participants Sample 1 (Prolific sample).

Sample 1 participants (N = 200) were recruited via Prolific, an online research platform with documented reliability for behavioral and self-report data (Douglas et al., 2023). Eligibility was restricted to U.S.-based adults aged 18 to 25 through Prolific's prescreening criteria. This age range was selected to minimize the influence of time elapsed since ELA onset on recall accuracy. After providing informed consent, participants completed a 60-minute online battery assessing ELA, psychopathology symptoms, and emotion regulation, and were paid $8 for their participation. All procedures were approved by the Yale University Institutional Review Board (IRB #2000039673). A priori power analysis was conducted as part of our preregistration prior to data collection (https://osf.io/vybf8/). Estimating that at least 15% of participants would have experienced a given adverse event, and aiming for at least 30 experiencers per event, N = 200 was determined to be sufficient to detect a statistically significant difference between experiencers and non-experiencers using a two-tailed independent-samples t-test at α = .05 with 80% power (Kim & Park, 2019).

Sample 2 (Community sample).

Sample 2 participants (N = 291) were drawn from an ongoing intensive longitudinal study of sexual and gender minority young adults across the United States. Participants were recruited through online advertisements and offline flyers distributed via local community partners. Eligibility required participants to be U. Sample 2 differs from Sample 1 in that it contains a community sample of sexual and gender minority young adults. The consensus values applied in Sample 2 were derived from Sample 1, enabling a test of out-of-sample transportability. However, given sample differences, the consensus weights derived in Sample 1 may fit Sample 2 imperfectly. Sample 2 is therefore best understood as a test of whether Sample 1 consensus weights transport to a population whose adversity experiences differ.

Participants who failed attention checks or with incomplete data were excluded from analyses (n = 3 for Sample 1, and n = 7 for Sample 2). Full demographic information for both samples is presented in Table 1.

Measures

The larger battery includes a wide array of measures. Below, we detail only those measures used in the present study.

Early Life Adversity

The present study incorporated items capturing adversity from the ABCD Study (Brieant et al., 2023) and the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ; Bernstein et al., 1994). Adversity items derived from the ABCD Study (Brieant et al., 2023) covered 57 items from eight dimensions: caregiver psychopathology, sociodemographic disadvantage and neighborhood safety, caregiver lack of support, caregiver substance use and separation from biological parents, family arguments, family aggression, trauma, and caregiver lack of supervision. Some items were adjusted to account for a greater range of family constellations (e.g., "My parent did not show their love for me" was changed to "My primary caregiver did not show their love for me").

From adversity items from the CTQ, three items from the Minimization/Denial subscale were excluded because they could not be rated across the four experiential dimensions (e.g., "There was nothing I wanted to change about my family"), resulting in 25 items. Please refer to Supplementary Material S1 for a comprehensive list of items used. Combining adversity measures from ABCD and CTQ, there are a total of 82 adversity items/events.

Because the item pool draws on two instruments developed independently, some content is represented in both sources; neglect-related and household-dysfunction content, in particular, appears in both the ABCD and CTQ item sets. To rule out that shared content as a possible source of the association among dimension scores, we evaluated it directly in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Material S3).

In Sample 1 only, to derive the consensus values, participants also rated each item/event on four dimensions: threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability from 0 to 100.

Threat was defined as feeling afraid of being in danger or hurt by a person, group, thing, or situation. Deprivation was defined as not receiving proper care needed, including concrete needs (food, shelter, clothing) or abstract needs (attention, emotional support, protection).

Uncontrollability was defined as one's actions not impacting the situation's outcome.

Unpredictability was defined as being unable to predict what will happen next based on previous experiences or knowledge of similar situations. These definitions were obtained from the Dimensional Inventory of Stress and Trauma Across the Lifespan (DISTAL; Cohodes et al., 2023) and can also be found in Supplementary Material S2. We note here that these ratings are prospective judgments made by adults about how a child would experience each event, and are not appraisals made during childhood. After rating experiential dimensions, participants completed a checklist indicating whether they had experienced each ABCD and CTQ event before the age of 18. Each item was answered yes or no, replacing the original response formats (e.g., the CTQ's 5-point scale from never true to very often true). CTQ items were prefaced with "When I was growing up," and positively worded CTQ items were reworded to describe the adverse event (e.g., "I felt loved" became "I did not feel loved"; Supplementary Material S1).

Frequency and severity within an event were therefore not assessed, and CTQ subscale scores were not computed. Based on these responses, participants were classified as experiencers (endorsed the event) or non-experiencers (did not endorse the event) on an item-by-item basis, such that a participant could be classified as an experiencer for some events and a nonexperiencer for others.

Psychopathology Symptoms

We measured depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms as indicators of psychopathology.

In Sample 1, depression was assessed with the 21-item Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II, Beck et al., 1996). The item about suicidality was excluded due to ethical considerations, resulting in a total of 20 items. Participants selected one out of four graded statements (scored 0 to 3) representing symptom severity. Internal consistency was excellent (α = .95). Anxiety disorder symptom severity was assessed using the 44-item Screen for Adult Anxiety Related Disorders (SCAARED, Angulo et al., 2017). Items were rated on a three-point scale from 0 (Not True or Hardly Ever True) to 2 (Very True or Often True). Internal consistency was excellent (α = .97). PTSD symptoms were measured with the 20-item PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015), assessing symptomatology over the past month. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (Not at all) to 4 (Extremely). Internal consistency was excellent (α = .96).

In Sample 2, depression and anxiety were assessed with the 30-item Mood and Anxiety Symptom Questionnaire (MASQ-D30; Wardenaar et al., 2010). Symptoms were rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (Not at all) to 5 (Extremely). Depression was indexed by the Anhedonic Depression subscale (α = .90) and anxiety by the Anxious Arousal subscale (α = .85). PTSD symptoms were assessed with the same 20-item PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Blevins et al., 2015) used in Sample 1 (α = .95).

Emotion Regulation

In both samples, cognitive reappraisal was assessed using the 6-item Cognitive Reappraisal subscale from the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ; Gross & John, 2003).

Items were scored on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree).

Internal consistency was good (α = .89 in Sample 1, α = .83 in Sample 2).

In both samples, difficulties in emotion regulation were assessed using the 18-item Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale Short-Form (DERS-SF; Kaufman et al., 2016). Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (Almost Never [0-10%]) to 5 (Almost Always [91-100%]). Internal consistency was good (α =.89 in Sample 1, α = .88 in Sample 2).

Depression and anxiety were assessed with different instruments in the two samples.

Sample 1 used the BDI-II and the SCAARED; Sample 2 used the Anhedonic Depression and

Anxious Arousal subscales of the MASQ-D30. The PCL-5, the DERS, and the ERQ were administered in both samples.

Data Analysis

R 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2025) was used for data analysis. Analyses were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/vybf8/).

For Aim 1, we obtained descriptive ratings of threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability for each ELA item and category (e.g., physical abuse), and used paired-samples t-tests to compare dimensions within each category. For Aim 2, we used two one-sided equivalence tests as the primary test of whether experiencer and non-experiencer ratings were equivalent on each event × dimension combination, and report independent-samples t-tests for completeness. For Aim 3, we computed sum, mean, and maximum dimension scores and used multiple regression to compare models based on individuals' self-ratings versus consensus-based ratings. Self-ratings reflected an individual's appraisals of events they personally experienced.

Aim 1 describes the item pool using ratings pooled across all of Sample 1. Aim 2 compares ratings across experiencers and non-experiencers. Aim 3 weights each participant's endorsed events using consensus values computed from non-experiencers only, that is, from Sample 1 participants who did not report having experienced that event. Non-experiencer values were used so that the weight applied to an event is not partly composed of appraisals from people who lived through it, and so that the mixture of experiencer and non-experiencer contributions does not vary with event prevalence.

Equivalence bounds were set at an effect size of interest of Hedges' g = 0.50, corresponding to median raw bounds of 13.19 to 13.92 points on the 0 to 100 rating scale across dimensions. Because experiencer subsamples are small for rare events, a bound of g = 0.40 renders 327 of 328 tests inconclusive and therefore yields no usable information; at g = 0.50, 239 of 328 tests are conclusive (238 equivalent and 1 different), with 89 inconclusive, and at g = 0.60, 286 are conclusive (285 equivalent and 1 different). Differences smaller than the bound are not necessarily negligible in relation to the spread of the item pool, and equivalence at g = 0.50 should be read as ruling out large experiencer effects rather than as establishing exact agreement.

Consensus-based exposure scores.

For each of the 82 candidate events, we computed the mean rating on each dimension across Sample 1 participants who had not experienced that event, yielding, for each event, ratings from 0 to 100 on threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability. These ratings were applied to both samples. Participants' adversity reports were binary at the item level, indicating whether each event had been experienced. Let Ei denote the set of events endorsed by participant i, let ni = |Ei| denote the number of events endorsed, and let wj,d denote the consensus weight of event j on dimension d. Three preregistered scores, Sumi,d, Meani,d, and Maxi,d were computed for each participant and each dimension:

The denominator of the mean score is the number of events the participant endorsed, not the number of items we asked. The mean score therefore indexes average dimensional severity conditional on exposure rather than cumulative burden; the sum score confounds dimensional severity with the number of events endorsed; and the maximum score is the highest consensus value on that dimension among the participant's endorsed events, which need not be the event the participant found most distressing. Sum scores were retained in the correlational analyses but not in the regression models, where they were collinear with the number of endorsed events and with one another to a degree that made coefficients uninterpretable (VIF > 100).

Seven Sample 1 participants endorsed no adversity items. Because all three scores are defined only over endorsed events, these participants have no defined dimensional severity and were excluded from all Aim 3 models; we chose not to assign these participants values of zero because this would conflate the absence of exposure with low dimensional severity among exposed individuals. The analytic sample for all Aim 3 models is therefore N = 193 in Sample 1 and N = 291 in Sample 2, the latter containing no participants without endorsed events.

All Aim 3 models were repeated with the total number of endorsed events entered as a covariate. For each outcome, we first fit a model containing event count alone, then added threat and deprivation, and evaluated the incremental variance explained by the dimensional scores.

These models were fit in both samples and under both scoring methods, yielding 20 models in total.

Sample 2 constitutes an out-of-sample application. Consensus values derived in Sample 1

were applied to Sample 2 participants' endorsed events; Sample 2 therefore tests the transportability of consensus-based associations.

Demographic covariates were not included in primary regression models, as our focus was on the feasibility of consensus-based scoring and dimension-outcome associations rather than the unique contribution of dimensions beyond demographics.

Deviations from Preregistered Analyses

High multicollinearity among sum scores of experiential dimensions was observed (VIF > 100), potentially due to their significant overlap with the number of ELAs. Additionally, high multicollinearity was seen when including uncontrollability and unpredictability in regression models (VIF > 50) due to their high correlations with each other and their respective associations with threat. Thus, for Aim 3, while we still reported correlations among all variables as preregistered, we decided to include only threat and deprivation and restricted our analyses to either mean or max scores for regression analyses.

Results

Aim 1: Descriptive Ratings of Experiential Dimensions

We first obtained sample-averaged ratings of threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability for each categorical adversity type derived from the ABCD Study and CTQ.

Given the large number of possible comparisons, α = .001 was adopted as a conservative significance threshold. As shown in Figure 1, mean ratings revealed a relatively clear distinction between threat and deprivation across categorical types of adversity. Threat ratings exceeded deprivation ratings for trauma (Mdiff = 13.27, 95% CI [10.77, 15.78], t(199) = 10.44, g = 0.74 [0.58, 0.89]), physical abuse (Mdiff = 5.17, t(199) = 6.05, g = 0.43 [0.28, 0.57]), and sexual abuse (Mdiff = 3.89, t(199) = 3.70, g = 0.26 [0.12, 0.40]). Conversely, deprivation ratings exceeded threat ratings for caregiver lack of support (Mdiff = -26.44 [-30.82, -22.06], t(199) = -11.91, g = -0.84 [-1.00, -0.68]), emotional neglect (Mdiff = -22.07 [-26.32, -17.82], t(199) = -10.24, g = -0.72 [-0.88, -0.57]), emotional abuse (Mdiff = -16.62 [-20.29, -12.96], t(199) = -8.94, g = -0.63 [-0.78, -0.48]), physical neglect (Mdiff = -15.71 [-18.81, -12.61], t(199) = -9.98, g = -0.70 [-0.86, -0.55]), caregiver lack of supervision (Mdiff = -10.07 [-12.85, -7.29], t(199) = -7.13, g = -0.50 [-0.65, -0.36]), and socioeconomic disadvantage (Mdiff = -8.11 [-10.35, -5.87], t(199) = -7.14, g = -0.50 [-0.65, -0.36]). Effect sizes ranged from small to large, with all ps < .001. Across most categories, paired comparisons of uncontrollability and unpredictability were nonsignificant or small, consistent with the conceptual overlap of these dimensions. However, caregiver lack of support showed a robust dissociation (Mdiff = 9.38, g = 0.48 [0.33, 0.62], p < .001), indicating that the inability of a caregiver to provide support was perceived as uncontrollable yet relatively predictable. Detailed ratings of threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability by events can be found in Supplementary Material S1.

Split-half reliability of the item-level consensus values, treating raters as the sampling unit, ranged from .92 to .98 across the four dimensions, with a mean standard error of approximately 2.3 points on the 0 to 100 scale (Supplementary Material S4). Agreement between any two individual raters was low (ICC(2,1) = .08 to .29 across dimensions; Supplementary Material S4), so the reliability of the consensus values rests on averaging across raters.

Aim 2: Concordance Between Experiencer and Non-Experiencer Ratings

To examine whether sample-averaged ratings capture perceptions of adversity comparable between experiencers and non-experiencers, we compared threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability ratings across these events using two one-sided equivalence tests. Equivalence at the moderate-effect threshold was established for 238 of 328 comparisons (72.6%); only 1 of 328 comparisons reached statistical significance, and 89 of 328 (27.1%) were inconclusive. Consistent with our preregistered hypothesis, conventional independent-samples t-tests, reported for completeness, showed no significant differences between experiencers and non-experiencers for most events (89.93%). Detailed comparisons for each individual event can be found in Supplementary Material S1.

The 89 inconclusive tests (27.1%) were not distributed at random across events. They concentrated on the most severe events (e.g., being shot, stabbed, or beaten brutally; sexual coercion; witnessing terrorism), which are also the least prevalent and therefore had the smallest experiencer subsamples (n = 20 to 30), and in these comparisons the point estimates trended in a consistent direction: experiencers rated the event as less severe than non-experiencers did (g range from -0.4 to -0.9). A mixed-effects meta-regression confirmed this. Pooling across dimensions with dimension entered as a fixed effect, the experiencer minus non-experiencer discrepancy became more negative as the event's consensus severity increased (b = -0.104 per consensus point, z = -4.59, p < .001). The corresponding slopes were negative in every dimension: threat b = -0.062 (p = .052), deprivation b = -0.141 (p = .004), uncontrollability b = -0.161 (p = .018), and unpredictability b = -0.162 (p = .004). The magnitude of the discrepancy remains small in absolute terms, with all four pooled mean differences falling within 1.4 points on the 0 to 100 scale, but the pattern is systematic and runs counter to the assumption that experience status is irrelevant for the most severe events (Supplementary Material S6).

Prevalence was also related to the discrepancy pooled across dimensions (b = 0.030, p = .011) and within uncontrollability (b = 0.049, p = .031) and unpredictability (b = 0.074, p = .002), but not within threat or deprivation (Figure 2). Residual heterogeneity was modest (I² = 14% to 33%), indicating that most of the item-to-item variability (67% to 86%) in discrepancies reflects sampling error.

Because item-level values are used as weights rather than interpreted individually, we additionally evaluated agreement at the level of participant scores. Substituting experiencer for non-experiencer weights left mean scores highly consistent (r = .86 to .97 across dimensions) and maximum scores less so (r = .72 to .94). By this criterion, the mean-score results are robust to rater experience status, whereas maximum-score results are more sensitive to it, since the maximum is determined by a single endorsed event. We then refitted the Aim 3 models using experiencer-derived values throughout, holding items, outcomes, scoring, and model specification identical (Table S5.3). Deprivation remained positive and significant in all ten models under both value sources. We retain non-experiencer values as reported, since they rest on more raters per item and are correspondingly more reliably estimated.

Aim 3: Experiential Dimensions and Emotion Regulation Outcomes

Feasibility Test (Sample 1): comparing self-ratings versus consensus-based ratings within Sample 1.

In Sample 1, we derived the consensus values and examined how experiential dimensions were associated with emotion regulation and psychopathology outcomes. Table 2 presents correlations between experiential dimensions and measures of emotion regulation and psychopathology, separately for self-ratings and consensus-based ratings in Sample 1 and for consensus-based ratings in Sample 2. As mentioned in the Deviations from Preregistered Analyses section, we excluded sum scores and the dimensions of uncontrollability and unpredictability from regression models due to multicollinearity (VIFs > 50). Our primary analyses focused on mean and maximum scores of threat and deprivation (two types of scoring), evaluated separately using exposure scores calculated by self-and consensus-based ratings (two types of rating), resulting in four regression models per outcome. Table 3 shows the side-by-side comparisons between models using self-and consensus-based ratings.

Compared with self-ratings, exposure scores derived from consensus-based ratings explained more variance in emotion regulation (self: R² = .00 to .03; consensus: R² = .03 to .15) and psychopathology (self: R² = .02 to .10; consensus: R² = .16 to .25). Because consensus values are fixed at the item level, consensus-based scores are more closely tied to the number of events a participant endorsed than self-rated scores are. Differences in explained variance between the two scoring approaches should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of superior dimensional validity. Models that control for the number of endorsed events are reported below. Consensusbased deprivation ratings, indexed by either mean or maximum scores, were consistently associated with emotion dysregulation (maximum: B = 0.69, p < .001; mean: B = 1.25, p < .001), depression, anxiety, and PTSD (ps < .001), including PTSD specifically (maximum: B = 0.93, p < .001; mean: B = 1.81, p < .001). Deprivation was also associated with lower cognitive reappraisal (maximum: B = -0.19, p = .026; mean: B = -0.31, p = .007). When exposure was instead scored from participants' own self-ratings, associations were weak overall: maximum threat was associated with emotion dysregulation (B = 0.18, p = .037) and PTSD (B = 0.32, p = .007), mean deprivation was associated with depression (B = 0.13, p = .049) and anxiety (B = 0.32, p = .004), and neither dimension was associated with cognitive reappraisal.

Threat and deprivation were moderately associated (r = .514 under mean scoring, VIF = 1.36; r = .660 under maximum scoring, VIF = 1.77), and the negative threat coefficients in Table 3 are a suppression effect arising from that association rather than a protective association between threat severity and symptoms. At the zero-order level, threat was positively correlated with every psychopathology outcome (Table 2). These coefficients should not be read as evidence that higher threat severity is associated with fewer symptoms. Consistent with a suppression interpretation, the negative threat coefficients are confined to mean scoring; under maximum scoring, threat coefficients are null across outcomes in both samples (for example, in Sample 1, DERS B = 0.017, p = .903 and SCAARED B = 0.030, p = .884, compared with B = -0.381, p = .016 and B = -0.745, p = .002 under mean scoring).

Rescoring the dimensions within each source instrument separately produced essentially the same association between threat and deprivation as the combined pool (mean scoring: r = .462 for ABCD items only and r = .461 for CTQ items only, versus r = .514 combined; maximum scoring: r = .652 and r = .674, versus r = .660). Because the two single-instrument pools share no items, overlapping content between the ABCD and CTQ sets does not account for the association among dimensions (Supplementary Material S3).

Transportability Test (Sample 2): applying Sample 1-derived consensus values to an independent sample. To test whether dimension-outcome associations established in Sample 1

transported to an independent sample, we applied Sample 1-derived consensus values to Sample 2 (N = 291). Consensus-based deprivation was consistently associated with emotional dysregulation (maximum: B = 0.40, p = .008; mean: B = 0.55, p = .005), anxiety, and PTSD (ps < .01); model R² ranged from .04 to .13. Deprivation was also associated with decreased cognitive reappraisal in the mean-score model (B = -0.05, p = .007). Threat showed no positive associations with outcomes; in the mean-score models, it was negatively associated with depression (B = -0.04, p < .001) and positively associated with reappraisal (B = 0.05, p < .001), the same suppression pattern described for Sample 1.

In Sample 2, deprivation predicted anhedonic depression under mean scoring (B = 0.04, p = .001) but not under maximum scoring (B = 0.01, p = .556), whereas in Sample 1 deprivation predicted depressive symptoms under both scoring methods. The discrepancy appears specific to the depression measure rather than to the sample: under maximum scoring in Sample 2, deprivation remained a significant predictor of emotion dysregulation, anxious arousal, and PTSD symptoms.

The number of endorsed events predicted symptom outcomes strongly in Sample 1 (R² = .27 to .40 for depression, anxiety, PTSD, and emotion dysregulation) and cognitive reappraisal weakly (R² = .02). Beyond event count, dimensional scores explained significant incremental variance in 3 of the 20 models, all under mean scoring: anxiety symptoms in Sample 1 (ΔR2 = .022, p = .032), anhedonic depression in Sample 2 (ΔR2 = .057, p < .001), and cognitive reappraisal in Sample 2 (ΔR2 = .048, p < .001). Under maximum scoring, dimensional scores did not add significant incremental variance in any model (all incremental p ≥ .067; the smallest was emotion dysregulation in Sample 2, ΔR2 = .018, p = .067, deprivation coefficient B = 0.31, p = .049). All 20 models are reported in Supplementary Material S7.

Discussion

This study used consensus-based ratings to quantify experiential dimensions of ELAincluding threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability-across a large, diverse set of ELA items obtained from the ABCD Study as well as the CTQ. We do not claim that consensus-based scores measure adversity dimensions better than participants' own dimensional appraisals. The comparison in Sample 1 is an in-sample one, conducted in the sample from which the consensus values were derived. These findings demonstrate the feasibility and potential contribution of consensus-based scoring. Dimensional self-appraisals are absent from nearly all existing datasets that contain adversity checklists and cannot be collected retrospectively from cohorts who have already completed studies. Consensus-based values can be applied to those datasets. By offering consensus-based estimates of ELA dimensions at the item level and making them publicly available (see Supplementary Materials S1), this approach enables more theory-driven and standardized measurement of experiential dimensions of ELA, helping to bridge the gap between conceptual frameworks and measurement.

Transportability was supported in the sense that deprivation scores related to internalizing symptoms and to emotion regulation difficulties in Sample 2, as they did in Sample 1. Sample 2 consists of sexual and gender minority young adults, who differ from the general-population rating sample in racial and ethnic composition and in exposure to minority-specific stressors, including experiences that the item pool does not capture and that a general-population rating sample may appraise differently. Future studies should assess consensus values across diverse populations to further estimate transportability.

Experience status is not irrelevant to dimensional appraisal. It is systematically related to it, most clearly for the most severe and less prevalent events, where people who lived through an event rate it as less extreme than people who have not. Because this tendency is small in absolute terms and because consensus values here are drawn from non-experiencers, its practical consequence for the scores is limited; it does, however, mean that consensus values for severe events should be read as anticipated rather than as recalled severity.

The following comparison of mean and maximum scoring across dimensions is exploratory, as preregistered, and the interpretations offered are post-hoc and better considered as hypotheses for future work rather than as definitive explanations. Importantly, the study also sheds light on how different scoring strategies may capture distinct facets of adversity exposure.

For threat, uncontrollability, and unpredictability, maximum scores, the highest consensus value among a participant's endorsed events, were more strongly associated with emotion dysregulation and psychopathology than mean scores. For these dimensions, the most extreme endorsed event, as rated by others, was more closely related to outcomes than the average across endorsed events; this does not identify which event the participant experienced as most consequential. In contrast, for deprivation, both mean and maximum scores were consistently associated with outcomes (the exceptions were anhedonic depression and cognitive reappraisal in Sample 2, which were associated with the mean score only), indicating that both the highest and the average consensus-rated absence of needed input (e.g., emotional support) among endorsed events are associated with difficulties in regulation and mental health. This distinction highlights the importance of selecting scoring methods (maximum versus mean) that are theoretically matched to the nature of the adversity dimension.

One post-hoc explanation for this pattern is the difference in depression measures. The MASQ-D30 Anhedonic Depression subscale is narrowly focused on low positive affect and loss of interest and pleasure, whereas the BDI-II used in Sample 1 covers a broader range of depressive symptoms. A maximum score reflects the single most deprivation-severe event a participant endorsed, and appears less well-suited than a mean across endorsed events to predicting a narrow anhedonic phenotype. Since this is a post-hoc interpretation, future tests of these findings are necessary.

Notably, threat showed relatively weaker associations with emotion dysregulation, a pattern that may initially seem inconsistent with dominant theories linking threat to heightened emotional reactivity (Lambert et al., 2017; Milojevich et al., 2019; Schäfer et al., 2023; Weissman et al., 2019). However, our findings are also consistent with research positioning deprivation-in addition to threat-as a key risk factor for impaired emotion regulation (O'Mahen et al., 2015; Tottenham et al., 2011). In contrast, threat may be more closely related to hypervigilance, salience bias, or physiological reactivity, which are not fully captured by the global self-report measures used in the present study. These results underscore the importance of aligning the dimension of adversity and the specific regulatory processes under investigation.

These findings also highlight the need for careful measurement considerations, not only in how adversity dimensions are defined, but also in how they are scored. Mean scores index the typical dimensional severity of the adversities a person reported; they are not a measure of cumulative burden.

Taken together, the four dimension scores share large variance at the person level in unselected community samples, and dimension-specific and general severity effects cannot be separated in these samples. Uncontrollability and unpredictability could not be estimated alongside threat and deprivation, and although threat and deprivation are separable, the negative threat coefficients that appear under mean scoring should be read as suppression rather than as protective effects; they do not appear under maximum scoring, where threat is null on every outcome in both samples. We therefore do not interpret any coefficient in Table 3 as evidence of a dimension-specific result. Because adversities co-occur in the general population, testing whether dimensions exert distinct mechanistic effects requires samples selected to contain individuals with distinctive exposure profiles. Our scoring approach may offer one way in which researchers can identify unique groups of individuals.

Consensus-based dimensional scores rarely explained variance beyond a simple count of endorsed events. This is consistent with what others have previously stated about dimensional frameworks. Cumulative-risk scoring "has proven informative when it comes to prediction" but is "rather lacking when it comes to identifying mechanistic processes" (McLaughlin et al., 2021 (McLaughlin et al., , p. 1464)), and the dimensional model was advanced to address the second of these, not to outperform cumulative risk on the first. Dimensional scoring is expected to identify which features of adversity are implicated in a given developmental pathway; it is not expected to account for more symptom variance than the number of adversities a person experienced. The present results are consistent with that division: dimensional information adds little where the question is total variance explained, and its value lies in characterizing the adversities under study. Dimensional scores added significant variance beyond the count only in 3 of 20 models, all under mean scoring. Consistent with McLaughlin et al. (2021), the present results do not support dimensional scores as a substitute for a count when prediction is the goal; their value lies in making dimensional information available in datasets that contain adversity checklists but no dimensional assessment.

The current study has several limitations. First, the most consequential limitation concerns what the consensus values represent. Raters were young adults judging how threatening, depriving, uncontrollable, and unpredictable each event would be for a child, not children appraising events as they occurred. The consensus values are therefore adult retrospective and hypothetical judgments about childhood experience, and they may diverge from the appraisals children actually form in the moment. Second, both samples were drawn from nonclinical community populations of young adults (Sample 1: Prolific; Sample 2: Online advertisement and community flyers). Adversity has been linked to a wide range of physical and mental health outcomes across diverse clinical and trauma-exposed groups (Kessler et al., 2010).

Whether the consensus-based dimensional scoring approach developed here captures meaningful variation in treatment-seeking populations where adversity exposure is often more severe and chronic remains an open empirical question and an important direction for future work. Third, our design is cross-sectional, with adversity exposure, emotion regulation, and psychopathology symptoms assessed concurrently; longitudinal designs are needed to establish temporal precedence and to test whether consensus-based dimensional exposure prospectively predicts changes in regulation and symptomatology. Fourth, ELA exposure was assessed via retrospective self-report of childhood events, which may be subject to recall bias and current mood-state effects; the consensus-based scoring approach mitigates but does not eliminate this concern, since event endorsement still depends on participant recall. Fifth, the high collinearity among the four dimensions observed at the person level constrains our ability to make clean specificity claims about which dimension is uniquely associated with outcomes; while we consider this collinearity as itself an empirically and theoretically meaningful finding consistent with recent consensus in the field (Pollak & Smith, 2026), we acknowledge that future work using alternative analytic approaches (e.g., person-centered analyses, latent profile analysis) may be needed to disentangle dimensional contributions. Sixth, while our consensus values did not differ by race and ethnicity, a small set of events differ in ratings by sex. A larger sample is needed in future research to develop group-specific consensus values. Seventh, the consensus values were derived in a general-population sample and applied to a sexual and gender minority sample. We could not evaluate whether identity-related stress shifts the appraised severity of specific events, because Sample 2 did not provide event-level dimensional ratings. Eighth, the two samples did not use the same depression and anxiety measures. Sample 1 used the BDI-II and the SCAARED, Sample 2 used the Anhedonic Depression and Anxious Arousal subscales of the MASQ-D30, and only the PCL-5, DERS, and ERQ were common to both. Differences between samples in the magnitude of dimensionoutcome associations therefore confound measurement with sample composition. Therefore, Sample 2 is best considered a test of the transportability of consensus-based associations rather than as a replication. Finally, the consensus values were generated only in Sample 1. Although they are internally stable and reproduce across raters within that sample, independent reproduction of the item-level values in a separate rating sample, ideally one differing in age, culture, and adversity history, remains necessary before they are treated as transportable consensus. We regard the present values as a first, openly available set to be revised as additional rating samples accumulate, and we have shared the full item-level table publicly for that purpose.

Future research should build on this work by using multi-method, developmentally sensitive approaches to emotion regulation, such as ecological momentary assessment (EMA) or behavioral tasks, which can more precisely capture the dynamic and flexibility of emotion regulation across contexts. It will also be important to consider the role of developmental timing: previous studies linking threat to regulatory disruptions have largely focused on children and adolescents, whereas our young adults reported on adversity at a greater temporal distance, and compensatory responses may attenuate associations between ELA and emotion regulation.

Person-centered approaches offer a potential alternative around the limits of variable-centered models in this setting. Latent profile analysis could identify subgroups characterized by distinct configurations of threat, deprivation, uncontrollability, and unpredictability, and dimensional specificity could then be tested by comparing outcomes across profiles rather than by partitioning variance among correlated predictors. Such an analysis would require a sample considerably larger than those studied here to identify classes stably and would benefit from oversampling of individuals with configurations that are rarer in community samples, such as high deprivation with low threat.

Ultimately, clarifying whether different types of adversity shape distinct emotional processes and identifying when and for whom these associations are most pronounced will be critical for designing targeted, mechanism-informed interventions.
