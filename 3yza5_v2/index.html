What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Research showed that social changes elicit threat, which is considered a negative response to be avoided to improve intergroup relations. However, this approach overlooks the transformative potential of positive stress (challenge) for progressive social changes. To address this gap, we investigated whether, and if so, under what conditions, social change is perceived as threatening or challenging. Results from a representative Dutch sample (N = 411) revealed that social change is more threatening than the status quo. Furthermore, the instrumental (vs. symbolic) aspects of change, and its perceived demands and uncertainty heighten threat, while the ingroup and system (vs. personal) dimensions of change, and social resources and support buffer against threat. Our results offer insight into what factors promote more adaptive (challenge) rather than maladaptive (threat) responses to social changes. They encourage a critical perspective on social change, framing threat not as inherently negative but as tensions to be navigated.

The Transformative Side of Societal Stress: Threat and Challenge Responses to Progressive Social Changes

In 2020, following years of advocacy and protests, the Dutch government proposed an amendment to the Gender Recognition Act to simplify the process for legally changing one's gender (Tweede Kamer der Staten-Generaal, 2020). Despite wide support from the Dutch

LGBTQ+ community, the proposed changes faced substantial backlash and ultimately did not pass (Slevin, 2022). To this day, grassroots organizations continue to struggle for improved healthcare for transgender people, showcasing their ongoing commitment toward trans rights (Westerink, 2024). This illustrates that, while social change 1 is crucial for addressing systemic inequalities, advancing minority liberation often involves overcoming entrenched opposition and sustaining efforts despite setbacks, underscoring the importance of understanding stress and coping associated with social change.

In social psychology, it is well documented that social changes can trigger negative stress ('threat') and hostility (Scheepers & Ellemers, 2018; Scheepers & Knight, 2020).

However, this field of study maintains a narrow perspective on how to address the stress of social change, mostly emphasizing threat avoidance strategies, while disregarding the transformative power of more positive stress ('challenge'). This might stem from previous work somewhat overlooking the complex range of psychological and societal factors that render change stressful. This paper addresses these limitations by providing a more comprehensive, and critical perspective on stress and coping with hierarchy-disruptive social change (vs. status quo), where with hierarchy-disruptive social change we mean material and systemic social change that aims to dismantle unjust hierarchies, and that is rooted in minoritized communities' 1 Social change refers to systemic transformations that shift power, influencing cultural norms, group dynamics, and societal institutions.

This concept is well-established in social psychology, and this paper specifically explores progressive changes designed to improve the status of minorities.

objectives and social movements initiatives. To achieve this, we investigated the role that personal relevance, demands, and resources appraisals play in eliciting either threat or challenge. Beyond such basic processes, we also addressed three levels on which such changes have an impact (personal, ingroup, system), and two facets of change (instrumental vs. symbolic). Finally, we explored the differential effects of several types of appraisal (personal vs. social demands and resources, uncertainty, support) on experiencing social change as a challenge or as a threat.

Beyond Avoidance: The Role of Stress in Social Change

Social changes have been associated with a loss of normative and structural integrity in society (de la Sablonnière, 2017), often leading to threat, uncertainty and anxiety (Scheepers & Ellemers, 2018).

In line with this perspective, Scheepers (2009) demonstrated that when a rigid hierarchy becomes unstable in a minimal group scenario, high-status members, in particular, felt psychophysiologically threatened by the potential loss of their privileged position. Jetten et al. (2017) found that during economic instability, people across socioeconomic statuses reported greater fear for the future and distrust of others, with wealthier individuals potentially reacting through increased competition and individualism to preserve the status quo.

Threat of social change was also observed among White American individuals interacting with a high-SES Latino confederate (Mendes et al., 2007), and in Dutch men discussing progressive gender roles with women (Scheepers et al., 2009), specifically in the form of cardiovascular responses. In the first study, heightened threat was linked to more negative treatment and avoidance of the confederate; in the second, it was associated with potentially suppressed sexism, suggesting men may conceal it when threatened in intergroup interactions.

Taken together, these findings illustrate that social changes are often threatening, especially for dominant group members, and may have negative effects on their attitudes toward oppressed communities and progressive initiatives. This has primarily led to recommendations for avoiding such threat responses, employing strategies such as dualidentity formation, or scripted interactions (Avery et al., 2009; Scheepers et al., 2014). Such an approach underscores a broader tendency within psychology to prioritize societal harmony and the absence of conflict as ideal states, while mainly considering negative emotions such as anxiety as undesirable (see Wright & Baray, 2012). However, such efforts aim to reduce symptomatic discomfort and thereby risk maintaining the status quo rather than address the range of psychological and structural factors underlying responses to change.

A more critical view of stress and coping with social change highlights the constructive role of conflict and negative affect in driving progress. According to critical theory (Marx & Engels, 1848; Bohrer, 2019), through the clash of opposing forces (i.e., oppression and resistance), exploitative power structures are problematized, opening the possibility of more equitable and just social relations. As a result, threat may be an unavoidable feature of successful progressive social changes.

Within this perspective, approach-based coping, rather than avoidance-based coping, with the stress of social change could represent a transformative opportunity, accompanied by challenge. In fact, exposure to stressful events can also enhance resilience and well-being, as individuals who experienced and actively coped with adversity report better functionality than those who did not at all (Rutter, 2012). Thus, while challenge remains a taxing, sometimes aversive, experience, it may nonetheless serve as a catalyst for adaptive functioning and growth when approached constructively.

However, limited empirical evidence has been accumulated on how people cope with hierarchy-disruptive social changes, compared to individual life events and encounters. As a result, central to the current paper's approach is appreciating the existence of both maladaptive (threat) and adaptive (challenge) forms of stress of systemic and progressive social change, manifesting in the general population.

A Threat and Challenge Perspective on Stress of Social Change

Stress responses have been examined using physiological measures based on the Biopsychosocial Model of Challenge and Threat (BPS-CT). This model was first introduced by Blascovich and Tomaka (1996) to explain cardiovascular reactivity and performance during stressful situations. According to the BPS-CT, whenever an individual engages in a motivated performance task, they may enter a state of negative stress (threat) or positive stress (challenge). A sense of engagement with the task is a prerequisite of threat and challenge, which for the case of social change can be conceptualized as the personal relevance of the issue at hand.

Threat negatively affects performance, as well as health outcomes and psychological well-being, whereas challenge positively relates to these indicators. More specifically, while both states may bring about negative emotions (e.g., anger for challenge, fear for threat), and increased reactivity of both the body (i.e., increased cardiovascular responses) and mind (i.e. cognitive engagement), only threat is considered a maladaptive response (Blascovich & Mendes, 2010). Applying the BPS-CT to social change thus has the potential of acknowledging a fuller spectrum of stress-related outcomes, some of which may be constructive for progressive goals.

Given the novel application of BPS-CT to hierarchy-disruptive social change, we used a self-report approach to conceptually validate this extension. While physiological measures are central to traditional BPS-CT research, they are impractical for large samples and offer limited insight into subjective appraisals. Our scale captures psychological evaluations of threat and challenge in response to social change. The perception of threat or challenge depends on whether the individual's resources are deemed sufficient to meet the demands of the situation.

In the context of social change, individuals navigate shifts in cultural norms, power struggles against the oppression of marginalized groups, and sociopolitical 'performances', for instance when voicing one's opinion during a discussion, or participating in protests. These dynamics can lead to various existential and epistemic stressors, like uncertainties and exposure to psychological and physical violence (Cropley & Jost, 2024; de la Sablonnière, 2017), encompassing possible demands of social change. Uncertainty in particular may play a crucial role in shaping perceptions of threat during social change, as individuals often face unprecedented circumstances.

To effectively manage these demands, factors such as support networks, and personal resilience play a critical role, encompassing forms of resources. Given the novelty, severity, and systemic character of social change, individuals are less likely to have immediate access to the resources needed to effectively cope with these exacerbated demands. In fact, relevant knowledge and abilities are considered vital resources to cope with demands, as well as social support, and personal disposition (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996). Yet, the assumed availability of such resources may be unrealistic when faced with the unprecedented nature of many hierarchy-disruptive social changes.

As a result, we hypothesized that hierarchy-dispersive social changes would elicit a stronger self-reported threat response compared to the existing status quo within a society. The Multilevel Nature of Stress of Social Change

According to Power Basis Theory (Pratto & Stewart, 2020), power struggles between dominant and minoritized groups are embedded in multi-level systems shaped by history, politics, and culture. Social change is therefore not self-contained but impacts institutions and affects personal, group, and systemic levels (de la Sablonnière, 2017).

That is, whereas personal-level change can be seen in shifts in individual well-being and the adoption of new behaviors, ingroup-level effects are observable in the shifts in status and relations among social groups and socioeconomic classes in society, such as achieving more equitable rights, or dynamics of discrimination and resistance. Finally, systemic effects are observable in the dismantlement, reform, or introduction of legislations, institutions, and norms, such as transitioning from insurance-based healthcare to universal healthcare.

Personal, (in)group, and systemic effects of social changes thus all manifest in individuals' psychological responses, influencing stress levels and shaping the cognitive appraisal of threat and challenge. In this context, the way individuals perceive and respond to these changes is significantly affected by the resources available to them. When individuals recognize the psychological and material support that their communities can offer, they may feel better equipped to handle the demands of social change, rather than relying solely on their potentially more limited personal resources. This highlights the importance of considering not just personal demands and resources, but also broader social resources, when studying the impact of social changes.

Previous research on social change has focused mainly on individual interactions or personal effects of systemic shifts like economic instability. This study examined threat and challenge responses across personal, ingroup, and system levels, and their interaction with both individual and social appraisals of demands and support resources.

The Multifaceted Nature of Stress of Social Change

Following de la Sablonnière's (2017) topological work, the loss of normative and institutional structures typical of social changes threatens the cultural identity of all community members, as well as their material conditions, and moral foundations. This suggests that social change elicits different threats and challenges to be categorized in symbolic and instrumental facets (see intergroup-threat theory; Rios et al., 2018). Shifts in cultural identities, values, and beliefs represent symbolic threat or challenge.

For example, the failed Dutch proposal to let people change their gender without the need of a medical diagnosis (Slevin, 2022) could have potentially brought about new gender practices and concepts for both trans and cis people in the Netherlands, as it would introduce a new definition of gender and a sense of fluidity and uncertainty around gender identity.

Losses or gains of power, resources, and safety represent more instrumental threats or challenges. For example, the advocated increase in minimum wage by a Dutch labor union (Hanse, 2022), would have impacted people's personal financial resources (e.g., increase purchasing power), as well as the Dutch market (e.g., increase inflation if business owners' pass labor costs to consumers). Similar to Morgenroth and Ryan (2021), who addressed shifts toward a more non-binary view of gender, we deem important to consider an interplay between different kinds of threats (e.g., safety, status, distinctiveness) in response to progressive social changes. However, there remains a scarcity of empirical evidence to support these theoretical insights.

To address this empirical gap, this study explored which facets of change may elicit threat and challenge, distinguishing between instrumental (power, material resources, safety, health) and symbolic (morality, identity, meaning, status).

Current Study

In the current research we investigated whether progressive social changes elicit a selfreported threat or challenge response among people from a representative sample of the Netherlands. To develop a comprehensive and critical account on what contributes to challenge and threat during hierarchy-disruptive social change we investigated three main classes of determinants.

First of all, we examine proximal psychological appraisal factors, such as the perceived personal relevance of the topic of change as well as the demands of social change, and the perceived resources to cope with these demands. Our hypotheses were: (1) compared to the status quo, people will perceive social changes to be more threatening; (2) social changes will elicit a stronger response the more participants find social change to be self-relevant (moderation); social changes will elicit a stronger threat response the more participants find social change to be demanding, and to overpower the resources available to them (mediation).

Second, we explored more distal factors which might in turn influence these appraisals, namely (1) the level where change has its impact (personal, ingroup, system), (2) the facets of this change, in terms of instrumental (rights, material resources, safety, health) or symbolic (identity, morality, meaning, status) changes, and (3) the type of appraisal (i.e., personal vs. social demands and resources, uncertainty, and support).

Finally, we modeled our social change manipulation on two real-world issues, food degrowth and trans rights, using a within-subjects design to reflect how change occurs. Our goal was not to directly compare these topics, but to test the generalizability of our perspective, so we selected timely, socially relevant yet distinct issues. Though not our main aim, we also explore how different contexts elicit varied stress appraisals. Doing so, we balanced conceptual insight with contextual sensitivity, answering calls for theory grounded in real-world complexity (Pratto & Stewart, 2020).

Methods

The preregistration (OSF Preregistration) included design, planned sample size, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and planned analyses.

Participants

To determine sample size requirements, we conducted an a-priori power analysis, using G*Power 3.0.10 (Faul et al., 2007). To achieve a power of 0.80 at α = 0.05, assuming a mediumsmall effect size (f = .10), the suggested sample size was 145 participants. As we wished to draw data from a representative sample of Dutch residents with regard to gender, age, province, and education level (see https://osf.io/hnf8b/overview?view_only=0be6dbcb31b14394a731fc47e99577bd section 2.1), we settled for a sample of 400 people.

Ultimately, 411 valid responses were collected, using the platform Flycatcher (https://www.flycatcher.eu/). We paid participants €3,00. Participants had to be at least 18 years old, Dutch speaking, and residents in the Netherlands.

The final sample consisted predominantly of white Dutch participants (396, 96.9%), with a majority identifying as cisgender (409, 99.3%) and heterosexual (383, 93.2%).

Additionally, 46.2% (197) of participants identified as women. Our participants' age varied between 18 and 85 (M = 50.92, SD = 16.95). As intended, we observed a good distribution in terms of subjective socio-economic status (SSES; M = 4.78 on a scale from 1 to 10, SD = 1.83) and political orientation (M = 4.09 on a scale from 1 to 7, SD = 1.26).

Design

We used a 2 (topic of change: food degrowth; trans rights) x 2 (state of change: status quo; social change) x 3 (level of change: personal; ingroup; system) x 2 (facet of change: instrumental; symbolic) within-subject design. The order of the topics was randomized. To mimic the natural progression of social changes, the order of statuses was fixed (i.e., status quo first, followed by social change). Level and facet were integrated in our measures of threat and challenge.

Procedure

Participants completed a 45-minute online Qualtrics (https://www.qualtrics.com/) survey in Dutch. First, after providing informed consent, participants indicated their familiarity, sense of responsibility, and attitudes toward sustainability and LGBTQ+ people. Afterwards, participants read four short pieces of text, which represented our manipulation of topic and state of change. After each text, participants completed the following measures: Manipulation check of state of change (single-item); self-reported threat and challenge; threat and challenge appraisal (i.e., personal relevance, demands, resources).

The survey closed with some demographic questions: political orientation, age, assigned sex at birth, gender identity, sexual orientation, ethnicity, subjective socio-economic status. Finally, they were presented with the debriefing and redirected back to Flycatcher to receive their payment.

Materials

Complete materials and scoring procedures are available on OSF (https://osf.io/jsbcq/overview?view_only=a13100d8ed4240b8a144b3b78a18783f).

Unless otherwise indicated, all questionnaires employed a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Not at all") to 7 ("Very much") to measure agreement with the given statements.

Manipulation of Topic and State of Social Change

Participants were presented with a text describing the status quo, followed by a text describing the social change.

Instability, disruption, and novelty are considered main characteristics of social change (de la Sablonnière, 2017). Thus, in the status quo condition, we described the situation as stable and structured ("[…] is a pillar of Dutch […] measures."), and long-standing ("This will remain much the same for a long time to come."). In the social change condition, we introduced an unprecedented social change ("It is very likely that changes will take place in the Netherlands in the near future […] with the introduction of various measures […]"), and an unstable disruption of the social and normative structure of Dutch society ("These changes are expected to have a significant impact on the country in general, and people's daily lives.").

For the food degrowth condition, the status quo focused on renewable energy production, which is well-established in the Netherlands. The social change text introduced the possibility of reducing food waste in the Netherlands by regulating more strictly food production and import. For the trans right condition, the status quo condition focused on samesex marriage; the social change text introduced the possibility of becoming more inclusive by removing binary-gender indicators in official documents.

Tailoring the content to the participants, the texts emphasized activities common to any Dutch citizen, such as "doing groceries", and "renewing documents". To further increase a sense of relevance of the social chance scenario, we stressed the vast impact that social change may have ("This will eventually affect […] everyone in the Netherlands."). All texts were less than 100 words long, to limit cognitive load. The two scenarios were selected as illustrative cases of social change, rather than as subjects of direct comparison.

Manipulation Check. We evaluated the effectiveness of the manipulation of state of social change by asking participant to rate whether the text they read suggested that drastic changes were about to happen in the Netherlands. This was done for both the status quo condition and the social change condition.

Discussion

This study investigated stress and coping responses to progressive social changes, examining their effects on self-reported threat and challenge in a representative Dutch sample across two social change topics. It further investigated how personal relevance, demands, and resources influenced these responses. Additionally, it explored differential effects on stress responses across personal, ingroup, and system levels of change; instrumental and symbolic facets of change; personal vs. social demands and resources, uncertainty, support types of appraisal.

Summary of Results

In line with our hypothesis, we found that social change related to food degrowth was viewed as more threatening compared to maintaining the status quo. However, social change related to trans rights was perceived as more of a challenge instead.

Personal relevance moderated the relationship between social change and threat and challenge, so that higher personal relevance amplified perception of stress in either direction.

Moreover, how individuals appraise demands and resources mediated the relationship between social change and threat and challenge. These results are generally in line with the appraisal part of the BPS-CT. Exploratory analyses showed that the impact of change at a personal level elicited more threat than the impact on the ingroup and system levels. Instrumental facets of change elicited more threat than symbolic facets. Finally, uncertainty appraisal played a prominent role in eliciting threat, while social resources and support appraisal help mitigating it, and more so than more personal factors.

Theoretical Implications

From Avoidance to Coping: Social Change Between Threat and Challenge

Our results suggest that social change generally elicited stronger perceptions of stress compared with maintaining the status quo. Social change was also perceived as more personally relevant and drastic than maintaining the status quo. Furthermore, consistent with BPS-CT predictions, social change heightened perceptions of demands and reduced perceptions of available resources compared with the status quo, and such demands and resources mediated the relationship between social change and threat and challenge. Previous research already provided extensive evidence on the threat responses individuals experience to shifting norms and relations between social groups (Scheepers & Ellemers, 2018; Scheepers & Knight, 2020). However, such work primarily focused on negative aspects of changes, such as the loss of status among dominant group members, and on intergroups rather than hierarchydisruptive social change. Our findings extend this work by applying the Biopsychosocial Model of Challenge and Threat (BPS-CT; Blascovich & Mendes, 2000; Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996) to more ecologically valid, systemic, and consequential forms of social change. They additionally provide correlational evidence that social change may function as an affective cue, shaping demand and resource appraisals that may, in turn, contribute to whether change is experienced as threatening or challenging (Blascovich & Mendes, 2000), supporting the applicability of BPS-CT to understanding how broader societal changes may shape individual responses.

Compared with previous work (Scheepers & Ellemers, 2018; 2019), our findings add nuance to how people may respond to social change by suggesting that whether change is appraised as threatening or challenging depends on a more complex interplay of situational and personal factors that previously considered, including its perceived self-relevance and drasticness, and the actual extent to which the demands associated with change are actually perceived to outweigh available resources. In the present study, participants perceived social change related to food degrowth as highly drastic and personally relevant, as well as more demanding than the resources they perceived as available, thus, ultimately threatening. In contrast, changing gender markers in official documents, which we expected to be highly threatening given the identity and safety threats associated with trans identities and rights (Morgenroth & Ryan, 2021), was perceived as moderately drastic and only weakly personally relevant, with perceived resources outweighing demands, and was therefore ultimately appraised as challenging.

Drawing on Fraser's (1995) distinction between redistribution and recognition, this divergence may partly reflect the specific characteristics of the social changes utilized here.

Although changing gender markers in official documents disrupts established gender norms and binary expectations of societal functioning, it may mainly constitutes a recognitional form of change, and thus less upsetting to existing material and socioeconomic arrangements than changes that reshape that ways food is produced, distributed, and sold. This may suggest that social change as particularly threatening when it entails broader material and systemic disruption, rather than changes that primarily alter recognition or inclusion within existing institutional arrangements.

Additionally, while previous work has often paired opposition with threat (Scheepers & Ellemers, 2018), our findings suggest that the broader population, tentatively including those who support or benefit from progressive changes, may experience some degree of threat. This is evidence by the persistence of the effect of social change on threat even after accounting for political orientation, and attitudes toward LGBT+ individuals and sustainability.

These findings have important ramifications. First, stress is closely tied to poor health outcomes, with maladaptive coping mechanisms exacerbating these effects. Societal changes, like economic crises, heighten chronic stress (Kopp et al., 2007), and can lead to depression, diminished well-being and vitality, and poorer physical health (McLoughlin et al., 2023).

Second, stress influences behavioral responses that may be critical for fostering progressive change itself. Engaging with social change initiatives, such as activism among transgender protestors, can elevate psychological distress, with detrimental effects on collective action intentions (Breslow et al., 2015). However, recognizing that stress can be framed as a challenge rather than only as a threat opens the door to a healthier and more constructive view of stress in the context of social change.

From Threat to Challenge: The Role of Demands and Resources Appraisal

Consistent with the BPS-CT, our findings suggest that personal relevance heightens stress responses, as it moderates the relationship between social change and threat and challenge. Furthermore, individuals viewed social change as more demanding compared to the status quo and felt less resourceful to handle these demands. In turn, higher perceived demands were associated with stronger threat, and higher perceived resources buffered against these threats.

Demands of Social Change. Silbereisen (2011) posited that social changes impact individuals indirectly by influencing their micro-contexts, such as job security, relationships, public life, and lifestyle. Expanding on this list, de la Sablonnière (2017) and Cropley and Jost (2024) explored how social changes additionally generate uncertainties related to physical safety, identities, morality, and the meaning of life. Collectively, these shifting scenarios can impose negative stress on the general population, potentially overshadowing the benefits of progressive social change.

These demands manifest in two broad classes of outcomes: health and behavior. On the one hand, the demands induced by social change can negatively affect mental and physical health, contributing to issues such as anxiety, depression, or even stress-related illnesses. On the other hand, these demands can also influence behavior, motivating actions such as lowered civic engagement and activism, or withdrawal, and resistance against change. Understanding these dual impacts is crucial for addressing the unintended consequences of the demands of progressive social change.

Consistent with this theoretical corpus, we found that uncertainty was the strongest predictor of threat of social change, even when accounting for more tangible demands.

According to Hogg (2000), uncertainty reduction is a key motivator in clarifying personal and social identities. This drive becomes especially significant during societal upheavals when the lack of clear norms fosters uncertainty. Such societal uncertainty may lead to ineffective coping mechanisms. For instance, uncertainty, defined as the perception that society lacks clear norms, such as during the dissolution of feudalism, was linked to individuals turning to materialism for fulfillment (Chang & Arkin, 2002), resulting in life dissatisfaction and increased anxiety.

Moreover, members of dominant groups may strive to maintain the status quo as a means of preserving their relative sense of certainty (Hogg, 2000). This further underscores the importance of addressing feelings of uncertainty and demands more generally, as these can impact individuals' willingness to engage with, and adapt to, progressive social changes.

Resources for Social Change. Following the BPS-CT, to handle the demands of social change, individuals may be able to draw various resources, such as personal attributes, social support networks, and institutional assistance (Blascovich & Tomaka, 1996). Psychological resilience, defined as the capacity to bounce back from adversity, is often bolstered by resources such as skills and networks, emphasizing their role in mitigating threats during social changes (Ungar, 2021). Additionally, social support, for instance, is a critical resource that can reduce fear and stress by fostering a sense of security and belonging (Hornstein & Eisenberger, 2017).

Together, these represent examples of resources that can be used to navigate the threat of social change. However, drastic social changes often disrupt access to resources due to its unpredictable and destabilizing nature. For example, social changes may challenge personal identity and social roles, making it harder for individuals to leverage social networks effectively.

Individual vs. Collective: Navigating the Multilevel Nature of Social Change

Crucially, our study centers on hierarchy-disruptive social changes (i.e., Food degrowth policies and expanded trans rights through gender-inclusive document reforms, both nationlevel initiatives), and so addresses a critical gap in social psychology: Prejudice and inequalities are often investigated from the narrower perspective of interpersonal relations, such as the interaction between dominant and marginalized group members (Pratto & Stewart, 2020). By concentrating solely on individual biases, we risk missing the structural forces at play (e.g., institutionalized discrimination, or economic systems reinforcing inequality), and, consequently, more systemic solutions necessary to drive progressive social changes.

Our exploratory analyses indicated that participants often perceive a greater threat at the personal level compared to when considering the ingroup or systemic levels. This somewhat contradicts cognitive appraisal theory (see Ellsworth, 1991), according to which individuals who focus on their personal circumstances may be more accurate and thus able to restore a greater sense of control.

Concurrently, we observed that the primary buffers against the threat of social change were social resources and support, whereas both personal demands and personal resources had a smaller effect on threat and challenge responses. These observations together suggest that communities and institutions play an important role in social changes, beyond just individuallevel factors.

Although the concept of collective resource mobilization is not new to the sociological literature of social movements (Jenkins, 1983; McCarthy & Zald, 1977), this is an initial attempt to bring this level of analysis to the study of a BPS-CT of social change. Resource mobilization theorists argue that grievances (e.g., injustice, losses) play a secondary role compared to the availability of resources in determining whether social movements, and their members, can sustain collective efforts toward progress. These resources may be instrumental, based on power, or mobilizing in nature. In line with this perspective, our findings suggest that collective resources may provide an effective way for individuals to cope with the very grievances brought about by social changes. So that dealing with social change may come down to managing the tension between, on the one hand, individualized risks and uncertainties, and on the other hand, collective resilience and resources.

Instrumental vs. Symbolic: Navigating the Multifaceted Nature of Social Change

Our results showed that instrumental facets of change (e.g., economic losses) were perceived as more intensely threatening than symbolic ones (e.g., threats to cultural norms).

Consistent with our findings, research on the impact of the COVID-pandemic suggested that realistic threat more consistently predicts anxiety than symbolic threat (Kachanoff et al., 2020).

This result might further explain why changes related to food degrowth, which more directly impacts resources and economic stability, were perceived as more threatening than changes related to trans rights, which primarily involve shifts in gender values and identity. This is particularly relevant for the Netherlands. In light of the Dutch farmers protests and material uncertainties deriving from the invasion of Ukraine-both happening around the time of data collection, material changes might have felt more threatening to Dutch residents than symbolic ones. The Dutch emphasis on individualism and privacy may further explain why changes to gender markers in personal documents felt less personally relevant, and thus less demanding or threatening. This likely contributed to the challenge responses observed. In contrast, more public or material changes, like access to gender-affirming healthcare, might have elicited stronger threat reactions. Globally, such policies often provoke significant resistance, as seen in recent U.S. legislation by Republican lawmakers targeting gender-affirming care, public spaces, and trans participation in sports.

While speculative, these interpretations highlight the need to consider historical and geopolitical context in studying social change, often neglected in social psychology's preference for abstract, decontextualized scenarios. Our choice of a concrete, locally grounded case reflects this concern and resists overgeneralizing Western-centric responses.

Practical Implications

Rather than an incidental obstacle, threat may be a recurrent aspect of social change to the extent that such change is systemic, disruptive to existing hierarchies and institutions, personally relevant, and perceived as entailing demands that exceed the resources available to individuals. Based on these observations, we argue that threat is not inherently negative and to be avoided or necessarily downregulated, but rather one that individuals and movements may benefit from learning how to navigate in order to transform threat into challenge.

From a critical theory perspective, such stress is intrinsic to transformation, as struggles for minority liberation involve tension and resistance. While reducing threat responses may seem ideal, avoiding them can mean ignoring inequality or disengaging from the risks and responsibilities of change, ultimately reinforcing the status quo by leaving root injustices unaddressed. For example, as the LGBTQ+ Pride movement grew in the 1970s, the mainstream agenda, largely led by white gay men, began to narrow its focus to sexual orientation (e.g., marriage equality), while excluding original demands around race and sex worker's rights. This was often accompanied by fearing that supposedly broader, more controversial demands would jeopardize the struggle's credibility and efficacy (Gorman, 2019). By not addressing such uncertainties and risks, the movement missed the opportunity to foster a truly intersectional vision of solidarity, ultimately leaving trans people and people of color feeling marginalized and forced to leave and organize their own separate spaces.

Rather than avoidance, we propose that approach-based coping (challenge) with the stress of social change could better promote outcomes beneficial to progressive social change itself, such as psychological wellbeing, critical awareness, and collective action intentions. For example, in the context of pro-environmental social change, an approach-oriented (challenge) coping mechanism was shown to link stress responses to increased collective action intentions despite the underlying existential threat appraisal (Stollberg & Jonas, 2021).

Our multilevel approach revealed that social resources significantly buffer the threat of social change. This highlights the need to critically reassess the overemphasis on individual responsibility in sustaining progress, common in sustainability discourse, where calls to reduce personal carbon footprints often ignore systemic barriers and collective solutions like degrowth or alternatives to capitalist consumption. In a context of neoliberal individualism, this focus on self-reliance overlooks the vital role of collective support. We argue that progressive change requires community-led efforts, including political education, direct action, and mental health support.

Limitations and Strengths

Despite our study made an initial contribution to a finer grained understanding of stress response to social change, there are limitations to take into account when interpreting our results.

First, the proponents of the BPS-CT caution against the reliance on self-reported measures of threat and challenge. Beyond issues of social desirability, stress responses are often too salient for people to fully grasp before coping emerges. Although we mitigated this concern by providing clear definitions and instructions on the nature of the response we were seeking, in our current line of work we investigate this mechanism employing more covert measures, namely cardiovascular indicators of threat and challenge. At the same time, the use of selfreported measures allowed us to gather initial evidence from a broader and more representative sample, while lab studies usually imply limiting samples to around 100 participants, as well as relying on student participants.

Nonetheless, our sample underrepresented several minoritized groups in the Netherlands, especially people of color and sexual minorities. Future studies should strive for greater inclusivity rather than representativeness, as this would shed light on how threat and challenge responses vary among minoritized groups who are often the most affected by and the most involved in progressive social changes.

Future Research

To further examine how demands and resources shape responses to social change, future research should consider utilizing verbal explanations, or more refined scales to identify specific demands and resources that individuals rely on, and possibly start classifying them in relevant categories, such as skills, moral resources, and so on. Such insights could reveal which particular demands and resources more specifically impact responses to different social changes.

In connection to our findings on the importance of social resources, future studies could explore who provides these resources and how they are mobilized within communities. For instance, investigating the roles of social movements, peer networks, or institutional support systems could help clarify the mechanisms through which individuals access social resources.

Furthermore, future research could experimentally manipulate whether individuals navigate these changes on a personal level, together with their communities, or integrated with the broader system. This deeper understanding could reveal strategies for improving access to essential resources, even in the face of unprecedented social changes, and highlight ways to strengthen collective resilience.

Conclusion

Ultimately, social change forces individuals to dynamically manage their personal demands, such as coping with uncertainty, alongside their access to resources, particularly collective ones like social support and community resilience. This dynamic balance determines whether people experience such changes as a threat or challenge, which in turn we theoretically speculate has implications for individual well-being and physical health, and sociopolitical behavioral responses: Those perceiving progressive social change as a threat may experience increased anxiety, and disengagement; those perceiving it as a challenge may experience positive growth, and motivation to participate in collective action.

Minoritized communities in Western societies continue to grapple with systemic exploitation and inequalities, and as progressive social changes unfold, these can often feel threatening to the general population. While this sense of threat is not inherently negative, it is crucial to understand and promote healthier coping strategies such as approach-based (challenge) ones. These insights are important not just for individuals navigating evolving norms and institutions, but potentially also for social movements, which frequently encounter backlash, and failures, and need to continue organizing and mobilizing despite setbacks. Given that progressive changes take conflict, and effort to establish and maintain, unhealthy coping mechanisms can drive those needing to adapt to or advocating for these changes into a state of paralysis, ultimately perpetuating the status quo.

Plan of Analyses

To test the impact of social change on self-reported threat and challenge, we used Multilevel Modelling (MLM) in RStudio (lmer, Version 1.1-29; Bates et al., 2015). The models employed Full Maximum Likelihood estimation, with TCI as the dependent variable. A stepwise approach based on 95% confidence intervals identified the best model specification.

To test whether social change is more threatening than the status quo (Hypothesis 1), we added the fixed interaction term between topic and state of change, the 3 levels (personal, ingroup, system), and 2 facets (instrumental, symbolic) of change; This was also meant to test exploratory analyses 1 (effect of levels of change) and 2 (effect of facets of change). In the second step we added the random interaction effect of topic and social change. Finally, we added the covariates, namely age, gender, sexual orientation, ethnicity, SSES, political orientation, familiarity, responsibility, and attitudes. In the final model we retained only the significant predictors, based on 95% confidence intervals.

To test whether personal relevance moderated, and demands, and resources mediated the relationship between social change and TCI (hypothesis 2), we ran separate MLM models.

First, we investigated whether social change (vs status quo) increased perceived demands, while decreasing perceived resources. Finally, we added personal relevance, demands and resources to our main MLM model. Indirect and total effects were calculated manually. Finally, to explore the differential effect of uncertainty, support, and personal and social demands and resources (exploratory analysis 3), we repeated the aforementioned moderation/mediation analyses, but testing separately for each item of the threat and challenge appraisal scales.
