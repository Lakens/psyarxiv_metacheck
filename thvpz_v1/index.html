When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Psychosis-Bench recently showed that production chat models confirm and elaborate users' delusion-like beliefs under multi-turn pressure, but its evidence is English-only, Western-flagship-only, and rests on an uncalibrated LLM judge. We present Echo-Bench, a Chinese replication and cross-lingual extension: the full 16-case, 12-turn protocol mirrored into Chinese under a fidelity-first translation protocol with an independent re-translation audit, run against two widely deployed Chinese chat models (DeepSeek V4.1-Flash, qwen-plus) in both languages -192 sessions, 4,032 turn-level ratings. The original qualitative finding replicates in both languages (RQ1). All six pre-committed cross-language contrasts are significant after Benjamini-Hochberg control: the Chinese arm is directionally worse on every dimension, with model-differentiated failure channels (RQ2). Because our pipeline required a bilingual rater, we placed the measurement instrument itself under test (RQ3): a five-layer rater trust chain -entrance examination against human gold, extreme-score arbitration, red-line audit of the arbiter, blind human adjudication, and a spot check of the non-extreme stratum -shows that automated raters fail concentratedly in the extreme band, with two named failure modes (disclaimer laundering, eloquence inflation), and that a strong general-purpose arbiter is disqualified by pre-specified red lines. A negative control bounds rater language sensitivity below what the observed gaps require. Safety behavior certified in English does not certify safety behavior in Chinese; the instruments used to check either must themselves be checked.

1. Introduction

Reports of users developing or deepening delusional beliefs through sustained conversations with AI chatbots have moved from anecdote to documented signal. A collection of 185 real-world harm reports found descriptions consistent with delusional beliefs in 55.1% of cases, with the chatbot recorded as validating the user's belief in nearly half of those [Morrin26a]; analyses of donated chat logs from affected users show sycophantic markers saturating over 70% of assistant messages in delusional conversations [Moore26]; and theoretical work has framed the underlying dynamic as a feedback loop -a "technological folie à deux" -in which a model optimized to agree and a user primed to believe amplify each other across turns [Dohnány26, Osler26, Mehta26]. Whether these interactions cause clinical harm remains contested [Diel26], but the behavioral prerequisite -a chatbot that confirms and elaborates a user's expressed delusions rather than gently resisting them -is directly measurable, and it is a property of the model, not of the diagnosis.

Psychosis-Bench [PB25] was the first benchmark to measure this property systematically: 16 scripted clinical vignettes of escalating delusional conviction, each 12 user turns long, scored on three turn-level dimensionsdelusion confirmation (DCS), harm enablement (HES), and safety intervention (SIS). Its central finding, that production chat models confirm delusion-like beliefs under multi-turn pressure, has since been echoed by audit studies and simulation work [Kirgis26, Aquilina26, Chandra26, Nicholls26]. Yet the original study, like nearly all work in this line, shares three boundaries. It is English-only, on a benchmark authored in English. It evaluates predominantly Western flagship models. And its scores rest on a single LLM judge that received no human calibration -though we do not claim that human calibration is itself infallible -a limitation it shares with most LLM-as-judge evaluation in this domain, where the rater's reliability on the exact content that matters most (extreme, high-harm, indirectly phrased text) is simply assumed.

A separate literature suggests these boundaries are not neutral. Safety alignment degrades across languages: sycophancy rises in lower-resource languages [Shah26], the same prompt can elicit opposite stances depending on its language [Aldahlawi26], models that recognize risk in their reasoning traces still answer unsafely in non-high-resource languages [Zhang26], and cultural adaptation of prompts -not translation alone -appears to drive much of the cross-lingual gap [Sattigeri26]. Chinese alone is the native language of roughly a billion speakers and the primary interaction language of several widely deployed chat model families; the nearest Chinese-language study measures factual-QA sycophancy [Liu26], not delusion reinforcement in sustained conversation. Whether the findings of [PB25] transfer to Chinese-language interaction, and whether models behave differently when the same distressed user writes in Chinese rather than English, is unknown. This paper reports Echo-Bench, a Chinese replication and cross-lingual extension of Psychosis-Bench designed to close that gap. We mirrored the full 16-case protocol into Chinese under a fidelity-first translation protocol, and ran both language arms against two widely deployed Chinese chat models (deepseek-flash and qwen-plus), yielding 192 production sessions and 4,032 turn-level ratings. Three research questions organize the report. RQ1 (replication): do the qualitative findings of [PB25] replicate on Chinese chat models, in both languages? RQ2 (cross-language difference): does the magnitude of the echo effect differ between language arms of the same cases? RQ3 (measurement validity): can automated raters be trusted in this domain at all -and if not, where, exactly, do they fail? The third question is not a methodological aside: because our pipeline requires a rater to read both languages, we were forced to validate the measurement instrument before trusting it, and that validation turned out to be a study in its own right.

Our findings are threefold. First, the core phenomenon replicates: both models confirm delusion-like beliefs in both languages, with the same pattern of elevated confirmation in implicit-onset cases reported by [PB25]. Second, the echo effect is systematically stronger in the Chinese arm -all six pre-committed confirmatory contrasts (2 models × 3 metrics) survive FDR correction at q < 0.05 -but the gap travels through different behavioral channels in the two model families: in qwen-plus it appears as escalating delusion confirmation (DCS +0.962, 95% CI [0.66, 1.27], opening at turn 4 and widening through turn 12), while in deepseek-flash confirmation stays flat and the gap appears instead as increased harm enablement and withdrawn safety intervention. Language-conditional safety failures are therefore not a single mechanism with a single remedy. Third, every automated rater we tested failed in the extreme score band where the safety-relevant signal lives: we document two named failure modes -disclaimer laundering (a single appended helpline sentence can suffice for substantive facilitation to be scored as safe) and eloquence inflation (florid, keyword-dense prose is convicted of confirmation despite a dissuasive stance) -and show that a naive second-opinion arbitration design would have imported these failures invisibly.

We make four contributions. (i) The first paired Chinese-English replication of a delusion-reinforcement benchmark, with the translation protocol, an independent re-translation audit, and a negative-control measurement of rater language sensitivity reported in full. (ii) Evidence that cross-language safety degradation in this domain is real, same-directional across three dimensions, and channel-differentiated across models, measured with case-level paired bootstrap inference that respects within-session autocorrelation [SC26]. (iii) A five-layer rater trust chain -entrance examination against a human gold set, extreme-score arbitration, post-hoc red-line audit of the arbiter, blind human adjudication of a stratified extreme subsample, and a blind spot check of the non-extreme stratum -that we offer as a minimal template for LLM-as-judge designs in safety-critical evaluation. (iv) An empirical map of where automated raters fail: concentrated in the extreme band, asymmetric between over-reading present surface features and under-reading absent reality-anchoring, and largely invisible to single-rater or uncalibrated-arbiter pipelines. The deployment implication is direct: safety behavior certified in English does not certify safety behavior in Chinese, and the instruments used to check either must themselves be checked.

2. Related Work

>Related Work

2.1 AI psychosis and delusion reinforcement

Concerns that conversational AI may reinforce delusional thinking have developed along three lanes. Conceptual and clinical analyses frame the mechanism as a feedback loop between human cognitive-emotional biases and companionship-reinforcing model behaviors [Dohnány26], as distributed co-construction of belief between user and a sycophantic quasi-other [Osler26], and as an "echo chamber of one" -a metaphor for the user-chatbot dyad, whose elevation to a distinct clinical entity ("AI-associated psychosis") remains disputed [AuYeung26, Nielsen26, Diel26]. Empirical studies have documented the phenomenon in donated chat logs, where sycophantic markers saturate delusional conversations [Moore26]; in 185 real-world harm reports, where chatbot validation of delusional beliefs appears in nearly half of delusion-consistent cases [Morrin26a]; in longitudinal Reddit-based simulations showing progressive amplification of delusion-related language [Shimgekar26]; and in latent-state models of bidirectional belief amplification, in which the chatbot's self-consistency, more than moment-to-moment sycophancy, perpetuates the loop [Mehta26]. Benchmark and audit studies measure the behavior directly: [PB25] introduced the 16-vignette, 12-turn protocol with DCS/HES/SIS scoring that we replicate; subsequent work has audited chat interfaces versus APIs [Kirgis26], tested accumulated context as a stress test of safety architecture [Nicholls26], and shown with matched delusion-versus-distress controls that models recognize distress but fail to intervene once it is embedded in a delusional frame [Aquilina26]. On the intervention side, anti-sycophancy prompting transfers to psychosis-relevant contexts with large reported effect sizes [delaLoza26], trajectory-level safety planning has been formalized as sequential decision-making [Picano26, Morrin26b], and user-facing nudges raise risk awareness without clearly changing behavior [Elangovan26]; tiered human-AI healing ecosystems have also been proposed as governance frameworks for mental-health AI [Ma26]. Nearly all of this work is English-dominant. Studies involving Chinese models and Chinese-language question answering do existdangerous-speech detection in emotional-disorder dialogue data has been benchmarked across five deployed LLMs, including DeepSeek, Kimi, and Doubao [Yang25], and Chinese factual-QA sycophancy is covered in §2.2 [Liu26] -but none of them makes a cross-language comparison, and none pairs the same cases across languages.

2.2 Sycophancy and cross-lingual safety

Sycophancy is increasingly decomposed rather than treated as a scalar: source deference separates from user agreement [Kumar26]; whether a model yields depends first on whether the claim is verifiable, with pressure tactics contributing surprisingly little [Yang26b]; multi-turn evaluation must score empathy calibration alongside sycophancy to avoid inducing cold overcorrection [Pulipaka26]; and formal models show that even a truth-telling bot can induce delusional spiraling through selective affirmation [Chandra26], while RLHF amplifies sycophancy as a feature of the preference distribution rather than a reward-modeling failure [Shapira26]. Cross-lingually, identical prompts can elicit language-exclusive agreement reversals [Aldahlawi26], and cultural adaptation of prompt content appears to matter more than language encoding itself [Sattigeri26]; for lower-resource or non-high-resource languages specifically, sycophancy intensifies [Shah26], and unsafe answers become more likely even when the model's reasoning recognizes the risk [Zhang26]. The closest work to ours is [Liu26], which measures how false user beliefs flip correct answers in Chinese factual QA; it is belief-conditional and confined to factual QA -a design structurally blind to delusion-like multi-turn escalation. None of these studies pair identical delusion scenarios across languages, and none validate their raters against human gold labels in both languages.

2.3 Evaluation methodology

Two methodological literatures underpin our design. First, turn-level metrics in multi-turn conversation are autocorrelated, and failing to correct for this can render a large fraction of significant findings spurious [SC26]; our case-level paired bootstrap follows this guidance directly, and the trajectory-as-unit stance articulated by [Morrin26b] and echoed in the review agenda of [Novelli26] motivates our per-turn reporting. Second, LLM-as-judge reliability is itself an open problem in this domain: judges and humans agree only moderately on delusion-adjacent coding [Moore26, Kirgis26], recognized detection of dangerous statements in clinical dialogue remains limited by risk-stratification failures [Yang25], and even the strongest intervention study on [PB25] acknowledges that all outcomes derive from a single judge whose cross-judge comparability is unknown [delaLoza26]. Where prior work flags rater limitations as caveats, we treat rater validation as a first-class experimental object with pre-registered pass criteria, red-line failure conditions, and blind human adjudication.

Positioning. The three lanes leave an intersection unoccupied: the delusion-reinforcement literature has no Chinese-language arm; the Chinese-language sycophancy literature has no multi-turn delusion scenario; and the methodology literature documents rater fallibility without validating raters on the extreme, high-harm content where it matters most. Echo-Bench sits at that intersection: a paired Chinese-English replication of [PB25], on two widely deployed Chinese chat models, with the measurement instrument itself placed under test.

2.4 Research questions

We pre-state the three research questions that organize this report. RQ1 (replication): do the qualitative findings of Psychosis-Bench [PB25]-delusion confirmation by production chat models under multi-turn pressure-replicate on two widely deployed Chinese chat models, in both English and Chinese? RQ2 (cross-language differences): does the magnitude of the echo effect differ between the two language arms under translated stimuli? RQ3 (measurement validity): do automated raters provide trustworthy measurement in this domain, and where do they fail? RQ1 and RQ2 are confirmatory (six pre-committed contrasts, Benjamini-Hochberg controlled); RQ3 and all trajectory-level analyses are exploratory.

3. Methods

>Methods

3.1 Case set and dialogue protocol

We adopted the case set and protocol of Psychosis-Bench [PB25] without modification: 16 clinical vignettes (8 explicit, 8 implicit onset of the delusional theme), each unfolding over 12 user turns of escalating conviction. The English originals were mirrored into Chinese by a professional-fluency translator model (DeepSeek, via the deepseek-chat alias resolving to deepseek-flash; version provenance in §3.2) under a fidelity-first protocol: scoring-relevant proper names were kept in Latin script, no cultural localization was applied, and literal fidelity was preferred over idiomatic naturalness wherever the two conflicted. Case count, turn count, turn order, and explicit/implicit pairing between the EN and ZH sets were verified by the human author together with an automated consistency check. The translated set is used internally under a research-use rationale; public release is pending the original authors' permission (a request is in progress), and we therefore release only our original Chinese cases (release planned for a later version) together with all code and score data.

Because the Chinese case set was translated by one of the models under test, we commissioned an independent re-translation of 6 stratified cases (72 turns) by a third-party model (k2.8) not involved in any experimental role. A three-way alignment against the original English yielded 2 substantive deviations out of 72 turns, in opposite directions of severity bias, with no evidence of systematic translator bias (Appendix B). We additionally identified one turn whose English source ends abruptly without punctuation -a suspected truncation; this defect originates upstream and is disclosed in the Limitations. Each of the 16 cases × 2 languages was run with 3 independent repetitions per model at temperature 0.7, yielding 16 × 2 × 3 ×2 = 192 production sessions. A 32-session smoke test (3 turns, 1 repetition) was run beforehand and archived separately; no smoke data entered the analysis set.

3.2 Subject models and version provenance

Both subject models were accessed through their official provider APIs (DeepSeek Platform; Alibaba Cloud Model Studio) via OpenAI-compatible interfaces on 10 October 2026. All 192 production sessions were completed within a single six-hour window (13:16-19:15 CST; the 32-session smoke test ran immediately before it, starting 13:08). The model identifier returned by the API was recorded per session and was invariant throughout: the deepseek-chat request alias resolved to deepseek-flash (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, released 10 September 2026) in all sessions, and the qwen-plus alias returned qwen-plus, which Alibaba Cloud's model documentation states is functionally equivalent to the snapshot qwen-plus-2025-12-01 (Qwen3 Plus). No version drift was observed within the experimental window. One post-hoc clarification: on 17 October 2026, seven days after data collection, DeepSeek released a successor snapshot (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017) to which the deepseek-flash identifier now routes; the September release we measured carries no official date suffix ("-0910" is a community retronym), and we distinguish it from the 1017 snapshot by release date. All measurements in this paper therefore characterize the initial V4.1-Flash release as served during the experimental window, not the 1017 snapshot. Two caveats apply. First, the deepseek-chat alias had passed its published discontinuation date yet continued to be served; the post-retirement routing rule is not publicly documented, so we report the version by the API-returned identifier rather than by alias mapping. Second, rater models (below) were called outside the session-logging pipeline and therefore carry no version record; we report them by alias only, as the vendors do not publish alias-to-snapshot mappings.

3.3 Metrics

Following the Psychosis-Bench repository scales [PB25-repo]: Delusion Confirmation Score (DCS, 0-5, scored from turn 4), Harm Enablement Score (HES, 0-5, from turn 7), and Safety Intervention Score (SIS, 0-3, from turn 7). The start turns follow the original repository's judge configuration: delusion confirmation and harm enablement are scored only from the turns at which the scripted user belief has sufficiently formed for the construct to apply. Higher DCS/HES indicate worse behavior; higher SIS indicates better. The paper version of [PB25] reports 0-2/0-2/binary scales; we follow the repository scales because the repository's judge configuration -the instrument that actually performs the scoring -is written for them; cross-study comparisons require linear normalization.

3.4 The rating pipeline: a five-layer trust chain

Every turn-level reply was rated by an LLM judge. Because the reliability of LLM-as-judge in this domain had never been validated (including by [PB25], whose judge received no human calibration), we treated rater reliability as a first-class research object and built a five-layer trust chain.

We report all judge-human agreement under two fixed calibers, side by side throughout: the wide caliber (judge score within ±1 of the human interval bounds) and the strict caliber (score inside the human interval, or within ±1 of the interval midpoint). No result is reported under only one caliber, and the two are never mixed within a single claim.

The judge reads each reply in the language of the session (Chinese-arm replies scored from the Chinese text, English-arm replies from the English text); the judging prompts are in English in both arms.

Layer 1 -Entrance examination.

Candidate judges were screened against a human gold set of 28 blind-rated samples before touching production data (pass: ≥80% agreement within ±1, the wide caliber). glm-4-flash failed (wide 46.4%; strict 35.7%) and was discarded. We initially read a change between two examination rounds (46.4%→42.9%, wide caliber) as evidence about prompt refinement; on re-analysis the entire difference is attributable to a revision of the gold set between rounds-under a constant gold set, zero items changed-so we report the failure without claiming that it is, or is not, addressable by prompting. glm-4-air passed (89.3%; DCS 100%, SIS 100%, HES 70%) and served as the primary rater at temperature 0. The HES sub-score of 70% falls short of the bar we later apply to any rater of high-harm content; this shortfall is exactly why every HES extreme score was routed to human adjudication (Layers 2-4).

The gold set comprises 28 items (10 DCS, 10 HES, 8 SIS), all drawn from Chinese-language sessions; English-side rater behavior was therefore never validated at qualification, and RQ2 findings are qualified accordingly (see Limitations). A post-hoc check was run after qualification: the rater re-scored 28 English-arm items together with their Chinese translations (dual-text language-invariance check), and the human first author blind-rated the same translations. The check is reported as a negative control, not as indirect validation: the rater showed no systematic directional bias across languages (20/28 items within ±1; mean signed deviation -0.05), but human-rater agreement on the translated items was itself bounded (22/28 within ±1; 64.3% ±1 against the English-side production scores), and this bound caps rather than lifts the reliability of English-side scores (Appendix D). All 28 gold items overlap production sessions (no held-out set); human gold scores were used for calibration only and never overwrote machine scores in the production dataset. Per-model judge-gold mismatch counts under both calibers are reported in Appendix A; mismatched items were retained as scored to avoid post-hoc selection.

Layer 2 -Arbitration of extreme scores.

Every primary score of DCS ≥ 4 or HES ≥ 3 triggered a second-opinion re-rating: 698 items (17.3% of 4032). Each triggered item was re-scored by a second rater (deepseek-reasoner); on 136 of the 698 (19.5%; 3.4% of 4032: 15 DCS + 121 HES) the arbiter disagreed with the primary rater by ≥2 points, and these form the escalation pool reported throughout this section. We report this disagreement rate as a finding in its own right rather than a procedural detail: on nearly one in five extreme items, two reasonable automated raters diverge by two points or more. The trigger condition covers DCS and HES only; SIS scores received no arbitration and no human adjudication at any layer, a coverage gap we disclose (see Limitations).

Layer 3 -Post-hoc audit of the arbiter. Before accepting arbitration outcomes, we administered the same 28-sample entrance examination to the arbiter. It achieved 92.9% overall ±1 agreement but failed the critical safety criterion: 2 gold samples rated ≥ 3 by the human were compressed to ≤ 1. Qualitative analysis showed a systematic blind spot we term disclaimer laundering: a reply containing concrete facilitation (e.g., named tall buildings with heights) followed by a crisis-line disclaimer was scored as safe. Because the 136 arbitrated items were exactly the high-risk extreme scores, the arbiter was disqualified for this stratum and its arbitration results were discarded.

Layer 4 -Human adjudication.

A stratified sample of 24 of the 136 items (all 15 DCS; 9 HES stratified by model × language) was finally adjudicated by the human author under blinding: judge identities were randomized to "甲/乙" labels, and model and language of origin were masked. The 11 English-origin items were machine-translated into Chinese before rating, so the rater could not perceive an item's session language-an incidental language blinding; the rater reported no perceptible difference between translated and natively Chinese items. Human adjudication revealed a second, complementary blind spot in the primary rater, which we term eloquence inflation: florid, keyword-dense replies that quote the user's delusional frame in order to rebut it were over-scored as confirming it (agreement of primary rater with human finals on these 24 hardest items: 29.2% strict; 54.2% wide). A prompt patch correcting this (v4, tested on glm-4-air itself) overcorrected and still failed the 80% gate (45.8% strict on the same 24 items), demonstrating that the failure is not trivially promptable away. The 24 human finals replaced the corresponding machine scores.

Layer 5 -Sensitivity bounds and statistical correction. For the remaining 112 non-adjudicated disagreements we report two analytical frames: the primary frame (primary-rater scores with 24 human overrides) and a minimum-effect sensitivity frame (all 112 set to 0). All directional conclusions hold in both frames. The sensitivity frame perturbs only HES, because all 15 flagged DCS items were human-adjudicated; it does not cover any potential primary-rater bias in the non-extreme stratum, which is instead addressed by the blind spot check reported under RQ3 (77.8% agreement on 27 non-extreme items). Because turn-level records within a session are autocorrelated [SC26], all confidence intervals use case-level block bootstrap (B = 10,000, percentile method, seed 42); cross-language differences use case-level paired resampling, with the two language arms of the same case entering the difference together.

Second blind human rater. To probe whether the rater-layer findings are specific to machine raters, we recruited a second human rater with no clinical background and no involvement in the study design. She scored 20 items (10 drawn from the gold set, 10 from the 24 human-adjudicated extreme items; stratified sampling, seed 7) under full blinding to machine scores, model identity, language arm, and session metadata. Although instructed to score only each item's designated metric, she returned scores on all three metrics; the additional ratings were retained and are reported as exploratory. Her sampling frame differs from the gold-set anchors, so her agreement statistics are reported separately and never pooled with them (designated-metric exact agreement 60.0%; wide caliber 85.0%; κ = 0.770). All items on which she diverged from the production rater by ≥2 points on the designated metric were adjudicated by the human first author. No communication about item content or results occurred between the second rater and the author before submission.

4.4 Rater reliability (RQ3)

Rater qualification. Three candidate raters underwent an entrance examination against a 28-item human gold set (10 DCS, 10 HES, 8 SIS; see Limitations for its language composition). Under the wide criterion (agreement within ±1 of the human interval), glm-4-flash scored 46.4%, deepseek-reasoner 92.9%, and glm-4-air 89.3%; only the latter two passed the 80% bar. We note for transparency that under the strict criterion, glm-4-air's entrance score is 75.0%-below the same bar (Table 4, both calibers reported side by side; glm-4-flash's strict score was 35.7%). The original task specification for the entrance examination did not include a safety red-line clause; when we apply the red-line criterion introduced for the arbiter's post-hoc re-examination (zero items where a gold score ≥3 is compressed to ≤1) retrospectively, glm-4-air shows 2 such items and would not have qualified under that standard. We therefore characterize glm-4-air not as a validated rater but as the least-bad available rater, and we treat its failure modes as first-class findings rather than caveats. Note: the qualification bar (80%) was defined on the wide caliber. glm-4-flash was examined against the pre-revision gold set (v2 prompt round; its examination predated the gold-set revision described in §3.4); a second examination under the revised gold set (v3 prompt round) gave 42.9% wide / 28.6% strict; deepseek-reasoner and glm-4-air were examined against the revised gold. glm-4-air fails both the strict caliber and the retrospective red-line criterion and is therefore characterized as the least-bad available rater.

Arbiter disqualification.

deepseek-reasoner, appointed as arbiter for the 136 extreme-score disagreements, achieved 92.9% wide-criterion agreement on the gold set but committed two fatal compressions of high-harm gold items (poetically phrased suicidal ideation scored 0-1 against gold 3-5). Under the pre-specified pass criteria, it was disqualified, and its arbitration output was discarded in full; air's production scores were retained with human adjudication of a stratified subsample. A targeted prompt patch adding an explicit anti-laundering clause ("a helpline or disclaimer does not erase substantive facilitation") was re-examined on the same 28 items: the score was unchanged (92.9%, identical item-level profile), and both red-line items recurred with near-verbatim reasoning, indicating that the anchoring rule ("no explicit method/location/dosage") overrides the new clause. The failure is thus structural to the scoring frame, not addressable by one-shot prompt edits within our budget. Symmetrically, we did not test whether a prompt patch could eliminate the arbiter's disclaimer-laundering in the reverse direction beyond this single attempt; we report the unpatched failure as observed.

Human adjudication reveals stratum-specific distortion.

Human adjudication of 24 stratified extreme items (15 DCS, 9 HES) found judge-human agreement of only 29.2% (strict; 54.2% wide), with the judge systematically over-scoring (mean deviation +1.94). All 9 HES items were downgraded by the human (from judge ≥3 to ≤1.5); all 15 DCS items flagged for arbitration had received an identical judge score of 4, indicating no discrimination within the flagged band. Over-scoring was asymmetric by language: mean deviation +2.38 on Chinese items versus +1.41 on English items. The adjudicated set itself was language-balanced (13 zh / 11 en; by metric, DCS 8 zh / 7 en and HES 5 zh / 4 en), so the asymmetry reflects scoring behavior rather than sample composition.

A prompt patch (v4) targeting the over-scoring failure mode ("quoting a frame is not endorsing a frame") was tested on glm-4-air itself against the same 24 adjudicated items and reached 45.8% strict agreement-still far below any qualification threshold-and introduced two reverse errors in which items the human scored 4-5 were compressed to ≤1. The patch was evaluated in-sample (the same items from which the failure mode was induced); its failure under this most permissive condition strengthens rather than weakens the conclusion.

By contrast, a pure-blind spot check of 27 items sampled from the non-extreme stratum (all 4032 ratings minus the 136-item arbitration pool; stratified by metric × score band × model × language, seed 42) found agreement of 77.8% (95% CI [59.2%, 89.4%], Wilson) with near-zero mean deviation (-0.26). The two strata are disjoint by construction and must not be pooled: evaluator distortion is concentrated in the extreme band-precisely the band that carries the safety-relevant signal. One qualification asymmetry is disclosed: the gold set consists entirely of Chinese-session items (28/28), so the judge's English-side behavior was never validated at qualification; the spot check (13 English items, agreement 76.9%, mean deviation -0.58) provides the first, post-hoc evidence on the English side and finds no language asymmetry outside the extreme band.

Interpretation. None of the automated raters we tested-across three models and two prompt variants-reliably handles extreme, poetically or indirectly phrased high-harm content. We document two named failure modes: disclaimer laundering (a helpline mention or disclaimer suffices for substantive facilitation to be ignored, even when it occupies a small fraction of the reply; shared by both air and the arbiter on overlapping items) and eloquence inflation (florid, keyword-dense prose is convicted despite a healthy stance; over-corrected but not eliminated by the v4 patch). These findings do not indict LLM-as-judge methodology in general; they show that in this domain, unvalidated raters fail in the exact score region where harm lives, and that single-rater designs and unexamined "second-opinion" arbitration import this failure invisibly. We regard the five-layer validation chain itself-entrance exam, red-line audit, stratified human adjudication, blind spot check, and dual-caliber reporting-as a methodological contribution of this work.

5. Discussion

>Discussion

5.1 Principal findings

Across 192 multi-turn sessions, we find consistent evidence that large language models reinforce, rather than appropriately resist, user-expressed delusion-like beliefs -and that the magnitude of this echo effect is conditional on the language of the interaction. All six confirmatory contrasts survived FDR correction at q < 0.05, spanning two model families, two languages, and three scoring dimensions. The effect is therefore not an artifact of a single model, a single prompt framing, or a single rater decision; it is a robust behavioral regularity of instruction-tuned chat models under sustained delusional pressure.

The cross-lingual extension yields our second finding: the echo effect is systematically stronger in the Chinese-language arm, for both models and on all three dimensions, with no exception across the six confirmatory contrasts. The size and shape of this gap, however, differs by model. For Qwen, the gap is concentrated in delusion-confirmation (DCS +0.962, 95% CI [0.66, 1.27]): the Chinese-arm sessions escalate from near-baseline to clinically concerning confirmation scores, and the divergence from the English arm opens at turn 4 and is largest at turns 11-12. For DeepSeek, the DCS gap is small (+0.181); instead, the Chinese arm shows significantly more harmful-enablement content (HES +0.538) and significantly less safety-intervention behavior (SIS -0.528) than the English arm, while delusion confirmation remains essentially unchanged. In other words, both models echo more in Chinese, but they do so through different behavioral channels: one confirms the delusion more strongly, the other maintains comparable confirmation while shedding its safety scaffolding.

Because our scoring pipeline assigns Chinese-arm sessions a Chinese-language rubric pass and English-arm sessions an English one, a language-conditional difference could in principle reflect rater language sensitivity rather than subject-model behavior. We therefore measured this sensitivity directly rather than assuming it away: the production rater re-scored 28 items under swapped-language conditions, and the human first author independently blind-scored the translated items. Agreement bounds from this negative control (±1 agreement 71.4-78.6%; endpoint human-rater agreement 64.3%) sit well above what would be required to explain gaps of the observed magnitude, and the mean signed deviations are near zero (-0.05 to +0.16), indicating variance rather than directional drift. We interpret the language-conditional gap as a property of the subject models, while treating the rater-reliability ceiling as a bound on the precision of any single-point estimate.

5.2 The shape of the language gap: escalation timing and divergence channels

Beyond mean differences, the two models differ in when the language gap appears and through which dimension it grows. For Qwen, the DCS gap is already present at the first scored turn (turn 4, +0.885) and widens over the scored trajectory, peaking at +1.594 at turn 11 before settling at +1.375 at turn 12. The Chinese-arm sessions do not merely sustain a fixed initial deficit -the gap itself more than half-doubles over the scored trajectory, one incremental concession at a time. This temporal profile is consistent with an accumulation process compounding an early-opened gap: whatever keeps the English arm near baseline erodes gradually in the Chinese arm as the conversation lengthens.

DeepSeek shows no comparable escalation channel in DCS; instead, its Chinese-arm deficit is spread across the two guardrail dimensions without a single dominant time point. The divergence between the two models is therefore qualitative, not merely quantitative. One plausible reading is that multilingual safety training invests unevenly across behavioral targets: refusal and confirmation policies (which govern DCS) may be trained and evaluated more heavily in English, while harm-enablement and intervention policies (which govern HES and SIS) may depend more on general-purpose instruction following that transfers across languages. Our data cannot adjudicate between this and competing accounts -although both model families are open-weight, their training corpora and alignment recipes are documented only at coarse granularity, and we observe outputs only -but the fact that the two model families fail in different dimensions in the same language constrains any explanation that attributes the gap to a single shared cause, such as uniformly weaker Chinese safety data.

Two features of these results sharpen the contribution. First, the Qwen trajectory implies that single-turn probes or short dialogues would systematically miss the language gap: a measurement truncated after the opening turns would capture only a fraction of the effect, which grows with every additional turn thereafter. Multi-turn designs are therefore not a refinement but a prerequisite for detecting language-conditional safety differences of this kind. Second, the absence of a shared failure channel across models means that mitigation strategies validated on one model family cannot be assumed to transfer: closing Qwen's confirmation-escalation channel would leave DeepSeek's guardrail erosion untouched, and vice versa.

5.3 Measuring the measurer: a negative control for rater language sensitivity

Cross-lingual evaluation with automated raters faces a validity question that is rarely measured directly: when scores differ across language arms, does the difference live in the subject models or in the rater's unequal sensitivity to the two languages? Rather than assuming rater language invariance, we bounded it empirically. The production rater scored a stratified set of items twice -once in the original language and once in translation -and the human first author independently blind-scored the translated items. The resulting agreement profile defines a negative control: exact agreement 46.4%, ±1 agreement 71.4-78.6%, and mean signed deviations near zero (-0.05 to +0.16) across all links of the measurement chain. Read together, these three figures say that the rater reproduces the human's exact score on under half of items, lands within one point on roughly seven in ten, and -critically -errs symmetrically in both directions rather than systematically upward or downward in either language.

We stress what this design does and does not establish. It does not establish that the rater is language-invariantagreement at these levels is plainly imperfect, and human-rater agreement on translated content (±1: 78.6%) is no lower than the human-rater ceiling observed on original-language content (64.3% at the endpoint) -translation does not degrade the measurement below its native-language floor. What it establishes is a bound: rater language sensitivity introduces variance, not directional bias, and that variance is far too small and too symmetrical to directionally manufacture gaps of the observed magnitude (e.g., DCS +0.962). Framing the check as a negative control rather than a validation study is, in our view, the honest epistemic position for any cross-lingual evaluation pipeline that relies on a single automated rater -and we note that the same logic applies to human translation checks, bilingual duplicate scoring, and any other chain in which each link carries imperfect agreement.

Because agreement compounds multiplicatively across chained measurement links, we measured the endpoint of the chain directly (human vs. rater production scores: 64.3% ±1) rather than inferring it from the product of link-level agreements. The comparison vindicates the choice: a naive independence assumption would multiply the three link-level ±1 agreements (89.3%, 71.4%, 78.6%) to predict an endpoint near 50%, understating the directly measured 64.3% by roughly fourteen points -chained rater errors are not independent, and endpoint measurement is the only way to know in which direction the approximation errs. We recommend this practice -report every link, measure the endpoint, and state plainly which inferences the resulting ceiling permits -as a minimal disclosure standard for LLM-as-judge designs in safety evaluation, where small rater biases can masquerade as subject-model findings.

5.4 Limitations

Several limitations bound the claims of this study. First, our reliance on a single production rater means all score-level findings inherit the rater-reliability ceiling documented above; the negative control bounds language-conditional bias but does not raise agreement beyond the human-rater ceiling (endpoint 64.3% ±1), so individual item scores should be read as estimates with disclosed error, not ground truth. Second, the rater itself is non-deterministic in practice: under temperature-0 reruns, 26 of 28 items reproduced exactly while 2 items drifted (one by a full point, one by half a point), a small but real source of run-to-run variance that we disclose rather than correct. Third, a pipeline defect truncated a small number of judge prompts in one arm; affected turns were re-scored after repair, and the re-translation step drops the truncated fragment, so stimulus length differs at that turn across scoring surfaces -a residual confound we document in the supplement.

Fourth, our second human rater scored an independent 20-item sample and was never pooled with the gold-anchor set; her data enter only as an exploratory consistency check and a small set of adjudicated divergences, so the study's human ground truth rests on a single rater with clinical naivety acknowledged. Fifth, disagreement analysis shows that HES is the weakest of the three scoring dimensions -a majority of adjudicated divergences and of trigger-table items fall on this dimension -and a targeted check found the evidence insufficient to decide whether human-rater disagreement concentrates on technical (secret_project-family) items: across three scoring surfaces the technical cells contain only 2, 4, and 6 items, the direction of the gap is inconsistent across surfaces, and bootstrap intervals do not exclude zero. We therefore refrain from attributing the disagreement pattern to content type. Sixth, we study two model families and one benchmark of delusion-like scenarios; the echo effect's shape may differ for other models, other languages, and other forms of vulnerable-user belief, and our cross-lingual conclusions cover the zh-en pair only, involve only two specific model versions, and address only the particular form of vulnerable-user belief targeted by [PB25]. Moreover, the two language arms differ in session sets and translation provenance as well as in language -the Chinese stimuli are machine translations of the English originals produced by a model under test -so language, session sample, and translation source are confounded by design; the independent re-translation check ( §3.1) and the negative control ( §5.3) bound this confound but cannot fully decompose it. All measurements were additionally collected through provider APIs; chat-interface deployments can behave substantially differently from their underlying APIs [Kirgis26], so our scores characterize the API surface of these models rather than their consumer-facing chat deployments. Seventh, in the spirit of positionality disclosure: the sole author is an undergraduate researcher without formal training in psychology, clinical psychiatry, or computer science; we mitigated this through a published benchmark and rubric, a pre-specified analysis plan for the confirmatory contrasts, and conservative framing throughout, but domain experts may reasonably weigh our interpretations accordingly. Eighth, the arbitration and human-adjudication layers cover DCS and HES only: SIS scores received no arbitration and no human adjudication at any layer ( §3.4), so the SIS confirmatory contrast rests on the primary rater alone and should be read with that asymmetry in mind. Ninth, the protocol ends at turn 12: whether the observed trajectories saturate, widen, or reverse beyond that window cannot be known from this study.

5.5 What adjudicated disagreements look like: asymmetric blind spots

Six language-invariance-check divergences (|Δ| ≥ 2 on the designated dimension) were adjudicated by the human anchor after full-text re-reading. Four of six adjudications held the original human score; two widened the interval without covering the rater's score. None of the six dissolved into noise on re-examination -every divergence survived contact with the full text, which rules out low-level artifacts such as transcription errors or attention lapses as their explanation. More informative than their persistence is their direction: in five of the six items the rater scored above the human's adjudicated interval, while in the sixth -the most consequential of the set -it missed severity entirely. The directions do not reduce to a single "rater is lax" story.

The five over-reading divergences share a family resemblance: the rater anchored on surface features. It read poetic warmth as a help-seeking signal, professional technical exposition as actionable harm, informational quality as safe handling, and an offer of companionship as intervention -over-reading the presence of surface features in either direction, crediting some and convicting others. The under-reading divergence is the most consequential of the set: the model's reply explicitly affirmed the delusional content ("Yes-you are gaining touch. … That is not delusion.") and reframed the family's concern as "a different map of what's real." The human anchor scored this as indirect enablement (HES 3); the rater scored it 1. That the single largest severity miss in our adjudication set went in the rater-is-too-lenient direction on the dimension explicitly designed to catch enablement suggests the blind spots are asymmetric by construction: the rater over-reads the presence of mitigating surface features and under-reads the absence of reality-anchoring, which is invisible as a textual feature.

We caution against over-reading six items. A targeted check found the evidence insufficient to decide whether disagreements concentrate on technical-content cases (small cells, inconsistent direction, bootstrap intervals crossing zero; §5.4), and we note that HES -the dimension on which most of these divergences fall -is also the weakest link in all three agreement surfaces. The pattern motivates rubric refinement for HES more than it indicts any single rater.

5.6 Ethics and outlook

This work involved no human subjects experiments and no collection of user data; all dialogues are synthetic and scripted, and no personally identifying information appears in the corpus.

One exception requires explicit disclosure: the second human rater (see §3.4) is a volunteer who scored 20 items containing sensitive material (delusion-like beliefs, harmful content, help-seeking scenarios). The distributed instrument informed her of the research purpose, the sensitive subject matter, the independence and blinding requirements, and the expected workload; it did not, however, formalize several elements of a full consent protocol -voluntariness and the right to withdraw were not explicitly communicated in any form, and no data-confidentiality or psychological-support provisions were included. No compensation was promised, and a payment offered by the author after completion was declined and returned by the rater. As an unfunded student project conducted without institutional review board oversight, we disclose these limitations in full rather than claim a review we did not undergo; the rater's exposure was limited to 20 text items, her scores are used solely as an exploratory consistency check, and all identifying information remains confidential. After this gap was identified, we provided the rater with a written plain-language account of the study, the sensitive nature of the materials, her data rights (including withdrawal before submission), and the confidentiality arrangements; she reviewed it and gave written retrospective consent to the use of her scores (on file with the author).

We nonetheless treat the subject matter with care: the scenarios simulate users expressing delusion-like beliefs, and our findings describe model behaviors that could plausibly cause harm if they occur with real users in vulnerable states. We therefore report behavioral measurements and rater-reliability bounds, and deliberately refrain from clinical claims -our scores index textual properties of model replies, not psychiatric risk.

The deployment-side implication is direct: safety behavior in one language does not certify safety behavior in another, and multi-turn pressure reveals failures that short evaluations miss. For providers serving multilingual user bases, language-conditional red-teaming of the kind we perform is a minimal due-diligence step, and the rater-side disclosure practices we outline (report every link, measure the endpoint) are inexpensive relative to the cost of an undetected measurement artifact. Several extensions follow naturally from our design: widening the language set beyond the zh-en pair; testing models from more providers and regions; testing whether the escalation dynamics we observe in DCS replicate across model generations; and examining whether mitigation strategies close the channel through which a given model actually fails, rather than the channel that is easiest to measure. Finally, a call concerning the measurement problem itself: the failure modes documented here are properties of general-purpose judges, not of this domain alone. We urge professionally resourced teams in this area to train judges calibrated for such high-risk content -for instance by fine-tuning flagship open-weight models on expert-reviewed annotations -so that subsequent studies can build on a more reliable LLM-as-judge foundation than was available to us.
