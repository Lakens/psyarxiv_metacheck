To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 17 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Age-related declines in reinforcement learning (RL) are commonly attributed to impairments in dopaminergic prediction-error learning and working memory. However, successful RL may also depend on discovering and applying higher-order learning strategies that reduce memory demands. We investigated whether age differences in RL partly reflect reduced spontaneous strategy discovery, whether providing support attenuates these differences, and whether these effects depend on stimulus verbalizability. Sixty younger and 59 older adults completed an RL task in which stimulusresponse mappings either contained no underlying structure or could be grouped according to semantic or visual regularities. Across four phases, participants received increasingly explicit information about these regularities. Strategy use was assessed using verbal reports and by fitting sigmoid functions to individual learning curves, estimating the transition to strategy-based responding. Older adults showed lower learning performance, reported using strategies less frequently, and transitioned later to strategy-based responding than younger adults. Providing structured stimulusresponse mappings and increasingly explicit information improved learning performance, increased reported strategy use, and accelerated strategy discovery in both age groups, with the largest performance gains observed in older adults and for concrete compared to abstract stimuli. Verbal fluency predicted both learning performance and strategy discovery. These findings suggest that agerelated differences in RL reflect not only declines in associative learning but also reduced spontaneous discovery and application of effective learning strategies. Facilitating strategy discovery may therefore represent a promising way to improve learning in older adulthood.

This study found that older adults were less likely than younger adults to spontaneously discover effective strategies for grouping related information during feedback-based learning. Providing support that helped participants recognize these groupings improved learning, particularly in older adults, suggesting that helping older adults recognize task structure may facilitate learning in everyday life.

1. Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL), the process by which action outcomes are used to optimize subsequent behavior, declines with increasing age (see, e.g., Mell et al., 2005; Simon et al., 2010; Weiler et al., 2008). Nevertheless, even in older adulthood individuals must continuously improve current behaviors and acquire new ones. Older adults need to cope with novel societal demands, such as making videocalls or completing electronic financial transactions, as well as adapt to changes in their own cognitive and physical capabilities. To sustain the independence of older adults, it is important to help them handle these continuing adaptations. The current study therefore aimed to investigate how older adults can be supported in developing and applying efficient learning strategies during such RL tasks.

Traditionally, age-related changes in RL have been attributed to declines in prediction-error-based learning (Chowdhury et al., 2013; Eppinger et al., 2013; Eppinger & Kray, 2011). Reward prediction errors (RPEs) reflect the subjective difference between expected and actual action outcomes. They are signaled by the dopaminergic system in the midbrain and basal ganglia (Glimcher, 2011), a neural system that declines with increasing age (Bäckman et al., 2006; Backman et al., 2010). However, when RL experiences occur in close temporal succession, learning can also be supported by working memory (WM; Collins & Frank, 2012). Consistent with this account, age-related differences in RL performance also depend on individual differences in WM capacity (van de Vijver et al., 2015; van de Vijver & Ligneul, 2020).

Importantly, age-related changes in RL performance may rely not only on alterations in PE-based learning or WM. To allow for fast and flexible learning, individuals often exploit similarities or relations between to-be-learned information to construct higher-level mental models of the task structure, as observed in chunking (Cowan, 2001; Miller, 1956; Thalmann et al., 2019), hierarchical RL (Eckstein & Collins, 2020; Eichenbaum et al., 2020), rule-based RL (Balcarras & Womelsdorf, 2016; Ballard et al., 2018; Schaaf et al., 2022), and category learning (Ashby & Maddox, 2005). Such processes may serve to reduce WM load (Thalmann et al., 2019). Indeed, participants also describe spontaneously searching for such relations between stimuli mapped to the same response button during simple stimulus-response learning tasks (van de Vijver & Ligneul, 2020). Stimuli that are associated with the same response are reported to be grouped into meaningful categories based on, for example, their similarity in content, visual features, or semantic relations.

The ability to generate and apply learning strategies such as chunking and rule formation decreases with increasing age (see, e.g., Badham et al., 2017; Frankenmolen et al., 2018; Oosterman et al., 2014; Worthy & Maddox, 2012). In line with this, in a previous study fewer older than younger adults reported using such strategies during a RL task, while strategy use was associated with better learning performance (van de Vijver & Ligneul, 2020). These findings suggest that age-related differences in learning success may partly be explained by younger adults being more likely to generate and apply relevant strategies that allow for more efficient learning. Interestingly, providing older adults with additional instructions may help them compensate for their reduced spontaneous strategy use (Frankenmolen et al., 2017; Rabi & Minda, 2017). For example, a pretraining in which all stimuli belonging to the same response category were presented together improved subsequent learning performance in older adults, but only in conditions in which rule or category use could benefit performance (Rabi & Minda, 2017).

Age-related differences in strategy use during RL may further depend on the type of stimulus material. Generally, associative learning is facilitated when stimuli can be easily verbalized or named (Radulescu et al., 2022; Schaaf et al., 2024). Moreover, rule-based and category learning appear to be less affected by aging when verbalizable material is used (Endemann & Kamp, 2022; Maddox et al., 2010). One possible explanation is that age-related decline in WM is not uniform across domains: verbal WM tends to be relatively preserved compared to visual WM (D'Antuono et al., 2022; Forsberg et al., 2019). In addition, verbal stimuli have been shown to promote the use of more sophisticated self-generated learning strategies compared to nonverbal, visual stimuli in WM tasks (Ritakallio et al., 2024). Together, this suggests that during RL, older adults may be better able to generate and apply efficient learning strategies when associations involve verbalizable stimuli than when they involve abstract visual material.

Thus, the aim of the current study was threefold. First, we investigated whether age-related differences in RL success could partly be explained by differences in the spontaneous use of learning strategies. Second, we examined whether older adults would be more likely to generate and apply such strategies when the possibility to do so was suggested to them more explicitly than when it was not. Third, we tested whether age-related differences in learning performance and strategy use would be smaller when stimuli were easily verbalizable compared to when they were more abstract. We hypothesized that learning performance would generally be lower in older than in younger adults, and that older adults would less often generate efficient learning strategies than younger adults (cf. van de Vijver & Ligneul, 2020). Strategy use was expected to result in higher learning success. Furthermore, we predicted that providing additional information about the optimal strategy would reduce age-related differences in both strategy use and learning performance (Frankenmolen et al., 2018; Rabi & Minda, 2017). Finally, we expected age-related differences in strategy use and learning performance to be smaller for verbalizable that for abstract stimulus material (D'Antuono et al., 2022; Forsberg et al., 2019; Maddox et al., 2010; Ritakallio et al., 2024).

To test these hypotheses, we systematically examined age-related differences in strategy use in an RL context. Specifically, we approached this in a stepwise fashion, comparing learning performance and strategy use between younger and older adults while providing the participants with increasing amounts of information to support them generating an efficient learning strategy. To assess the influence of the type of stimulus material, we compared conditions in which associations could be formed on the basis of semantic versus visual stimulus characteristics. Strategy use was measured in two ways. First, participants provided verbal reports describing how they learned the stimulusresponse associations after each block. Second, to obtain a more objective measure of strategy use, we fitted a sigmoid function to the accuracy data for each response button. This approach allowed us to quantify the point at which participants transitioned from gradual stimulus-response learning to the use of a higher-level strategy (Löwe et al., 2024).

Because individual differences in WM capacity and cognitive reserve have been shown to predict preserved strategy use in explicit and category learning (Frankenmolen et al., 2017 (Frankenmolen et al., , 2018;; Racine et al., 2006), we additionally examined the relation between strategy use and WM capacityfoot_0 . To assess whether the advantage of verbalizable stimulus material was related to verbal abilities, we also examined the relation with verbal fluency, which may support the formation of meaningful stimulus associations. Finally, we explored whether cognitive flexibility, reflecting the ability to shift between task sets, was associated with strategy generation.

2.2 Participants

A convenience sample was recruited through the network of the experimenters. In total, 60 younger and 62 older adults participated in the experiment. Three older adults did not complete the experiment and were therefore excluded, resulting in a final sample of 60 younger (19-23 years, M = 20.9, SD = 1.29; 16 male, 44 female) and 59 older adults (65-89 years, M = 71.5, SD = 5.71; 32 male, 26 female, 1 unspecified). All older adults scored above 16 on the Cognitive Screening Test 20 (CST-20; (Deelman et al., 1989; Ponds et al., 1992) indicating the absence of cognitive impairment beyond what is typically expected in normal aging. Participants received a reimbursement of €12 for their participation. All participants provided informed consent prior to the start of the experiment. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the Faculty of Social and Behavioural Sciences at Utrecht University (22-0491).

2.3 Materials

2.3.1 Reinforcement learning task

On each trial of the RL task, a visual stimulus was presented until the participant pressed one of three response buttons (keyboard keys 'R', 'Y', and 'I'; see Figure 1a). The goal for participants was to learn the correct response for each stimulus through trial-and-error, based on the feedback they received after each choice. After a delay of 200 ms, feedback was presented for 1000 ms and consisted of a smiley face for positive feedback or a frowny face for negative feedback. If no response was given within 1500 ms after stimulus onset, the words 'TE LAAT' (too late) were presented as feedback. Trials were separated by an inter-trial interval (ITI) of 1000 ms.

Half of the participants were assigned to the experimental ('Structure') condition of the task (N=30/30 younger/older), while the other half were assigned to the control ('No Structure') condition (N=30/29). In the No Structure condition, stimuli were randomly mapped to the response buttons in all eight blocks. In the Structure condition, stimuli were randomly mapped in the first two blocks. In the remaining six blocks, stimuli were mapped to the response buttons according to specific rules (see Figure 1b). Concrete stimuli that were mapped to the same button were linked by a shared semantic theme. For example, three pictures related to the theme 'play' were mapped to one button (swing, ball, wheelbarrow), three pictures related to 'rain' to the second button (boot, umbrella, cloud), and three pictures related to 'sea' to the third button (fish, anchor, sailboat; see Figure 1c). Abstract stimuli that were mapped to the same button shared a visual feature, such as their dominant color or the number of elements that they contained (e.g., one vs. two vs. four colored circles; see Figure 1d).

Across blocks, participants in the Structure condition received increasing information about the underlying organization of the stimulus-response mappings. In blocks three and four, this organization was present but not explicitly communicated, allowing participants to discover it independently. In blocks five and six, participants were informed beforehand: 'The pictures are not randomly distributed across the buttons. Try to find out how the pictures are organized.' In blocks seven and eight, the hint depended on the stimulus type. For the concrete blocks, the hint was: 'Pay attention to pictures related to the themes "laundry", "holiday", and "music" to find out which picture corresponds to which button.' For the abstract blocks, the hint was: 'Pay attention to the number of elements in the pictures to find out which picture corresponds to which button.' Participants were not informed about the specific mapping between categories and response buttons.

Before the start of the experiment, participants received detailed instructions and completed a practice version of the task. In the practice block, only three stimuli were presented, each mapped to a different response button. Participants were informed that correct responses earned them two points, whereas incorrect responses resulted in a loss of one point. This unequal reward distribution was used to prevent participants from ending up with a negative number of points and to remain motivated throughout the task. After each block, participants were asked: 'Please tell the experimenter: how did you try to remember the associations in this block?' They provided verbal responses, which were recorded using a voice recorder. Finally, the number of points earned in the block was displayed on screen, after which participants could take a break before starting the next block. Depending on individual response times, the total duration of the task was approximately 60 minutes.

2.3.2 Verbal Fluency task

To assess verbal fluency, participants were asked to name as many animals as possible within 60 seconds (Tombaugh et al., 1999). Both animal categories and specific animal types were considered correct responses. Responses were recorded using a voice recorder, and the number of unique responses was scored offline by an experimenter, excluding repetitions and intrusions.

2.3.3 Cognitive Flexibility Inventory

The Cognitive Flexibility Inventory (CFI; Dennis & van der Wal, 2010) measures three aspects of cognitive flexibility: (1) the tendency to perceive difficult situations as controllable, (2) the ability to perceive multiple alternative explanations for life events and human behavior, and (3) the ability to generate multiple alternative solutions to difficult situations. The questionnaire consists of 20 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 'strongly disagree' to 'strongly agree'. Item scores were summed to obtain a total cognitive flexibility score, with higher scores indicating greater cognitive flexibility. Accuracy on the mathematical operations was monitored to ensure task compliance; all participants achieved at least 85% accuracy.

2.3.4 Operation Span task

Participants performed a version of the Operation Span task (Ospan; Turner & Engle, 1989), in which vowels had to be maintained in WM while solving simple mathematical operations. Set size varied between 3 and 5 items, with three sets of each size presented in pseudo-randomized order. The task lasted approximately 10-15 minutes. Performance was quantified using a partial-credit scoring procedure (Conway et al., 2005), whereby the proportion of correctly recalled vowels in the correct serial position was calculated for each set. These proportions were averaged across sets of the same size and subsequently across set sizes to yield a total score.

2.4 Procedure

The experiment was conducted on a laptop in a quiet location convenient for the participant. The reinforcement learning task was programmed in Presentation (www.neurobs.com), and questionnaires were administered via the Qualtrics platform (www.qualtrics.com). The CST-20 was administered by a trained experimenter. For the Ospan, stimuli were presented on a computer screen, while participants recorded their responses on an answer sheet. Older adults first completed the CST-20. Subsequently, all participants performed the reinforcement learning task, followed by the verbal fluency task and the CFI. Finally, participants completed the Ospan and an exit questionnaire, which included questions about demographics, education level, retirement status, motivation, and fatigue.

2.5 Computational modeling

We fit a standard logistic regression model (a sigmoid) separately to the data of each participant, condition and response button (cf. Löwe et al., 2024). Specifically, the sigmoid was parametrized as follows:

Thus, the probability of a correct response P(c) was modelled as a function of the number of repetitions r of stimuli associated with a particular response. Here, t0 represented the switch point, k represented the slope of the function, and U represented a lapse parameter to account for suboptimal performance after the switch (Ramotowska et al., 2024). Because on each trial there are three response options, the lower asymptote is fixed at 1/3, indicating that in the absence of information, participants perform at chance level. Optimal parameters were estimated using a particle swarm optimizer with default settings (Clerc, 2010), minimizing the squared difference between observed and predicted accuracy. We used a bounded optimization with lower bounds t0=0.5, k=0.1, and U=1/3 and upper bounds t0=10 (the maximum number of repetitions), k=100, and U=1. The optimizer was initialized with t0=5 (the median number of repetitions), k=5, and U=1. Because k is more discriminative on the lower end of the scale we log-transformed this parameter before fitting.

Two control models were implemented and estimated in the same way. A linear model was estimated in which P(c) could increase as a linear function of the number of repetitions. For the linear model we only estimated the slope within bounds [0,10], initialized at 0.1; the intercept was fixed at change level 1/3. 2.6 Statistical analysis

To test whether response accuracy on the RL task and sigmoid switch points (t0), as well as nuisance parameters sigmoid slopes (k) and upper bound (U), differed between age groups (younger, older), conditions (Structure, No Structure), information phases (1-4), and stimulus types (concrete, abstract), we performed linear mixed-effects models (Baayen et al., 2008). Specifically, we first fitted the maximal model with age, condition, phase, and stimulus type as well as their interactions as fixed effects, and with by-participant random intercepts. Then, we interpreted the estimated effects of this model using approximate F tests with denominator degrees of freedom estimated from the mixed-model framework. For the generalized mixed model, fixed effects were evaluated using Wald chi-square approximations. Significant interactions were followed up with targeted post-hoc comparisons using estimated marginal means, using Tukey correction for multiple comparisons.

Strategies that participants described after a block were considered as chunking when at least two stimuli that were mapped to the same button were being related to each other based on shared characteristics, an overarching theme, an event, or a story. This way, for each participant and block we obtained a dichotomous score indicating whether a (chunking) strategy was used or not. We compared the number of younger and older participants reporting a strategy per condition, phase, and stimulus type using a generalized linear mixed model with a binomial link function. Moreover, to examine whether strategy use resulted in more efficient learning, we added strategy use as a factor to the previous analyses on accuracy, t0, k, and U to see if this explained additional variance. We compared these more complex models with the standard model without strategy use with likelihood ratio tests.

Where appropriate, we report significant interactions with strategy use with similar approximate Ftests as before.

To assess the role of individual differences in the ability to discover efficient learning strategies, we standardized (z-scored) individual difference metrics Ospan, verbal fluency, and CFI within each age group, focusing on the Structured condition. We averaged response accuracy as well as switch points over the second to fourth phases as proxies of strategy use. Phase 1 was excluded because it was identical to the No Structure condition and no strategy use was expected. Using linear mixed models, we assessed whether (standardized) Ospan, fluency, and CFI as well as their interactions would explain variance in averaged accuracy and switch points, after controlling for age and stimulus type. Participant was added as a random intercept.

3.1 Reinforcement learning behavior

Response accuracy was analyzed with a linear mixed-effects model including age group, condition, stimulus type, phase, and their interactions, with a random intercept for participant. Task performance increased over phases, F(3, 2709) = 318.00, p < .001 (Figure 2). Although there was a main effect of condition, F(1, 115) = 71.87, p < .001, this was further nuanced by an interaction effect of phase and condition, F(3, 2709) = 90.53, p < .001. Post hoc tests indicated that accuracy was higher in the Structure than in the No Structure condition from phase 2 onwards, whereas no condition difference was present in phase 1 (phase 1: p = .41; phases 2-4: all p-values < .001). Thus, the condition advantage emerged only after the initial, identical phase and increased across the task. These results confirm that participants were able to use the organization of stimulus-response mappings and the information provided about this to improve their learning.

As expected, older adults were less accurate than younger adults overall, F(1, 115) = 91.91, p < .001. A significant interaction effect of age and condition demonstrated that the difference in accuracy between the Structure and No Structure conditions was larger in older than in younger adults, F(1, 115) = 4.70, p = .032. This effect was driven by a three-way interaction of condition, age, and phase, F(3, 2709) = 8.68, p < .001. Follow-up contrasts indicated that the age difference decreased over phases in the Structure condition (phase 1 vs phase 3: p = .029; phase 1 vs. phase 4: p < .001; phase 2 vs. phase 4: p < .001; phase 3 vs. phase 4: p = .0065, other contrasts n.s.), whereas it remained stable in the No Structure condition (all p-values ≥ .066). Thus, older adults benefitted more from the organization of stimulus-response mappings and the information provided about these mappings than younger adults did.

Accuracy was also higher for concrete than for abstract stimuli, F(1, 2709) = 324.38, p < .001. An interaction of age and stimulus type showed that, as hypothesized, the age difference in accuracy was smaller for concrete than abstract stimuli, F(1, 2709) = 19.46, p < .001. There was also a significant interaction of stimulus type and phase, F(3, 2709) = 4.07, p = .006. Pairwise comparisons showed that accuracy improved across all phases for abstract stimuli (all p-values < .001). For concrete stimuli, performance likewise improved strongly across all phases (all p-values < .001) except the phase 3-4 contrast which was not significant (p = .21). These effects were further nuanced by a three-way interaction of age, stimulus type, and phase F(3, 2709) = 6.58, p < .001. The age-related accuracy difference changed across phases for concrete stimuli, with the older-younger gap significantly decreasing from phase 1 to phase 2 (phase 1 vs. phase 2: p = .013; phase 2 vs. phase 3: p = .66; phase 3 vs. phase 4: p = .21). For abstract stimuli these contrasts were not significant. These results confirm that older adults indeed benefitted from the concrete stimulus materials.

No three-way interaction of condition, age, and stimulus type and no four-way interaction were observed (both p > 0.64). However, to better understand how much older adults were able to improve their performance, we explored whether this group were able to approach the accuracy level of younger adults in the condition where they performed best. Interestingly, when older adults were provided with specific hints in phase 4 and were learning about concrete pictures, their response accuracy was indeed not distinguishable from that of the younger adults, t(58) = 0.86, p = .39.

3.2 Effect of verbal reports on strategy use

Self-reported strategy use was analyzed with a binomial generalized linear mixed-effects model including age group, condition, stimulus type, phase, and all interactions, with a random intercept for participant. Generally, strategy use increased over task phases, χ²(3) = 41.59, p < .001 (Figure 3a). More participants reported using a strategy in the Structure compared to the No structure condition, χ²(1) = 29.59, p < .001. In addition, there was a strong condition-by-phase interaction, χ²(3) = 54.85, p < .001, indicating that changes in strategy reports over phases differed across conditions. Specifically, in the Structure condition, reported strategy use increased strongly from phase 1 to phase 2 and phase 3, and further from phase 2 to phase 3 (all p-values ≤ .001). Contrasts involving phase 4 in the Structure condition yielded very large standard errors and were therefore not interpreted. In the No Structure condition, however, reported strategy use changed only modestly over phases; after Tukey correction, only the contrast between phase 1 and phase 4 was significant, z = -2.72, p = .033. The results confirm that our manipulations of rule presence and the information provided about this resulted in increased strategy use.

As expected, younger adults reported more strategy use than older adults did, χ²(1) = 4.78, p = .029. This pattern was further qualified by an age-by-stimulus-type-by-phase interaction, χ²(3) = 8.35, p = .039, and a four-way age-by-condition-by-stimulus-type-by-phase interaction, χ²(3) = 11.11, p = .011. However, due to the diversity of self-reported strategy use across design cells, this pattern was only significant in some cells (Appendix A, Table A1 in the Supplementary material shows all follow-up contrasts).

No main effect of stimulus type was observed, χ²(1) = 0.85, p = .36. Post hoc analyses including stimulus type did not reveal a meaningful or consistent pattern of effects (see Appendix A Table A1). All remaining interactions were not significant.

3.3 Relation between reported strategy use and learning success

To examine whether performance covaried with self-reported strategy use, strategy use was added to the model for accuracy. A likelihood ratio test revealed that this model was preferred over the simpler model without strategy use, χ²(30)=193.53, p < .001. Overall, learning performance was higher in participants that reported using a strategy than participants that did not, F(1, 2743.52) = 34.70, p < .001 (Figure 3b). This effect depended on condition, F(1, 2749.46) = 12.64, p < .001, age, F(1, 2748.44) = 11.47, p < .001, and stimulus type, F(1, 2725.23) = 6.54, p = .011. There were no interactions of reported strategy use with phase, nor any higher-order interactions. From the previously reported interactions, only the condition-by-phase, F(3, 2750.29) = 7.16, p<.001, and the age-by-stimulus-type interaction, F(1, 2718.86) = 7.06, p = .0079, remained.

Follow-up contrasts between all cells where there were both participants that did and participants that did not report using a strategy showed that the Structure advantage was especially evident when participants reported using a strategy: In those cases, accuracy was higher in the Structure than in the No Structure condition (most p < .001). Thus, reported strategy use was associated with particularly high accuracy in the Structure condition. This effect was driven by older participants, as they showed a strategy-related increase in accuracy in more design cells than younger participants (most p < .001, see Appendix A Table A2).

3.4 Computational modeling results

When comparing the sigmoid model to its two competitor models, the linear and power functions, we found that, overall, the preferred model was the sigmoid. On average, the sigmoid model also provided an adequate fit to the data (Figure 4b and c).

3.5 Strategy use as reflected in model-derived switch points

The switch-point parameter t0 was analyzed with the same mixed-effects model as response accuracy (analyses of nuisance parameters k and U are presented in Appendix B in the Supplementary material). We found significant main effects of condition, F(1, 115) = 75.80, p < .001, and phase, F(3, 2709) = 96.00, p < .001 (Figure 5a). In addition, a condition-by-phase interaction was present, F(3, 2709) = 36.63, p < .001. In the Structure condition, as predicted, the switch point occurred earlier in later phases, when more information was provided. This effect was significant across all phases (all p-values ≤ .001, see Appendix C Table C1 in the Supplementary material). In the No Structure condition, by contrast, the switch point occurred earlier only in phase 3 and 4 compared to phase 1 (phase 1 vs. phase 3: p < .001; phase 1 vs. phase 4: p < .001), while the remaining contrasts between phases were not significant. Thus, the timing of the switch point changed much more over phases in the Structure compared to the No Structure condition, again suggesting that our manipulations resulted in more efficient strategy use.

In line with the age differences in response accuracy and verbal reports, switch point were later in older than in younger adults, F(1, 115) = 63.09, p < .001. Abstract stimuli were associated with later switch point than concrete stimuli, F(1, 2709) = 79.28, p < .001. However, no interactions of age or stimulus type with condition or phase reached significance in the baseline model (all p-values > .10), indicating that the main condition-by-phase pattern was not further qualified by these factors.

4. Discussion

The present study had three main aims. First, we investigated whether age-related differences in RL performance could partly be explained by differences in the spontaneous use of learning strategies. Second, we examined whether older adults would be more likely to generate and apply effective strategies when the opportunity to do so was made more explicit than when it was not. Third, we tested whether age-related differences in learning and strategy use would be reduced when stimuli were easily verbalizable rather than abstract. To address these questions, younger and older adults completed an RL task in which the information that was provided about the structure of stimulusresponse mappings as well as stimulus verbalizability were systematically manipulated. Strategy use was assessed using both verbal reports and model-based analyses of learning trajectories. Specifically, the timing of sudden improvements in response accuracy was quantified as the switch point of a sigmoid function fitted to individual response accuracy data, which was assumed to reflect a shift from gradual associative learning to explicit strategy-based responding.

As expected, older adults showed lower learning performance and reported strategy use less frequently than younger adults, and younger adults also transitioned more rapidly from incremental learning to strategy use. Introducing more structured stimulus-response mappings and providing more explicit information about this improved learning performance in both groups, with older adults showing relatively greater performance benefits. Both manipulations also increased reported strategy use and led participants to discover efficient strategies earlier. Contrary to our expectations, however, these benefits were comparable across age groups, suggesting that younger adults were equally responsive to the manipulations. Learning performance was higher for nameable than abstract stimulus materials, again particularly in older adults, whereas the effects on strategy use and strategy discovery were again comparable across groups. Finally, individual differences in verbal fluency seemed the strongest predictor of learning performance and strategy discovery, especially in older adults.

Traditionally, age-related differences in RL have often been explained in terms of declines in dopaminergic PE-based learning (e.g., Chowdhury et al., 2013; Eppinger et al., 2013; Eppinger & Kray, 2011) and WM (van de Vijver et al., 2015; van de Vijver & Ligneul, 2020). However, the present findings indicate that differences in learning performance may also reflect age-related differences in the spontaneous discovery and application of higher-level learning strategies. Importantly, this was suggested by multiple, converging findings. Participants who reported using a strategy generally showed higher learning performance and earlier switch points in their learning trajectories, suggesting that performance improved rapidly once an effective strategy had been discovered. In addition, the effects of task condition (Structure vs. No structure) on switch points were primarily observed in participants and phases in which strategy use was reported, suggesting that the benefits of the Structure condition depended on the discovery and application of effective learning strategies. Finally, models incorporating abrupt performance transitions more often provided the best fit in conditions in which strategy use was expected, and for participants that reported strategy use. Together, these findings suggest that abrupt increases in response accuracy reflected transitions from gradual trial-bytrial learning toward the use of more efficient higher-level strategies, consistent with participants' reports of spontaneously grouping stimuli into meaningful response categories.

Such abrupt transitions are consistent with the idea that successful learning in more complex environments does not only depend on incremental RL, but also on the ability to detect and exploit higher-order regularities in the task structure (Ashby & Maddox, 2005; Balcarras & Womelsdorf, 2016; Eckstein & Collins, 2020; Farashahi et al., 2017). In the context of categorization, such learning has been linked to explicit hypothesis testing and the construction of higher-level rule representations supported by frontal cortical systems, in contrast to more gradual procedural learning mechanisms mediated by the striatum (Ashby et al., 1998; Ashby & Maddox, 2005; Minda et al., 2024). Consistent with this account, recent work by Bähner et al. (2025) showed that humans as well as rats learn multidimensional tasks by sequentially testing hypotheses about underlying task rules, a process associated with abrupt behavioral transitions and prefrontal representations of abstract rules.

Similarly, studies using multidimensional RL tasks suggest that humans often initially rely on rule-or feature-based strategies and spontaneously construct hierarchical task representations rather than learning individual stimulus-response associations (Collins et al., 2014; Eckstein & Collins, 2020; Farashahi et al., 2017 Farashahi et al., , 2020;; Schaaf et al., 2022). Such hypothesis testing may reduce task dimensionality and thereby facilitate rapid and flexible learning, consistent with the abrupt improvements in performance in the present study. The observation that strategies were also reported in the No Structure condition and in the first phase of the Structure condition further suggests that participants spontaneously attempted to search for -and succeeded to find -meaningful regularities even when no underlying structure was constructed, in line with previous findings (van de Vijver & Ligneul, 2020). Indeed, reported strategy use in these conditions was again associated with improved performance.

If successful learning indeed depends on the spontaneous generation and testing of higher-order task representations, such higher-order learning processes may be particularly vulnerable to aging. Previous studies have shown that older adults are less likely to spontaneously generate and apply effective learning strategies in the context of category learning (Badham et al., 2017; Frankenmolen et al., 2018) and RL (van de Vijver & Ligneul, 2020). Specifically, older adults were shown to create fewer conceptual clusters linking stimuli that shared relevant characteristics, suggesting reduced spontaneous abstraction or restructuring of task representations (Badham et al., 2017). Consistent with this idea, Maddox and colleagues (2010) showed that older adults switched later from explicit hypothesis-testing strategies toward the task-appropriate strategy during category learning. Interestingly, age-related deficits in rule-based learning largely disappeared when analyses were restricted to participants who eventually adopted the appropriate strategy, suggesting that age differences may partly reflect difficulties in discovering or consistently applying efficient strategies rather than deficits in learning per se. Such difficulties may be related to age-related changes in frontal systems involved in hypothesis testing and switching between alternative task representations (Maddox et al., 2010; Yao & Hsieh, 2022).

Crucially, older adults benefitted from the provision of additional structure and cues. Previous studies similarly demonstrated that additional instructions or category-relevant information can help compensate for reduced spontaneous strategy use in older adults (Frankenmolen et al., 2017; Rabi & Minda, 2017). Together, these findings suggest that older adults may be less impaired in using higherlevel strategies than in spontaneously discovering or switching toward them during learning. Providing additional structure or cues may therefore reduce demands on self-initiated rule discovery and abstraction processes that rely on hierarchical control mechanisms in the prefrontal cortex (Badre et al., 2010; Badre & Nee, 2017; Crescentini et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2015). Interestingly, the age difference in strategy use did not decrease over phases while the current manipulation did result in a reduced age difference in learning performance. This difference in the effect of the task manipulation on strategy use versus learning performance may partly reflect ceiling effects in learning performance in younger adults. However, it could also suggest that the manipulation primarily improved the efficiency of strategy application rather than explicit strategy awareness itself.

As expected, learning performance was higher for nameable than abstract stimulus material, with this effect being particularly pronounced in older adults. This is in line with previous studies showing that associative, rule-based, and category learning are facilitated when stimuli can be more easily verbalized or linked to existing semantic knowledge (Endemann & Kamp, 2022; Maddox et al., 2010; Radulescu et al., 2022; Schaaf et al., 2024). One possible explanation is that verbalizable stimuli support chunking and category formation, thereby reducing WM demands during learning (Norris et al., 2020; Thalmann et al., 2019). This may be especially beneficial for older adults, given evidence that verbal WM is relatively preserved compared to visual WM in aging (D' Antuono et al., 2020; Forsberg et al., 2019). In addition, studies using more naturalistic or concrete stimuli suggest that such stimuli are processed more efficiently and may facilitate the construction of higher-level stimulus representations during RL (Farashahi et al., 2020). Interestingly, when stimuli could be verbalized this had a positive effect on learning performance while we found no clear effect on reported strategy use, suggesting that verbalizable material may support learning not only through explicit strategy discovery, but also through more implicit forms of semantic scaffolding.

Individual differences in verbal fluency predicted learning performance and earlier strategy discovery not only when stimuli were easily verbalizable, but across task conditions. This suggests that the contribution of verbal processes may extend beyond attaching labels to concrete stimuli. Even with abstract stimuli successful performance depended on discovering higher-order groupings to be able to meaningfully categorize stimuli. Individuals with higher verbal fluency may be better able to construct and maintain such category or rule representations, thereby facilitating strategy discovery and application (Troyer et al., 1997; Zemla, 2022). This interpretation is also consistent with verbal fluency emerging as the most consistent cognitive predictor of learning performance and strategy discovery in the present study, suggesting that efficient semantic organization may contribute uniquely to strategybased learning.

Several methodological limitations should be taken into account when interpreting the current findings. First, our results show that strategy use cannot be fully captured by a single modeling approach. In some conditions, participants reported using strategies even though models assuming more gradual learning provided the best fit to the data. This was particularly apparent in conditions with very high overall performance, where early abrupt transitions may have been more difficult to detect reliably. Moreover, not all participants appeared to show clear switch-like transitions, suggesting that estimated switch points may not always reflect meaningful qualitative changes in learning. This may be exacerbated because the sigmoid function that we applied to quantify switch points does not explicitly consider gradual RL-like learning prior to the switch point. Future studies could therefore further improve the assessment of strategy-based learning by combining self-report and computational modeling with more direct behavioral or neural markers of strategy discovery.

Analyses restricted to participants or blocks showing clear evidence of switching may help to further validate the interpretation of switch-point estimates and guide the development of novel and more accurate computational models.

A limitation of our task design is that once participants discovered a useful strategy in one phase, they likely continued to use it in later phases, potentially resulting in carry-over effects. Nevertheless, performance continued to improve and switch points continued to decrease across successive phases, suggesting that both task structure and the additional information provided benefits beyond those attributable to prior strategy acquisition. Additionally, although the observed age differences in the relations between cognitive measures and task performance were theoretically interesting, these effects were relatively modest and should therefore be interpreted with caution until replicated.

Finally, assessing spontaneous strategy use remains challenging, as asking participants about strategy use may itself encourage the use or reporting of strategies. Although this raises the possibility that age differences in self-report partly reflect differences in reporting or awareness (Price et al., 2008; Schwarz, 2006), the convergence between verbal reports and model-based parameters suggests that the self-report measures captured meaningful aspects of strategy use. At the same time, participants may not always be fully aware of the strategies they use or able to verbalize them accurately. Computational modeling based on behavioral patterns is thus an important additional tool to identify strategy use.

More generally, the present findings may also have implications beyond laboratory RL tasks. Older adults particularly benefitted when useful regularities in the task were made more explicit, suggesting that age-related differences in learning may partly reflect reduced spontaneous strategy discovery. This may be relevant for everyday learning situations, such as education, rehabilitation, or technology learning, in which making task structures more transparent could facilitate learning in older adults. Importantly, the present findings provide an initial indication of the processes that may underlie agerelated differences in strategy-based learning, suggesting that older adults may experience particular difficulties in spontaneously discovering or switching toward efficient higher-level strategies. Future research should further investigate under which circumstances learners spontaneously rely on higherlevel strategies during RL, and in which situations older adults experience the greatest difficulties in discovering or applying such strategies, as well as the types of support that are most effective in facilitating this process.
