Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are increasingly used to support decision-making, automate tasks, personalize services, and improve system performance across domains such as healthcare, education, transportation, cybersecurity, manufacturing, and workplace productivity. As AI adoption expands, clearer guidance is needed to connect human-centered design principles with measurable human outcomes. This systematic review synthesized 44 empirical studies on human-centered AI (HCAI) and human-AI interaction (HAX) to examine measurement approaches, framework categories, AI roles, and domainspecific findings. The reviewed studies showed that AI support was often associated with improved objective outcomes, including task performance and decision quality. Subjective outcomes, including trust, satisfaction, usability, workload, and perceived control, were more variable and depended on transparency, explanation design, task fit, user expertise, and the level of human control. Across studies, human-led AI support was the most common role, especially in high-stakes domains where humans retained final authority. Collaborative AI appeared in tasks requiring shared activity, feedback, or adaptive support, while AI-led support appeared mainly within mixed-role systems. Domainspecific findings showed that healthcare emphasized accuracy, reliability, and overreliance; education emphasized engagement, scaffolding, and ownership; transportation emphasized safety, timing, modality, and trust calibration; and other domains emphasized fairness, transparency, adoption, and acceptance. This review contributes a cross-domain synthesis that connects HCAI and HAX principles with AI roles, evaluation measures, and human outcomes, supporting future frameworks that are easier to evaluate and apply in real-world settings.

AI systems refer to tools that embed these approaches in tasks where people interact with, rely on, or oversee system behavior. However, the adoption of AI in these domains is advancing faster than the development of clear frameworks to guide its responsible and human-centered use. This gap creates challenges for researchers, designers, and organizations seeking to define appropriate AI support while maintaining meaningful human oversight.

In response to these challenges, human-centered AI (HCAI) and human-AI interaction (HAX) have emerged as important areas of research and practice, because they help define what AI systems should support and what users experience when working with those systems. HCAI focuses on the design of AI systems that improve human outcomes while supporting human values, control, and well-being [1], [2]. HAX focuses more directly on the quality of interaction between humans and AI systems, including user understanding, interpretation, and collaboration with AI. These two areas inform each other. HCAI provides the guiding values that shape HAX design goals, while HAX findings help refine and validate HCAI principles in real-world use. This relationship makes the two fields inherently interdependent, as reflected in Fig. 1. Even with this close relationship, findings on these shared concerns remain divided across domains. This fragmentation limits the development of shared design guidance and makes it difficult to transfer lessons across domains [3], [4].

This issue is also reflected in practice. According to the World Health Organization, only 8% of countries in the European Region have a dedicated national AI strategy for health [5]. A survey by Anandarajan [6] of more than 500 data professionals also reported that 41% of companies used agentic AI in daily operations and only 27% of organizations had governance frameworks to manage and monitor these systems. These findings suggest that AI adoption is advancing, while guidance on design, evaluation, and governance remains limited. Without a unified framework, organizations may need to independently determine which decisions should be handled by AI systems and which should remain with human users. This can lead to inconsistent design choices and uneven evaluation practices. These problems become especially concerning in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, transportation, and cybersecurity, where poor design decisions can affect safety and the quality of human judgment.

A. Human-Centered AI and Human-AI Interaction

To understand the relationship between HCAI and HAX, their roles need to be clearly defined. Drawing on humancentered practices, HCAI can be understood as an approach to designing AI systems that enhance human capabilities while keeping the design process centered on human needs and values, with user control built into the system [7]. HCAI emphasizes systems that protect human well-being and follow ethical design principles. It also focuses on aligning AI behavior with human values, making system actions transparent, and keeping people meaningfully involved in oversight. Because AI systems can process large amounts of data and support adaptive decisionmaking, HCAI also enables personalization and customization, helping AI systems better serve individual and social needs.

HCAI is also closely connected to the broader tradition of human factors. Concepts such as supervisory control, automation bias, and trust calibration are especially relevant to AI system design. Supervisory control emphasizes the need for humans to monitor and guide automated systems. Automation bias explains why users may over-rely on AI recommendations even when those recommendations are incorrect. Trust calibration focuses on designing systems that support appropriate levels of trust. These concepts show that HCAI extends beyond technical performance and considers the influence of AI systems on human judgment, behavior, and downstream outcomes.

HAX focuses on the study and design of interactions between humans and AI systems. It examines the user's experience with AI systems, including user interpretation of system outputs and collaboration with AI. It also considers the influence of interface features on user behavior. In this context, HAX addresses whether an AI system is usable, whether its outputs are clear, whether its reasoning can be explained, whether users receive helpful feedback, whether people have enough control, and whether human-AI collaboration supports good decisions [3]. In this review, HAX is used to refer to the interaction and experience aspects of human-AI systems. HCAI provides broader design principles, and HAX examines the expression of those principles in real-world interactions and measurable outcomes.

B. Literature Landscape

The current body of HCAI and HAX research spans several disciplines and application areas. Prior studies have examined these concepts in manufacturing, healthcare, transportation, education, business, and other domains. For instance, Liu et al. [8] examined HCAI as an enabler of the transition from technology-centered to human-centered manufacturing in Industry 5.0. The authors introduced the HER framework, which includes human-centric design, ethical management, and responsible implications, to identify HCAI practices across 4,693 Chinese firms. Their findings showed that HCAI can help firms balance manufacturing efficiency with human and social values.

Other studies have examined HAX design for improving work outcomes. Krakowski et al. [9] conducted a field experiment at a pharmaceutical firm and found that tailoring HAX to match users' cognitive preferences was associated with improved sales performance. This finding suggests that AI system design may be more effective when it accounts for differences in people's information processing and decisionmaking. Similarly, Passalacqua et al. [10] reviewed HCAI research and found that psychological experiences and human factors remain underrepresented. They also identified a gap between empirical and non-empirical studies, especially in the development and evaluation of theoretical frameworks.

Transportation research has also shown the importance of human-centered AI design. Kumar et al. [11] examined HCAI in autonomous vehicles and identified several challenges related to AI-driven human-machine interaction, including limited global standardization, trust, and data privacy. These concerns are similar to challenges found in other domains, where AI systems support human understanding, appropriate reliance, and safe decision-making. In addition, Vaccaro et al. [12] reviewed experimental studies on human-AI collaboration and identified factors that contribute to both success and failure in collaborative performance.

These studies show that HCAI and HAX have been examined across many domains, and the evidence base remains difficult to compare. Studies often use different frameworks, outcome measures, terminology, and assumptions. Some studies focus on ethics and governance, while others focus on usability, trust, transparency, decision-making, or performance. As a result, the field lacks a clear synthesis of which principles are most often used, which outcomes are measured, and which findings can be transferred across domains. This limits the ability to build a shared foundation for human-centered AI design and evaluation.

C. Research Objectives

Although several HCAI and HAX frameworks have been proposed, many remain difficult to apply in real-world settings. Alelyani [13] reported that existing frameworks, including ISO 21448, Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF), and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, remain largely theoretical and require more empirical validation in real-world contexts. This makes it difficult to apply these frameworks in dynamic and cross-disciplinary settings. AI systems used in healthcare, transportation, cybersecurity, and education face different risks, user needs, and evaluation requirements. At the same time, these domains also share common design concerns, such as trust, transparency, usability, safety, accountability, and human oversight.

Therefore, a key gap remains because the field lacks a shared approach for aligning AI design with human values and measurable human outcomes across domains. It remains unclear which HCAI and HAX principles are linked to outcomes such as trust, usability, workload, safety, transparency, decision quality, and user acceptance. It is also unclear which measures are most commonly used to evaluate these outcomes and whether these measures can be compared across application areas. This fragmentation makes it difficult to determine which design principles are supported by empirical evidence and which principles remain mainly conceptual. To address this gap, the present review systematically synthesizes HCAI and HAX research across application domains, with a focus on the definition, evaluation, and translation of human-centered design principles into measurable human outcomes. Specifically, this review organizes existing frameworks, concepts, methodologies, and empirical findings to identify common patterns, shared challenges, and remaining gaps across domains. This review has three main goals. The first goal is to synthesize existing HCAI and HAX frameworks, concepts, and methodologies, with attention to their application and evaluation in empirical studies. The second goal is to compare applications across domains such as healthcare, education, transportation, and cybersecurity, with attention to both shared design concerns and domain-specific constraints. The third goal is to identify directions for future HCAI and HAX frameworks that more clearly connect design principles with measurable outcomes, including trust, usability, safety, transparency, workload, decision quality, and ethical alignment. This review makes several related contributions. It brings HCAI and HAX into a single review structure, allowing the values that guide AI design to be examined alongside the interaction evidence used to evaluate AI use in practice. It also compares findings across application domains to identify where design concerns are shared and where domain-specific needs shape evaluation. In addition, it connects proposed design principles with measurable human outcomes, which helps distinguish principles supported by empirical evidence from those that remain mainly conceptual. This synthesis provides a foundation for future HCAI and HAX frameworks that are easier to evaluate, compare, and apply across real-world settings.

To conduct this review, we systematically identified and reviewed relevant literature on HCAI and HAX with applications in specific domains. We categorized the literature into distinct application areas, synthesized the major findings, assessed overlap across studies, and identified variations in frameworks, measures, and outcomes. The coded information was then organized into summary tables and visual summaries to support cross-domain comparison. Finally, we provide recommendations for a future HCAI framework and discuss the alignment of these recommendations with existing approaches to responsible and human-centered AI design.

II. METHODS

This systematic review was conducted using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) method [14] to identify relevant research articles on human-centered AI and human-AI interaction. A total of eight databases were searched: SCOPUS, Web of Science, Compendex, Inspec, TRID, PsycINFO, ACM Digital Library, and PubMed. Additional grey literature was searched through arXiv. The initial search was conducted in June 2025, and all searches were completed between June and August 2025. The inclusion criteria were articles written in English, articles published or made available online between January 1, 2010, and the final search date, and document types including peerreviewed academic journals, conference proceedings, and grey literature. Because some online-first articles indexed during the search period were assigned a later publisher issue year, articles labeled as 2026 were retained when they were available in the searched databases before the final search date. The syntax used to search the databases was as follows: TITLE-ABS-KEY (("human-centered AI" OR "user-centered AI" OR "human-AI collaboration" OR "human-AI interaction" OR "human-AI teaming") AND ("usability" OR "collaboration" OR "trust" OR "teaming" OR "safety" OR "ethic*" OR "interface*")). In this article, we define AI systems broadly to include machine learning-based systems, natural language processing systems, recommendation engines, autonomous and robotic systems, and decision-support tools, provided that the system involves direct or mediated interaction with a human user.

Two reviewers searched the databases separately using the same syntax, and the larger set of results was retained to maximize coverage. The initial search identified 9,110 records from databases and 608 additional records from grey literature sources. After duplicate removal, 4,060 records remained for screening. The full search, screening, eligibility, and inclusion process is summarized in Fig. 2. Two reviewers screened each paper independently to reduce reviewer bias. Papers were excluded if they (1) lacked design principles, methodologies, applications, or impacts on human outcomes that are relevant to HCAI or HAX, (2) lacked clearly defined independent and dependent variables (IVs and DVs), (3) were review papers without original empirical data, including literature reviews, scoping reviews, or meta-analyses, (4) only compared the presence of AI with human agents without focusing on interaction or collaboration, or (5) were product overviews or interface introductions. Of the 66 articles selected for fulltext review, 22 were excluded because they did not meet the five criteria stated above. After study selection, the included articles were coded by domain, framework or concept, AI type, interaction modality, AI role, and reported metrics. These codes were used to generate the synthesis structure shown in Table I and the article-level extraction provided in Supplementary Table S1.

C. Framework Categories and Design Principles

The 44 included studies were organized into eight framework categories based on their main conceptual focus. These categories included automation and human control, initiative dynamics, affective and engagement orientation, cognitive support, fairness, trust, personalization and adaptivity, and technology adoption with human performance. This categorization shows that current HCAI and HAX frameworks focus most strongly on control, collaboration, trust, and explainability, while broader adoption and long-term implementation remain less represented.

Automation and human control was the most common framework category (n = 10). This emphasis reflects a central concern in HCAI: AI systems should improve performance while preserving meaningful human oversight. For example, Huang et al. [32] proposed a triadic human-AI collaboration framework for autonomous mobility. In this framework, Advisor AI provides context-aware information without taking control, Co-pilot AI shares control with the human user, and Guardian AI acts as a safety override during emergencies. This framework shows a graded view of AI support, with different levels of control depending on task risk and user need.

Other studies also emphasized the balance between automation and human control. Van der Waa et al. [33] proposed team design patterns for morally complex decision-making, ranging from human decision-making with AI advice to supervised autonomy. Batra et al. [34] applied Shneiderman's HCAI framework to examine personal control and automation in AI agents. Hemmer et al. [28] and Gal et al. [35] examined AI delegation and task allocation, where AI systems routed tasks based on confidence or task difficulty. Across these studies, the main design concern was not whether AI should be used, but which task elements should remain with humans and which should be supported or handled by AI.

Initiative dynamics formed another major framework category (n = 6). These studies examined whether humans or AI systems should act first, respond second, or shift initiative during the task. Initiative shaped task performance, perceived AI competence, responsibility, and trust. For instance, Schecter and Richardson [36] examined generative AI as either a driver or an advisor. When AI acted as the driver, it produced an initial solution that humans refined. When AI acted as the advisor, humans worked first and then received AI critique or suggestions. Cabitza et al. [29] also compared different human-AI collaboration protocols, including AI-first and human-first configurations. These studies show that the order of human and AI input can meaningfully shape performance and user perception, even when the same AI system is used.

Affective and engagement-oriented frameworks appeared in three studies. These frameworks focused on emotional response, motivation, and user engagement. For example, Gal et al. [35] used motivational messages to support participation in a citizen science workflow. Song et al. [37] used cooperative chatbot interaction to influence engagement and attitudes toward mental illness. These studies suggest that HCAI and HAX frameworks should account for users' emotional and motivational responses, especially in learning, health communication, and public-facing systems.

Cognitive support frameworks appeared in two studies. These frameworks focused on helping users evaluate AI outputs more carefully and avoid shallow reliance. For example, Chen et al. [20] examined cognitive framework features such as explanations, confidence information, feedback, AI-driven questions, and performance visualization. These features were intended to slow automatic judgment and encourage more reflective decision-making. Cognitive support is especially important when AI systems provide recommendations in highstakes tasks where users may over-rely on system outputs.

Fairness frameworks appeared in two studies, both in HR or technology-related contexts. These studies focused on fairness perceptions in AI-assisted hiring and decision-making. For instance, Ling et al. [38] examined AI-led, balanced, and human-led screening structures and found that applicants perceived human-led and balanced structures as fairer than AI-led structures, especially when the outcome was rejection. This finding suggests that perceived fairness depends on both the outcome and the decision process, including the relative weight given to humans and AI.

Personalization and adaptivity appeared in two studies. These frameworks examined AI systems that adjusted to user behavior, task context, or environmental conditions. For example, Lerch et al. [31] evaluated generated user interfaces in automated vehicles that adapted notifications based on motion-sicknessrelated conditions. Adaptive systems were often perceived as useful when they responded to the user's situation, yet they could also increase workload when cues were repetitive or unclear. This pattern suggests that adaptivity needs to be guided by user needs, task timing, and information clarity.

Trust frameworks appeared in three studies. These studies focused on trust calibration, overtrust, undertrust, and reliance. For example, Okamura and Yamada [24] proposed adaptive trust calibration cues that appeared when user behavior suggested overtrust. The cues helped users adjust their reliance when AI reliability changed. Trust frameworks were closely connected to transparency and control because users needed enough information to judge whether AI support should be accepted.

Technology adoption and human performance appeared in one study. Loske and Klumpp [25] examined professional truck drivers' responses to assistance systems with different levels of controllability. Their findings showed that adoption depends on more than perceived usefulness. Users also need meaningful control and the option to override or disengage from automation. The limited number of studies in this category suggests that future HCAI and HAX research should place more attention on adoption, long-term use, and real-world implementation.

Across the framework categories, automation and human control received the most attention, while adoption and longterm performance received less attention. This imbalance shows that the field has made progress in defining design principles, but less progress in testing whether those principles support sustainable and transferable human outcomes across domains.

D. AI Roles in Human-AI Interaction

The included studies were also organized by the role AI played in human-AI interaction. Three broad role categories were identified: human-led AI support, human-AI collaborative support, and AI-led support. Human-led support refers to systems that provide information, recommendations, explanations, or decision support while humans retain final authority. Human-AI collaborative support refers to systems that share control, exchange feedback, or coordinate task activity with humans. AI-led support refers to systems that initiate or complete tasks with minimal human input. Several studies included mixed-role systems where AI shifted between these roles depending on context, task demands, or risk level.

Human-led AI support was the most common role across the reviewed studies. This pattern reflects the strong presence of high-stakes domains such as healthcare, transportation, and HR, where final responsibility often remains with human users. Collaborative support appeared when tasks required ongoing coordination between humans and AI. AI-led support rarely appeared as a stand-alone role. Instead, it most often appeared as part of mixed-role systems in which AI could act autonomously under some conditions and provide support under others.

1) Human-led AI Support: Human-led AI support appeared most frequently across the included articles. In this role, AI mainly functions as an assistant that provides information or recommendations without directly taking final control. Clinical diagnosis was a common example. In Niraula et al. [39], AI provided treatment suggestions based on cancer progression, while clinicians reviewed the suggestions and decided whether to follow, revise, or reject them. In Reverberi et al. [15], endoscopists integrated their own judgments with AI outputs when diagnosing colonoscopy lesions. In Cabitza et al. [40], AI acted as an additional reader in radiology, and its judgments were combined with radiologists' judgments under different collaboration protocols.

Human-led AI support also appeared in patient-facing healthcare. Sayres et al. [41] examined a dermatology application that helped users compare their own skin concerns with visual examples and AI-supported information. In this case, AI supported patients' understanding and also helped clinicians communicate with patients. This example shows that humanled AI support can assist expert users, lay users, or both groups within the same care process.

Outside healthcare, advisory AI appeared in decision-making, transportation, and work tasks. Smith et al. [23] examined AI advice in facial recognition decisions, where participants could decide whether to follow or reject the AI recommendation. Lee and Tok [42] examined AI predictions, uncertainty scores, visualizations, and example-based explanations in rehabilitation assessment. Lerch et al. [31] examined an in-vehicle AIgenerated interface that notified users about upcoming curvy roads linked to motion sickness risk. Across these examples, AI supported the user's decision rather than replacing it.

Explainability was a recurring feature of human-led AI support. Because humans retained final control, the AI needed to provide enough information for users to judge the recommendation. Chen et al. [20] examined text explanations, visual explanations, confidence levels, feedback, AI-driven questions, and performance visualizations in diabetes management. Senoner et al. [43] compared a black-box AI system with an explainable AI system that used heatmaps during a visual inspection task. Schmitt et al. [44] examined salient feature explanations and free-text explanations in disinformation detection. These studies show that human-led AI support often depends on explanation quality because users need to understand the AI output before deciding whether to rely on it.

Overall, human-led AI support was most common when users needed to process complex information, make final judgments, or remain accountable for decisions. This role supports HCAI principles by preserving human authority, while HAX findings show that the quality of explanations, timing, and presentation strongly affects user trust, reliance, and performance.

2) Human-AI Collaborative Support: Human-AI collaborative support reflects a shift from advice to shared task activity. In this role, AI systems interact with users more actively, respond to context, and share parts of the task. Gao et al. [17], for example, examined a surgical field-of-view adjustment system that interpreted surgical scenes and responded to surgeons' voice commands. The system helped stabilize the view and reduced workload, while the surgeon remained actively involved in the procedure. This example shows collaborative support in a high-skill setting where AI helps manage task demands without removing the human operator. Collaborative AI also appeared in writing, rehabilitation, ethics, learning, and emergency response. Dhillon et al. [26] examined AI scaffolding in co-writing, where AI provided next-sentence or next-paragraph support. Hemmer et al. [28] examined AI delegation in image classification, where AI routed task instances to humans or handled them automatically. Schwalb et al. [45] examined a drone-based AI system that detected hostages and communicated findings through a visual interface. These systems did more than present advice; they participated in task coordination and shaped the flow of human activity.

A key feature of collaborative AI was contextual alignment. Effective systems interpreted task conditions, user input, or environmental cues and then provided support that matched the situation. In education, Gyasi et al. [46] examined AI systems that provided feedback, feedforward, and chatbot partnership during collaborative learning. In this case, AI became part of the learning process rather than a separate tool. In Batra et al. [34], AI agents were discussed as systems that could support safety, auditability, fairness, and oversight. These examples show that collaborative AI can support both task activity and broader governance goals.

Overall, human-AI collaborative support was most useful when the task required ongoing coordination, adaptive feedback, or shared control. This role helps connect HCAI and HAX because it requires both value-based design principles and careful evaluation of interaction quality during use.

3) AI-Led Support and Mixed-Role Systems: No included study focused only on AI-led support as a stand-alone role. AIled support appeared mainly within mixed-role systems where AI could shift among advisory, collaborative, and automated functions. This pattern is important because it suggests that empirical HCAI and HAX research has not centered on full AI replacement. Instead, most studies examined AI systems that changed their level of involvement based on task context, risk, or user control.

Several studies combined human-led and collaborative AI roles. For example, Gyasi et al. [46] compared AI feedforward and feedback, AI partnership, and a combined mode in online collaborative learning. Brachman et al. [47] examined conflict resolution in data labeling, where participants could rely on human labeler agreement, AI probability, or both. Cabitza et al. [40] compared workflows in which humans led first, AI led first, or disagreements were resolved through structured protocols. Schecter and Richardson [36] compared AI as a driver and AI as an advisor in generative AI-supported work. These studies show that AI roles can shift during interaction rather than remain fixed.

Some studies combined collaborative and AI-led roles. Madathil [48] compared human-AI-generated advertisements with fully AI-generated advertisements. Li and Kang [49] compared human-centered AI with machine-centered AI in retail, where AI could personalize services or automate task operations. Loske and Klumpp [25] compared assistance systems that could be controlled by drivers with systems that could not be turned off. Across these studies, users responded more positively when AI support preserved meaningful human involvement.

A small set of studies included all three roles. In automated driving, advisory AI could inform drivers, collaborative AI could provide stronger adaptive support, and AI-led support could intervene during unsafe conditions [32], [50]. Zhao et al. [18] also showed a workflow in which AI searched autonomously, provided candidate solutions, and handed control to humans when needed. These studies suggest that mixed-role systems may be especially relevant in dynamic environments where risk and task demands change over time.

Across AI roles, the main pattern was clear: users often accepted AI support when it strengthened decision-making, reduced effort, or improved safety, but they were less accepting when AI removed control or made decisions difficult to question. This finding supports the need for HCAI and HAX frameworks that specify not just what AI can do, but when AI should advise, collaborate, or act. E. Domain-Specific Findings 1) Healthcare: Healthcare was the largest domain in the review, with 12 included studies. The healthcare studies focused mainly on decision support, diagnostic support, surgical assistance, and knowledge adoption. Across these studies, AI was most often used to support human judgment in highstakes tasks where accuracy, reliability, and accountability were important.

Decision support was a major theme in healthcare. AI systems supported tasks such as diabetes management, rehabilitation assessment, adaptive radiotherapy, dietetics planning, and resource allocation during public health emergencies. Across these studies, explainable AI was commonly used to help users interpret recommendations. Explanations appeared as confidence scores, visual indicators, text explanations, counterfactual explanations, uncertainty information, and contrastive explanations. These explanation formats were intended to help users judge whether AI outputs were reliable and relevant to the task.

Confidence information was one common form of explainable AI. For instance, Lee and Tok [42] compared numerical confidence scores with distance-based confidence information in rehabilitation assessment. Distance-based confidence information improved accuracy, especially for novices, while numerical confidence scores were often preferred by users. This finding shows that the explanation format users prefer may not always be the format that produces the strongest performance.

Textual and visual explanations were also common. Chen et al. [20] found that textual explanations improved trust and perceived reliability in high-stakes healthcare decision-making, but they could increase cognitive load and perform less well under time pressure. Visual explanations improved satisfaction and perceived accuracy in some cases, yet they did not consistently improve reliance calibration or performance. Lee and Chew [51] showed that counterfactual explanations helped reduce overreliance on incorrect AI outputs. Van der Waa et al. [33] showed that contrastive explanations could support moral decision-making by explaining why one allocation decision was favored over another. These findings suggest that XAI can support decision-making, but its effects depend on explanation type, task pressure, and user expertise.

Expertise was an important factor in healthcare decision support. Several studies found that novices and experts both benefited from AI support, but they responded differently to incorrect AI outputs. Novices often showed larger performance gains when AI was correct, yet they were more vulnerable to incorrect AI recommendations. Experts sometimes took longer when working with AI, but they were less affected by erroneous outputs. This pattern suggests that AI explanations should be designed differently for novice and expert users, especially in clinical tasks where overreliance can affect patient outcomes.

Diagnostic support was another major healthcare application. Studies examined MRI, ECG, X-ray, colonoscopy, and skin condition diagnosis. Across these tasks, human-AI collaboration often improved diagnostic accuracy and was perceived as useful, consistent, or satisfying. Cabitza et al. [29] showed that welldesigned human-AI collaboration protocols could outperform either humans or AI alone. Senoner et al. [43] found that visual heatmap explanations improved performance in medical and manufacturing tasks. Sayres et al. [41] showed that AI support could help patients better understand skin concerns and support communication with clinicians. These findings suggest that diagnostic AI is most useful when it supports interpretation, communication, and final human judgment.

Surgical support appeared in Gao et al. [17], which examined an AI-powered surgical field-of-view adjustment assistant. The system interpreted the surgical scene and surgeon intent, then adjusted the field of view to support the task. The system reduced workload and supported stable performance, suggesting that collaborative AI can help manage complex perceptual and motor demands in surgical settings.

One study examined knowledge adoption in chronic disease prevention. Lu and Peng [52] compared factual, interpretive, and exploratory tasks. Participants performed best on interpretive tasks and lowest on factual tasks, suggesting that AIsupported knowledge work may be most useful when users need to interpret and apply information rather than simply recall facts.

Overall, healthcare studies showed strong potential for HCAI and HAX because AI often improved accuracy, efficiency, and decision support. The same studies also showed clear risks, especially overreliance, explanation burden, and different effects for novice and expert users. Healthcare findings therefore show the need for AI systems that are accurate, explainable, and designed around the user's role, expertise, and level of responsibility.

2) Education: Five studies examined HCAI and HAX in education. A common theme was engagement. Across the education studies, AI systems were more effective when they supported active participation, meaningful interaction, and task-relevant scaffolding [26], [35], [37], [46], [53]. Song et al. [37] examined chatbot interaction in the context of mental illness stigma. Two-way cooperative interaction helped participants view the chatbot as more intelligent and likable, and it supported stronger engagement. The effects were strongest when the chatbot activity was tied directly to mental illness content. When the cooperative task was not connected to the mental illness content, participants did not report a sense of cooperation. This finding suggests that educational AI should support interaction that is meaningful for the learning goal.

Gyasi et al. [46] further showed the value of active AI support in collaborative learning. The combined AIFFP condition, which included feedforward, feedback, and an AI partner, produced the strongest collaborative knowledge building, cognitive engagement, and socially shared regulation. This condition outperformed AI feedback alone and AI partnership alone. These results suggest that AI support is most useful when it gives learners task-specific guidance and also participates in the learning process. Kim and Lee [53], examined student-AI collaboration in a drawing task. Students who framed the problem first, including goals, constraints, and options, showed larger creativity gains than students who began drawing immediately. The findings also showed that student attitude and skill level shaped the effect of AI support. Students with different skill levels wanted different forms of guidance, suggesting that educational AI should provide adaptive scaffolding rather than a single fixed level of automation. Furthermore, Dhillon et al. [26] examined AI scaffolding in co-writing. Low-level sentence guidance did not significantly improve writing quality or productivity, while higher-level paragraph guidance produced significant improvement, especially for writers without a regular writing habit. The study also showed a moderate decrease in satisfaction and text ownership under scaffolded writing. This finding suggests that educational AI should match the level of scaffolding to the writer and support performance without weakening the user's sense of ownership.

Overall, education studies showed that AI can support engagement, performance, and collaborative learning when it provides task-relevant feedback and adaptive scaffolding. The main risk is that AI can reduce ownership, disrupt learning flow, or create dependence when the level of support is poorly matched to the learner.

3) Transportation:

Five studies examined HCAI and HAX in transportation. The main themes were adaptive interfaces, interaction modalities, technology adoption, user control, and explainable AI. These studies were often concerned with safety, timing, trust, and the need for fast user response in dynamic environments.

Adaptive interfaces were a major theme. Bellet et al. [50] examined adaptive human-machine transitions in vehicle automation, where the system monitored driver behavior and situational risk to guide information, warning, or intervention. Lerch et al. [31] examined AI-generated motion sickness notifications in automated vehicles. Kaufman [54] examined AI coaching for autonomous vehicle interaction. Across these studies, adaptive interfaces were useful when they responded to the driving context and provided timely support. Users often rated these systems as helpful, intuitive, and safe when the information was clear and well timed.

Interaction modality was another important transportation theme. Visual, auditory, tactile, and multimodal cues were used to support driver understanding and response. Huang et al. [32] and Kaufman [54] examined how information format and modality shape user experience in driving contexts. In Huang et al. [32], the Advisor, Co-pilot, and Guardian roles, delivered through visual, auditory, and tactile channels, were rated as equally useful and satisfying. In Kaufman [54], visual-spatial cues combined with auditory reasoning provided the best user experience in a driving coaching context. These findings suggest that transportation AI should match modality and information type to the task demand, urgency, and driver state.

User control and adoption were also central. Bellet et al. [50] and Loske and Klumpp [25] showed that users were more accepting of assistance systems when they retained meaningful control. Loske and Klumpp [25], in particular, found that drivers resisted systems that could not be turned off, which led to poorer efficiency. This finding suggests that user control is not only a preference. It can affect real performance outcomes such as fuel use and driving efficiency.

Explainable AI also appeared in transportation. Kaufman [54] showed that AI coaching improved driving performance, confidence, expertise, and trust, and that modality and expla-nation type significantly affected learning effectiveness. This finding is especially important for transportation because trust needs to be calibrated under time pressure and safety risk, and explanation design can shape whether drivers rely on AI support appropriately.

Overall, transportation studies showed that HCAI and HAX design needs to support timely communication, clear modality selection, calibrated trust, and meaningful human control. These concerns are especially important in automated driving because users need to understand AI support quickly and decide whether to rely on it during changing traffic conditions.

4) Other Domains:

The remaining studies covered HR/technology, workplace productivity, media and advertising, cybersecurity and digital ethics, retail, emergency response, manufacturing, gender, mixed reality, infrastructure, and sustainability. Although these domains varied widely, several shared patterns emerged. The most consistent pattern was that users preferred AI systems that supported their decisions without dominating them.

In HR and technology contexts, users responded strongly to the level of AI control. Smith et al. [23] found that participants who chose to view optional AI advice were more likely to align with it, although optional access reduced alignment overall compared with mandatory advice. Signal strength or fit showed no significant effect on convergence, unlike the ability to choose whether to view the AI advice. Ling et al. [38] found that human-led and balanced screening structures were perceived as fairer than AI-led structures, especially when applicants were rejected. These findings show that perceived fairness and acceptance are shaped by control, responsibility, and the user's ability to understand the decision process.

Similar patterns appeared in creative and retail contexts. Madathil [48] found that advertisements created through human-AI collaboration were rated more positively than fully AIgenerated advertisements, with higher perceived creativity, stronger brand morality, and lower AI anxiety. Batra et al. [34] showed that usage intention increased when users perceived both high automation and high personal control. Li and Kang [49] found that human-centered AI in retail could support personalization and trust, although users could also blame AI more strongly when service failures occurred. These findings suggest that users may accept advanced AI capabilities when they feel that human values, control, and accountability remain visible.

Explainability and transparency were also important across the smaller domains. Shulner-Tal et al. [22] found that fairness perceptions in algorithmic decision-making were shaped by system characteristics, personality characteristics, and demographic characteristics. Certification-based and sensitivity-based explanations improved perceived fairness, while demographicbased explanations reduced fairness perceptions and certification stamps alone had no significant effect. Molina and Sundar [30] found that trust in AI content moderation increased when users had interactive transparency and could provide feedback on system rules. Schmitt et al. [44] found that AI support significantly improved misinformation detection accuracy and increased subjective ratings such as trust and usefulness, while free-text explanations introduced a risk of blind trust. These findings show that explanations can improve user perception, but they need to be evaluated against actual task outcomes.

The presentation of AI also mattered. Sánchez Olszewski [55] found that participants were less likely to agree with AI when it appeared human-like, compared with robot-like or generic AI identities, in a hiring context. Li and Kang [49], in a retail context, found more positive responses to humancentered AI that appeared personalized and attentive, compared with machine-centered AI that appeared more task-oriented and mechanical. These findings suggest that anthropomorphic or human-like AI design does not have one fixed effect. Its value depends on task context, user expectations, and the risk associated with the decision.

Overall, the smaller domains supported the same broader pattern found in healthcare, education, and transportation. Users were more likely to trust, accept, and use AI when they retained meaningful control, understood the AI's role, and perceived the system as fair and useful. Systems that were overly dominant, unclear, or difficult to challenge were more likely to create resistance, anxiety, or overreliance.

F. Cross-Domain Synthesis

Across the reviewed studies, HCAI and HAX evaluation depended on both task outcomes and user experience outcomes. Many AI systems improved accuracy, speed, consistency, or decision quality, yet these gains did not always lead to stronger trust, satisfaction, usability, lower workload, or perceived control. This pattern shows that performance outcomes and human experience outcomes need to be evaluated in relation to each other.

Another cross-domain pattern concerned the role of AI in the interaction. Human-led AI support appeared most often in high-stakes settings where users retained final authority. Collaborative AI appeared in tasks that required shared control, feedback, or adaptation during the interaction. AI-led support appeared mainly as part of mixed-role systems, especially in dynamic settings where risk or task demands changed over time. These findings show that AI role is an important factor for comparing HCAI and HAX studies across domains.

Domain context also shaped the outcomes that received the most attention. Healthcare studies emphasized accuracy, reliability, expertise, and overreliance. Education studies emphasized engagement, scaffolding, ownership, and learning. Transportation studies emphasized safety, timing, modality, trust calibration, and user control. The remaining domains emphasized fairness, transparency, adoption, creativity, and acceptance. These patterns show that similar HCAI and HAX concerns appear across domains, while evaluation priorities vary based on users, risks, and task demands.

IV. DISCUSSION This review synthesized 44 empirical studies on HCAI and HAX across multiple application domains and used this evidence to connect design principles, AI roles, measurement choices, and human outcomes within a single synthesis structure. The findings show that HCAI and HAX are closely connected areas of work. HCAI provides the values and design goals that guide AI system design, and HAX provides evidence about user interaction, system use, and measurable outcomes. This structure helps clarify which design concerns appear across domains, which outcomes have empirical support, and where future frameworks need stronger links between human-centered values and measurable interaction outcomes. Across the reviewed studies, HCAI and HAX research has moved beyond broad design principles toward empirical evaluation of human outcomes. The review also shows continued fragmentation across domains, measures, and interaction contexts.

A. Measurement and Evaluation Patterns

The reviewed studies used both objective and subjective measures to evaluate human-AI interaction. Objective measures most often focus on task performance, including accuracy, error reduction, completion time, and decision quality. Across domains, AI assistance was often associated with improved objective performance, especially in tasks involving diagnosis, classification, detection, information search, and decision support. These findings suggest that AI can support human performance when it helps users process complex information, identify patterns, or focus on task-relevant cues.

Subjective measures showed more variation. Trust, satisfaction, usability, and workload changed based on the design of the AI system, the clarity of its output, the user's level of control, and the task context. This variation is important because a system can improve performance while still creating a poor user experience. AI explanations may increase trust and perceived usefulness, yet they can also increase mental workload when users need to process too much information under time pressure. This pattern suggests that HCAI and HAX evaluations should include both performance outcomes and user experience outcomes. A system should not be considered human-centered only because it improves accuracy or efficiency. It also needs to support appropriate trust, clear understanding, manageable workload, and meaningful user control.

The workload findings are especially important for future evaluation. AI systems are often designed to reduce cognitive demand, but the reviewed studies show that this benefit is not automatic. Explanations, alerts, and adaptive cues can reduce workload when they are clear and timely. They can increase workload when they are too frequent, too complex, or poorly matched to the user's task. Future studies should therefore evaluate workload as a sign of whether AI information is being integrated smoothly into human cognition and action, not just as a general measure of task difficulty.

B. Framework Categories and Design Principles

The reviewed frameworks were organized into eight categories: automation and human control, initiative dynamics, affective and engagement orientation, cognitive support, fairness, trust, personalization and adaptivity, and technology adoption with human performance. Among these categories, automation and human control appeared most often. This emphasis reflects a central issue in HCAI: AI systems should improve human capability while preserving human oversight and responsibility.

Initiative dynamics also emerged as a key framework concern. The order of human and AI input can shape performance, trust, and responsibility. When AI acts early in the task, it may increase efficiency by reducing the user's initial effort, but it may also shape the user's later judgment. When humans act early in the task, they may remain more engaged, but the process may require more time and effort. Future frameworks should treat initiative as a design choice rather than a fixed feature of AI systems. The same AI capability can produce different outcomes depending on whether it advises, critiques, initiates, or responds.

Affective, engagement, and cognitive categories show that HCAI and HAX are not limited to performance and control. AI systems can influence motivation, emotional response, perceived cooperation, and critical reasoning. Engagementoriented systems may be helpful in education and public-facing contexts, while cognitive support may be especially important in high-stakes decision-making. These benefits still need to be balanced with workload and efficiency. Features that encourage reflection can reduce overreliance, but they may also slow users down or increase mental effort.

Fairness and trust were also important framework categories. Fairness was especially visible in HR and algorithmic decisionmaking contexts, where AI-supported decisions can affect employment and access to opportunity. Trust appeared across many domains and was closely tied to transparency, reliability, and control. Overtrust can lead users to accept incorrect AI outputs, while undertrust can prevent users from benefiting from helpful AI support. These findings support the need for frameworks that treat trust as an outcome to be calibrated, not simply increased.

Personalization, adaptivity, and technology adoption were less represented. This gap is important because AI systems are increasingly designed to adapt to users and operate in real-world settings. Future HCAI and HAX frameworks should give more attention to long-term use, user adaptation, and implementation in realistic environments. Without this attention, frameworks may remain difficult to apply outside controlled studies.

C. AI Roles and Shared Control

The reviewed studies showed that AI roles can be organized into human-led AI support, human-AI collaborative support, AI-led support, and mixed-role systems. Human-led AI support appeared most often. This pattern likely reflects the current use of AI in high-stakes domains, where humans remain responsible for final decisions. In healthcare, for example, AI commonly supported diagnosis, treatment planning, and clinical judgment, while clinicians retained final authority. This structure respects existing accountability and safety expectations while allowing AI to surface patterns that humans may miss.

Explainability was especially important in human-led AI support. When humans are final decision-makers, they need enough information to judge whether AI recommendations are reliable. However, explainability is not always beneficial by itself. The effects of explanations depend on format, timing, user expertise, task complexity, and workload. Future research should move beyond asking whether explanations help and should examine which explanation formats help which users under which task conditions.

Human-AI collaborative support appeared when tasks required shared activity, ongoing feedback, or adaptive control. In these systems, AI did more than provide recommendations. It helped coordinate the task, respond to user input, or adjust support based on context. This role is especially relevant for surgery, transportation, emergency response, and collaborative learning, where interaction unfolds over time and conditions can change quickly.

AI-led support appeared mainly within mixed-role systems rather than as a stand-alone role. This pattern suggests that most empirical HCAI and HAX research is not focused on full replacement of human users. Instead, studies often examine systems where AI shifts between advising, collaborating, and acting based on task demands or risk. This finding is important for future framework development because it shows that AI role should be treated as dynamic. A system may need to advise during routine conditions, collaborate during complex tasks, and intervene during emergencies.

D. Domain-Specific Implications

Healthcare was the largest domain in the review. The main healthcare applications were decision support, diagnostic support, surgical assistance, and knowledge adoption. AI support often improved accuracy, consistency, and efficiency, especially when paired with explainable AI. Healthcare also showed clear risks related to overreliance, explanation burden, and user expertise. Novices often benefited more from AI support, but they were also more vulnerable to incorrect AI outputs. Experts were sometimes slower when working with AI, but they were less affected by errors. These findings suggest that healthcare AI should be designed around user expertise, clinical responsibility, and safeguards against erroneous recommendations.

Education studies showed that AI was most useful when it supported active engagement and task-relevant scaffolding. AI systems that provided feedback, feedforward, partnership, or adaptive guidance were more effective than systems that only delivered passive information. At the same time, some forms of scaffolding reduced ownership or created dependence. Future education research should examine whether AI support improves long-term learning and skill development.

Transportation studies emphasized adaptive interfaces, multimodal communication, user control, trust calibration, and explainable AI. These concerns reflect the time-sensitive and safety-related nature of transportation tasks. Adaptive interfaces can improve safety and comfort when they provide timely and clear support, but they can create frustration when they intervene unexpectedly or reduce user control. Multimodal cues also need to be designed around urgency, driver state, and task demands. Future transportation research should examine shared control and multimodal communication under more realistic edge cases, such as poor weather, sensor failure, construction zones, or unexpected system limitations.

Other domains, including HR, workplace productivity, media, digital ethics, retail, emergency response, manufacturing, mixed reality, infrastructure, and sustainability, showed a recurring preference for meaningful human control. Users were more willing to accept AI advice when they could choose whether to view it, reject it, or override it. These domains also showed that explainability can improve subjective outcomes, while objective performance gains may vary by explanation type, task context, and the risk of blind trust. This gap between subjective and objective outcomes is important because explanations may make users feel more confident without improving their decision accuracy. Future XAI research should therefore evaluate both perceived usefulness and actual decision quality.

Across domains, one repeated finding was that humancentered AI depends on fit between the AI role, the task, the user, and the risk level. Healthcare requires reliability and safeguards against overreliance. Education requires engagement and learning support. Transportation requires timing, modality, and trust calibration. HR and other decision contexts require fairness, transparency, and control. These differences show that HCAI principles can transfer across domains, but their implementation and evaluation need to reflect domain-specific risks and user needs.

E. Limitations

This review has several limitations. The review included only studies with empirical experiments, and thus conceptual papers, perspective papers, and design guidelines without empirical data were not included. This decision helped focus the synthesis on measurable outcomes, but it may have excluded important theoretical contributions. The review also included only articles written in English, which may introduce language bias. In addition, although the search followed a systematic procedure across multiple databases and grey literature sources, some relevant studies may not have been captured. Finally, the included studies varied widely in domain, task, AI type, sample, and evaluation method, which limited direct comparison across studies.

V. CONCLUSION

This systematic review synthesized empirical evidence on HCAI and HAX across 44 studies and multiple application domains. Its main contribution is the integration of design principles, AI roles, and outcome measures into a cross-domain structure for evaluating HCAI and HAX. The review connected design principles, AI roles, evaluation measures, and human outcomes within one synthesis structure. Across studies, AI support was often associated with improved objective task performance, including accuracy, efficiency, consistency, and decision quality. Subjective outcomes were more variable and depended on transparency, explanation design, user control, workload, and task fit.

The findings show that HCAI and HAX evaluations should report both objective and subjective outcomes. Performance gains alone are not enough to show that an AI system is humancentered. Human-centered evaluation should also examine whether users understand the system, trust it appropriately, retain meaningful control, manage workload, and remain able to question AI outputs.

This review also shows that AI roles matter. Human-led AI support was most common, especially in high-stakes domains. Collaborative AI appeared in tasks that required shared activity or adaptive support. AI-led support appeared mainly in mixedrole systems where AI involvement changed with task demands or risk. Future HCAI and HAX frameworks should more clearly describe the role of AI and link each role to measurable human outcomes.

Future research should validate HCAI and HAX principles in more realistic settings, with diverse users, longer study periods, and higher-fidelity AI systems. Stronger links are needed between human-centered values, interaction design, and measurable outcomes so that future frameworks can guide AI systems that are effective, understandable, trustworthy, and safe in real use.
