Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Rumination is commonly experienced in response to stressful events and has been linked to adverse psychological outcomes. However, research has predominantly examined rumination as a predictor or consequence of psychological functioning, whereas less is known about factors associated with its day-to-day fluctuations. The current study addressed this gap by examining factors associated with rumination using an intensive longitudinal design. A total of 91 young adults (81,5% female; 14,1% male; 4,3% diverse or preferred not to disclose their gender; aged 18-30 years) participated in a 28-day experience-sampling study. Daily self-reported rumination, stress, and subjective sleep quality (SSQ), as well as accelerometryderived total sleep time (TST), sleep efficiency (SE), and physical activity, were assessed.

Multilevel analyses showed that daily stress was associated with greater rumination.

Although physical activity was not directly associated with rumination, it moderated the association between stress and rumination. Higher-than-usual physical activity levels were associated with a smaller difference in rumination between stressful and non-stressful days.

Better SSQ was independently associated with lower rumination, whereas TST and SE were not. Higher baseline depressive symptom severity was also associated with greater rumination but did not moderate the stress-rumination association. These findings highlight physical activity as a potential protective factor in stress-related rumination and demonstrate the value of examining rumination as a dynamic outcome in everyday life.

Introduction

Extensive research has documented the detrimental consequences of rumination for the onset and maintenance of poor mental health, including depression and anxiety (Watkins & Roberts, 2020). However, considerably less is known about the everyday conditions under which rumination emerges or subsides in daily life. Identifying the daily experiences, mental states, conditions, and behaviors that initiate, exacerbate or mitigate and interrupt rumination could help close this important gap in the literature.

Rumination is typically conceptualized as a form of repetitive negative thinking. In contrast to worry, which is future-oriented (Kircanski et al., 2015), rumination involves repetitive thoughts about past events or experiences and their causes or consequences (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008). Although both forms of repetitive thinking are linked to psychopathology, the present study focuses specifically on rumination, given its strong association with depressive symptoms (Connolly & Alloy, 2017; Moberly & Watkins, 2008).

According to the Response Styles Theory (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991; Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 2008), rumination is characterized by several core features, including perseverative thinking about problems, brooding self-reflection, and recurrent negative self-evaluations. Individuals engaging in rumination repeatedly revisit distressing experiences and mentally replay problematic events. From the perspective of the Process Model of Emotion Regulation (Gross, 1998), rumination can also be understood as a maladaptive emotion regulation strategy that involves sustained attention toward negative emotional experiences.

Beyond its role in psychopathology, rumination has been shown to emerge as a response to stress in otherwise healthy individuals. During the university years, students may experience substantial academic stress. Gil et al. (2023) found that rumination mediated the relationship between academic stress and symptoms of anxiety and depression among university students. Additionally, longitudinal research suggests that exposure to major life stressors, such as divorce, the death of a loved one, or job loss, can predict increased rumination a year after the event (Michl et al., 2013). Experimental studies provide similar evidence, showing that individuals exposed to major life stress exhibit greater state rumination in response to a laboratory-induced stressor six months later (Rodriguez-Stanley et al., 2025). In addition, rumination following an initial and novel stressor has been linked to heightened physiological stress response, including increased cortisol reactivity (Gianferante et al., 2014).

More recently, ecological momentary assessment (EMA) studies have begun to investigate how rumination unfolds in response to stress in everyday life. Consistent with earlier findings, these studies show that rumination occurs in response to immediate daily stressors (Int-Veen et al., 2024; Ruscio et al., 2015; Sladek et al., 2020). For example, Lopez et al. (2025) found that rumination was among the most frequently reported strategies of college students particularly in response to academic and interpersonal stressors. Moreover, rumination appears to be particularly prevalent when stressors are perceived as highly intense (Blanke et al., 2022; Socastro et al., 2022). Together, these findings suggest that increases in perceived stress are closely linked to increases in momentary rumination in daily life.

In addition to being stress-reactive, rumination also appears to vary systematically across the day. Using experience sampling methodology, Takano & Tanno (2011) observed a U-shaped diurnal pattern in rumination with higher levels reported in the morning and evening and lower levels in the afternoon. Importantly, individuals with higher depressive symptoms reported elevated rumination particularly during evening hours. These findings suggest that temporal factors may shape when individuals are most vulnerable to engaging in ruminative thinking.

Once activated, rumination also appears to be highly persistent. Evidence from EMA studies indicated that rumination at one time point predicts rumination later in the day. For example, Takano et al. (2014) found that rumination in the morning was associated with rumination in the afternoon, which in turn predicted rumination in the evening. Similar temporal carry-over effects have been observed in several EMA studies (Bean et al., 2020; Int-Veen et al., 2024; Rosenbaum et al., 2022). These findings are consistent with the Perseverative Cognition Hypothesis (Brosschot et al., 2006), which proposes that individuals repeatedly reactivate mental representations of stressors through rumination, thereby prolonging emotional, psychological, and physiological stress responses.

Given the strong link between rumination and the exacerbation of psychopathological symptoms, rumination is often classified as a maladaptive strategy due to its limited effectiveness in reducing distress (Aldao et al., 2010). Identifying daily factors that may help interrupt or buffer ruminative thinking may therefore be important for understanding how individuals regulate negative thoughts in everyday life.

One potential factor that may disrupt the link between stress and rumination is physical activity. Across several research designs, physical activity has been associated with reductions in stress and ruminative thinking. For example, a longitudinal study among medical students found that eight-weeks of combined mental and physical training significantly reduced stress and rumination compared to the control group (Lavadera et al., 2020). Experimental evidence similarly suggests that moderate-intensity physical activity can reduce self-reported rumination among individuals with depression compared to sedentary condition (Welkerling et al., 2026). Evidence from EMA studies, however, has been more mixed. While Schmitter et al. (2025) report no association between physical activity and repetitive negative thinking, Takano et al. (2013) suggest that physical activity may reduce rumination tendencies, particularly among individuals with elevated depressive symptoms.

Sleep is another important determinant of mental health that has been linked to stress.

Research suggests a bidirectional association between stress and sleep, with greater stress being associated with lower sleep quality and sleep efficiency (SE) and shorter total sleep time (TST), while poorer sleep is, in turn, associated with greater stress (Bloomfield et al., 2024; Marcusson-Clavertz et al., 2022; Petak & Maričić, 2025; Yap et al., 2020).

Importantly, this association may depend on how sleep is assessed (Slavish et al., 2021).

Perseverative cognition has been proposed as a potential mechanism through which stress may impair subsequent sleep (Petak & Maričić, 2025; Van Laethem et al., 2016; Zagaria et al., 2023). Consistent with this perspective, greater rumination closer to bedtime has been associated with longer sleep-onset latency (SOL; Pillai et al., 2014; Takano et al., 2014), while higher average levels of rumination across the week have been associated with lower average sleep quality (Ariu et al., 2026).

Although existing cross-sectional and EMA studies suggest that stress and rumination may impair sleep, considerably less is known about the temporal association in the opposite direction, namely, whether day-to-day variations in sleep are associated with rumination on the following day and whether sleep alters the association between stress and subsequent rumination. In a recent EMA study of older adults, greater sleep disturbances during the preceding night were associated with higher levels of rumination the following evening at both the within-person and between-person levels (Zhou et al., 2024). Exploratory supplementary analyses further indicated that sleep disturbances were found to also moderate the association between daily stress and evening rumination. However, the positive relationship between daily stress and rumination was found to be weaker among individuals who generally reported more sleep disturbances than among those who reported fewer sleep disturbances. This finding contrasts with evidence suggesting a restorative role of sleep in psychological resilience (Arora et al., 2022) and in stress-buffering (Flueckiger et al., 2017).

Taken together, the limited and partly exploratory evidence regarding the roles of sleep and physical activity highlights the need for future research examining whether these modifiable daily behaviors are associated with subsequent rumination and whether they attenuate or amplify the association between daily stress and rumination.

To address these gaps in research, the present study examines state rumination in students' daily lives, focusing on the period leading up to high-stakes academic exams, which can be considered as a naturalistic stress context (Koudela-Hamila et al., 2020). Specifically, we investigate how daily fluctuations in stressful events, physical activity, and prior sleep predict rumination. In addition, we examine whether these associations are moderated by individual differences in the levels of depressive symptoms at baseline.

We hypothesize that experiencing stress will be associated with increased rumination (Hypothesis 1a), whereas higher levels of physical activity (Hypothesis 1b) and better sleep the night before (Hypothesis 1c) will be associated with lower levels of rumination. We further expect that individuals with higher levels of depressive symptoms at baseline will report higher levels of rumination overall (Hypothesis 1d).

Moreover, we expect that physical activity and sleep moderate the association between daily stress and rumination, such that the positive relationship will be weaker on days with higher levels of physical activity (Hypothesis 2a) and better sleep the night before (Hypothesis 2b). Furthermore, we expect that the association between daily stress and rumination will be stronger among individuals with higher levels of depressive symptoms at baseline (Hypothesis 3). The investigated hypotheses are illustrated in Figure 1.

Methods

Design and Procedure

The present study is part of a larger research project, portions of which have been reported in prior publications (Brueckmann et al., 2025; Hachenberger et al., under review.; Werner et al., 2025). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Bielefeld University (reference number: 2022-280). Data were collected during university exam periods in Germany between January 2023 and February 2024. Participants were recruited via social media, mailing lists from various German universities, and through an online study participation management system of the Department of Psychology at Bielefeld University.

During registration, participants were informed regarding the study procedure, participation requirements, and their rights, including data protection regulation and the option to withdraw at any time. To be eligible, individuals had to be between 18 and 30 years old, proficient in German, either own an Android smartphone or be willing to use a device provided by the research team. Additionally, participants were required to be enrolled in at least one oral or written university exam during the four-week study period. All eligible participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

This study lasted 29 days, with the first day dedicated to baseline assessment.

Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) began on the second day and was administered via movisensXS app (movisens GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany). Participants were prompted three times per day over the subsequent 28 days: in the morning (8:30 a.m. on weekdays, 9:30 a.m. on weekends), in the afternoon (1:30 p.m.), and in the evening (8:30 p.m.). Each survey remained accessible for 60 minutes, after which it was recorded as missing if not completed.

Participants were instructed to refrain from responding in situations where doing so could compromise their safety or the safety of others (e.g. while driving).

In addition to the self-report assessment, an actigraphy was utilized during the EMA period. Participants wore an accelerometer (GENEActiv, Activinsights Ltd, Kimbolton, UK) on their non-dominant wrist. Participants were assisted that the device should be worn as much as possible but can be removed if needed (e.g. when showering).

Participants

A total of 96 participants enrolled in the study. Five participants were excluded from the analyses because they did not provide any complete sleep observations (i.e., the morning questionnaire and/or accelerometry data were missing). The final sample for the analysis therefore consists of 91 individuals (81.5% female, 14.1% male, and 4.3% preferred not to report their gender). The mean age was 22.5 years (SD = 2.8; range = 18-30 years).

Descriptive characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1.

Measures and Instruments

Baseline Measures PHQ-9; Depressive symptoms. At baseline, participants completed the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001) to assess depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 consists of nine items measuring the frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks. Items are rated on a 4-point scale ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 3 ("nearly every day"). A sum score was computed, with higher scores indicating greater severity of depressive symptoms. The internal consistency in this sample was  = 0.87.

EMA Measures

Rumination. Rumination was assessed using a measure developed for ecological momentary assessment (Hoebeke et al., 2022). The scale captures key features of rumination, including perseveration ('How much time have you spent thinking about one or more negative emotional experiences, problems, or something that has happened to you?'), brooding ('How much have you thought about the causes and consequences of emotional experiences?'), negativity (`To what extent were your thoughts negative? `), self-criticism ('To what extent were your thoughts self-critical?'), and repetition ('To what extent have you mentally replayed negative emotional experiences that you have had?'). Items were rated on a visual analogue scale ranging from 0 ("not at all" or "none") to 100 ("very much").

Assessments were administered twice daily, at noon and in the evening. The present study focused on the rumination assessed at the evening prompt and experienced since the preceding midday assessment. Restricting the outcome to the final prompt provided a consistent end-of-day assessment. This decision was based on the rationale that the precise temporal ordering of the individual predictors and rumination cannot be fully established.

For each assessment, a mean score across the five items was calculated. Higher values indicate more rumination. Across all measurement points, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability between = 0.94 and within = 0.88. The calculation was based on the procedure by Geldhof et al. (2014).

Stress. Daily stress was assessed once each evening using a binary item indicating whether participants had experienced stress or a stressful event during the day (0 = no,1 = yes). This method of stress evaluation is a commonly employed within the field of EMA (Vaessen et al., 2023).

Subjective sleep quality.

Subjective sleep quality was assessed each morning using an item indicating their satisfaction with the previous night's sleep on a visual analogue scale ranging from 0 ("not at all") to 100 ("very much"). The item was adapted for EMA use from validated measures of subjective sleep quality (Ohayon & Zulley, 2001). In previous research, the item was predicted by objective sleep parameters and was shown to be a reliable indicator of sleep perception (Hachenberger et al., 2025).

Accelerometry-based Measures

Physical activity. Physical activity was measured using wrist-worn triaxial accelerometers (GENEActive, Activinsights Ltd., Kimbolton, UK) recording raw acceleration data at 60 Hz rate. The raw accelerometry data was processed with the R-package GGIR (version 3.1.4; Migueles et al., 2019), applying automatic calibration procedures (van Hees et al., 2014).

Acceleration signals were converted to the Euclidean Norm Minus One (ENMO; milligravity unis, mg), an omnidirectional metric of movement acceleration by subtracting gravitational acceleration (1 g = 9.81 ms 2 ) from the vector magnitude of the three axes (van Hees et al., 2013). Negative values were set to zero. ENMO were aggregated into 1-min epochs, and each epoch was classified according to validated ENMO cut-points proposed by Hildebrand et al. (2014 Hildebrand et al. ( , 2017)): sedentary behavior (< 45.8 mg), LPA (45.8-93.2 mg), MPA (93.2-418.3 mg), and VPA ( 418.3 mg).

Daily minutes spent in LPA, MPA, and VPA were calculated. In addition, mean ENMO was computed as an indicator of overall movement volume. All physical activity metrics were derived for the period between wake-up and completion of the evening survey. Wake-up time was determined using the GGIR sleep-detection algorithm (van Hees et al., 2015).

Objective sleep measures.

Objective sleep parameters (TST and SE) were derived from the same wrist-worn triaxial accelerometers (GENEActiv, Activinsights Ltd, Kimbolton, UK) sampled at 60 Hz. Raw accelerometry data were processed using GGIR (Version 3.0-9; (Migueles et al., 2019), applying autocalibration (van Hees et al., 2014) and sleep detection procedures (van Hees et al., 2015). The sleep detection algorithm was supplied with participants' daily sleep diary data (sleep and wake-up times), which were served as temporal boundaries for sleep window. In the first survey of each day, participants reported about what time they went to sleep ("When did you turn off the light and go to sleep?") and at what time they woke up in the morning ("What time did you wake up this morning?").

Statistical Analyses

Data preprocessing and all statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.1; R Core Team, 2025). Multilevel models were estimated using the R-package lme4 (version 1.1-37; Bates et al., 2015) with restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML). All models included random intercept and random slopes for daily stress.

To address the research questions, rumination served as the outcome variable.

Predictor variables included daily stress experience (0 = no stress,1 = stress), daily physical activity and sleep parameters, baseline depressive symptoms, and the interaction terms between daily stress and the respective physical activity and sleep variables. To examine whether depressive symptom severity moderated the association between daily stress and rumination, a cross-level interaction between depressive symptoms and daily stress was included.

Physical activity and sleep variables expressed in hourly time units (LPA, MVPA, and TST) were person-mean centered based on the participant's individual mean) to estimate within-person effects while retaining their original hour-based metric. ENMO, SE and SSQ were person-standardized by centering each observation on the individual's mean and dividing by the individual's standard deviation. Baseline depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 scores) were grand-mean centered to facilitate interpretation relative to the sample mean.

Rumination scores were grand-mean standardized to express model estimates in standard deviation units.

In total, three models were estimated, each including one physical activity parameter (LPA, MVPA, or ENMO), while all the other predictors retained in the model. For each model, simple slopes were computed using the R-package interactions (Long, 2024) to probe and visualize significant interaction effects.

Exploratory and Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings: (a) models including random intercept only, (b) models excluding the cross-level interaction between depressive symptoms and daily stress, and (c) models excluding observations with extreme MVPA values. Significant interaction effects were further explored using estimated marginal means (EMMs) derived from the fitted multilevel models. EMMs were calculated to estimate rumination scores at selected durations or levels of physical activity under different stress conditions. For continuous physical activity variables, EMMs were estimated at the mean and at  1 and 2 hours relative to the mean for LPA and MVPA, and at the mean and  1 and 2 SD for ENMO. Pairwise comparisons were conducted to examine the simple effects of stress at each activity level.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Demographic characteristics of the sample and descriptive statistics of the baseline questionnaires and ecological momentary assessment surveys are presented in Table 1.

Based on 28 days, three prompts per day, and 91 participants, there were 7644 possible observations. For the present analyses, however, only data from the relevant assessment prompts were considered. The morning and evening assessments were combined to create one observation per day, resulting in 2548 possible observations. Of these, 1658 observations (65.1%) contained complete data for all variables relevant to the analyses.

Effect of Stress on Rumination

Across all models, experiencing stress during the day was consistently associated with higher levels of rumination (Model 1: b = 0.33, p < .001; Model 2: b = 0.34, p < .001; Model 3: b = 0.34, p < .001). Results of multilevel models are presented in Table 2.

Physical Activity Moderates the Association Between Stress and Rumination

Within-person fluctuations in light physical activity (LPA; b = -0.03, p = .465), moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA; b = 0.02, p = .477), overall physical activity level (ENMO; b = 0.00, p = .889) were not significantly associated with rumination.

However, significant interactions between stress and physical activity were observed.

Higher levels of LPA (b = -0.14, p = .035), MVPA (b = -0.15, p = .011), and ENMO (b = -0.11, p = .005) attenuated the positive association between daily stress and rumination. Specifically, on stressful days, higher-than-usual levels of physical activity were associated with lower rumination. Interaction effects are displayed in Figure 2.

Exploratory analyses based on EMMs further demonstrated that rumination was lower on stressful days when physical activity was heightened, in contrast to stressful days when physical activity was lower. At high levels of physical activity (+2 hours above the mean for LPA and MVPA and +2 SD for ENMO), estimated rumination scores on stressful days did not differ significantly from those observed on non-stressful days (LPA: b = -0.05, p = .756; MVPA: b = -0.07, p = .832; ENMO: b = -0.12, p = .213). Detailed results of the exploratory analyses are presented in Table 3.

Sleep and Rumination

Higher subjective sleep quality in the preceding night was consistently associated with lower rumination across models (Model 1: b = -0.05, p = .031; Model 2: b = -0.05, p = .027; Model 3: b = -0.05, p = .029). In contrast, neither TST nor SE were significantly associated with rumination. Furthermore, none of the sleep measures significantly moderated the association between daily stress and rumination. Interaction effects are displayed in Figure 2.

Depressive Symptoms

At the between-person level, individuals reporting higher depressive symptom severity at baseline exhibited higher level of rumination across all models (b = 0.07, p < .001). However, depressive symptoms did not significantly moderate the association between daily stress and rumination.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed that all substantive conclusions remained unchanged across all alternative model specifications.

Discussion

The present study examined whether daily stress, physical activity, sleep and baseline depressive symptoms were associated with rumination in everyday life and whether these associations differed as a function of daily stress. Four main findings emerged.

First, perceived daily stress was consistently associated with higher levels of rumination reported for the afternoon-to-evening period. This finding supported Hypothesis 1a and is consistent with previous research suggesting that repetitive negative thinking may be elicited in response to everyday stressors in young adults. The present findings extend this literature by demonstrating this association in an intensive longitudinal design.

Second, within-person fluctuations in physical activity were not significantly associated with rumination on non-stressful days. Thus, Hypothesis 1b was not supported.

This finding suggests that being more physically active than usual was not associated with lower levels of rumination. The result is consistent with previous research reporting mixed associations between physical activity and rumination and with findings of an EMA study by Schmitter et al. (2025).

Importantly, however, physical activity significantly moderated the association between stress and rumination, supporting Hypothesis 2a. This pattern was consistent across the three operationalizations of physical activity: LPA, MVPA, and ENMO. Exploratory EMM analyses further indicated that greater-than-usual physical activity was associated with smaller difference in rumination between stressful and non-stressful days. At high levels of physical activity, rumination on stressful days was not significantly higher than rumination on non-stressful days. The findings suggest that physical activity may attenuate the association between daily stress and rumination rather than being directly associated with lower rumination in general. This moderating role of physical activity on stress is consistent with the broader concept of stress-buffering (Cohen & Wills, 1985) and with previous findings linking physical activity to improved affective and mental well-being under conditions of stress (Flueckiger et al., 2016; Guerriero et al., 2025; Hachenberger et al., 2023).

Third, better-than-usual SSQ was associated with lower rumination, partially supporting Hypothesis 1c. In contrast, neither TST nor SE was significantly associated with rumination. Furthermore, none of the sleep parameters significantly moderated the association between stress and rumination. Thus, Hypothesis 2b was not supported.

The significant association between SSQ and rumination suggests that better perceived sleep may be associated with more effective cognitive functioning, which could facilitate disengagement from repetitive negative thinking. Previous research has linked sleep to better cognitive functioning (Cheval et al., 2022) and cognitive functioning to individual differences in rumination (De Lissnyder et al., 2012; Whitmer & Gotlib, 2013). Similarly, better sleep quality has been associated with greater use of adaptive emotion regulation strategies (Parsons et al., 2022). However, cognitive functioning was not directly assessed in the present study, and this proposed mechanism therefore remains speculative. Future research incorporating direct assessments of cognitive functioning could test whether cognitive restoration mediates the association between sleep quality and next-day rumination.

A potential interpretation of the association between rumination and SSQ, but not TST or SE, may involve the so-called common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2024), given that both variables are assessed with self-report questionnaires on the same day. It is important to note, however, that SSQ was assessed in the morning questionnaire and rumination in the evening questionnaire, which may have decreased the risk of common method bias to a certain degree. In addition, the reliability and validity of actigraphy sleep indices, including TST and SE, have been found to be limited (Sadeh, 2011).

The absence of significant interactions between sleep and stress suggest that, in the present sample, sleep parameters did not substantially alter the association between daily stress and rumination. This finding contrasts with exploratory evidence from previous research suggesting that sleep disturbances may moderate the stress-rumination association (Zhou et al., 2024). Together with the present findings, this suggests that the role of sleep in the stress-rumination relationship may be more complex than a stress-buffering effect.

Finally, higher baseline depressive symptom severity was associated with higher levels of rumination, supporting Hypothesis 1d. Thus, participants with higher depressive symptom severity generally reported greater rumination across the study period. However, baseline depressive symptoms did not significantly moderate the association between daily stress and rumination. Consequently, Hypothesis 3 was not supported. Rather than indicating greater stress-related reactivity, the findings suggest that depressive symptom severity was primarily associated with between-person differences in overall rumination levels. This distinction is consistent with the conceptualization of depressive symptoms as a vulnerability factor for repetitive negative thinking while suggesting that, in the present sample, this vulnerability did not translate into stronger day-to-day rumination responses to stress.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

The present study has several strengths. First, the intensive longitudinal design enabled the examination of within-person fluctuations in rumination and their associations with daily stress, physical activity, and sleep. Rather than conceptualizing rumination solely as a relatively stable individual characteristic, the present study examined rumination as a dynamic and potentially modifiable outcome in everyday life. Second, EMA enabled these processes to be investigated in participants' natural environments. Finally, the combination of self-reported and objectively assessed measures provided a more comprehensive assessment of the daily factors associated with rumination.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Due to the observational nature of the study, the reported associations do not permit causal conclusions.

The sample consisted of generally healthy young adults, a limitation that may constrain the generalizability of the findings to other age groups and to populations with more elevated levels of psychopathology.

In the present study, stress was assessed using a self-report item including the type, intensity, and timing of the occurrence. However, it should be noted that the analyses did not include these characteristics of stress, which have been shown to be relevant for emotional responding and emotion regulation (Blanke et al., 2022; Braet et al., 2025; Gallagher et al., 2024). Future studies could therefore combine momentary assessments of stress with physiological measures, such as cortisol (Puterman et al., 2011), or heart rate variability to capture more fine-grained fluctuations in stress and physiological arousal (Kim et al., 2024).

Emerging wearable technologies may additionally provide opportunities to assess physiological processes associated with cognitive and affective processes in everyday life related to emotion regulation (Bettis et al., 2022), specifically rumination (Arnold & Young, 2025).

Moreover, the analyses of the study were confined to only one rumination assessment at the final prompt of the day. Consequently, the analyses fail to capture the fluctuations in rumination across the entire day and thus cannot determine how rumination develops or changes following stressful experiences. Finally, physical activity was quantified as an accumulation over the day, thereby preventing conclusions about whether the timing or pattern of physical activity is relevant. Therefore, an important avenue for future studies is to employ more frequent assessments, considering the temporal sequence of physical activity and rumination, using more detailed indices of patterns of physical activity, and lastly to experimentally increase physical activity at relevant timepoints via just in time adaptive interventions using encouragement.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study contributes to research on stress and rumination by examining rumination as a dynamic, potentially modifiable process in everyday life and by integrating intensive longitudinal assessment with both subjective and objective measures of physical activity and sleep. The findings indicate that stress was consistently associated with greater rumination, whereas greater-than-usual physical activity was associated with weaker stress-rumination association. Subjective sleep quality, in contrast, was associated with lower rumination independently of stress. Taken together, the findings of the study suggest that objectively measured physical activity and subjective sleep quality may be relevant to day-today fluctuations of rumination and provide a foundation for future research.
