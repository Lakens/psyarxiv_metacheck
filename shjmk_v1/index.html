What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Recruitment is often a challenge in health behaviour surveillance studies, especially when working with young children. The International Study of Movement Behaviours in the Early Years (SUNRISE Study) creates a unique opportunity to explore how barriers to recruitment and subsequent recruitment rates vary across countries. The present study aimed to examine global differences in recruitment rates, identify prominent barriers to recruitment, and explore researchers' perceptions of recruitment practices.

Methods: Research teams from 48 countries participating in the SUNRISE Study completed an online survey to examine perceived barriers and facilitators to recruitment of early childhood education and care (ECEC) settings, parents and preschoolers in the SUNRISE study. Descriptive analyses were performed to examine recruitment rates and barriers across countries, and directed content analysis, guided by the socio-ecological model were used to identify researchers' perceptions of effective facilitators to overcome barriers.

Results: Significant differences in recruitment rates and barriers were observed at both the country income and geographical region level for ECEC and parent/child recruitment with European and high-income countries reporting significantly lower recruitment rates. Across countries, the main barrier to ECEC and parent/child recruitment was time commitment concerns and parental concerns regarding accelerometer use, respectively. At the ECEC level, organisational barriers and centre beliefs regarding parent willingness were also identified to be prominent barriers by African & Eastern-Mediterranean, South-East Asia & Western Pacific, and Americas regions. These barriers were also considered prominent by low & lower-middle-income and upper-middle-income countries. Additional barriers to parent/child recruitment were time commitment concerns and lack of interest, as identified by European and high-income countries. Globally, facilitators at intrapersonal, interpersonal, and community level were found to be beneficial in overcoming recruitment barriers. This may involve providing education on the importance of movement behaviours to improve understanding, delivering participants in-person information sessions, and enlisting community organisations and professional networks to aid recruitment. Conclusion: Recruitment for child health surveillance and accelerometery research is inherently complex and may require context-specific approaches to overcome barriers. Our findings provide context-specific recruitment approaches to support the design and implementation of future global surveillance recruitment strategies, and to help ensure greater representativeness in global health research.

Background

The monitoring of health behaviours through national surveillance is essential for the identification of trends and patterns and evaluation of key public health concerns (1, 2). Specifically, healthy levels of physical activity (PA), sedentary behaviour (including screen time), and sleep-collectively known as the 24-hr movement behaviours-are linked to a range of child health outcomes including cardio-metabolic health, cognition, and mental health (3) (4) (5). In 2019, the World Health Organisation (WHO) released international 24-hr movement behaviour guidelines for the early years which provide recommendations for time spent in each behaviour to promote positive health outcomes (6). These guidelines recommend that preschool children aged 3 -4 years should spend at least 180 minutes engaged in PA, with 60 minutes of activity at moderate-to-vigorous-intensity, not be restricted in sedentary behaviour for more than 60 minutes at a time and should be limited to 60 minutes of sedentary screen time, and have 10 -13 hours of good quality sleep, which may include a nap, and have regular sleep and wake times. Global monitoring of these movement behaviours is important to provide an understanding of the extent to which guidelines are being met, allowing for the identification of potential health inequalities (e.g., region, socioeconomic status, and sex) (1).

However, surveillance studies of health behaviours bring numerous challenges such as ethical clearance requirements, methodological choices, and recruitment of broadly representative samples (7, 8). These are magnified when considering the complexities of global surveillance and child population. Unbiased recruitment in health surveillance studies is essential to ensure a representative sample is achieved and sampling errors are minimised (9, 10). Therefore, additional considerations need to be made around differing cultural contexts impacting recruitment and study implementation; utilising data collection instruments that are appropriate for global measurement; and informed consent/assent and ethical challenges of data collection with diverse child populations (11, 12). Furthermore, methodological challenges arise from varying ethical requirements across countries such as the distinction between active and passive parental consent, which can lead to inconsistent response rates and potential non-response bias, which complicates cross-national comparisons (13, 14). This demonstrates a need for robust planning and continual methodological review from initial study design through to data analysis and dissemination of findings.

Early childhood education and care (ECEC) settings are often a primary setting when recruiting participants and implementing child-based research. ECEC settings can provide many advantages such as convenience, better retention rates, a diverse range of participants from the target population, and can allow education staff to aid in the recruitment of parents and children (15) (16) (17). However, utilising ECEC settings for recruitment can also bring about many challenges. For example, multiple levels of approval are often required before accessing ECEC settings and parents and children can then be contacted (18, 19). This process can often be lengthy and complex resulting in delays in research timelines, or even blocked access. Additionally, utilising ECEC settings may bring about challenges specific to educational settings such as curricula constraints, lack of staff, and lack of space or resources which researchers also need to consider and navigate throughout recruitment processes (16, 19). Regarding parental challenges, utilising educational and care settings, such as ECEC, to recruit may limit contact opportunities with parents, resulting in mistrust and difficulty obtaining parental consent (16, 18). Previous research which has investigated why educational settings and parents may be reluctant to participate in research identified high staff burden, time constraints, and curriculum constraints as prominent ECEC barriers (20) (21) (22), while parental barriers may include lack of understanding and time constraints due to additional work and family commitments (23). Furthermore, health surveillance research has often employed the use of self-report measures despite the validity and reliability of these measures being questioned. As such, the use of device-based measurement tools, such as accelerometery, is becoming increasingly popular for health surveillance research. Though this approach may also bring about challenges due to the novelty and unfamiliarity of these devices to participants, and the increased cost in comparison to subjective measures for researchers (1). While it is important to recognise these challenges, it is equally important to identify specific barriers to health surveillance and accelerometery research, how these barriers may impact recruitment rates and identify facilitators to overcome barriers in addition to how this may vary across countries-a research area that is limited in the current literature.

The SUNRISE Study is a global surveillance study of 24-hr movement behaviours in children aged 3 -4 years across 66 countries (24). The study implements a standardised, robust protocol, employing the use of accelerometery to collect data on the 24-hr movement behaviours, across diverse settings. This provides a unique opportunity to evaluate how global research processes and measurement instruments (particularly the use of accelerometery) perform across different cultural, political and geographical landscapes (24). As with all global studies, recruitment rates and challenges encountered differ greatly between countries. Some evidence from the SUNRISE pilot phase studies highlights that, while the protocol is robust, research must still navigate significant practical hurdles, including technical issues with device-based measurements, parental concern on the use of device-based measurement tools, the reliance on parental-proxy reporting, and the complexities of local ethics approval (25) (26) (27). However, the full extent of the various recruitment barriers and differences in recruitment rates has not been explored systematically. In doing so, valuable insights on the practicalities of recruitment in large-scale international child health surveillance studies and studies that employ the use of accelerometery, may be provided.

Therefore, the aims of this study were to (a) examine differences in recruitment rates of ECEC settings and parent/child dyads at global, regional, and country income levels, (b) identify researchers perceived barriers to recruitment, and (c) explore perceptions of effective facilitators to overcome prominent barriers.

Methods

Study Design

The present study was conducted as part of the SUNRISE Study and adopts a mixed-methods approach to gather insights into recruitment rates, barriers to the recruitment of ECEC settings and parent/child dyads, and researchers' perceptions of facilitators to overcome prominent barriers.

The SUNRISE Study brings together researchers from countries across the globe to monitor adherence to the WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity, Sedentary Behaviour, and Sleep for Children Under 5 Years of Age. Currently, there are 66 countries are involved in the SUNRISE study with 22 of high-income, 19 of upper-middle income, and 25 of low and lower-middle income. The SUNRISE Study comprises of a pilot study and main study phase. The pilot study aims to determine the feasibility of recruiting 100 male and female children from urban and rural settings and to test the feasibility and acceptability of the standardised protocol. The main study aims to recruit 1000 children (sex-balanced) from rural and urban settings to determine the proportion of children who meet the WHO Global Guidelines (24).

Participants

At the time of data collection (September 2025), a total of 51 countries participating in the SUNRISE Study were in the process of completing, or had completed, the pilot or main study phase. The research team for these countries were asked to complete an online questionnaire to gather relevant information. The questionnaire was to be completed once for each study phase (pilot and main) where applicable.

Data Collection

Data were collected through an online questionnaire consisting of both open-and closedended questions, administered through the REDCap platform, a secure web-based application designed to support data capture for research studies (24, 28). An initial survey collected data regarding barriers to recruitment and facilitators to overcome barriers between September and November 2025. As this initial survey did not gather information regarding recruitment rates, a follow-up survey was provided to gain insight into the recruitment rates of ECEC settings and parent/child dyads between February and May 2026. A copy of both surveys is provided in Additional File 1.

Data Analysis

Quantitative Analysis.

Descriptive analyses were conducted to provide summaries of recruitment rates and barriers to recruitment at a global level and according to WHO region and World Bank income categories at the time of country data collection. Regional and income categories were collapsed to allow for an appropriate sample size for analyses and interpretation in each group. Therefore, regional categories include African & Eastern-Mediterranean (AF-EM), Americas (AM), South-East Asia & Western-Pacific (SEA-WP), and European (EU). Income categories include low & lower-middle-income countries (L/L-MICs), upper-middle-income countries (UMICs), and high-income countries (HICs). Recruitment rates and barriers were discussed for ECEC recruitment and parent/child recruitment.

ECEC recruitment rates were calculated as a percentage by dividing the total number of settings that were contacted by the number of settings that participated. The recruitment rates of parents/children were then calculated as a percentage by dividing the total number of eligible parents/children in each participating ECEC setting by the number of parent/child dyads that participated. Kruskal-Wallis analyses were used to compare recruitment rates across regional and income categories. Follow-up Dunn's pairwise comparisons were conducted applying Bonferroni corrections to test for significant differences between groups. Rank-biserial (rrb) correlation effect sizes were calculated for pairwise comparisons, rrb = 1 -2U/(n1*n2). A small effect size is defined as ≥ .11, a medium effect size is ≥ .28, and a large effect size is ≥ .43 (29). Statistical significance was set at p < .05. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac version 32 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Qualitative Analysis.

Directed content analysis (DCA) (30) was used to explore researchers perceived facilitators to address barriers to recruitment. The analysis consisted of three main phases: preparation, organisation and reporting (30) (31) (32). The socio-ecological model (33) was employed as a theoretical framework-providing a structured foundation for coding and interpretation-to explore at which levels of the socio-ecological model researchers perceive effective strategies to overcome prominent barriers. This resulted in the following 5 categories for data coding: (1) intrapersonal, (2) interpersonal, (3), organisational, (4) community, and (5) public policy. Independent coding was completed by multiple researchers (HM, AJW, CIM) using the a priori coding matrix to enhance the credibility and consistency of the analysis. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion (HM, AJW, FB). The categorisation matrix can be found in Additional File 2.

From the 51 countries invited to participate, a total of 48 countries completed the questionnaire with a total of 61 responses. Forty-seven (77%) responses referred to the pilot phase and 14 (23%) responses referred to the main phase. Data regarding recruitment rates were only available from 39 (76%) and 36 (71%) country responses for ECEC and parent/child recruitment, respectively. Information regarding each participating country can be found in an Additional File 3. Table 1 reports country characteristics for each study phase.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify and discuss global differences in recruitment rates in the SUNRISE Study, identify barriers to recruitment and differences experienced between regions and income categories, and to explore researchers' perceptions of effective facilitators to overcome prominent barriers. Recruitment rates across categories were mixed, however, SEA-WP and AF-EM generally had higher recruitment rates for both ECEC and parent/child recruitment. Barriers to recruitment were also mixed. Whilst ECEC concerns for time commitment of involvement to participate was the biggest barrier at the global level, many differences were observed across regional and country income levels. Barriers to parent/child recruitment were less diverse with parental concern around their child wearing an accelerometer was generally reported as the biggest barrier. Lastly, researchers perceived facilitators at the interpersonal level as a successful strategy to overcome both ECEC and parent/child barriers experienced. However, these strategies may be improved when applying more context-specific related facilitators.

Findings from the present study suggested that AF-EM, SEA-WP, and LLMIC had generally higher recruitment rates. It is important to note that global differences in recruitment rates in global health surveillance studies can be impacted by several factors such as differences in varying regulatory frameworks, infrastructural gaps, socioeconomic status, cultural norms, and research participation novelty or fatigue. Differences in regulatory frameworks may explain the difference observed between regions. A range of ethical approval processes are required before participating in research which vary by country and may consist of minimal procedures to highly complex procedures which require ethical approval at multiple stages. For example, Scotland requires consent to firstly be obtained from the local government authority before ECEC settings within that area can be contacted. Similarly, the European Union introduced the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) which adopts strict privacy laws in the collection, processing, and storage of health data in member countries (34). Whilst this regulation aims to balance the use of personal data for scientific research to protect participants rights, research has suggested GDPR may produce additional barriers in health research (35). For example, GDPR requirements have led to lengthier consent and data protection information forms, which likely increased participant burden and may dissuade participation (35). Previous research indicates that lengthy paperwork is often complex and participants may not fully understand terms and descriptions or have adequate time to provide fully informed consent (36, 37). GDPR is regulated at a national level by independent states which may introduce limits to global research cooperation resulting in researchers often being confused in how these regulations should be implemented (35, 38). Therefore, researchers experience more challenges in gaining access to ECEC settings and parents/children and may hinder the representativeness of samples due to the possibility of specific populations groups being inadvertently excluded.

Whilst the SUNRISE protocol has received ethical approval by the University of Wollongong, Australia, Human Research Ethics Committee and each participating country's institutional ethics committees, additional ethical approval may be required by government departments or organisations who oversee ECEC settings in the different countries (24). These additional requirements add to the already lengthy and challenging process of recruiting ECEC settings (39, 40). Governing bodies each have their own ethical approval processes meaning researchers must complete multiple ethics applications before recruitment can begin. Research conducted with young children also requires both parent consent and child assent to ensure children are adequately protected (41). As young children's selfdetermination is not yet matured, the principle of respect ensures that children are protected by requiring permission from parents or legal guardians whilst still providing children with the opportunity to engage in and develop self-determination. Child assent involved the explanation of participation in an appropriate manner and children subsequently deciding whether they would wish to take part. However, parental consent is more complex, requiring parents to be adequately informed of the nature and purpose of the research, any risks or benefits expected from participation, and information regarding data protection, before written consent can be obtained (41, 42). The additional requirements of including data protection information forms add to the already lengthy and complex parental consent forms, which may add to parent burden and lack of understanding resulting in parents possibly being deterred from participation in early stages. Considering the differences observed between country income levels, the present study found that LLMIC reported higher recruitment rates than HIC for ECEC recruitment and both LLMIC and UMIC reported higher recruitment rates than HIC for parent/child recruitment. This finding may be explained by the overrepresentation of HIC in public health research, resulting in these countries being overwhelmed with opportunities to participate in research and having to be more selective in what they participate in (43, 44). In contrast, LLMICs may see research participation as a novel and exciting opportunity. Furthermore, current literature indicates that a high proportion of research on the 24-hr movement behaviours in preschool children is conducted in HICs (5, 45). The SUNRISE Study aims to overcome this research inequality by limiting the recruitment of HICs into the study-over one third of participating countries being of low-income. SUNRISE provides these countries novel insights into the movement behaviours of their young children, information which may not be as easily accessible through health services in comparison to HICs. Therefore, parents from LLMICs may be more willing to participate in health surveillance research as they view it as being beneficial to their child, offering a possible explanation to the differences observed in recruitment rates. Furthermore, LLMIC indicated that facilitators at the intrapersonal level were successful in overcoming parent/child barriers to recruitment which focussed on ensuring participants fully understand the nature and importance of the study before consent was obtained. Employing these facilitators may also build strong trust and rapport between researchers and parents and children. In doing so, concerns could be adequately addressed, and parents may have obtained a stronger understanding than those from HIC where facilitators at the interpersonal level were more heavily adopted. Whilst interpersonal facilitators are beneficial in providing adequate communication, such strategies may assume parents already have a basic understanding of the importance of research participation.

Looking at barriers to ECEC recruitment, concerns for time commitment of involvement and bureaucratic or institutional barriers were a challenge. Research participation may bring about additional burdens to ECEC practitioners. A previous study reported that school leaders, such as head teachers, refused to participate in research due to time constraints, perceived burden, concerns for student wellbeing, or a lack of alignment with institutional priorities (20). Despite practitioners viewing PA as an essential aspect of child development, practitioners are aware of the prioritisation of educational subject areas (46). An increase in the implementation of governmental curriculum policies often focus on educational outcomes, such as being able to read and write, to ensure that children are "school-ready", with less attention to community engagement and social responsibility and a lack of awareness of the importance of healthy movement behaviours (21, 22, 47). However, "school-readiness" also comprises of independence, the ability to follow instruction, communication and socialisation skills, and fine and gross motor skills attributes which are supported and developed through participating in PA (22, 48).

Despite common barriers to ECEC recruitment being organisational barriers, researchers involved in the SUNRISE Study indicated that interpersonal strategies were successful in overcoming ECEC barriers. Such strategies focus on the importance of establishing strong relationships between the research team and prospective ECEC settings to build trust and rapport allowing for meaningful interactions to occur which subsequently supported recruitment. Similar findings have been reported in previous research that aimed to identify successful strategies to "Recruit, engage, and retain children in behavioural health risk factor studies" (REACH), where building trustful relationships was also identified as a successful strategy (49) and face-to-face recruitment approaches were most suitable in order to build relationships (50). However, prominent barriers to ECEC recruitment referred to concern for time commitment of involvement and bureaucratic or institutional barriers. Whilst interpersonal strategies may be useful in first obtaining a strong relationship with ECEC settings, more specific organisational strategies may be better equipped to overcome such barriers. For example, logistical barriers such as staff shortages, high staff workload, and daily schedule requirements (51) are possible reasons as to why ECEC may decline participation in research. Therefore, organisational strategies which focus on working closely with key staff members to discuss their concerns and create flexible participation procedures which minimise staff burden and ensure minimal disruptions to daily activities may facilitate participation more successfully than interpersonal strategies alone. ECEC incentives, including financial compensation for staff time, may also be a successful strategy when resources are available.

Regarding barriers to parent/child recruitment, concerns around child wearing an accelerometer was reported as the biggest barrier overall and by AF-EM, AM, and SEA-WP countries, and by LLMIC and UMIC. The use of accelerometery to assess movement behaviours has become an increasingly popular measure providing an alternative to traditional self-report measures which often raise questions regarding reliability and validity of results (1, 52). Previous evidence has suggested that the use of self-reporting measures in national health surveys may produce misleading data resulting in unintended adverse consequences for public health policy. Limitations to such measures call for a focus on moving towards device-based surveillance systems, especially in national and international research where comparisons of movement behaviours can be made more easily (1, 53). However, previous research has suggested that parents may be hesitant to use device-based measurement tools due to concerns regarding the device being lost or broken, the use of large devices possibly having a negative impact on their child's sleep, the device having direct contact with the skin, and safety and privacy concerns (54). Further research has suggested the importance of establishing trust with parents through face-to-face interactions may aid in alleviating such concerns (55, 56). These interactions allow researchers to discuss and demonstrate the use of these devices to address concerns and allow parents to understand why and how the device is used.

Despite device-based concerns being a barrier at a global level, EU countries and HICs indicated that parental lack of interest and concerns for time commitment of involvement to be a more influential parent/child recruitment barrier. Such barriers have been reported in nutrition, PA, and obesity interventions where time constraints and lack of understanding and the complexity of information were prominent barriers to participation (23). EU countries and HICs may have additional work and family commitments that may deter parents and children from participating in research if they view the time commitment of involvement to be burdensome (23, 57, 58).

Strategies at interpersonal level were also indicated as successful facilitators to overcome parent/child barriers globally. Again, building trust and rapport between researchers and parents is important and may best be done through in-person visits and conducting information sessions. However, when looking at regional-and country income-level responses, only EU countries, UMICs, and HICs reported interpersonal strategies whilst AF-EM, AM, and SEA-WP countries and LLMICs indicated intrapersonal strategies to be more beneficial. This may be linked to the prominent barriers experienced by some regions and income categories. For example, AF-EM, AM, and SEA-WP countries, and LLMICs and UMICs reported concerns around child wearing an accelerometer as the most prominent barrier as noted above. Strategies at intrapersonal level may be better suited to overcome such barriers as these strategies focused on providing parents with adequate education and information on the nature, purpose, and methods of the research. The use of accelerometery may be novel to parents resulting in hesitations around allowing their child to wear the device. By providing parents with details and examples on how and why the devices are used may alleviate their concerns and facilitate participation. Parent/child incentives, including providing child-level movement behaviour reports, may also be a successful strategy when resources are available.

Recommendations for Future Research and Surveillance

Recruitment of participants to health surveillance studies involving the childhood population and accelerometery is inherently complex and requires the consideration of multiple factors. Ultimately, successful recruitment of both ECEC and parent/child dyads requires potential participants having a clear understanding on the value of the study topic and their involvement. Across all regions and income categories, it should not be assumed that participants are knowledgeable on 24-hr movement behaviours. Research teams involved in recruitment should ensure that both ECEC settings and parents understand the nature and importance of the study's objectives. This approach to recruitment creates trust between the research team, ECEC settings, and parents. It also ensures participants hold the required knowledge and understanding of the importance of the research to be able to provide fully informed consent. This approach may also be specifically relevant when the use of accelerometers is a large barrier as this measurement tool may be uncommon to participants resulting in many concerns regarding its use. Addressing the concerns effectively may be achieved through holding in-person information sessions with participants allowing researchers to thoroughly show and explain how the device works and address participant concerns efficiently.

Recommendations to overcome ECEC-specific barriers are more complex with differences being observed across regional and income categories. Generally, it can be recommended that recruitment of ECEC settings may be improved by receiving support from higher-level government departments or organisations. This support may encourage ECEC settings to participate if the research is viewed as being trusted by such departments. Additionally, it is important to be flexible in the data collection procedures by taking into consideration specific time or staff constraints experienced by ECEC settings.

Future research and surveillance should plan recruitment strategies taking into consideration barriers they may encounter, both globally and locally. In doing so, recruitment processes may become more efficient and result in more representative samples. An initial recruitment strategy may be developed during pilot work with the subsequent strategy for main study recruitment being enhanced by knowledge and experience gained throughout the pilot (36). This allows countries to identify specific barriers they may encounter during their own recruitment processes which can be addressed using suggested facilitators identified from the present study to help make recruitment processes more efficient.

Strengths and Limitations

The present study collected data from the SUNRISE study, a real-world international accelerometery based surveillance study on the 24-hr movement behaviours of preschool children, to gain insight into global differences in recruitment rates and researchers perceived barriers and facilitators to recruitment. In doing so, the study offers a novel multi-country perspective to provide insights into the recruitment processes of a global surveillance study and the use of accelerometery across diverse international contexts. Further, the study provides a more comprehensive understanding into barriers encountered and identifies possible facilitators that may promote recruitment in future global health surveillance and accelerometery research. Nonetheless, this study is not without limitations. One limitation is the small sample size of this study for statistical comparison. Given the small sample size and the absence of prespecified hypotheses regarding regional and income group differences, these findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Further research is needed into group differences in recruitment rates in international surveillance of 24-hour movement behaviours. Another limitation refers to the data reported were collected through self-report surveys completed by SUNRISE researchers resulting in findings being subjective to the experiences of these researchers. Therefore, whilst the results may provide insights into facilitators to overcome barriers and improve recruitment, these findings may not be generalisable outside the SUNRISE study. Despite this, the SUNRISE study provided a unique opportunity to capture insights from respondents across a wide geographical and income landscape. Lastly, contextual factors which were not captured in the present study, such as differences in policy, culture, resources available, use of incentives, and recruitment duration, may also influence recruitment rates. Further research is required to identify the impact of these factors to gain a better understanding into recruitment.

As the present research only collects data regarding the perceptions of the SUNRISE study researchers, additional research is required to gain insight into parent and ECEC staff perceptions of barriers which result in them declining participation to research and strategies which would aid in the promotion of their participation. This further research would allow more context-specific recommendations, considering a whole-systems approach to recruitment and participation in research.

Conclusion

The present study identified global differences in recruitment rates, identified top barriers to ECEC and parent/child recruitment across global, regional, and country income levels, and identified potential facilitators to overcome prominent barriers. Concerns regarding time commitment and accelerometer use were prominent ECEC and parent/child barriers, respectively. Facilitators at interpersonal level to overcome barriers were indicated by researchers to be successful in overcoming both ECEC and parent/child barriers. However, these strategies may be improved with more context-specific approaches, such as organisational strategies for ECEC recruitment and intrapersonal strategies for parent/child recruitment. Additional factors may also impact recruitment resulting in recruitment procedures being inherently complex requiring flexible, context-specific approaches to promote recruitment. These findings aim to provide context-specific recruitment approaches to support the design and implementation of future global surveillance recruitment strategies to help research findings be more representative and recruitment elements of research are more efficient.
