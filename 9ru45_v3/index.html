Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

Abstract

This paper addresses the situation in which treatment effects are reported using educational or psychological outcome measures comprised of multiple questions or "items." Drawing from item response theory, we distinguish among three estimands of potential interest: (a) a treatment effect on the latent variable representing the construct of interest, which is referred to as impact, (b) test-level treatment effects computed using aggregates of assessment items (e.g., the unweighted mean), and (c) item-level effects. We show that test-level treatment effects and impact are generally not equivalent estimands in the presence of item-level treatment effect heterogeneity. Consequently, failing to distinguish these estimands can have important implications for the validity of research studies.

To address this issue, we propose a diagnostic test to infer whether test-level treatment effects are robust to item-level heterogeneity. We illustrate the use of the test with a case study. We also provide some initial evidence about the prevalence of this issue using a small meta-analysis. Results from the meta-analysis indicated that treatment effects based on the unweighted mean of assessment items often biased upwards when compared to a robust altnerative, and that this pattern was more apparent with researcher-developed assessments than independently developed assessments.

In both studies, the type of outcome measure was more strongly related to reported effect sizes than any other methodological factor, including experimental controls (e.g., randomization). The differences associated with the type of outcome were substantially larger than the focal comparisons among interventions.

Several hypotheses have been proposed about why researcher-developed outcome measures are associated with larger effect sizes (Wolf and Harbatkin, 2023). In the usual case that the outcome measures are educational or psychological assessments (e.g., achievement tests, self-report surveys), some of these hypotheses can be interpreted in terms of how treatment effects depend on the specific questions or "items" that make up the assessments. For example, it has been argued that researcher-developed assessments may be more narrow in scope and more closely aligned with target learning domains than independently developed assessments (de Boer et al., 2014; Lipsey et al., 2012; Francis et al., 2022).

The rationale here is that assessment items less closely aligned with the intervention should show diminished treatment effects. If independently developed assessments contain relatively larger proportions of such items, this could explain the reported discrepancies between researcher-developed and independently developed outcome measures.

Others have argued that the content of researcher-developed measures may be either directly or indirectly pre-exposed to participants in the treatment group (Cheung and Slavin, 2016; Slavin, 2008), or may be otherwise over-aligned with the intervention being evaluated (What Works Clearinghouse, 2022). For example, some of the questions on a researcher-developed assessment may be drawn directly from program materials seen only by the treatment group. In this situation, we would expect relatively larger "effects" on items that included pre-exposed material. More generally, the pattern of effects on pre-exposed items is unlikely to be consistent with the pattern implied by a treatment effect on the underlying construct being measured, which is a point we elaborate on below.

These two assumptions are important for mathematically defining the concept of robustness in the present context. In particular, we define a robust test-level treatment effect as one that remains equivalent to impact on the latent trait in the presence of IL-HTE. It is important to clarify that a robust treatment effect is not necessarily preferable to one based on the unweighted mean of assessment items, and, more generally, that outliers may contain useful information (Rousseeuw and Hubert, 2018). Rather, the main argument of this paper is that a substantial discrepancy between the two estimates provides a clear signal to researchers that the usual practice of reporting treatment effects using the unweighted mean of assessment items warrants further scrutiny. 1.2 Implications for Validity

To conceptualize the practical implications of IL-HTE in program evaluation research, it is helpful to consider the distinction between construct-relevant and construct-irrelevant DIF (e.g., Markus and Borsboom, 2025, chap. 3).

Construct-irrelevant DIF arises when the item-specific effects are not related to what one wishes to measure. For example, when a mathematics test is written in respondents' second language, they may perform worse than expected on word problems (Shealy and Stout, 1993). Here, the source of the item-specific effects is reading comprehension, but the target construct is mathematics. The overall concern is that the unweighted mean score would lead us to systematically underestimate the mathematics proficiency of students who are tested in a second language. For this reason, construct-irrelevant DIF is typically interpreted in terms of measurement bias and regarded as a threat to test fairness (Joint Committee for Educational and Psychological Testing, 2014).

By contrast, construct-relevant DIF can arise when pre-existing characteristics of the respondents are systematically related to some but not all of the content being assessed. For example, after controlling for overall levels of verbal aggression, Belgian men are more likely than Belgian women to scold or curse (De Boeck and Wilson, 2004). Here scolding and cursing are clearly related to the construct of interest (verbal aggression).

It is often the case that construct-relevant DIF can be interpreted as evidence that the construct in question is multidimensional in nature. In the example, men and women matched on overall verbal aggression did not differ in their desire to scold or curse, but only in whether they would actually carry through with those acts. This has been taken as evidence that verbal aggression can be differentiated along affective and behavioral dimensions (De Boeck and Wilson, 2004). Importantly, the overall implications of ignoring DIF are similar to the previous example. In this case, the unweighted mean score would lead us to systematically underestimate the overall level of verbal aggression for women, whereas it would be more accurate to conclude that there were gender differences with respect to behavioral but not affective aspects of verbal aggression.

In the context of program evaluation research, construct-relevant and construct-irrelevant sources of IL-HTE can be similarly distinguished. As discussed in the introduction to this paper, pre-exposure of assessment materials in the treatment group would be a clear instance of construct-irrelevant IL-HTE. This situation is a threat to internal validity, because differential exposure to test materials could be mistaken for a treatment effect on the target construct (Shadish et al., 2002, Table 2.4). On the other hand, IL-HTE may often be explainable with reference to scientifically interesting features of the items (e.g., content, difficulty), and documenting these sources of variation can provide fertile grounds for refining education theory (e.g., Gilbert et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2023). In this situation, the concern is mainly about the consequences of ignoring IL-HTE when reporting overall treatment effects, which would constitute a threat to construct validity (e.g., due to treatment-sensitive factorial structure or reactive self-reports; see Shadish et al., 2002, Table 3.4).

There are also clear implications for external validity. By definition, impact generalizes to other assessments of the same construct (i.e., assessments that use different items). On the other hand, IL-HTE depends on the specific items that appear on an assessment, and therefore may not generalize to assessments that do not use those same items. As discussed in the introduction to this paper, concerns about generalizability are particularly relevant to the ongoing debate about the role of researcher-developed versus independently developed outcome measures in educational research. Regardless of whether the source of this discrepancy is construct-relevant (e.g., hidden multidimensionality of independently-developed assessments) or construct-irrelevant (e.g., pre-exposure of content on researcher-developed assessments), the overall result has been that reported treatment effects tend not to generalize well across the different types of measures (Wolf and Harbatkin, 2023).

In summary, when IL-HTE is ignored, it may present a threat to the internal validity, construct validity, and/or external validity of research studies. In particular, it is important to understand the extent to which the usual practice of reporting treatment effects using the unweighted mean of assessment items reflects impact on the target construct as opposed to sources of variation that are dependent upon the specific items used to measure the construct. This issue can be addressed using the statistical methodology discussed next.

2 Statistical Methodology

This section draws from the literature on IRT-based scaling and linking (e.g., Kolen and Brennan, 2014, chap. 6) to distinguish among three interrelated estimands of potential interest in program evaluation settings: (a) impact on the latent trait representing the target construct, (b) test-level effects that aggregate over assessment items, and (c) item-level effects. We show that all three are equivalent in the absence of DIF/IL-HTE but may differ in its presence.

We then introduce a diagnostic test that addresses whether estimated test-level effects computed using the unweighted mean of assessment items are influenced by IL-HTE. The test is constructed by comparing the unweighted mean of assessment items to an outlier-robust alternative.

In practice, the proposed methodology can be implemented by first estimating a focal psychometric model separately in the treatment and control groups, and then applying the procedures described below in a post-estimation step.

This paper focuses on maximum likelihood estimation of the two-parameter logistic (2PL) IRT model for binary response data, although the main considerations extend to other IRT models (e.g., for ordered categorical data) and other estimators with known asymptotic distributions (Bayesian, weighted least squares).

2.3 Shortcomings of Conventional Approaches to DIF

Traditional methods of DIF analysis proceed item-by-item, with the usual goal of omitting items that exhibit nonnegligible degrees of DIF during test development. These procedures typically involve conducting a large number of statistical tests, often in combination with iteratively re-fitting many IRT models to the data, and manually selecting so-called "anchor items" that are assumed not to exhibit DIF (Teresi et al., 2021). The choice of anchor items is especially problematic, as it can dramatically affect the results of DIF analyses, in some cases even changing the direction of impact (Lewis, 1993). It has also been shown that these methods can fail badly when a relatively large proportion of items (> 1/4) exhibit DIF in the same direction (Kopf et al., 2015a,b), although recent contributions have made improvements here (Chen et al., 2023; Halpin, 2024; Wang et al., 2022).

Item-by-item tests of DIF can be replaced with omnibus tests of whether any item exhibits DIF, which are often referred to in terms of measurement invariance (e.g., Millsap, 2011). However, if an omnibus test of measurement invariance indicates the presence of DIF, researchers will typically proceed with the usual item-by-item tests to identify potentially problematic items, and then refit a "final" model that omits those items or allows their parameters to vary over groups (so-called partial measurement invariance). Consequently, while measurement invariance tests are an important diagnostic, they typically lead to DIF analyses.

The problem of how DIF accumulates to affect overall test scores has received attention in the psychometric literature (e.g., Chalmers et al., 2016; Raju et al., 1995; Shealy and Stout, 1993). However, these approaches require that items with DIF are known beforehand. This amounts to first conducting item-by-item tests of DIF, and then examining the consequence for test scores in a second stage. These procedures are therefore subject to the same shortcomings as the item-by-item tests. Moreover, the uncertainty from the initial item-by-item tests is not accounted for in subsequent analyses of test scores, resulting in a post-selection inference problem (e.g., Hastie et al., 2015, chap. 6). The methodology described in the following sections circumvents these issues by directly comparing two IRT-based test-level treatment effects.

3.2 Meta-Analysis

In this section, we re-analyze 34 datasets from 22 unique RCTs. The data are a subset of a collection of publicly available datasets on the item response warehouse (Domingue et al., 2023). In this re-analysis, we focus on a subset of studies that reported educational interventions with children or adolescents. The datasets are summarized in Table 2. Together, the datasets include responses from 174, 630 individuals to 775 items, representing a wide range of geographic settings and outcome measures. Our re-analysis used initial treatment assignment and considered the first post-treatment follow-up assessment. We included only datasets with at least 500 subjects and at least 7 items, after excluding any items with endorsement rates outside of the 2-98% range, items with abnormally high (> 10) or low (< .35) discrimination parameter estimates, or items responded to by less than 10% of the total sample. A handful of items with polytomous responses were re-coded to binary.

Our re-analysis was guided by the following research questions (RQs). First, is there evidence that naive treatment effects based on the unweighted mean of assessment items were influenced by item-level heterogeneity in this collection of studies? Second, is the magnitude of the Delta statistic (i.e., the discrepancy between naive and robust estimates) related to the magnitude of the naive estimates or the type of outcome measure (researcher developed vs independently developed)? Third, to what extent does the Delta statistic provide information that is not already available from an item-by-item analysis of IL-HTE? And finally, what can we learn from the pattern of IL-HTE across studies? This latter question complements prior analyses that have examined the degree of item-level variation over studies (Gilbert et al., 2025) by focusing specifically on the direction of outlying item-level treatment effects.

3.3 RQ1: Influence of IL-HTE on Naive Treatment Effects

Figure 3 reports the Delta statistic for each dataset. Positive values indicate that the naive estimate was larger than the robust estimate, which was the case in 22 out of 34 datasets (65%). At the 95% level, the Delta statistic was significantly different from zero in 11 out of 34 datasets (32.4%), although 3 of these were from a single study.

Standard errors showed an association with sample size (r = -.40, using log(N students)) and the number of items (r = -.36 using log(N items)), indicating that larger studies with longer outcome measures led to more precise estimates. We conclude that naive test-level treatment effects were influenced by IL-HTE in an appreciable number of studies and that the pattern of results suggested that, when statistically significant deviations arose, the naive effects were typically but not always upwardly biased when compared to the robust estimator.

The regression results suggest that the well-documented relationship between reported effect sizes and the type of outcome measure is at least partially due to this source of upward bias.

3.5 RQ3: Delta vs. Proportion of Items with IL-HTE

It is reasonable to ask whether the Delta statistic provides information that is not already available from an item-byitem analysis. Figure 5 shows the association between the Delta statistic and the per-dataset proportion of items that exhibited IL-HTE at the 5% level of significance (r = 0.51). While researcher-developed assessments tended to show higher proportions of IL-HTE, this was not consistently associated with larger values of Delta.

As with the Delta statistic, the proportion of items that exhibited IL-HTE was strongly associated with the magnitude of the naive treatment effects (r = .56). However, as shown in the last column of Table 3, the Delta statistic remained a significant predictor after controlling for the proportion of items with IL-HTE, indicating that each predictor provided unique information. The two exceptions were Studies 3 and 7, where the proportions were 5/9 and 10/19, respectively. 4 Conclusion

This paper contributes to a growing body of literature documenting the prevalence and importance of IL-HTE in program evaluation research. Previous approaches to studying this phenomenon have focused on random-effects models that assume IL-HTE varies symmetrically about the true treatment effect (Ahmed et al., 2024; Gilbert et al., 2023 Gilbert et al., , 2025)). This paper has considered the implications of relaxing this assumption. In particular, we distinguished among the three estimands of potential interest -a treatment effect on the latent trait representing the target construct, test-level treatment effects based on aggregates of assessment items, and item-level effects. It was shown that, while all three estimands coincide in the absence of IL-HTE, they may differ in its presence. In particular, treatment effects computed using the unweighted mean of assessment items may be influenced by outlying item-level effects, analogously to how outliers affect the sample mean.

To address this issue, we presented a diagnostic test for inferring whether treatment effects based on the unweighted mean of assessment items are influenced by IL-HTE. The test is constructed by comparing the unweighted mean to a robust alternative that down-weights outlying item-level effects. We illustrated the use and interpretation of the test in a case study. We also provided a re-analysis of 34 item-level datasets from 22 unique studies. This small metaanalysis led to the conclusions that naive treatment effects were influenced by IL-HTE in an appreciable number of datasets (11/34, using a 5% level of significance). The overall pattern suggested that naive treatment effects tended to be upwardly biased when compared to the outlier-robust alternative, as quantified by the Delta statistic in Equation ( 11). This pattern was more apparent in studies that reported larger naive test-level treatment effects. Moreover, the well-documented association between naive effect sizes and the type of outcome measure (researcher developed vs independently developed) was largely accounted for by this upward bias. Together, the empirical results suggest that the potential threats to internal, construct, and external validity discussed in this paper warrant further attention from researchers in education, especially when using researcher-developed assessments.

4.1 What to do About Item-Level Heterogeneous Treatment Effects?

This paper has addressed whether reported treatment effects are robust to IL-HTE. When the answer is in the negative, this raises the question of what to do about IL-HTE. We offer some suggestions here. 1) Provide evidence that any items flagged for IL-HTE were not pre-exposed or otherwise compromised during the experiment. If the possibility of construct-irrelevant IL-HTE cannot be ruled out, researchers may consider reporting sensitivity analyses that omit or down-weight items with abnormally large effects.

2) Assuming that construct-irrelevant IL-HTE has been ruled out, researchers can caution readers that naive test-level treatment effects (e.g., the unweighted mean) belie underlying heterogeneity among items. If the naive treatment effect appears to be driven by a relatively small subset of items, it can be helpful to identify those items and discuss their implications for construct and external validity.

3) In situations where IL-HTE is hypothesized to be due to scientifically interesting aspects of the items (e.g., content, difficulty), ad-hoc modeling of these sources of variation can provide opportunities to refine educational theory by identifying subdomains that are more or less responsive to the treatment (e.g. Gilbert et al., 2023) and can yield more accurate estimates of the precision of overall treatment effects (Gilbert et al., 2025). While exploratory item-level modeling can offer interesting avenues for theory refinement, item-level effects can also be highly unreliable (see Figure 2) and their interpretation warrants due caution. 4) Provide item-level data for reproducibility and re-analysis. Data availability facilitates further study of IL-HTE, which can be useful for addressing ongoing concerns about the role of measurement in evaluation contexts (Shear and Briggs, 2024; Flake and Fried, 2020; Soland et al., 2025) as well as broader issues related to treatment effect heterogeneity (Bryan et al., 2021).

4.2 Limitations

There are several important limitations of the proposed diagnostic test that researchers should keep in mind. The first two have been mentioned previously, but are repeated here with an emphasis on future research directions.

1) The methodology is based on the underlying assumption that, in the absence of IL-HTE, a unidimensional IRT model provides a suitable representation of the outcome measure of interest. This assumption is generally plausible in the intended applications where treatment effects are reported using the unweighted mean of assessment items (e.g., McNeish and Wolf, 2020). However, if assessment data are not unidimensional, the methodology is not applicable. This limitation indicates the value of future research on less restrictive methods for diagnosing the robustness of reported treatment effects to IL-HTE, for example, using resampling of items (e.g., Ahmed et al., 2024).

2) The robust treatment effect estimator used to compute the proposed diagnostic test assumes that fewer than 1/2 of items exhibit IL-HTE in the same direction. If this is not the case, the diagnostic test may be underpowered and/or result in false negatives. This situation can be diagnosed by item-level analyses and related analyses (Halpin, 2024), but methods that have better performance under empirically plausible patterns of IL-HTE remain an important area of research.

3) Power analysis for the proposed test is available but requires explicitly specifying the pattern of IL-HTE, which is a practical limitation. Additionally, while the proposed diagnostic test has been shown to perform reasonably well when sample sizes are at least 500 per group, it can be quite under-powered at smaller sample sizes (Halpin, 2025).

Consequently, methods to improve statistical power at smaller sample sizes are needed.

4) The diagnostic test assumes that groups are randomly assigned and does not currently incorporate inclusion of pre-treatment covariates, instrumental variables, or other extensions to observational settings. Extensions that separate person-level from item-level sources of variation would expand the applicability of the proposed methodology to a broader range of research designs.

In summary, researchers should exercise caution when applying the proposed diagnostic test if the assumptions of the underlying psychometric model are not met, if the proportion of items that exhibit IL-HTE in the same direction exceeds 1/2, if the sample sizes are smaller than 500 per group, and in non-experimental settings. Additionally, while the results of the reported meta-analysis were suggestive, they do not necessarily generalize beyond the current sample. We used a small convenience sample based on the availability of item-level data. Replication in a wider range of studies is required before drawing stronger conclusions.

4.3 Summary

This paper has presented a diagnostic test that can provide a clear signal to researchers, meta-analysts, and policymakers about whether reported treatment effects are robust to item-level sources of variation that may not generalize to other measures of the same construct. The test is relatively straightforward to implement as a post-estimation step using standard IRT models that are widely available in general-purpose statistical software. Future research along these lines can help move education sciences towards statistical methodology and empirical results that evaluate the sensitivity of treatment effects to the specific outcome measures used.
