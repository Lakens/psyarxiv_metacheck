Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Most young people with mental health disorders do not receive any mental health care. Digital single-session interventions adapted from evidence-based treatments help address this problem by making potent therapeutic skills widely accessible. There is increasing demand and interest in developing digital single-session interventions, yet there is no guide to promote best practices. Objective: This paper guides readers through the development of effective, selfguided, digital single-session interventions for youth. Method: We convened eight researchers with extensive experience developing, testing, and disseminating digital single-session interventions for youth. This team collected and distilled lessons learned from over a dozen single-session intervention projects into this guide. Results: This paper briefly summarizes the value of single-session interventions, provides a step-by-step guide for developing effective single-session interventions from larger evidence-based programs, and outlines how to use this guide through a case study. The present step-by-step guide breaks down the development process into five phases. During the preparation phase, you cultivate a single-session mindset. During collaboration, you assemble the right team to reach your target audience. During adaptation, you select a standalone component from an evidence-based treatment. During design, you create the content of the single-session intervention. Finally, during engagement, you learn best practices to increase uptake and learning. In addition to this guide, two supplemental materials are included, including https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24544197 for use during development and an https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24544286 to answer frequently asked questions. Conclusion: This manuscript serves as a practical guide for the creation of effective, scalable, self-guided, digital single-session interventions, expanding access to mental health support for diverse and disadvantaged youth.

A Practical Guide to Adapting Evidence-Based Treatments into Single-Session Interventions for Youth

Youth across the United States (US) face a worsening mental health crisis (Reinert et al., 2024). Disparities in accessing high-quality mental healthcare are exacerbated by structural and systemic barriers, such as national provider shortages, high costs of care, stigma, time restraints, and mental health illiteracy (Chorpita, 2019; Kazdin, 2019). Scientists and practitioners are diversifying their scope to develop digital forms of mental health support for youth (Muñoz et al., 2016). Digital interventions mitigate the barriers associated with face-to-face mental health appointments, offering customizability, simplicity, and flexibility (Hollis et al., 2017; Wies et al., 2021). Furthermore, single-session interventions (SSIs) intentionally designed to be just one impactful encounter (Schleider, Dobias, et al., 2020) are increasing in popularity due to their accessibility and acceptability amongst youth. Many experts are eager to develop their own digital single-session interventions; yet, they lack a clear guide or starting point. In this manuscript, we bring together a team of digital intervention scientists to illuminate how to design self-guided, digital single-session interventions (SSIs) for youth from larger existing evidencebased interventions.

The Promise of Evidence-Based, Digital Single-Session Interventions

Digital SSIs have improved health outcomes in a variety of settings, spanning across the disciplines of behavioral health, public health, and medicine (Eaton et al., 2012; Wasil et al., 2020). Digital SSIs are often brief and can range in length between 20-30 min; however, their brevity does not impact their efficacy (Kaveladze et al., 2025). Within the field of mental health, these one-time, online interventions have effectively reduced symptoms and improved mental health outcomes across disorders, including harmful substance use (Armitage et al., 2014), chronic pain (Ziadni et al., 2021), anxiety (Sung et al., 2023), and depression (Schleider, Burnette, et al., 2020). In a meta-analysis reviewing 50 randomized trials including 10,508 youth, SSIs significantly reduced youth mental health problems including anxiety and depression when compared to their respective controls, with effect sizes only slightly below those for traditional, longer-term treatments for youth (Schleider & Weisz, 2017; Weisz et al., 2017).

Single-session interventions targeting anxiety showed an overall effect size of Hedges g = .56 at post-intervention (Schleider & Weisz, 2017), while interventions targeting anxiety that were 12-14 sessions long showed an overall effect size of Hedges g = .61 at post-intervention (Weisz et al., 2017). Other online SSIs have targeted parents as a way to impact youth, increasing distress tolerance for parents with high anxiety children and improving the endorsement of positive parenting practices (Sung et al., 2021). Moreover, in a nationwide study of adolescents (N = 2,452) recruited via social media during the COVID-19 pandemic, self-guided digital SSIs significantly reduced depression, anxiety, hopelessness, and restrictive eating behaviors up to 3 months after the single-session (Schleider et al., 2022). Overall, digital SSIs have demonstrated the potential to improve diverse mental health outcomes and offer an effective way to disseminate evidence-based interventions. This article guides readers through the development of effective, self-guided, digital SSIs for youth. While these guidelines may be generalizable to supporting mental health across the lifespan, our team's expertise and experience has primarily focused on developing SSIs for youth. We describe a step-by-step methodology that our team has previously used to design over a dozen SSIs. Then we present a case study demonstrating how we followed the steps in the development of an SSI. Finally, we offer answers to frequently asked questions.

Preparation: Developing a single-session mindset

The single-session mindset. Before creating a self-guided, digital single-session intervention, we must adopt a single-session mindset; that is, we must rethink how we believe change occurs (Hoyt, 2024). As scientists and clinicians, we might assume that change can only occur across a long period of time. However, inherent in the SSI model is the assumption that change is always possible, even in just one session (Hoyt et al., 2020). A single-session mindset encompasses three core ideas: (1) meaningful, lasting change can occur in a single session of support (Campbell, 2012; Schleider & Weisz, 2017), (2) change can and is expected to occur in the moment ("Parkinson's Law"; Appelbaum, 1975), and (3) a person already has what it takes to enact meaningful change (Dryden, 2020). While longer interventions are an integral part of mental health treatments, it is essential for scientists and clinicians to remain optimistic and confident that many people struggling with mental health issues can experience meaningful, lasting change after just a single session. SSI strengths and limitations. Self-guided, digital SSIs can expand access to care, providing evidence-based skills and support to thousands of people who would otherwise receive no care at all. Single-session interventions can also be embedded into diverse systems, serving as "just in time interventions," meeting hard-to-reach clients, anytime, anywhere, and nudging them along their journey (Cohen et al., 2023; Wang & Miller, 2020). However, self-guided, digital SSIs are not meant to replace traditional forms of psychotherapy-we still need in-person multisession interventions delivered by skilled therapists (Schleider, Dobias, et al., 2021). Therefore, as you embark on the journey of developing an SSI, keep in mind that the intervention you are developing is not the be all and end all treatment. Rather, these SSIs can create small psychological changes, and these small psychological changes can pave the way for bigger changes to come (Schleider & Weisz, 2017).

Small but mighty changes. Practically, this means that you will not be able to deliver all the content from a multi-week evidence-based treatment via a self-guided, digital SSI. You will have to make difficult decisions about the skills you wish to teach and the content you will have to shelf for a different time, and that is okay. Although this may feel disappointing, it is an opportunity to home in on the most potent aspects of your intervention. The good news is that you can teach a mass amount of people a few core skills that might open the door to new perspectives, behaviors, and treatments.

Collaboration: Assembling the right team to reach your population of interest

Identifying an audience. After cultivating a single-session mindset, you must assemble your SSI development team. The first step in assembling your team is to specify your audience, the population you are trying to reach. For example, an SSI targeting middle school kids with social anxiety needs a different design team than an SSI targeting healthcare trainees experiencing burnout. Defining the context and the problem will help you determine the expertise you need to join your SSI development team.

Assembling your team.

Your SSI development team should include people with expertise in the treatment of the target problem (e.g., social anxiety) and knowledge about the target context (e.g., middle school). In our experience, it is helpful to include team members with different levels of education (e.g., scientists, graduate students, research assistants), professional roles (e.g., middle school teachers and/or counselors in addition to scientists), and even members who are a part of your target audience (e.g., middle school aged youth who experience social anxiety).

Co-design with youth (e.g., middle school aged youth who experience social anxiety) involves collaboration with members of your target audience to establish their priorities and assess proposed features. Co-design may be the key to an intervention succeeding, because tailoring digital interventions to youth characteristics and preferences enhances their acceptance, engagement, and efficacy (Baker et al., 2015; Baumel et al., 2019; Stawarz et al., 2018). There are a variety of ways to engage in co-design with youth. For example, you may invite members of your target audience to join an advisory council or conduct focus groups that inform the recruitment or marketing materials for your intervention. When engaging in co-design, it is important to consider how all stakeholders can be involved; thus, exploring different co-design methodologies can guide you in the co-design process, helping you consider factors such as the quality of relationship building, trust, confidentiality, and power dynamics (for a review of codesign in youth mental health, see Chinsen et al., 2025). Regardless of who you choose to design the intervention with, be prepared to go through many iterations of your program and remain open to change as you receive new input from various stakeholders. We recognize this can be a frustrating process that requires perseverance, communication skills, and patience. Remember, the best SSIs have been piloted and revised multiple times.

Dissemination and implementation partners.

A final and critical component of assembling your design team is planning for dissemination and implementation. Too often, we design digital interventions that remain inaccessible to youth because we face implementation challenges after we have already developed the intervention. Ideally, the people who will help your SSI reach its intended audience should be involved in the design process from the beginning. The movie cliché, "If you build it, they will come," does not apply to online interventions if no one knows they exist. You must think carefully about where you want your SSI to live to maximize its long-term impact and accessibility. For instance, for an SSI targeting middle school kids coping with social anxiety, this may be the school district that plans to feature the SSI on school computers and tablets. For an SSI targeting healthcare trainees struggling with burnout, this may be the hospital system that plans to include the SSI in yearly required training activities. While we recommend finding implementation partners as soon as possible so you can include them in your design process, if you are not able to locate such partners, you can adapt and refine your SSI for different implementation settings after development.

Adaptation: Selecting an evidence-based treatment and standalone component

During this phase, you will identify the core content of your SSI. To do so, we suggest a set of steps that will seem linear in how we present them but, in truth, often occur in different orders or in an iterative fashion depending on the project. These steps include selecting an evidence-based treatment, selecting a standalone component, and developing your theory of change.

Selecting an evidence-based treatment.

It can feel overwhelming to figure out where to begin when drafting SSI content. We propose that you start by selecting an evidence-based treatment to adapt. The evidence-based treatment you select should fit both the context and the target problem of your population (Chorpita et al., 2005). Some evidence-based treatments are readily adaptable for online platforms, and some are not. Evidence-based treatments that may be difficult to adapt for an online platform are those that require in-person interaction, rely heavily on the therapeutic alliance as the mechanism of change, or focus on multiple or complex targets simultaneously that may be difficult to teach online. Evidence-based treatments that are well-suited to adaptation are those that include discrete, digestible components that maintain their potency when delivered alone (Embry & Biglan, 2008). Selecting a standalone component. Next, you will conduct a literature review to identify standalone components of your selected evidence-based treatment. A standalone component, or "evidence-based kernel," is the smallest part of an intervention that still produces the desired outcome (Embry & Biglan, 2008). Timeout as a means of behavior change in children is a famous example of a standalone component that is prevalent in popular culture and in evidencebased treatments to improve child wellbeing (Embry & Biglan, 2008). To identify your standalone components, you should search for empirical dismantling studies, component studies, or microtrials of the evidence-based treatment you selected (Leijten et al., 2021). These types of studies will allow you to determine which components of your selected evidence-based treatment retain their potency when delivered alone.

Once you have identified the standalone components of your evidence-based treatment, you will choose one standalone component that will be the focus of your SSI. This decision is not always straightforward. To start, you should assess which standalone component is best suited to the population you aim to help and the online setting where your SSI will live.

Additional literature review, consultation with implementation partners, and co-design with your target population should inform this selection. Further review of the literature may highlight one standalone component with specific evidence showing its fit for your population of interest.

Consultation with implementation partners may reveal that one standalone component fits the partner's needs or market research. Co-design with your population of interest may generate enthusiasm for one standalone component and produce many ideas for bringing it to life online.

Finally, single-session intervention developers may take an empirical approach to selecting one standalone component. Teams may develop a prototype SSI for each candidate component then compare the prototypes in a pilot trial.

Developing your theory of change. Your theory of change explains how and why you believe your SSI will work, and it helps guide your plans for evaluating the impact of your SSI (Weiss, 1995). The theory of change model often includes five parts: inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact. Inputs refer to the resources necessary for the project (e.g., funding, staff).

Activities refer to what happens during the intervention itself (e.g., teaching of the standalone component). Outputs refer to the short-term measurable aspects of the intervention (e.g., user engagement or pre-post symptom measures). Outcomes refer to the longer-term measurable effects of the intervention (e.g., sustained symptom improvement over months or years). Impact refers to the grandest aspirations of the project and is often not measurable in the scope of the project (e.g., prevention of major depression in adolescence).

Applying theory of change to your project often starts at the end, with the impact you seek, and works backward (Taplin et al., 2013). The goal is to map out the detailed process by which you believe your SSI will work. Your team builds your project out of causal chains that include specific, measurable indicators of success at each step. This process aims to strengthen study design and the effectiveness of your SSI by challenging the project team to back up their claims and hopes with evidence. This process may also help you catch problems with your project early by emphasizing planning and monitoring of indicators.

Design: Creating the content of your intervention

With your evidence-based treatment, standalone component, and theory of change in hand, you are ready to draft the content of your intervention. Our interventions include four key parts: psychoeducation, skill building, personalized action plan, and back-up plan (see Table 1).

Psychoeducation. We begin our SSIs by orienting our audience. We describe the importance of our intervention, providing background about our topic to increase motivation to interact with a digital program. To humanize our program, we might also introduce ourselves as scientists, describe the mission of the project, and enlist their help in the present study. We focus on "hooking" our audience with key, science-backed facts about the topic. To foreshadow what they will learn, we may also provide them with a quick outline of the program ahead.

Skill building.

We spend the majority of SSI time teaching the building blocks to implement our standalone component. We usually frame the standalone component as a "skill."

Because we only have one session with participants, rather than trying to help them master a skill, we focus on building skill "readiness" by breaking a skill into small, achievable steps. Take, for example, cognitive restructuring. Cognitive restructuring helps participants challenge negative automatic thoughts. To create readiness to challenge negative automatic thoughts, we need to teach participants the importance of pausing when they are having negative emotions, the ability to notice the link between their thoughts, emotions, and behavior, and the capacity to identify negative automatic thoughts. We advocate working hard to "hold your participants' hand" throughout this process by illustrating the small, sequential steps of a skill to help them feel empowered for skill implementation in daily life.

Personalized action plan.

By the end of the intervention, we want participants to feel capable of applying the new skill they have learned. We achieve this through the careful creation of a "personalized action plan," a series of steps that pinpoint how participants can implement new skills into their lives. The action plan can be a step-by-step plan that is emailed to participants. It can be personalized by giving participants choices about what they want to remember, what challenges they struggle with, and how they wish to overcome those challenges.

We find that it is useful to email participants their personalized action plans as a reminder of what they learned.

Back-up plans.

Equally as important as the action plan is the back-up plan, that is, what participants will do if they cannot implement a skill or if the skill did not achieve their desired result. To help prevent participants from feeling hopeless and disappointed, we help them create a back-up plan to set up the expectation that change is not easy and that challenges are a normal part of the journey. The previous sections focused on the content of your digital SSI and provided an outline for how to organize that content. In other words, we have offered guidance for what should go in your digital SSI. In this section, we offer guidance for how to present that content in a way that increases online engagement and learning.

The B.E.S.T.T. principles are a theory-guided approach that uses findings from social and clinical psychology to make digital SSIs more effective for youth (see Table 2; Schleider, Dobias, et al., 2020). Let's take a closer look at these 5 principles. First, at the beginning of many of our SSIs we use brain science, or science-backed information, to help normalize challenges participants face and provide validity for the program we have created, as demonstrated in research on the usefulness of psychoeducation for improving attitudes towards mental health struggles (Nussey et al., 2013). By grounding our program in science, we help improve buy-in and motivate participants to continue. Second, instead of "teaching" participants skills and seeing ourselves as the experts, we align ourselves with frameworks of participatory action research to elevate participants through empowerment, encouraging them to see themselves as the experts on their lives (Baum et al., 2006). Third, we use saying-is-believing techniques, drawing from social psychological theory to give participants opportunities to provide others with advice, helping them internalize the core messages we wish to teach (Aronson, 1999). Fourth, we use narrative testimonials from peers to demonstrate how the target skill may be implemented in daily life and to leverage the power of story-like endorsements to increase uptake (Hamby et al., 2015).

Finally, we make every effort to keep our SSIs targeted to pointedly and efficiently address the underlying mechanism of change (Gawrysiak et al., 2009). To sustain people's attention, we make sure our digital SSIs are brief, simple, and self-explanatory. We focus on using easy to understand language, and we concentrate on teaching fewer skills to increase the uptake of this knowledge.

It is important to consider how your SSI can enact your mechanisms of change in the most engaging yet concise and effective way. How you convey information will need to balance interactivity (e.g., using engaging questions and activities instead of using only large blocks of text or information) with the risk of increasing dropout. Some digital mental health researchers have found that digital activities that take longer to complete may increase dropout or suppress engagement (Boucher & Raiker, 2024). Simultaneously, other researchers have found that digital users prefer visual learning and interactive activities instead of passively learning through reading large amounts of text (Ludlow et al., 2023). Tailor your SSI's design to the specific preferences of your population of interest and consider which formats for information delivery will engage them most effectively.

The ABC Project Example: Iteratively adapting behavioral activation into a digital singlesession intervention

As an illustration of how the five phases of digital SSI development from multi-session protocols have unfolded in practice, we describe the design process for The ABC Project, or Action Brings Change: a self-guided digital SSI that condenses behavioral activation into a brief, youth-centered experience (Schleider et al., 2019, OSF). We guide the reader through the various stages of SSI development: preparation, collaboration, adaptation, design, and engagement.

Development of ABC began in 2018; the program (which is now freely accessible for public use at http://www.tryprojectyes.org/lsmh) has now been evaluated through an open-access online platform, tested in a large randomized trial, culturally adapted with bilingual youth and community partners for deployment across a city, repackaged and condensed for delivery within social media, and experimentally varied in length. Its history illustrates that preparation, collaboration, adaptation, design, and engagement often unfold over time and iteratively recur, with findings from each stage informing subsequent decisions about content and delivery. Preparation. ABC program development began with a question about scope: What facet of existing, multi-session behavioral activation treatments could be meaningfully conveyed within a single, self-guided session? Behavioral activation is grounded in the notion that depressive symptoms are maintained by behavioral withdrawal from values-aligned activities that produce accomplishment, connection with others, pleasure, and other positively reinforcing experiences. Importantly, the feasibility of a single-session behavioral activation approach had already been established; prior randomized trials of therapist-delivered SSIs had already found that structured, single-session behavioral activation interventions could reduce depression and increase environmental reward in older adolescents (Armento et al., 2012; Gawrysiak et al., 2009). Existing adaptations of BA for a single-session format drew from the Brief Behavioral Activation Treatment for Depression (BATD; Lejuez et al., 2001), which uses values assessment and activity planning to increase engagement with reinforcing experiences. As such, we drew from SSI-adapted BA protocols and BATD to inform content for ABC.

We did not attempt to reproduce the entirety of BATD within ABC; this would have been impossible, given the repeated reflections on past-week changes in activity engagement built into the original protocol's consecutive sessions. Instead, we focused on a narrow, actionable lesson from BATD that had been the focus of prior therapist-led single-session BA protocols: Defining and taking small, personally meaningful action may begin to shift your mood in a positive direction, even when you do not yet feel intrinsically motivated or hopeful that change is possible. Our goal was to help youth understand this principle, identify a positively reinforcing activity connected to their personal values, and create a concrete plan for trying it. Per the singlesession mindset frame, our aim was to ensure that ABC was worthwhile even without unpacking or addressing every determinant of the adolescent user's distress.

Collaboration. Our initial ABC development team brought together expertise in adolescent depression, behavioral activation theory and intervention, clinical practice, social psychology, and digital SSI design. Additionally, adolescent and young adult advisors played an important role in reviewing and revising early ABC prototypes. Notably, these formative activities were conducted as informal advisory sessions, not as a research study, and all advisors were compensated for their time. Our team has since begun leveraging more robust youthpartnered human-centered design methods in digital SSI development processes. Youth advisors provided feedback on the early intervention's content, clarity, length, pacing, and design. They also reviewed candidate videos (for use within ABC's built-in 'behavioral experiment', where adolescents observe how a positive action can positively shape their mood) which they rated for their effectiveness as positive mood inductions; they also shared qualitative feedback to help us gauge the acceptability and usability of early versions. This informal advisory feedback helped us identify psychoeducation explanations that were unnecessarily long or complex, examples that felt removed from young people's lives, and activity options that felt unrealistic.

Youth involvement from the start of ABC development also shaped its built-in emphasis on promoting adolescent autonomy. Adolescents often have limited control over whether, when, and how they receive mental health support (Schleider & Fox, 2025). By virtue of their design, digital SSIs can help increase adolescents' control over mental health support-seeking by enabling direct access independently, often anonymously, and precisely when and where they feel motivated to use it. Early feedback on ABC reinforced the importance of promoting adolescent autonomy through the program's activities and structure. As such, in addition to designing and evaluating ABC as a free, private, digital tool that any adolescent can use, we structured ABC to support youth in choosing the values, activities, and goals on which they most wanted to focus. That is, users of ABC have full control over the content of their action plan. Our decision to create youth-centered onramps to ABC has directly influenced our design of other SSIs for adolescent mental health concerns (Schleider, 2023).

Over the next few years, we began to evaluate ABC formally in a variety of settings. As such, our team of collaborators expanded. In a social media-based deployment of ABC, we partnered with a digital mental health nonprofit, Koko, whose team contributed expertise in embedding very brief mental health activities within digital platforms where people were already seeking support (Dobias et al., 2022). Separately, we worked closely with colleagues at University of Texas Teen Health, San Antonio youth leaders, schools, community organizations, and local health partners later guided its cultural and linguistic adaptation of ABC for young people in South Texas (Shroff et al., 2023). These collaborations shaped the intervention's content, language options, dissemination pathways, and uptake by youth across diverse contexts.

Adaptation. In keeping with multisession BA protocols, the standalone intervention component selected for ABC was values-aligned activity engagement. The SSI's theory of change combined behavioral activation (specific to this digital SSI) with self-determination theory (which has been theorized to apply across multiple digital SSIs; Schleider & Beidas, 2022). From a behavioral activation perspective, engaging in meaningful activities creates opportunities for pleasure, accomplishment, social connection, and positive reinforcement. From a self-determination theory perspective, motivation and wellbeing are supported when people experience autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000). ABC supports autonomy by allowing participants to select personally meaningful goals, competence by helping them translate those goals into manageable actions and anticipate barriers, and relatedness through peer narratives and opportunities to share advice with others.

This theory of change also guided later adaptations. When shortening ABC, we sought to preserve the experiences most central to the intervention's proposed effects: understanding that action can influence mood, choosing an activity that matters personally, developing a feasible plan, and preparing for likely obstacles. This provided a principled basis for deciding which material could be condensed without removing the intervention's evidence-based core.

Design.

The original version of ABC was designed to take approximately 20 to 30 minutes. Adolescents learned how behavior can directly influence mood (and vice versa), identified life areas that provided or had previously provided meaning and enjoyment, and devised a concrete action plan to engage in activities directly linked to those values. They were then guided to identify an anticipated obstacle to using their action plan, along with a strategy (self-talk) to help them overcome that obstacle when it emerged (Schleider et al., 2020). This sequence incorporated the four design components described above: psychoeducation, skill building, a personalized action plan, and a back-up plan. ABC also incorporated the B.E.S.T.T.

principles through science-based explanations, participant choice and empowerment, peer narratives, opportunities to offer advice at the end of the program to future users struggling with depression and mood difficulties, and a streamlined focus on a single actionable skill.

Engagement and optimization. ABC was first evaluated in a naturalistic open trial as one of three SSIs offered through Project YES, a free, anonymous platform that allowed youth to choose which intervention they wanted to complete (Schleider et al., 2020).

Between September 2019 and March 2020, 694 youth accessed Project YES via social media advertisements; among the 228 youth who selected ABC (among the three different SSI options), 75 completed it, corresponding to a 37.1% completion rate among those who began. ABC completers rated the program as highly acceptable and reported immediate increases in perceived agency and reductions in hopelessness. While the study was uncontrolled and could not determine causal intervention effects, it offered preliminary evidence for our theory of intervention change (i.e., ABC users reported shifts in autonomy) and suggested that high-symptom youth would independently select and engage with a digital BA SSI, outside the context of a paid research study (Schleider et al., 2020).

Subsequently, we evaluated ABC more rigorously within a nationwide randomized trial involving 2,452 adolescents ages 13 to 16 with elevated depressive symptoms (Schleider et al., 2022). Adolescents, who were recruited through Instagram advertisements in late 2020 and early 2021, were randomly assigned to receive ABC, a growth mindset digital SSI, or a structurally similar supportive control. Relative to the control condition, ABC led to greater reductions in depression symptoms at three months, with a small between-group effect (d = 0.18). It also significantly reduced hopelessness compared to the control, both immediately and at three months, and increased perceived agency immediately post-program. Nearly 89% of adolescents assigned to ABC completed it, and youth rated it highly across acceptability domains. These findings provided robust evidence that ABC could produce both immediate changes in proximal targets and longer-term reductions in depression symptoms in adolescents experiencing depressive symptoms.

We also had the opportunity to explore whether ABC could be adapted to engage youth across contexts and cultures. Through a partnership with University of Texas Teen Health, their Youth Advisory Board, and the City of San Antonio Metropolitan Health District, we culturally and linguistically adapted ABC to reflect the experiences and needs of youth in South Texas.

Fourteen San Antonio youth advisors contributed 33 narratives about emotional distress and coping, which were iteratively revised and incorporated directly into ABC (along with the two other digital SSIs in Project YES at the time) in both written and audio formats. Other design features and activities within ABC stayed consistent with the original version. All three digital SSIs on Project YES were then translated into Spanish, and schools, clinics, and community organizations supported local dissemination (Shroff et al., 2023). Between April and December 2021, 1,801 San Antonio youth began Project YES and 894 completed one of its 30-minute SSIs, yielding an overall completion rate of 49.6%. Among the 343 youth who began ABC in English, 206, or 60.1%, completed it; completion rates were similar among Spanish-speaking youth who initiated ABC (11/19 or 58%). ABC completers reported immediate improvements in agency, hopelessness, and self-hate, with within-person effect sizes ranging from d = 0.25 to 0.37.

Results suggested that cultural and contextual adaptation of ABC seemed feasible-per relatively high completion rates for a naturalistic, uncompensated evaluation-while preserving the program's evidence-based kernel-per the replicated shifts in near-term outcomes of interest.

Across all three ABC evaluations noted above, qualitative youth feedback for improving the program was strikingly consistent. Specifically, youth often shared that the SSI felt too long and burdensome (e.g., writing exercises demanded motivation that many users lacked) and requested a briefer version that would be easier to complete in moments of distress (McDanal et al., 2021). To address these concerns, we partnered with Koko to condense ABC into a 5-to 8minute minicourse embedded within Tumblr. Longer psychoeducation-related explanations and writing activities were markedly reduced and replaced with more interactions using multiplechoice response options, while the core sequence of values selection, action planning, and obstacle planning remained intact (Dobias et al., 2022). In a naturalistic deployment within a large social media platform (Tumblr) across 12 months, the abbreviated ABC was initiated 14,434 times and completed 3,679 times. Users who rated the intervention gave it an average of 4.27 out of 5 stars, and completers reported a substantial immediate reduction in hopelessness (dz = 0.81)-an improvement equal or greater in magnitude than those observed in prior studies.

This result raised the possibility that, if their core design features and activities are retained, digital SSIs could be made even briefer-5-8 minutes rather than 20-30-while retaining their clinical impact. An additional randomized trial tested this possibility directly. Kaveladze and colleagues randomly assigned 1,145 adults experiencing mental health difficulties to 15-, 9-, 5-, or 3-minute versions of ABC. The four versions did not differ significantly in their effects on depression eight weeks later, but shorter versions were completed at substantially higher rates than longer ones (Kaveladze et al., 2026). Because the study involved adults, it could not gauge whether ABC duration related to adolescents' response to the program. However, results do suggest that digital SSI length is not necessarily a reliable indicator of potency, so long as shorter versions preserve the theory-driven components expected to produce change. This work provides useful next steps for continued refinement and improvement of ABC through iterative collaboration and evaluation moving ahead.

Taken together, ABC's evolution illustrates how the stages of digital SSI development operate iteratively, through preparation, collaboration, adaptation, and design. Engagement and clinical outcome data have prompted us to revisit earlier phases to make ABC more accessible, culturally relevant, and responsive to real-world youth use, a cycle of adaptation and testing that will continue as new contexts emerge.

Limitations and Future Directions

While we offer this guide with enthusiasm, we recognize its limitations. First, digital SSIs cannot replace psychotherapy, and long-term, face-to-face interventions are still needed for complex clinical problems. Digital SSIs are intended to complement psychotherapy and extend the reach of our strongest interventions to the people who might otherwise receive no effective mental healthcare at all. Second, developing an SSI according to this guide requires that there be an existing evidence-based treatment for the target problem. This requirement limits this guide's utility for clinical issues that have a less robust evidence base. For an example of how to develop a digital SSI for one such issue, see our digital SSI for self-injury (Dobias et al., 2021). Third, while we touch on the importance of rigorous dissemination and implementation practices, this guide does not adequately address how to deliver your digital SSI in a way that maximizes its effectiveness and reach. We direct the reader to the reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance (RE-AIM) framework, including an application of RE-AIM to digital SSIs (Cohen et al., 2024).
