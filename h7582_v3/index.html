Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. Abstract

Collective action often unfolds in real time, where individuals choose when to commit and thus determine decision order. We study how this timing structure shapes cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma, comparing real-time interaction with simultaneous and assigned-order sequential decisions. Across two experiments (N = 713), real-time interaction produced the highest observed rate of mutual cooperation. Analyses of decision timing suggest that first-mover defection tended to be delayed, making cooperators more likely to move first. Yet the share of participants willing to cooperate first was lower than under assigned order. These opposing effects left first-mover cooperation rates similar across the two conditions. Higher mutual cooperation instead reflected more reliable reciprocation: second movers were more likely to cooperate after observing first-mover cooperation. We then show that a belief-dependent reciprocal preference model can account for these patterns. In the model, voluntarily cooperating first is more demanding than cooperating when assigned to move first. In turn, such a decision can signal stronger cooperative preferences to partners and encourage them to reciprocate. Our findings connect models of endogenous leadership to real-time interaction and highlight its potential to help people coordinate on mutual cooperation.

1 Introduction

Producing mutually beneficial outcomes through costly cooperation is a fundamental challenge for teams, organizations, and societies. Such efforts, often described as collective action, range from organizing informal reading groups to negotiating international treaties. Although collective action can generate benefits for all members, individuals may enjoy those benefits regardless of their contribution and therefore have an incentive to withhold cooperation (De Dreu et al. 2024 , Kollock 1998 , Olson 1974 , Ostrom 2015).

Decades of behavioral research have nonetheless shown that a substantial share of people are conditional cooperators, who are willing to incur the cost of cooperation if others do the same (Bicchieri and Xiao 2009 , Blanco et al. 2014 , Cartwright and Lovett 2014 , Eichenseer and Moser 2020 , Fischbacher et al. 2001 , Frey and Meier 2004). The prevalence of such individuals means that collective outcomes depend critically on how cooperation is initiated. Early cooperation begets further cooperation, whereas early defection may discourage it. Indeed, individuals' beliefs about others' cooperation matter when decisions are made simultaneously (Mori et al. 2024 , Santos et al. 2021 , Struwe et al. 2024 , Vives and FeldmanHall 2018), and early movers' decisions largely shape collective outcomes when decisions are made sequentially (Clark and Sefton 2001 , Gächter et al. 2010 , Gächter and Renner 2018 , Malthouse et al. 2026 , Romano and Yildirim 2001 , Steiger and Zultan 2014).

In many real-world settings, however, collective action fits neither simultaneous nor fixed-order sequential decisions. Instead, it unfolds in real time, where individuals can decide the timing of their deci-sions. For example, in the context of international climate negotiations, countries could submit emissionsreduction pledges in the run-up to the 2015 Paris Agreement at any time before a deadline (Falkner 2016 , Hale 2020). Early public pledges by the EU, the US, and China helped build political momentum toward a broader agreement (Schreurs 2016), illustrating how early commitments may mobilize broader participation. Two features of real-time settings are particularly important. First, individuals are free to choose when to act. Second, once decisions are made, they become public and are not reversible. These features together make the decision order endogenous and turn decision timing into a potentially strategic variable. Individuals may act early to influence others' decisions, wait to observe others' moves, or ignore timing altogether.

Despite the strategic significance of decision timing in real-time settings, systematic investigations of its dynamics remain limited. Whether real-time interaction facilitates cooperation is not obvious a priori. Individuals may simply act once they have decided, without treating timing as a strategic choice. If they do use timing strategically, early movers may be disproportionately cooperative because defectors would have nothing to gain from committing early and can instead wait to observe others' actions. The option to wait in order to learn from others' actions is a familiar source of strategic delay (Chamley and Gale 1994, Gul and Lundholm 1995). Yet waiting is also attractive to those willing to cooperate, who risk being left unreciprocated if they act first. Real-time interaction may therefore enable cooperative leadership while also allowing everyone to wait for someone else to take the first step. Whether voluntary leadership emerges and whether others respond cooperatively remain open questions.

Here, we examine these questions using one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma (PD) games under three timing structures: simultaneous (SM), exogenously sequential (SQ), and real-time (RT). In SM, both players make decisions simultaneously. In SQ, players are exogenously assigned to be first or second movers and decide in that order. In RT, both players share a continuous time window (60 seconds) in which they can make an irreversible decision at any moment and that decision is immediately revealed to the partner. The player who commits earlier becomes the first mover, rendering the decision order endogenous.

Across two experiments, we found that real-time interaction yielded the highest observed rate of mutual cooperation; in the main experiment, nearly two-thirds of pairs cooperated in RT, compared with roughly half in SQ and less than a third in SM. This advantage, however, was not accompanied by a higher first-mover cooperation rate: first-mover cooperation rates were nearly identical between RT and SQ. Reconstructing potential first-mover decisions from the timing data suggests two countervailing effects that (1) cooperators tend to act earlier than defectors, making them more likely to become first movers; (2) yet the estimated share willing to cooperate first in RT is lower than that among assigned first movers in SQ. RT's advantage in promoting mutual cooperation was instead driven by second movers, who reciprocated first-mover cooperation more reliably when the first move was voluntary rather than assigned.

To interpret these findings, we develop an endogenous timing variant of the Prisoner's Dilemma with belief-dependent reciprocal preferences. In the model, committing early to cooperation entails forgoing the option to wait and observe the partner's decision. This makes first-mover cooperation more selective under endogenous than under assigned order, and therefore more informative about the first mover's reciprocal preference. Observing such voluntary early cooperation provides stronger grounds for inferring that the partner has a stronger preference for reciprocity, which increases the second mover's willingness to respond in kind. The model accounts for both the reluctance to initiate cooperation and the stronger willingness to reciprocate it. Neither the self-interested benchmark nor an outcome-based reciprocity model explains both patterns.

Our paper contributes to research on voluntary leadership and endogenous move order in social dilemmas (Abe et al. 2014 , Arbak and Villeval 2013 , Cappelen et al. 2016 , Feess et al. 2025 , Güth et al. 2007 , Préget et al. 2016 , Rivas and Sutter 2011). We share this intuition, but examine a symmetric environment in which both players can make a commitment at any moment within a shared time window. In this real-time setting, the order of play emerges from participants' timing decisions, and voluntary first-mover cooperation is followed by stronger reciprocation than cooperation under assigned order. Our theoretical analyses connect these behavioral patterns to the logic of endogenous timing models, in which players strategically choose when to act and draw inferences about their partners' preferences from voluntary cooperation. Our findings also connect to research on real-time collective decision making, in which decision order shapes how information is transmitted and aggregated (Kuroda et al. 2025 , Tump et al. 2020 , 2022). 2 Experimental Design and Procedures

We conducted two preregistered behavioral experiments using one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma (PD) games. The pilot experiment compared real-time (RT) and simultaneous (SM) interaction, whereas the main experiment added an exogenously sequential (SQ) condition. In the main text, we report results from the main experiment (N = 561). Results from the pilot experiment (N = 152) are reported in Online Appendix OA.3.

2.1 Game and Timing Structure

Players began with an endowment of 100 monetary units (MUs), and were told that they and their partner were engaged in a joint project. Each player could either invest (Cooperate; C) their 100 MUs or keep (Defect; D) them. If a player invested 100 MUs, the amount was multiplied by 1.5, yielding 100 MUs for the partner and returning 50 MUs to the investing player. Table 1 shows the resulting payoff matrix. We manipulated the temporal structure of interaction in a between-subjects design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: real-time (RT), simultaneous (SM), and sequential (SQ) (Figure 1). In the RT condition, pairs shared a 60-second window during which each player could choose C or D at any moment. Once a participant made a decision within this window, it was immediately communicated to the partner and could not be revised. Participants were instructed that if neither participant made a decision within 60 seconds, both would be prompted to decide simultaneously at the end of the time limit without observing the partner's choice. In our experiments, however, at least one participant made a decision within the 60-second window in every pair.

In the SM condition, the interface and time window were identical to RT, but no decisions were revealed during the 60 seconds. Participants' choices were collected independently and revealed only after the time limit elapsed, rendering the interaction equivalent to a simultaneous PD. In the SQ condition, the experimenter exogenously assigned first-and second-mover roles. The first mover had an initial 60-second window to decide. Their choice was revealed to the second mover only after this window had elapsed, and the second mover then had another 60 seconds to decide.

Crucially, these different time structures create distinct decision-making positions (Figure 1). In SM, all players are in identical positions: they form beliefs about their partners and decide independently, as no player can observe the partner's decision before acting. In contrast, pairs in SQ and RT have a decision order. In SQ, this order is exogenously assigned by the experimenter, creating "assigned" first and second movers. In RT, because players choose when to commit, the decision order emerges endogenously, resulting in "voluntary" first and second movers.

To ensure that decision timing was not driven by a desire simply to finish the experiment sooner, participants in all conditions had to wait until the full decision window had elapsed before proceeding, even if decisions had been completed sooner.

2.2 Participants and Procedures

Across the two experiments, the final sample comprised 713 US-resident, English-speaking participants recruited online through Prolific Academic (main N = 561; pilot N = 152). The pilot experiment was conducted on January 31-February 1, 2023, and the main experiment on March 20, 2024. Participants were explicitly informed that they were paired with another real human participant taking part in the experiment at the same time, and all interactions were anonymous. Before playing the PD, participants received instructions specific to each condition and completed a comprehension check. Only those who answered all questions correctly proceeded to the PD. We applied the preregistered completion criteria described in Appendix A.1 and Online Appendix OA.3.1.

After the PD, participants completed additional measures of Social Value Orientation (SVO), risk attitudes, and ambiguity attitudes, as well as a post-experimental questionnaire. SVO was elicited using the Slider Measure (Murphy et al. 2011), in which participants made a series of choices about how to allocate money between themselves and another participant, allowing us to quantify the extent to which individuals prioritize their own payoffs versus those of others. The full list of additional tasks and questionnaire measures is reported in Appendix A and Online Appendix OA.1; regression analyses relating these measures to behavior in the PD game are reported in Online Appendices OA.3 and OA.2.2 for the pilot and main experiments, respectively.

3.3 Second-Mover Reciprocity

We next examine second movers, who reciprocated first-mover cooperation more reliably in RT than in SQ (93.8% vs. 74.6%; Fig. 2B-C).

In principle, the difference in reciprocity could reflect both within-individual changes and betweenindividual compositional differences. Under the within-individual account, the same person reciprocates cooperation more readily when acting as a voluntary second mover in RT than as an assigned second mover in SQ. Under the between-individual (compositional) account, more prosocial individuals disproportionately end up as second movers in RT through self-selection.

To help distinguish these accounts, we examined participants' SVO. Among second movers who observed first-mover cooperation (RT, n=65; SQ, n=63), SVO scores did not differ significantly between RT and SQ (two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, p = .255; Fig. 4B, lower panel). Figure 4B's upper panel shows the estimated probability of reciprocal cooperation as a function of the second mover's own SVO angle, separately in each condition (locally smoothed logistic regression; see caption for details). The curves suggest higher reciprocation in RT than in SQ, especially at lower SVO angles, with high reciprocation in RT even among less prosocial participants (Fig. 4B, upper panel). Similarly, when we categorized second movers using conventional SVO categories, reciprocation was significantly higher in RT among individualistic participants (RT-SQ difference: 46.3 percentage points, 95% CI [14.4, 67.9]), whereas the difference among prosocial participants was not significant (RT-SQ difference: 7.6 percentage points, 95% CI [-4.9, 21.3]; see Appendix C for details). Furthermore, after standardizing the two groups to a common SVO distribution, conditional cooperation remained 17.2 percentage points higher in RT than in SQ (95% bootstrap CI [5.4, 29.0]; see Appendix C.2 for details). Thus, most of the reciprocity gap remained after accounting for differences in measured SVO. These patterns are consistent with a within-individual increase in the propensity to reciprocate as a second mover.

In summary, real-time interaction yielded the highest observed rate of mutual cooperation despite a lower estimated share of potential first-mover cooperators than under the assigned order. Potential cooperators tended to act early, while many potential defectors delayed, and voluntary first-mover cooperation was reciprocated more often than assigned first-mover cooperation. These patterns suggest that participants use the flexibility of real-time interaction strategically, both in choosing when to act and in responding to their partners. To understand how these behaviors fit together, we develop a game-theoretic model of how the option to wait shapes the incentives to initiate and reciprocate cooperation (full derivations in Appendix D and Online Appendix OA.4).

4 A Theoretical Interpretation

4.1 Model

We begin with a two-stage variant of the Prisoner's Dilemma (the "endogenous-order game") to approximate real-time interaction. In Stage 1, both players simultaneously choose Cooperate (C), Defect (D), or Wait (W). If exactly one player chooses C or D, that action is implemented. If both do, only one of their actions is randomly implemented, while the other is discarded without being revealed. In either case, the other player observes the implemented action and then chooses C or D in Stage 2. If both players wait, they choose C or D simultaneously in Stage 2. ³ We compare this two-stage endogenous-order game with an exogenous-order game corresponding to the SQ condition, in which players are assigned to move first or second. Section 4.3 shows that the comparison with the exogenous-order game is unchanged with more stages or in continuous time.

4.2 Equilibrium Predictions

We can distinguish two kinds of cooperation in both exogenous-order and endogenous-order games: initiating cooperation before observing the partner's action, and reciprocating cooperation after observing the partner's cooperation. Under exogenous order, these choices are made by the assigned first and second movers, respectively. Under endogenous order, these roles emerge from the players' timing decisions.

A crucial observation about the endogenous-order game is that waiting is at least as good as committing to defection in Stage 1 (Proposition 1). Intuitively, this is because early defection can preempt the partner's cooperation, whereas waiting does not. Consider the Stage 1 choices of a player and their partner. Suppose that the partner chooses C. Choosing D can preempt the partner's C and lead to mutual defection, whereas choosing W allows the player to respond with either C or D in Stage 2, both of which yield higher utility. If the partner chooses 𝐷, either choice leads to mutual defection. If the partner waits, committing to 𝐷 yields 𝑃, and waiting guarantees at least 𝑃 because the player can still defect in Stage 2. Thus, waiting is strictly preferable whenever the partner may initiate cooperation, ruling out early defection in endogenous-order equilibria with positive initiation.

In each game, we focus on equilibria characterized by two thresholds, one for initiating cooperation and one for reciprocating it. 5 Discussion

In teams, organizations, and societies, collective action often unfolds in real time, allowing people to make publicly observable commitments at any moment. How does this freedom shape leadership and reciprocity among conditional cooperators? We examined this question through behavioral experiments that directly manipulated the timing structure of the Prisoner's Dilemma. Real-time interaction yielded the highest observed rate of mutual cooperation, with almost identical observed first-mover cooperation rates in RT and SQ but more reliable second-mover reciprocation in RT. Estimates based on decision times suggest that potential cooperators tended to act earlier than potential defectors and were therefore more likely to become first movers, offsetting the lower estimated share willing to cooperate first in RT than in SQ. An endogenous timing model with belief-dependent reciprocal preferences provides a coherent account of these initiation and reciprocation patterns. According to the model, the option to wait makes initiating cooperation more demanding, so voluntary cooperation signals stronger reciprocal preferences and encourages others to reciprocate.

Our findings extend the connection between voluntary leadership and reciprocal cooperation studied by Feess et al. (2025). The shared mechanism is that choosing to lead reveals a willingness to forgo the advantages of responding to another player's action. This differs from leading by example as studied by Hermalin (1998) and Potters et al. (2005 Potters et al. ( , 2007)), where moving first conveys information about the value of cooperation rather than about the leader's preferences. Here, this choice emerges in a symmetric environment where neither player is given authority to determine the order of play. The theoretical extension shows that, under the maintained assumptions, the resulting selection and reciprocation mechanism survives the introduction of additional decision opportunities and continuous time. Although equilibrium initiation may occur at different times, those times convey the same mean reciprocal preference. Our findings also relate to recent work on collective problem solving in real time (Kuroda et al. 2025 , Tump et al. 2020 , 2022). In these studies, individuals face cognitive tasks in which each decision generates informational externalities for others, and collective outcomes depend on how information cascades unfold over time. The social dilemma studied here involves payoff externalities instead. The option to wait changes the incentives to initiate cooperation and, in the model, what early cooperation reveals about the first mover's preferences.

Our findings complement prior work on cooperation in continuous-time and dynamic contribution settings. In environments where actions can be revised or contributions made incrementally, individuals can respond repeatedly to others' behavior, reducing the risk of unilateral exploitation (Bigoni et al. 2015 , Duffy et al. 2007 , Friedman and Oprea 2012 , Kurzban et al. 2001 , Oprea et al. 2014). By contrast, in our design each participant makes a single, irreversible commitment while still choosing when to make it. In this setting, the model suggests that real-time interaction can support mutual cooperation by making early cooperation more demanding, not less, and therefore more informative about the initiator's reciprocal preference. Late defection also takes on a different meaning. For example, Bigoni et al. (2015) observe defection concentrated near a known deadline in a continuous-time Prisoner's Dilemma, as an end-game effect of repeated interaction; in our one-shot setting, it is instead consistent with waiting to respond to a partner's potential cooperation.

These insights have implications for individuals participating in collective action and for managers who set the rules of interaction among team members. For participants, early commitment may function as a strategic investment that encourages others to reciprocate. For example, early microfinance initiatives such as the Grameen Bank involved accepting the risks of lending without collateral (Armendariz and Morduch 2010 , Cook et al. 2005 , Yunus 2003). Such voluntary risk taking may help elicit reciprocal cooperation. For managers, providing a shared decision window in which members can make voluntary, observable commitments may be one way to support cooperation. The rules governing commitment timing may matter alongside material incentives.

Finally, we discuss several limitations and directions for future research. First, although our betweensubjects design allowed us to examine responses to different timing structures without explicitly making these differences salient to participants, complementary within-subjects studies could provide a more direct test of the mechanism we propose. Second, while we focused on dyadic settings, many collective action problems involve larger groups. Further work could examine how the trade-off identified in our model extends to these settings, where the option to wait may make voluntary early commitments harder to obtain but more effective in triggering cooperative cascades. Lastly, our model treats the distribution of reciprocal preferences in the population as exogenous. Because games and institutions may shape not only behavior under fixed preferences but also the distribution of preferences itself in the long run (Bohnet et al. 2001 , Lowes et al. 2017), future research could examine whether sustained exposure to real-time interaction changes this distribution.

Given that many individuals have social preferences that support conditional cooperation, a central challenge is how to allow potential cooperators to signal one another and initiate and reciprocate cooperation (Dannenberg and Gallier 2020 , DeCaro et al. 2021 , Ostrom 2000 , Tacchetti et al. 2025). The findings suggest that real-time interaction provides one such temporal structure through which these preferences can be more readily translated into mutual cooperation.
