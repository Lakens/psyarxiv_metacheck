To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 17 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Callous-unemotional (CU) traits, defined by deficient empathy and remorse, characterize a distinct subgroup of antisocial individuals with more severe behavioral problems and unique affective profiles. Two processes implicated in the development of CU traits are hyporeactivity to and inaccurate recognition of others' emotions. Although many studies link CU traits to emotional processing deficits, findings have been inconsistent across age and sex. We conducted multilevel meta-analyses of emotional reactivity and recognition, accounting for age and sex as moderators, to clarify these discrepancies. A four-level meta-analysis indicated CU traits were negatively associated with emotional reactivity to both broad emotional stimuli (r = -.23, p < .0001; k = 106, nES = 402) and distress-specific stimuli (r = -.22, p < .0001; k = 83, nES = 234), with no moderation by age or sex. Similarly, a three-level meta-analysis showed CU traits were negatively associated with recognition accuracy for both broad facial emotions (r = -.09, p < .0001; k = 97, nES = 437) and distress (e.g., fear, sadness; r = -.10, p < .0001; k = 60, nES = 140).

Sample age moderated broad (r = .003, p = .01) and distress (r = .004, p = .04) recognition deficits, such that effects diminished with sample age. These results have important implications for causal theories of CU traits and support emotional hyporeactivity as a core deficit that may cause delays in the development of emotion recognition abilities. Such developmental changes have implications for improving treatments for individuals with elevated CU traits.

Callous-unemotional (CU) traits, captured by the diagnostic specifier, "with Limited Prosocial Emotions (LPE)" (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; World Health Organization, 2018), are characterized by deficits in several affective, interpersonal, and motivational domains theorized to result from atypical conscience development (Frick & Kemp, 2021). Specifically, CU traits are defined by impairments in moral emotions such as guilt and remorse for wrongdoing, deficient empathic concern for others, limited concern about performance in important activities (e.g., school, work), and shallow or constricted affect (Frick et al., 2014). These traits are theorized to represent the affective facet of psychopathy observed in adults (Hare & Neumann, 2008) and their presence denotes a clinically significant subgroup of antisocial individuals across the lifespan (DeBrito et al., 2021).

Antisocial youth with elevated CU traits are distinct from other antisocial individuals in several clinically relevant ways. In particular, children and adolescents with elevated CU traits tend to: (1) exhibit conduct problems earlier in development, often meeting criteria for childhood-onset conduct disorder (i.e., onset before age 10; Frick et al., 2014); (2) engage in more severe, stable, and versatile forms of antisocial behavior, including higher rates of proactive aggression and premeditated violence (Andrade et al., 2015; Ciucci & Baroncelli, 2014; Lawing et al., 2010; Ritchie et al., 2022; Thomson, 2018; Thornton et al., 2013); and (3) show reduced responsivity to traditional treatments for conduct problems in that they often continue to display elevated conduct problems and aggression during and after intervention (see Perlstein et al., 2023 for a meta-analysis). Even when controlling for their associations with more severe behavior problems in adolescence, CU traits uniquely predict worse outcomes in adulthood, such as aggression, criminal behavior, and poor social adjustment (Vaughan et al., 2025). Importantly, individuals with elevated CU traits exhibit distinct cognitive-affective characteristics relative to other antisocial individuals, suggesting unique etiological processes that could be targeted by tailored prevention and treatment approaches (Frick, 2022).

CU Traits and Empathy Development

A callous-disregard or lack of empathy is central to most definitions of CU traits.

Empathy broadly encompasses two major components -affective and cognitive -capturing emotional responsiveness to others' feelings and the capacity to understand and recognize those emotions, respectively (Decety, 2011; Eisenberg et al., 2014). These components have been the focus of a significant amount of developmental research that is important for theoretical models developed to explain CU traits.

Affective Empathy and Emotional Reactivity

Affective empathy is defined by the reaction a person has in response to the emotions of another (de Wied et al., 2010). Affective empathy involves the emotional reactivity (e.g., empathic distress) experienced in response to others' emotions. In research, emotional reactivity has been operationally defined and empirically measured with multiple levels of analysis, including self-and informant-report, behavioral measures of attentional capture, psychophysiological measures of autonomic stress reactivity (e.g., pupil, electrodermal, and neuroendocrine reactivity as measures of arousal), and neural measures of brain activity in socioemotional processing regions (e.g., amygdala).

There is research suggesting that emotional reactivity to others' distress is a basic and reflexive process underlying more complex empathy development. For instance, evidence of affective empathy in the form of emotional reactivity is shown early in development via reflexive crying (Martin & Clark, 1982; Sagi & Hoffman, 1976) and it is theorized that typically developing children have a biological predisposition to experience others' distress cues as aversive, which is an adaptive response that facilitates threat detection (McDonald & Messinger, 2011). Further, increasingly complex forms of emotional reactivity (e.g., grimacing) are shown to develop as infants grow into toddlers (Eisenberg et al., 2014), a time during which children typically display age-related advances in empathic concern that are associated with increased engagement in prosocial helping behaviors (Decety & Jackson, 2004; Hawes & Dadds, 2012; Roth-Hanania et al., 2011). Based on this evidence, it is theorized that humans display an innate capacity for emotional reactivity and affective empathy that then persists and matures across the lifespan (Eisenberg et al., 2014; Roth-Hanania et al., 2011).

Research spanning multiple decades has supported sex differences in emotional development, such that females typically display higher levels of overall empathy starting very early in development (Christov-Moore et al., 2014; Eisenberg et al., 2014). For example, female infants are more likely to engage in and display longer reflexive crying, in addition to showing increased eye contact and attentional orienting to emotionally salient facial and vocal cues (see Christov-Moore et al., 2014 for a review). These sex differences appear to remain stable through early development, as female toddlers demonstrate higher levels of emotional contagion, empathic concern, and prosocial behaviors (Christov-Moore et al., 2014; Eisenberg et al., 2014) and these sex differences continue and possibly even increase during adolescence (Spear, 2009).

Emotional Hyporeactivity and CU Traits.

As noted above, limited empathetic concern is a core part of the definition of CU traits and these traits have been consistently associated with hyporeactivity to distress cues, especially depictions of fear or distress, in others. For instance, Marsh and Blair (2008) conducted a meta-analysis of 20 studies that examined facial affect processing with samples spanning adolescence to adulthood in antisocial populations. Results showed a specific, robust association between elevated CU traits and reduced responses to fearful faces, irrespective of task difficulty. Further, deficits in fearful face processing were found to be significantly greater than that of any other emotion.

Associations between CU traits and hyporeactivity to others' fear and distress cues have been replicated using a diversity of empirical paradigms, including: a) self-report measures (Waller et al., 2020); b) cognitive and behavioral indices (e.g., emotional dot-probe tasks that assess attentional facilitation as captured by response time and accuracy) of reduced attentional orienting to and capture by distress cues and negative emotional stimuli (e.g., images depicting people in distress, facial expressions of fear; Hodsoll et al., 2014; Kimonis et al., 2006; Kimonis et al., 2008; Kimonis et al., 2017; Loney et al., 2003); c) psychophysiological and neuroendocrinological indices of reduced autonomic reactivity and hypothalamic-pituitaryadrenal (HPA) axis activity (e.g., lower levels of cortisol reactivity) in response to others' distress (see Hawes et al., 2009 for a review; Wagner & Waller, 2020) along with lower respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA; Wagner et al., 2017); and d) reduced brain activation in regions responsible for processing salient emotional cues, such as fear (e.g., amygdala; see Blair, 2010 Blair, , 2015 for reviews) and brain wave (i.e., EEG) data that show reduced reactivity to auditory vocal stimuli depicting fear (Hoyniak et al., 2018). Thus, there is strong support, shown across many levels of measurement and a variety of emotional stimuli, that CU traits are associated with deficient emotional reactivity to distress cues in others.

Research also suggests that these associations between CU traits and emotional hyporeactivity are present across development. For instance, behavioral paradigms that assess attentional orienting to negative emotional stimuli report a negative correlation between emotional reactivity and CU traits in children as young as age 7 (Ciucci et al., 2018; Kimonis et al., 2006; Kimonis et al., 2008; Loney et al., 2003). In addition, several studies report a negative association between CU traits and cortisol reactivity in children as young as 6 (see Hawes, et al., 2009 for a review; Loney et al., 2006; Stadler et al., 2011) and heart rate variability in detained male adolescents (Duindam et al., 2021). Functional neuroimaging research that uses both static and dynamic emotional faces show associations between CU traits and reduced neural activation in regions responsible for emotional and fear processing in samples as young as 10 (see Blair, 2010 Blair, , 2015 for reviews; Jones et al., 2009; Lockwood et al., 2013; Marsh et al., 2008; Viding et al., 2012). Similar findings have consistently been shown in adult samples, and these too have been replicated using behavioral measures of reduced attentional orienting in samples of males ages 20 through 55 (see, e.g., Benning et al., 2005; Dargis et al., 2018), physiological measures of reduced autonomic reactivity in samples of males with elevated CU traits up to a mean age of 39 (see, e.g., Pfabigan et al., 2014), and reduced amygdala activation in samples with elevated CU traits up to a mean age of 33 (see Blair 2005 Blair , 2007 Blair , 2010;; De Brito et al., 2021 for reviews).

One way in which research on the association between CU traits and emotional hyporeactivity has not been as a consistent is for findings across sex. That is, studies, particularly those using psychophysiological indices of emotional reactivity, often report findings stronger for male youth (Isen et al., 2010) and adults (Delk et al., 2020; Kavish et al., 2019; O'Leary et al., 2007; Verona et al., 2010). However, one recent study by Thomson and colleagues (2019) reported an association between elevated CU traits and reduced resting RSA in a sample of female undergraduates. In contrast to these conflicting findings using laboratory measures of emotional reactivity, a recent meta-analysis of studies using self-report of affective empathy did not find a moderating role of female composition on the association with CU traits (Waller et al. 2020). One possibility is that these differences may be due to the use of different paradigms.

However, the inconsistent findings depending on the sex composition of the sample are also found in studies that use direct physiological assessments of emotional reactivity. Another reason for the inconsistent results is that research on antisocial samples tend to show a "male bias," with samples largely focusing on either all male or mostly male samples (see Cale & Lilienfeld, 2002 for a review). This potential problem may be particularly relevant for laboratory studies that tend to utilize smaller samples. Also, the limited inclusion of females in antisocial samples could be problematic in that females, due both to biology and cultural expectations, may be less likely to show antisocial behavior and empathy deficits and, as a result, require stronger biological predispositions to do so (Eme, 2007 (Eme, , 2015 (Eme, , 2018;; Moffit & Caspi, 2001).

Thus, it may be that emotional reactivity is less associated with CU traits in females. If this is the case, such sex differences would need to be considered in causal theories of CU traits.

However, it may also be that females with elevated CU traits do show similar profiles of emotional processing deficits (i.e., emotional hyporeactivity) as males but that this is the case in a significantly smaller subsample of antisocial females who are often underrepresented in research (Centifanti et al., 2015; Marsee & Frick, 2007; Thomson et al., 2019). One way to overcome limitations inherent in making conclusions from single studies with limited female representation is to test potential differences across multiple studies using meta-analytic techniques, especially across multiple studies that include persons with very high levels of antisocial behavior, such as forensic or clinic-referred samples.

Cognitive Empathy and Emotion Recognition

The second type of empathy, cognitive empathy, refers to the ability to accurately recognize and identify others' emotions, along with the ability to both understand and adopt others' perspectives (Decety & Jackson 2004). As such, cognitive empathy is thought to represent a more intentional phenomenon (de Waal & Preston, 2017) that requires several complex cognitive functions. Given the requirement of higher order cognition, typically developing children begin to show abilities for cognitive empathy and emotion recognition later than what is found for affective empathy, with cognitive empathy not emerging until later in toddlerhood (Hawes & Dadds, 2012). These perspective taking skills continue to develop through middle childhood (Durand et al., 2007) and some advancements in these skills even continue through adolescence and adulthood. For instance, studies by Schwenck and colleagues (2014) and Abbruzzese and colleagues (2019) reported significant moderation by age, such that age was positively associated with behavioral measures of recognition accuracy, across samples of youth ranging from middle childhood to adolescence and younger adulthood to older adulthood, respectively. Thus, compared to affective empathy, cognitive empathy seems to emerge later and continues to develop throughout development. However, similar to research on affective empathy and emotional reactivity, research consistently finds females show higher levels of cognitive empathy, and these differences are found across development and using multiple paradigms for measuring cognitive empathy (Abbruzzese et al., 2019; Christov-Moore et al., 2014; Jolliffe & Farrington, 2004; Thayer & Johnsen, 2000; Thompson & Voyer, 2014).

CU Traits and Emotion Recognition.

Research has also explored associations between CU traits and cognitive empathy. Compared to affective empathy, the findings from this body of work are considerably less consistent. For example, a meta-analysis by Waller and colleagues (2020) reported significant associations between CU traits and behavioral rating scale measures of cognitive empathy, including emotion recognition (meta-analytic effect size of ρ = -.43).

However, these associations were stronger when rated by parent or teacher than by self-report.

Thus, children with elevated CU traits may be perceived by others as being poorer in recognizing others' emotions, even if they fail to view themselves as deficient. These findings could explain a similar failure to find such associations in research on adults, wherein self-report measures of emotion recognition are typically used (see for reviews, Igoumenou et al., 2017; Richell et al., 2003).

However, developmental differences are also found when using behavioral tasks to assess emotion recognition accuracy. That is, CU traits have been significantly related to deficits in recognizing sadness (Aspan et al., 2014; Woodworth & Waschbusch, 2008) and fear (Aspan et al., 2014; Leist & Dadds, 2009) in samples of young children, while other studies have failed to report these associations and have even sometimes even found a positive association between CU traits and fear recognition in older children and adolescents (see Jones et al., 2009; Schwenck et al., 2014; Sharp et al., 2015; Woodworth & Washbusch, 2008). In addition, tasks examining recognition of simple or basic (e.g., sad, angry) versus complex (e.g., shame, guilt) emotions show poorer recognition of complex emotions in children with elevated CU traits (Sharp et al., 2015) but do not find these deficits in emotion recognition in adult samples (Igoumenou et al., 2017; Richell et al., 2003; Wilson et al., 2011).

These findings have led to several explanations regarding developmental changes in the associations between CU traits and cognitive empathy deficits. Specifically, the early deficit in emotion recognition abilities may be related to deficits in emotional reactivity. That is, developmental theories suggest that early physiological responses to other negative emotions are experienced as aversive to the child, and this emotional response motivates the child to learn to recognize others' emotions as a way of avoiding this unpleasant response (Frick & Kemp, 2021).

Thus, the deficit in emotional hyporeactivity leads to reduced intrinsic motivation to acquire accurate emotion recognition abilities. However, this does not necessarily mean that youth with elevated CU traits are unable to later develop emotion recognition skills. Rather, youth with elevated CU traits may eventually experience increased extrinsic motivation to acquire cognitive empathy skills, as these skills will enable them to use such social cues to their advantage (e.g., to manipulate, coerce, and/or obtain dominance over others; Dadds et al., 2009). As such, the absence of emotional reactivity and its motivational value for promoting emotion recognition skills may lead to delayed emotion recognition acquisition in youth with elevated CU traits.

Thus, potential developmental changes in the association between CU traits and emotion recognition abilities could have important implications for causal theories of CU traits. Further, if youth with elevated CU traits can improve their emotion recognition abilities naturally across development, it suggests that such improvements could be brought about earlier with intervention. However, a critical limitation in the ability of existing research to address this question is the failure to test developmental changes in cognitive empathy in a single sample. This is especially limiting for studies using behavioral measures of emotion recognition that are more time-consuming than self-report measures and are rarely used as repeated measures in longitudinal studies with relatively large samples. Further, the most direct test of the theorized developmental differences in the associations between emotional reactivity and CU traits (no predicted differences) and emotion recognition and CU traits (predicted decrease across age) would be to test both predictions in the same study. Again, one possible way to address this limitation in single studies is to utilize meta-analytic techniques to test age differences in the associations between CU traits with laboratory and behavioral measures of emotional reactivity and recognition and to compare the strength of their associations across samples that vary in age.

Present Study

To address these limitations in past research, the primary aims of the present study were to conduct two complementary multilevel meta-analyses on the association between CU traits and emotional processing, in order to: (1) quantify the overall strength of the link between CU traits and emotional hyporeactivity and CU traits and emotion recognition deficits; and (2) test whether these associations are moderated by sex (i.e., percentage of female participants) and age (i.e., mean age) of the sample. Based on prior research, we hypothesized that CU traits would show a significant negative association with both emotional reactivity and emotion recognition (Hypothesis 1). Further, we expected sex to moderate the association with emotional hyporeactivity, such that the associations would be stronger in samples with a greater proportion of male participants; however, it was further hypothesized that significant associations between CU traits and emotional hyporeactivity would be present in more clinically severe female samples. Thus, we predicted that, when controlling for sample type (i.e., "referred" vs. "nonreferred"), the moderation by sex would no longer be significant (Hypothesis 2). Finally, we hypothesized that age would moderate the association with emotion recognition deficits, such that associations would be weaker in older mean-aged samples (Hypothesis 3).

In testing these hypotheses, we conducted multilevel meta-analytic models that account for dependency among multiple effect sizes drawn from the same studies. We additionally evaluated alternative random-effects structures, including models that added levels for paradigm type (i.e., behavioral vs. psychophysiological vs. neural for emotional reactivity outcomes) and sample type (i.e., referred/enriched vs. community/representative) to determine whether these sources of clustering improved model fit. Because emotional processing tasks vary widely in design and samples differ in enrichment and measurement sensitivity, testing these expanded structures allowed us to assess whether effect sizes clustered beyond the study level in ways that could bias precision or inflate heterogeneity. Final model specifications were selected based on comparative fit and interpretability. We also examined distress-specific emotional outcomes (reactivity or recognition involving others' distress, such as fear, pain, or sadness cues) separately from broader emotional outcomes to test whether CU-related deficits were particularly pronounced for distress cues. Publication bias analyses and robustness checks were performed to evaluate the reliability and stability of the findings.

Methods

Transparency and Openness

We adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews (Page et al., 2021).

All data, analysis code, and research materials (including our coding scheme) are available at [OSF project site]. Data were analyzed using R, version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025) and the package metafor, version 4.8-0 (Viechtbauer, 2025). This project was preregistered at [OSF project site; see supplement for masked preregistration details].

Search Strategy

We, the first and last author, conducted a comprehensive literature search to identify studies reporting on the relationship between CU traits and measures of emotional reactivity or emotion recognition. The search covered multiple databases (e.g., PsycINFO, PubMed, Web of Science) from their inception through June 2025. Search terms included combinations of keywords related to CU traits (e.g., "callous-unemotional traits," "limited prosocial emotions," "psychopathy") and emotional processing (e.g., "emotion* reactivity," "emotion* responsivity," "physiological arousal," "emotion recognition," "facial expression recognition," "empathy," "emotional processing"). We also included terms for specific emotions (e.g., "fear," "sadness," "distress cues") and for common tasks or measures (e.g., "startle reflex," "SCR," "eye tracking," "accuracy," "identification"). In addition to database searches, we examined reference lists of prior reviews and meta-analyses on related topics (e.g., emotion deficits in psychopathy) and performed forward citation tracking on key articles. We also searched for unpublished or "grey literature" by scanning conference proceedings and dissertation/thesis databases (e.g., ProQuest, institutional repositories) to reduce publication bias.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if the following criteria were met: 1) published in English and after 1980 (in order to align with contemporary definitions of psychopathy); 2) adequately powered according to level of analysis (i.e., N > 10 for neuroimaging studies; any pilot or single-case designs excluded); 3) reported quantitative results regarding the relationship between CU traits and either emotional reactivity and/or recognition; 4) used a validated measure of CU traits or CU traits as captured by the broader construct of psychopathy (e.g., the Inventory of Callous-Unemotional Traits, Antisocial Process Screening Device, Psychopathy Checklist or subscales thereof); 5) utilized an objective measure of "preconscious" emotional reactivity, including behavioral (e.g., computerized paradigms assessing attentional facilitation or distraction by affect cues), psychophysiological (e.g., measures of skin conductance, startle, or heart rate reactivity in response to stimuli), or neural (e.g., functional brain activation in limbic regions during passive viewing task) indices, and/or emotion recognition (i.e., behavioral paradigms assessing response accuracy); and 6) reported requisite data, including sample characteristics (i.e., age, sex, and recruitment type) needed for multilevel modeling and moderator tests, and test statistic information needed for effect size conversion (e.g., N or n, t-statistic, group means and standard deviations, Pearson's or Spearman's r). Both full-text published peer-reviewed journal articles and unpublished graduate (i.e., masters-or doctoral-level) theses and dissertations were considered for inclusion.

We excluded studies that did not meet all inclusion criteria. Notably, studies using only subjective (i.e., questionnaire) measures for emotional reactivity or recognition were excluded because a) sex differences in these studies may be confounded by cultural stereotypes and demand characteristics and b) Waller et al.'s (2020) meta-analysis already reported on these associations with CU traits among youth. Further, for the emotional reactivity meta-analysis, studies that used paradigms targeting mid-to-late stage processing and conscious or attentional control (e.g., tasks manipulations meant to tap into or measure emotional regulation ability by prompting participants to become aware of and attempt to manipulate their subjective emotional state) were excluded. In addition, treatment studies that reported only changes in recognition or reactivity over the course of treatment were excluded. However, treatment studies that reported baseline, pre-treatment levels of emotional processing and their association with CU traits were considered eligible for inclusion. Finally, studies that only reported on samples identified to meet diagnostic criteria for serious mental illness (i.e., bipolar or psychotic spectrum disorders), autism spectrum disorder, or substance use disorder were excluded as the mechanisms underlying these diagnostic presentations may influence emotional processing findings.

Study Selection and Final Sample

The online tool Covidence was utilized for study screening, selection, and extraction of study variables. Upon searching electronic databases using the key terms and publication date range (i.e., after 1980), along with conducting backward citation search of eligible studies and related published literature reviews, a total of k = 5,787 articles were identified. Of these, 3,481 were identified to be duplicates and removed. Regarding duplicates, measures were taken to ensure that no same dataset and outcomes were included across multiple studies from the same author. For instance, any included thesis or dissertation was investigated to see if a corresponding peer-reviewed journal article that reported on the same data was included, in which case, the thesis/dissertation was subsequently removed. After duplicate removal, a remaining k = 2,306 studies were screened for relevance using title and abstract, and k = 1,059 were excluded. Of the remaining 1,247 studies that were assessed using full-text, k = 1,063 were found to be ineligible and removed. Failure to meet inclusion criteria for these studies included one or more of the following reasons: wrong outcomes (e.g., resting physiological or neural data, structural neural data), wrong intervention or task (e.g., use of non-visual stimuli, use of inanimate or non-living subjects for stimuli, task manipulations prompting participants to blunt or increase emotional responsiveness during stimuli exposure), wrong comparator (i.e., missing data for either CU traits or broader psychopathy), missing requisite extraction data (e.g., sample or subsample size, mean sample age, sample composition by females), wrong study characteristics (e.g., review or meta-analysis, qualitative), and wrong participant population (e.g., non-human, sample exclusively composed of patients in treatment for substance use disorder).

Thus, the final number of included studies, across both emotional reactivity and recognition findings, was a total of k = 184, 106 of which were included in the emotional reactivity metaanalysis and 97 of which were included in the emotion recognition meta-analysis (see Figure 1 for a PRISMA flowchart summary of study search and selection results).

Data Extraction and Coding

Each included study had the following data extracted: first author name, publication year, and country or countries in which data was collected; article type (i.e., peer-reviewed journal article vs. committee-passing graduate thesis or dissertation); sample characteristics, including sample size, mean age, and percent of the sample reported to be female, all of which were reported continuously; sample type and recruitment method (i.e., non-referred vs. referred); CU measure type, including whether the study utilized a direct measure of CU traits (yes/no) rather than a broader measure including other dimensions of psychopathy, informant type (self, parent, teacher, or a combination), and whether level of CU traits was analyzed continuously or categorically; measure of emotional reactivity or recognition, including the type of paradigm used for emotional reactivity, emotional stimuli information (e.g., partial vs. full faces, static vs. dynamic/morphing faces, full-scene images from IAPS, or dynamic cartoon or movie clips), and emotion depicted by the stimuli (e.g., distress as captured by fear, sadness, or pain vs. broad emotional valence); outcome type (e.g., fearful face recognition accuracy vs. total recognition accuracy across multiple emotions; attentional facilitation score that incorporates task accuracy and reaction time vs. number of eye gaze fixations to distress cues); and analysis type (e.g., correlational vs. group comparisons between high-and low-CU groups).

Specific to the emotional reactivity meta-analysis, level of analysis was coded as 1) behavioral, 2) psychophysiological, or 3) neural. For this study, measures of brain responsivity from both fMRI and EEG paradigms were included in the neural level of analysis, and only effect sizes from neural regions of interest (ROI; e.g., amygdala) or event related potential (ERP) components (e.g., late positive potential (LPP)) set a priori (i.e., those with strong theoretical foundations for their relevance to emotional reactivity) were extracted and included. Similarly, only theoretically relevant reactivity outcomes for behavioral (e.g., attentional facilitation scores, eye tracking outcomes to include fixation count or dwell time on affective cues in facial processing tasks, reaction time as a measure of attentional-emotional distraction) and physiological (e.g., heart rate, skin conductance, or pupil reactivity; facial electromyography outcomes to include eye blink or startle potentiation) paradigms were extracted. In addition, for the purposes of both meta-analyses, studies that included and reported effect sizes from "mixed" sample types that recruited and compared representative, community participants to high-risk, referred participants were categorized in the referred sample type. This decision was made to increase the power to detect effect sizes reflecting emotional correlates to CU traits in samples enriched for elevated CU traits. Follow-up sensitivity analyses were conducted separately, with effect sizes from either mixed sample types or EEG paradigms removed, to assess any influence these methodological factors may have had on effect size estimates.

Reliability

A second "blinded" or masked coder, who was a predoctoral student in clinical psychology (either the third or fourth author), extracted and coded for the same variables for 20% of the total included studies. Inter-coder agreement was 95% across all variables and all discrepancies were resolved as part of a consensus meeting among coders and the first author.

The reliability for the individual primary and moderator variables were as follows: first author name and publication year (100%), sample type (non-referred or referred; 99%) and size (84%), whether a direct measure of CU traits or psychopathy was used (97%), mean sample age (97%), percent of the sample that was female (92%), outcome type (reactivity or recognition; 95%), paradigm or level of analysis for emotional reactivity (behavioral, physiological, or neural; 100%), and types of facial stimuli used (i.e., static or dynamic/morphing faces; full or partial face region; 91%).

Quality Assessment

We conducted a systematic quality assessment of all included studies to evaluate methodological rigor and reporting adequacy. The assessment tool, adapted from established guidelines for meta-analyses in developmental and clinical psychology (Luchini et al., 2021), comprised items evaluating (1) sample description and recruitment clarity, (2) measurement quality and reliability of CU instruments, (3) validity and psychometric adequacy of emotional reactivity or emotion recognition paradigms, (4) statistical transparency (e.g., provision of means/SDs or effect sizes, clarity of analytic decisions, missingness of data), and (5) potential risks of bias (e.g., selective reporting, small-cell analyses, non-independent samples). We derived an overall quality score (range = 0-9) for each study by summing the results across all items, and higher scores indicated greater methodological rigor. Of all included studies, the mean quality score was 6.4 (SD = 1.5), with the first quartile scoring between 5-6, and the top quartile scoring between 7-8. Quality ratings were used for sensitivity checks rather than as exclusion criteria. Specifically, we repeated all main analyses and tested for moderating effects of QC total score.

Analytic Strategy

We used multilevel meta-analytic models with a random-effects framework to synthesize effect sizes. Given that many studies contributed multiple effect sizes (e.g., for different emotions or tasks), and that effect sizes could be clustered by study and by other design factors, a multilevel approach was necessary to handle the dependency among effects. We followed the approach outlined by Assink and Wibbelink (2016) for three-level (and higher-level) metaanalysis.

Individual Effect Sizes

For both meta-analyses, all inferential statistics were transformed to Pearson's r correlations using the effect size converter by Wilson (2013) and formulas from Lipsey and Wilson (2001). If a study reported an association or effect only as non-significant, the effect size was coded as 0, as recommended by Lipsey and Wilson (2001), to avoid inflating effects due to selective reporting of significance. Pearson's correlation coefficients (r) were then transformed into Fisher z-values for the meta-analysis, before standard errors and sampling variances of the effect sizes were calculated using formulas by Lipsey and Wilson (2001), and these z-scores were transformed back into Pearson's r correlation coefficients using the R package "compute.es" (Del Re, 2013) for interpretability (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). To assess the magnitude of effect sizes, those around r = .10 were considered small, around r = .30 medium, and around r = .50 large. All correlations were keyed in the same direction so that they may be compared to each other. That is, a negative correlation between CU traits and emotional reactivity or recognition measures would be in line with hypotheses in that individuals with higher levels of CU traits would show lower levels of emotion reactivity to and less accurate recognition of others' emotions or distress.

Model Structure

In testing associations between CU traits and emotional reactivity, we proposed a fivelevel model: the sampling variance for the observed effect sizes across all participants (level 1), the variance between the individual effect sizes (level 2), variance between clusters of effect sizes categorized by the study they came from (level 3), variance between clusters of effect sizes as categorized by level of analysis (i.e., behavioral, psychophysiological, or neural; level 4), and variance between clusters of effect sizes as categorized by sample type (i.e., referred or nonreferred; level 5; Cheung, 2014). Log-likelihood-ratio tests were performed to compare the fit of the full model to the fit of the model with one fewer variance parameter, making it possible to determine whether significant variance was present at any level and whether a five-level model provided greater fit over that of a four-level model (Assink & Wibbelink, 2016). Significant variance at any level past the first was considered indicative of heterogeneity in effect size distribution, and the distribution of variance at each level was calculated using Cheung's (2014) procedure. In the case of heterogeneity, we proceeded with tests of moderators. All moderator analyses could be performed, as data were available from three or more studies in all cases (Crocetti, 2016). All significant moderators were entered into a multivariate model to examine the unique contribution in the explanation of the variance in the effect size distribution.

In testing associations between CU traits and emotion recognition, we proposed a fourlevel model: the sampling variance for the observed effect sizes across all participants (level 1), the variance between the individual effect sizes (level 2), the variance between clusters of effect sizes as categorized by the study or article they came from (level 3), and variance between clusters of effect sizes as categorized by sample type (i.e., referred or non-referred; level 4; Cheung, 2014). Notably, level of analysis was not included as a level of variance across effect sizes because only behavioral paradigms assessing accuracy of recognition were included.

Again, the same steps as described above, beginning with performing log-likelihood-ratio tests, were conducted to test for model fit and heterogeneity in variance across effect size across levels, and significant heterogeneity was followed up with moderator analyses (i.e., sex and age) also described above.

Publication Bias

The presence of publication bias, for each meta-analytic model, was first examined with funnel plots visualization. Funnel plots with symmetrically distributed effect sizes around the mean effect size were interpreted to imply no publication bias (see Duval & Tweedie, 2000).

Further, the symmetry of the funnel plots was examined with Egger's regression and rank correlation tests (Egger et al., 1997), in which a significant p-value indicates the presence of publication bias and a positive beta indicates bias for reporting or publishing significant effects.

In the case of publication bias, an adjusted correlation was calculated and applied by using the "trim-and-fill" method by Duval and Tweedie (2000). In addition, Rosenthal's Fail-safe N was calculated for each model to indicate how many missing studies with a mean effect of 0 would be required to produce a non-significant (p > 0.05) correlation (see Borenstein et al., 2021; Rosenthal, 1979).

Discussion

Individuals with elevated callous-unemotional (CU) traits represent an etiologically and clinically meaningful subgroup of individuals who display serious antisocial behavior, marked by distinct cognitive-affective characteristics and a severe pattern of aggressive behavior (Frick et al., 2014). As a result, research on the processes leading to the development of CU traits could be critical both for causal theories of antisocial behavior and for the development of more effective treatment for this group of youth at high risk for problems throughout development (Frick, 2022). Substantial research has linked CU traits to deficits in empathy development, both affective empathy, involving blunted or reduced emotional reactivity to others' emotions, and cognitive empathy, involving impairments in recognizing emotional expressions in others (Frick & Kemp, 2021). However, it is unclear if the deficits in emotional reactivity generalize across samples with larger female composition, and the deficits in emotion recognition have not been consistently found in samples of adolescents and adults. The current study used multilevel metaanalytic techniques to clarify these inconsistencies in past research.

Overall Associations

Our meta-analyses supported associations between both measures of emotional reactivity and emotion recognition accuracy with CU traits. Across 106 studies with a total of 402 individual effect sizes, CU traits were negatively associated with laboratory measures of emotional reactivity, and this overall effect was robust across the type of paradigm used to measure emotional reactivity (i.e., behavioral, psychophysiological, neural). Also, the pooled effect size for studies of reactivity to a range of emotional stimuli (r = -.23) was quite similar to the pooled effect size for the 84 studies (234 effect sizes) assessing reactivity to distress-specific stimuli (e.g., fearful or sad faces, people in pain; r = -.22). These findings are important given the debate as to how broad or how specific the deficit in reactivity to emotional stimuli shown by those with elevated CU traits might be (Brook et al., 2013; Del Casale et al., 2015; Marsh & Blair, 2008). Our results, providing a quantitative summary across studies using a range of methods to measure reactivity, suggest the deficit may be to a broad range of emotional stimuli, rather than to only distress related stimuli.

Also consistent with our hypotheses, our meta-analysis found that across 97 studies and a total of 437 individual effect sizes, CU traits were significantly associated with poorer emotion recognition accuracy, with the effect size again being fairly similar across studies using a broad range of facial emotions (r = -.09) and studies assessing recognition accuracy for facial emotions depicting distress (r = -.10).

Moderation By Sex and Age

Our tests of potential moderators could help explain the differences in the overall strength of the associations for CU traits with measures of emotional reactivity compared to the association with measures of emotion recognition. Specifically, sex and age did not moderate the strength of the association between measures emotional reactivity and measures of CU traits, whereas both age and sex moderated the association between emotion recognition accuracy and CU traits. Both sets of findings have important theoretical and practical implications.

The finding that the association between emotional reactivity and CU traits is stronger and more consistent across samples that differ in age and sex of the participants supports the contention that this emotional deficit may be a core deficit for explaining the development of CU traits (DeBrito et al, 2021; Frick, 2022; Viding & McCrory, 2018; Waller & Wagner, 2019). That is, deficits in emotional reactivity seem to be present very early in life and the absence of this emotional contagion to others distress may reduce the intrinsic motivation for children to learn to recognize emotions in others (Frick & Kemp, 2021). This lack of emotional reactivity could also result in a failure to experience transgression related arousal in response to others' displeasure over one's behavior, resulting in reduced experiences of guilt over wrongdoing (Blair, 1997).

The importance of emotional reactivity to theories developed to explain the causes of CU traits is also supported by the fact that deficient emotional reactivity is consistently found to differentiate children with conduct problems with elevated CU traits from those children with conduct problems but with normative levels of these traits, with the latter group often showing the opposite profile of emotional responses (i.e., hyperreactivity) to emotional stimuli (Frick, 2022).

Further, the pattern of emotional hyporeactivity in those with elevated CU traits has also proven important for differentiating this group of children from children with other diagnoses involving empathy deficits, such as those with autism spectrum disorder, who often show deficits in cognitive empathy but not deficits in affective empathy (Schwenck et al., 2012).

In contrast to these robust findings for emotional reactivity, the pooled effect size for the negative association between emotion recognition accuracy and CU traits was smaller in magnitude and was modified by age and sex. The moderation by age of the sample was hypothesized and consistent with predictions. That is, the pooled effect sizes of the negative association between CU traits and recognition accuracy decreased as the mean age of the sample increased. These results provide support for the theory that associations between CU traits and emotion recognition deficits are present early in development due to deficient emotional reactivity to others' emotions failing to provide intrinsic motivation to learn to accurately identify and recognize others' emotions (Frick & Kemp, 2021). However, emotion recognition abilities may successfully develop in youth with elevated CU traits over time due to increasing external motivators, whereby the youth learns that these skills lead to benefits in social influence (e.g., enhancing a person's ability to manipulate or dominate others). In addition, the fact that the association between emotion recognition deficits and CU traits decreases over development, but the association between emotional hyporeactivity and CU traits does not, suggests emotion recognition skills can change, even without concomitant changes in the internal motivator of affective empathy. The malleability of emotion recognition supports targeting these skills in interventions focused on treating children with elevated CU traits, such as the enhancements to evidence-base parenting interventions developed by Kimonis and colleagues that help parents teach their children with elevated CU traits how to recognize others' emotions and how to motivate their children to use these skills in prosocial, healthy social interactions (Fleming et al., 2022; Kimonis et al., 2019).

The association between emotion recognition deficits and CU traits was also moderated by sex, with effect sizes of the association being stronger in samples with higher proportions of females. These results need to be interpreted cautiously due to the fact that they were not predicted a priori, samples included in the meta-analysis were heavily weighted towards greater male representation, the effect size of the interaction was modest, and it was only found for broad emotion recognition deficits and not for recognition accuracy of faces depicting distress emotions. Even more importantly, this moderation by sex was qualified by a two-way interaction between the proportion of females in the sample and the mean age of the sample. Specifically, the weakening of the negative association between CU traits and emotion recognition accuracy across age was not found in samples with a greater female composition. This same pattern (e.g., weakening of the association in aging males, but not females) was also reported by Dadds and colleagues (2009) who used parent-report measures to assess cognitive empathy. These authors proposed that the more severe or pervasive recognition deficits in girls with elevated CU traits may reflect a greater "liability threshold" for developing CU traits in girls, since it is contrary both to biological and societal expectations that females would show greater levels of empathy, guilt, and other prosocial emotions. As a result, females may be less likely to develop elevated CU traits but, when they do, they represent a more severe subgroup with more intractable risk factors.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

In addition to our tests of these theoretically important moderators, we tested several methodological moderators to determine if they influenced our overall results. Across metaanalyses of both emotional reactivity and emotion recognition, our findings were not moderated by sample type (i.e., referred vs. non-referred), CU (vs. psychopathy) measure, stimulus characteristics, publication type (i.e., inclusion of non-peer-reviewed "gray" literature), or overall study quality. In addition, in the meta-analyses of emotion recognition, whether (yes/no) IQ or cognitive ability was controlled in analyses did not moderate associations between CU traits and recognition accuracy. Further, all model results remained meaningfully unchanged after removing outliers and remained significant when adjusting for potential publication bias.

Our results also need to be interpreted considering several limitations. Fewer than half of studies provided information on race/ethnicity of the study sample and the studies that met inclusionary criteria were predominantly conducted in North America and Europe. Thus, our conclusions regarding associations between emotional processing deficits and CU traits still need to be replicated in samples with different racial, ethnic, and cultural compositions. Further, the causal and clinical implications of these findings need to be considered in light of effect size magnitude across studies, with the pooled association between CU traits and measures of emotional reactivity being approximately r ≈ -.23 and between emotion recognition accuracy and CU traits being approximately r ≈ -.10. These effects sizes clearly suggest that there are likely multiple other factors, including environmental influences such as low parental warmth (see Waller & Hyde, 2017), that also contribute meaningfully to the emergence and maintenance of CU traits.

Conclusions

With these cautions in mind, our meta-analysis provides important data on two emotional processes that have been linked to CU traits and have played an important role in causal theories to explain how these traits develop and for guiding treatment to help reduce these traits in children. Our results support the robust association between CU traits and hyporeactivity to emotional stimuli that has been the focus of many causal theories for how CU traits develop and has been one of the key characteristics that differentiate children with elevated CU traits from other youth with conduct problems. Our results suggest that deficits in emotional reactivity are not specific to stimuli depicting distress in others (e.g., fear, sadness, pain) and that they are associated with CU traits across development and for both males and females. While emotion recognition deficits were also found not to be wholly specific to distressed faces, associations between emotion recognition deficits and CU traits tended to be weaker than what was found for emotional reactivity, and these effects were moderated by both age and sex. Consistent with theories proposing emotion recognition deficits are delayed in those with elevated CU traits, the negative association between emotion recognition accuracy and CU traits decrease as the mean age of the sample increased, at least for largely male samples.
