Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

This study examined the impact of culture and attitudes towards motherhood (ATM) on the effectiveness of traditional/non-traditional gendered advertisements. Korean and British participants viewed either traditional or non-traditional female portrayals in adverts and rated them for purchase intention and brand attitude. They also completed the Attitudes towards Fertility and Childbearing Scale (AFCS) containing three subscales: Fertility, Hindrance, and Social Identity. The results showed that there was a significant difference between cultures on ATM with more negative attitudes in Korean participants. However, there was no interaction effect between culture and advert type on advertising effectiveness, but main effects of culture and advert type were found. Favourable attitudes towards motherhood significantly positively correlated with the effectiveness of traditional adverts, but not non-traditional adverts except for Hindrance with PI.

Introduction

Femvertising, advertising that challenges traditional female stereotypes (Åkestam et al., 2017), has become an increasingly prominent advertising strategy over the past decade, with many campaigns demonstrating high effectiveness on consumer response (Perez, 2015; Schultz, 2014). Coined by Samantha Skey of SheKnows Media (Becker-Herby, 2016), the term has become widely adopted in both industry and academic literature. Campaigns such as P&G Always' "#Like a Girl"have attracted widespread public attention, generating 76 million YouTube views, 4.5 billion impressions, and over 597,261 mentions of the campaign's slogan (Perez, 2015). However, research on the effectiveness of femvertising has largely focused on Western populations (Um, 2021), despite evidence that gender-role stereotype content in advertising varies across cultures because of differences in cultural values (Furnham & Farragher, 2000; Paek et al., 2010). Although content knowledge of gender stereotypes are culturally shared; individuals differ in the extent to which they endorse and accept these stereotypes (Zawisza & Cinnirella, 2010).

The present study examines the effectiveness of femvertising among British and Korean women, focusing on attitudes towards motherhood, a construct that has received considerably less attention than attitudes towards feminism despite its importance in feminist discourse (Corradi, 2020). South Korea's exceptionally low fertility rate (0.8 babies per woman) makes it a particularly relevant context in which to examine attitudes towards motherhood, comparative with the UK, where the fertility rate is 1.53 babies per woman (Clark, 2024).

Specifically, the study investigates whether attitudes towards motherhood influence the effectiveness of non-traditional gender role advertisements, measured by brand attitude and purchase intentions, and whether these effects differ between British and South Korean participants.

Gender stereotypes in advertising

Representation of gender stereotypes (generalised beliefs about specific genders; APA Dictionary of Psychology, 2023) in advertising media has long contributed to gender inequality (Grau & Zotos, 2016), by reinforcing stereotypical portrayals of women, sexism, objectification of women, and unrealistic body ideals (Cortese, 2004; Jalees & Majid, 2009; Plakoyiannaki et al., 2008). Women continue to be portrayed as decorative, family-oriented and submissive, whereas men are more often depicted in independent, professional and authoritative roles (Reichert & Carpenter, 2004; Uray & Burnaz, 2003). Similar patterns persist in both British and Korean advertising (Furnham & Saar, 2005; Paek et al., 2011), with women predominantly depicted in family and recreational settings across television and web advertisements (An & Kim, 2007; Furnham & Saar, 2005; Paek et al., 2011).

Femvertising: Feminism in Advertising

The emergence of the fourth wave of feminism (Grady, 2018) has seen a convergence of feminist ideology and advertising, giving rise to the concept known as 'femvertising'.

Femvertising seeks to promote feminist social values by portraying women in ways that challenge traditional gender stereotypes (Åkestam et al., 2017). Typical characteristics include: (1) the use of diverse female talent, (2) pro-female messaging, (3) rejection of gender stereotypes, (4) reduced sexual objectification, and (5) authentic portrayals of women (Becker-Herby, 2016).

Campaigns such as Dove's "Real Beauty" campaign, Girl Scouts' "Ban Bossy", and Microsoft's "#MakeWhatsNext" exemplify this approach by promoting body confidence, female leadership, and women's participation in STEM careers.

Femvertising has become an increasingly popular advertising strategy because it enables brands to communicate socially progressive values, strengthen emotional connections with consumers, and enhance brand performance (SheKnows, 2015; Stanton, 1984; Um, 2021).

As consumers increasingly value brand ethics alongside product attributes (Accenture, 2024; Schaverien, 2018), femvertising has become an important marketing approach. However, critics argue that such campaigns often represent commodity feminism, whereby feminist ideals are used primarily for commercial purposes rather than genuine social change (Goldman et al., 1991).

Despite its growing popularity, evidence for the effectiveness of femvertising remains inconsistent. Some studies report more favourable responses to non-traditional portrayals of women, featuring women in egalitarian/superwomen roles over traditional depictions (Jaffe & Berger, 1994), or stereotype flips of portrayals of females as superior to males (Orth & Holancova, 2004), whereas others have found greater preference for traditional portrayals (Vantomme et al., 2006; Zawisza & Cinnirella, 2010). According to congruity theory, advertisements that are consistent with consumers' existing social and cognitive schemata are evaluated more positively (Ko et al., 2015), suggesting that responses to femvertising may depend on individuals' pre-existing gender-related attitudes. For example, Vantomme et al. (2006) found that both men and women preferred fictitious mobile phone advertisements depicting women in traditional roles over non-traditional roles. Similarly, Zawisza and Cinnirella (2010) reported that UK female undergraduates showed more favourable affective and cognitive responses to advertisements portraying women in traditional rather than nontraditional roles. Thus, it remains unclear under which conditions femvertising is most effective.

The Role of Culture and Gender Attitudes in Ad Effectiveness

One explanation for the inconsistent findings regarding the effectiveness of femvertising is the difficulty of generalising results across cultures. Consumers perceive and interpret advertisements differently according to their values, experiences, and demographic characteristics (De Meulenaer et al., 2017; Jalakas, 2016; Kapoor & Munjal, 2017). However, research on femvertising remains largely confined to Western populations (Um, 2021), despite evidence that advertising reflects culturally shared values and assumptions about consumer behaviour (An & Kim, 2007; Frith & Mueller, 2010. According to An and Kim (2007), advertising is created based on the assumption that members of a culture think and behave in uniform and predictable ways (Markin, 1974) and advertisers incorporate these cultural values into their messages. Consistent with congruity theory, differences in social and cognitive schemata across cultures are therefore likely to influence preferences for traditional and nontraditional gender portrayals in advertising (Ko et al., 2015). Accordingly, the first aim of the present study was to examine the effect of culture on the effectiveness of non-traditional gender role advertising.

Congruity theory further suggests that advertisements consistent with consumers' gender-related attitudes should elicit more favourable responses (Ko et al., 2015). For example, recent research found that Korean women with more positive attitudes towards feminism evaluated femvertising more favourably (Um, 2021). Similarly, social judgement theory (Sherif & Hovland, 1981) proposes that if counter-attitudinal appeals are used in adverts, they may be rejected, thus decreasing advertising effectiveness. Consequently, femvertising's advertising effectiveness may be affected by individual gender attitudes and type of ad in an interactive way. In their study on London undergraduate students, Zawisza and Cinnirella (2010) found that there was an interaction between ad type and gender attitude. Attitudes towards female gender roles influenced responses to gender stereotyped ads, with women with more traditional attitudes having more positive feelings towards traditionally gendered adverts (e.g., housewife) than liberals (Zawisza & Cinnirella, 2010).

Moreover, in a study by De Meulenaer et al. (2017) across two culturally different countries, they investigated how cultural values influence responses to gender role stereotyping in advertising and found that consumers' responses (attitude to brand and ad) to gender stereotyped adverts (occupational stereotypes) were moderated by a number of cultural values (i.e. power distance, assertiveness, feminine-role orientation). More feminine-role oriented individuals, defined as the 'manifestation of women's autonomy and consciousness of their societal roles' , responded less favourably to gender role stereotyping than less feminineoriented ones, aligning with congruity theory.

Attitudes towards motherhood in Korea and the UK

In a recent study by Kim and Kim (2023), Korean female undergraduates exhibited lower willingness for childbearing compared to male students, consistent with a national health study revealing women's more negative attitudes toward parenthood than men (이 et al., 2016).

Similarly, in another study, female students were found to perceive motherhood as having a more negative impact on their careers and lives consequently assigning lower value to childbearing than men (Shin et al., 2020). Furthermore, parenthood values were found to predict actual childbearing behaviour in a Korean national health study tracking values toward parenthood and childbearing behaviour over time among married women (이 et al., 2016). Contrarily, fertility rates are not a significant issue in the UK and research indicates that British women continue to have babies although at a later age (Harper & Botero-Meneses, 2022). Thus, it is anticipated that there will be a difference in attitudes towards motherhood between cultures, which may impact the effectiveness of femvertising.

Feminism and motherhood are often considered as opposing concepts in feminist discourse, in which motherhood is perceived as inhibiting women's liberation, framing it as a form of subordination within patriarchal societies (Corradi, 2020). However, there appears to be little or no research on the relationship between attitudes towards motherhood and the effectiveness of femvertising. Instead, recent research on Korean females has indicated a relationship between attitudes towards feminism and femvertising, with more positive attitudes towards feminism eliciting more positive attitudes to the femvertising brand/ad (Um, 2021).

Thus, more positive attitudes towards motherhood are anticipated to decrease the effectiveness of femvertising. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether the effectiveness (attitude towards brand and purchase intentions) of femvertising varies as a function of the female participants' attitudes towards motherhood, and if this is influenced by cultural backgrounds by comparing a western and non-western country (UK vs South Korea). In consideration of the literature, it is hypothesised that: H1 -Attitudes towards motherhood will be more negative in South Korean participants than British participants.

H2 -There will be a significant interaction between advert type and cultural background on advertising effectiveness (attitude towards brand and purchase intentions).

• a) -Purchase intentions (PI) and attitude towards brands (AB) will be higher in the nontraditional than traditional conditions for South Korean participants.

• b) -PI and AB will be higher in the traditional than non-traditional condition for British participants as found in Zawisza and Cinnirella (2010).

• c) -PI and AB in the non-traditional condition will be higher in Korean participants than in British.

H3 -Advert effectiveness will be a) positively correlated with more favourable attitudes towards motherhood in the traditional advert condition and b) negatively correlated in the nontraditional advert condition.

Method Participants

Female participants aged 18-40, falling within the typical fertility age range (Extend Fertility, 2019), were recruited by opportunity sampling from South Korea and the United Kingdom. Participants were required to have 'White' ethnicity and British nationality or 'Asian' ethnicity and South Korean nationality, and no children to mitigate potential confounding influences stemming from parenthood. A total of 198 participants were retained for analysis, with a mean age of 30.10 years (SD = 6.19). This comprised 134 British participants (Mean age = 31.03 years, SD = 6.11) and 64 Korean participants (Mean age = 28.16 years, SD = 6.00).

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the UCL Experimental Psychology Department Ethics Committee (EP/2018/007), and informed consent was secured from all participants before their engagement in the experimental procedures.

Materials

The experiment was conducted online using Gorilla, an online behavioural experimentation builder (https://gorilla.sc/) and to increase accessibility, the experiment was made to be available on mobiles, tablets, laptops and PCs.

Stimuli

The stimuli consisted of two sets of advertisements created specifically for this study using Adobe Photoshop (see Figure 1). Each advertisement featured either a Korean or British model depicted in a traditional role (pregnant woman) or a non-traditional role (businesswoman) endorsing a fictitious bottled water brand. To facilitate cross-cultural comparisons while controlling for group membership effects, matched versions of each advertisement featuring Korean and British models were generated using generative AI (Gencraft; https://gencraft.com/). Identical prompts were used across image generation sessions, with only the model's ethnicity and role varied between conditions. The use of generative AI enabled precise manipulation of visual features (e.g., pregnancy, business attire, and facial expression) while maintaining consistency across stimuli. A fictitious brand was used to minimise potential confounding effects of brand familiarity and pre-existing brand preferences.

Following Zawisza and Cinnirella (2010), bottled water was chosen as the advertised product as it was pretested in their study and found to be a unisex and low-involvement product, meaning a product that requires little pre-purchase consideration. In line with their methodology, the role of a mother was used to represent a traditional female role and portrayed using a pregnant model to reduce confounds of additional characters in the advert and the effect of children in advertising (Kinsey, 1987; Scandura, 2018; Seu, 2015; Smith et al., 2008). For the non-traditional female role, a career woman was chosen, represented in the stimuli as a businesswoman in formal corporate attire. The pairs of Korean and British advertisements for the traditional and non-traditional conditions. Measures Attitude toward Brand & Purchase Intentions Attitudes toward Brand (AB) and Purchase Intentions (PI) were measured using Spears and Singh (2004)'s five item 7-point semantic differential scales as used in Um (2021). Both Likert scales were selected as they have been shown to be psychometrically valid and have good reliability with composite reliabilities of .94 and .97 respectively (Spears & Singh, 2004).

Ratings from each item are summed to form an overall score and a high score means a more positive attitude/purchase intention. One item was removed from the PI scale due to vague wording (1 = Never,5 = Definitely) in response to the instruction 'Please describe your overall feelings about the brand described in the ad you just read.'

Attitudes Towards Motherhood

Attitudes towards motherhood were measured using Söderberg et al.'s (2013) Attitudes Towards Fertility and Childbearing Scale' (AFCS) designed specifically to measure the attitudes of women who are not yet mothers. The scale has been found to be a reliable and valid measure when utilised in other cultures (Kossakowska & Söderberg, 2020). It comprises 27 items and consists of three underlying dimensions of the motherhood construct: 1.

Importance of fertility for the future (Fertility): 'Children are seen as an essential part of life, a part of personal development, and something to look forward to.' 2. Childbearing as a hindrance at present (Hindrance): 'Children are considered to limit one's personal life and imply a responsibility that does not fit the woman's current life situation.' 3. Social identity (Social Identity): 'Family values and the importance of fertility to a woman's identity.'

In this study the Cronbach's alphas for the three scales were .90, .91, .81, indicating high internal consistency (Söderberg et al., 2013).

Procedure

Having provided demographic information, participants were randomly allocated, to either the mother or businesswoman advertising condition. Participants were then presented with the advert for 30 seconds, the duration used by Zawisza and Cinnirella (2010). Participants then completed the PI, AB and AFCS scales, with statements presented in random order. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the UCL Experimental Psychology Department Ethics Committee (EP/2018/007), and informed consent was secured from all participants before their engagement in the experimental procedures

Results

Each participant's score was summed on each of the five DV measures and a mean score of each measure for every group was calculated (see Appendix for graphs). Cronbach's alphas indicated high internal consistency for all measures: AB (.95), PI (.95), Fertility (.95), Hindrance (.92), and Social Identity (.84).

Correlations between attitudes towards motherhood and advert effectiveness

To test H3, Pearson's correlations were conducted between advertising effectiveness (PI & AB) and AFCS subscales (Fertility, Hindrance, Social Identity) separately for the traditional and non-traditional advert conditions.

In the traditional condition, Fertility and Social Identity were positively correlated with both PI (Fertility: r(96) = .27, p = .008; Social Identity: r(96) = .30, p = .002) and AB (Fertility: r(96) = .23, p = .02; Social Identity: r(96) = .31, p = .002), whereas Hindrance was negatively correlated with both outcomes (PI: r(96) = .23, p = .02; AB: r(96) = .23, p = .02). The results show a more positive attitude towards motherhood was correlated with higher PI and AB for traditional adverts containing a mother. These findings supported H3a.

In the non-traditional condition, Hindrance was positively correlated with PI (r(98) = .21, p = .03), but not with AB (r(98) = .15, p = .12). All remaining correlations were nonsignificant. Therefore, H3b received partial support.

Discussion

We tested three main hypotheses in this study. In support of the first hypothesis, the results showed that there was a significant difference between cultures on the attitudes towards motherhood subscale Hindrance. Korean participants had significantly higher scores on the Hindrance subscale than British participants. However, there were no significant differences between the two cultures on the other two AFCS scales: Fertility ('the importance of fertility for the future') and Social Identity ('Family values and the importance of fertility to a woman's identity'). Values regarding the importance of fertility for the future and the role of family in shaping a woman's identity may be shared across cultures (Barlow & Chapin, 2010), transcending specific cultural differences between Korean and British women. Thus, the lack of differences between cultures in Fertility and Social Identity scores may have occurred due to the potential universality of certain aspects related to fertility and social identity.

The finding of higher hindrance scores in Korean women is similar to the findings of Shin et al. (2020) in which female Korean students perceived motherhood as having a more negative impact on their careers and lives, assigning a lower value to childbearing than men.

Although not comprehensive, the difference in attitudes towards motherhood found only in the Hindrance scale may be indicative of greater challenges that Korean women face following childbearing in Korea than British women in the UK. This may be because Korean women face a larger 'motherhood penalty' (disadvantages in pay and perceived competence in working mothers in the workplace (Budig & England, 2001)), than British women.

Korean women face a variety of difficulties when choosing to have a child, and research indicates that the traditional culture of work has created a particularly stark trade-off, confronting women with the choice of having a career or a child and putting pressure on women to choose one (Dynan et al., 2022; Fioretti, 2023). Moreover, this trade-off has increased because of rising costs -as Korea now has the world's highest cost of raising a child (Lee, 2023), due to the intensely competitive education environment in which the norm is for most children to attend after regular school most children attend private schooling (known as 'hagwons' in Korea), costing parents around $315 per month per child (Lee, 2023).

Furthermore, despite being highly educated relative to women in other OECD countries, Korean women encounter a significant gender pay gap, earning an average of 20% less than men (in the UK the gap is 7.7% (White, 2023)), and confront male-centric corporate cultures that impede career progression, especially for mothers (Dynan et al., 2022; Fioretti, 2023; Mackenzie, 2024).

The second hypothesis was not supported, as no interaction between culture and advert type was observed with respect to advertising effectiveness. This contrasts with the findings of

De Meulenaer et al. (2017), who identified that consumers' responses to gender-stereotyped adverts were influenced by several cultural values (i.e., Hofstede's (2001) cultural dimensions: masculinity and power distance, and House et al.'s (2004) cultural dimensions: assertiveness and female role orientation). One possible explanation for the failure to replicate De Meulenaer et al.'s findings is that that the cultural differences between the UK and South Korea, while significant in other aspects, may not have been large enough to play a role in influencing responses to the advert types tested in this study. In addition, the present study employed more implicit stimulus manipulations by solely varying the model's role while keeping the background consistent across conditions, whereas De Meulenaer et al.'s (2017) study portrayed traditional and non-traditional female characters engaging in distinct activities within different settings (i.e., woman cleaning in a kitchen, woman working on a car in a garage).

Despite the absence of an interaction effect, our study identified significant main effects of culture and advert type on purchase intentions. Participants from South Korea had higher PI than British participants and those exposed to the non-traditional advertising condition (businesswoman) demonstrated higher PI than those in the traditional advert condition (mother). This finding replicates Åkestam et al. (2017), where femverts which challenged female role stereotypes elicited more positive ad responses (higher ad and brand attitudes) compared to traditional gender advertising portrayals. In addition, the identification of a main effect of culture on advertising effectiveness is consistent with Frith and Mueller's (2010) suggestion that individuals may interpret advertisements by associating them with their cultural background and the communal values they share with others. These findings suggest that cultural background and advert type independently influence consumers' purchase intentions, underscoring the importance of considering both factors in advertising strategies targeting diverse cultural audiences.

Finally, the results indicated that attitudes towards motherhood scores were indeed positively correlated with the advertising effectiveness of traditional adverts; in terms of both purchase intentions (PI) and attitude towards brand (AB), supporting the third hypothesis This suggests that individuals with more positive attitudes towards motherhood were more receptive to traditional advertisements. However, the traditional female role was depicted using a mother; thus, further investigation is necessary to ascertain whether this correlation remains consistent when employing alternative depictions of traditional female roles.

In the non-traditional advert condition, no significant correlations were found between attitudes towards motherhood scores (Fertility and Social Identity) and either PI or AB, except for a significant positive correlation between the Hindrance scale and PI. This suggests that individuals who perceive childbearing as a greater hindrance may exhibit a higher propensity to contemplate purchasing the advertised product when it is promoted by women depicted in non-stereotypical roles. These findings align with congruity theory, which proposes that portrayals consistent with individuals' social and cognitive frameworks are more valued, and thus more impactful (Ko et al., 2015). Therefore, the portrayal of a woman in a non-traditional role may have resonated more with participants who held less favourable attitudes towards motherhood, leading to heightened advertising effectiveness in terms of purchase intentions.

Overall, these findings highlight the complex relationship between attitudes towards motherhood and advertising effectiveness, particularly in the context of traditional and nontraditional advertisements. While positive attitudes towards motherhood may enhance the effectiveness of traditional ads, the impact of negative attitudes towards motherhood on nontraditional ads appears to be less straightforward and warrants further investigation.

Limitations

One limitation of the present study is its focus on a single product category, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other consumer products. The study also relied on English-language stimulus materials and questionnaires, which may have introduced bias in multicultural contexts where English proficiency varies. Furthermore, the AFCS scale was originally developed using a sample of Swedish women and may not fully capture attitudes towards motherhood in non-Western cultural contexts. Finally, the predominantly White British sample may not adequately represent the diversity of attitudes within the wider UK female population, limiting the broader generalisability of the findings.
