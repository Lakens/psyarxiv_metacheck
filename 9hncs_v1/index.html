Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Much of human knowledge distills patterns that are consistent across many similar experiences over time, such as the typical layout of a bedroom. This is particularly true of word knowledge. The thousands of words we learn form a stable, interconnected network of word meanings that capture regularities in the way words are typically combined to express ideas, such as that "oranges" are something you can "eat" and similar to "apples". Yet, we know surprisingly little about accumulating knowledge from regularities over time, especially in childhood when building knowledge is paramount. Here, we investigated 4-6-year-old children's abilities to accumulate interconnected word knowledge from regularities experienced repeatedly over a month. Younger children appeared to relearn the regularities anew at each exposure, whereas older children accumulated increasingly strong connections across encounters. These findings point to substantial developmental changes in gradual learning processes that build knowledge over time.

[div-01]

Childhood is a time of building a large, stable foundation of semantic knowledge about the world, from everyday routines (e.g., getting ready to go to school) to conceptual understanding (e.g., birds have wings) and facts about the environment (e.g., food is kept in kitchens). Words make up a critical part of this knowledge by anchoring knowledge in the mind (Garrard, Ralph, Patterson, Pratt, & Hodges, 2005; Lupyan & Thompson-Schill, 2012) and providing the medium for communicating it with each other. Building word knowledge requires learning not just individual words, but also connections between them, such as that "oranges" are "yummy", something you can "eat", and similar to "apples". These semantic connections pervade all aspects of language fluency. For example, connections fill in the blanks common in everyday language, such as comprehending that "I'd love some of those oranges, they're so yummy" expresses a desire to "eat" oranges even though eating was never mentioned (Ouellette, 2006; Perfetti, 2007; Tannenbaum, Torgesen, & Wagner, 2006). Semantic connections likewise fuel the limitless generativity of language by allowing us to generalize across words similar in meaning (e.g., Colunga & Smith, 2005), such as our ability to discuss "ripe apples" even if we have previously only heard about "ripe oranges". Yet, despite the importance of these connections, we understand little about the development of semantic learning processes that build interconnected knowledge.

Intuitively, building stable semantic knowledge involves picking up on consistent regularities (e.g., noticing that getting dressed always happens before going to school). When it comes to word knowledge, there is an emerging consensus that regularities of word co-occurrence constitute a potentially powerful source for semantic learning (Fourtassi, 2020; Huebner & Willits, 2018; Landauer & Dumais, 1997; Unger, Yim, Savic, Dennis, & Sloutsky, 2023). Indeed, the remarkable successes of Large Language Models amply demonstrate the wealth of interconnected word knowledge that can be derived just from regularities in the way words occur with other words (Mikolov, Chen, Corrado, & Dean, 2013; Petroni et al., 2019; Webb, Holyoak, & Lu, 2023). For example, a learner could connect "orange" and "apple" to "yummy" based on the tendency of these words to directly cooccur with each other. Moreover, a learner could integrate information across these direct co-occurrence regularities to connect "orange" to "apple" based on their shared pattern of cooccurrence with "yummy". This distributional hypothesis (illustrated in Figure 1) highlights how direct co-occurrence regularities may foster connections between words that can be combined in meaningful expressions, and shared co-occurrence may foster connections between words similar in meaning. Indeed, evidence suggests that child language input is rich in regularities in which words that can be meaningfully combined tend to directly co-occur, and words similar in meaning tend to share co-occurrence and often also directly co-occur (such as "dog" and "cat"; Fourtassi, 2020; Huebner & Willits, 2018; Li, Farkas, & MacWhinney, 2004; Peters & Borovsky, 2019; Unger et al., 2023).

Critically, for children to acquire interconnected word knowledge from these regularities, they must be able to learn connections from language experienced on different occasions over time. In just a single experience (such as a conversation or story), words might only co-occur once or a few times. Moreover, even repeated co-occurrences might be idiosyncratic. For example, a child might hear a story about a mouse who tries to eat a big apple in which "mouse" and "apple" repeatedly co-occur, but then never hear "mouse" and "apple" together again. To instead pick up on co-occurrences that are consistent over time and contexts, a child must possess semantic learning mechanisms that at minimum: (A) retain some trace of past experiences, and (B) build upon these past traces from subsequent experiences (McClelland & Rogers, 2003).

It is hardly controversial that some degree of semantic learning must begin early in childhood, as even young children have begun to learn words (Bergelson & Swingley, 2012 , 2015) and connections between them (Arias-Trejo & Plunkett, 2009 , 2013; Styles & Plunkett, 2009; Willits, Wojcik, Seidenberg, & Saffran, 2013) that play a vital role in organizing word knowledge (e.g., Borovsky, Ellis, Evans, & Elman, 2016). Indeed, some have proposed that a semantic learning system that builds knowledge from repeated experiences over time is the foundational memory system of the mind and emerges early in child development (Gómez & Figure 1. Edgin, 2016; Newcombe, Benear, Ngo, & Olson, 2024; Ngo, Benear, Popal, Olson, & Newcombe, 2021). Yet, we have surprisingly little insight into the emergence of learning that accumulates knowledge across experiences. Prior investigations into children's sensitivity to cooccurrence regularities have instead focused on learning from a single one-off experience (e.g. Hay, Pelucchi, Estes, & Saffran, 2011; Saffran, Aslin, & Newport, 1996; Savic, Unger, & Sloutsky, 2022; Wojcik & Saffran, 2015). Moreover, the few studies of learning over time in development have primarily compared adults to older (8-12-year-old) children on a disparate variety of learning phenomena (Bishop, Barry, & Hardiman, 2012; James, Gaskell, & Henderson, 2019; Smalle, Page, Duyck, Edwards, & Szmalec, 2018). Although these studies intriguingly suggest that late childhood may be a period when the benefits of repeated experience are particularly strong, late childhood is preceded by years of development. We therefore do not know whether knowledge accumulation develops early, or results from substantial developmental change. In what follows, we review evidence from single-session studies, highlight the critical importance of accumulating knowledge over time, and present a systematic investigation into its development.

Forming

Connections from Co-Occurrence Regularities

To assess connections formed from direct and shared co-occurrence, a typical approach is to present participants with triads of items in which one item (item A) separately co-occurs with two other items (B and C). Subsequently, participants are tested on their formation of connections between items that directly cooccur (A-B, A-C) and share patterns of cooccurrence (B-C) (Bauer & San Souci, 2010; Benton, 2026; Benton, Rakison, & Sobel, 2021; Miller-Goldwater, Cronin-Golomb, Porter, & Bauer, 2021; Savic et al., 2022; Schlichting, Guarino, Roome, & Preston, 2021; Schlichting, Guarino, Schapiro, Turk-Browne, & Preston, 2017; Yermolayeva & Rakison, 2016). Across studies that have used this approach, a consistent developmental picture has emerged. An ability to pick up on direct co-occurrence emerges early in childhood (see also Wojcik & Saffran, 2015) and continues to improve with age. If children learned like the language models highlighted above, then forming connections between items that co-occur would automatically foster connections between items that shared co-occurrence. Yet, this does not appear to be the case. Learning that both B and C directly co-occur with A is insufficient to also connect B and C. Instead, abilities to form connections from direct co-occurrence appear to be gradually supplemented by abilities to integrate across these connections to form shared co-occurrence connections. For example, in Savic et al.'s 2022 studies of forming connections between A-B-C triads of words embedded in sentences, even 4-year-olds formed connections based on direct co-occurrence, but only older children and adults also reliably integrated across direct co-occurrences to form connections based on shared co-occurrence. These findings reveal that it is not enough for regularities to be part of everyday experience: instead, abilities to form connections from these regularities must also develop.

While these studies shed light on forming connections from a single experience, building interconnected word knowledge is a necessarily protracted process. As explained above, any one experience may contain few co-occurrences, and even repeated co-occurrences within an experience may be idiosyncratic. Therefore, semantic learning processes that build knowledge from regularities should accumulate such knowledge as regularities are experienced repeatedly across experiences. Indeed, it is possible that failure to engage such learning processes accounts for the relatively poor learning in young children observed in prior single-session studies (particularly for connections based on shared co-occurrence), such that even young children might learn well given multiple experiences with the same regularities. Yet we know little about the development of semantic learning processes that accumulate word knowledge over time.

Because some semantic knowledge begins to emerge even in infancy, it has been posited that semantic learning is robust from early in development (Gómez & Edgin, 2016; Newcombe et al., 2024; Ngo et al., 2021). However, few studies have actually examined semantic learning in childhood. In apparent support of early semantic learning, a handful of studies point to better knowledge accumulation in late childhood (8-12 years) than in adults (e.g., James et al., 2019; Smalle et al., 2018). Yet, these studies focused on older children with years of development behind them. Moreover, these studies only examined children's knowledge for the material that they directly experienced, and not abilities to build knowledge across experiences. In contrast, one recent study comparing knowledge accumulation in younger children to adults points to substantial developmental change. Savic et al. (2022) compared the strength of connections formed in 4-year-olds after hearing the stories embedded with A-B-C triads across multiple days to those formed in adults after a single day. Although multiple repetitions led 4-year-olds to form direct co-occurrence connections similar to strength to those formed from one session in adults, unlike adults, they failed to form shared co-occurrence connections. This finding points to a failure in young children to capitalize on opportunities to build semantic knowledge over time. However, because the focus of the Savic et al. (2022) study was on contrasting the formation of direct and shared co-occurrence connec-tions in children and adults, it was not designed to illuminate the development of knowledge accumulation. Specifically, this study contrasted two different amounts of exposure at two distant points in development: a single session of exposure in adults versus repeated exposure over days in 4-year-olds. Furthermore, the task for each session probed only the connections that spontaneously came to mind, rather than the full set of direct and shared A-B, A-C and B-C connections that could be formed, and a targeted evaluation of shared co-occurrence connections only took place after the final session. Moreover, there was no assessment of whether young children and adults similarly understood the task. Therefore, this study does not provide a systematic investigation of the development of accumulating knowledge of connections across experiences. Together, contrasting these few studies with younger and older children hints that semantic learning may undergo substantial developmental change, but does not reveal the nature of this change.

Present Study

The present study aimed to illuminate the development of a fundamental property of semantic learning: the accumulation of knowledge from regularities experienced over time. We tackled this aim using a version of the A-B-C approach described above in which children were repeatedly exposed to direct and shared co-occurrence regularities over time, embedded in stories that children heard in three sessions across one month. In contrast with the handful of prior studies on learning over time, we examined the accumulation of knowledge from one session to the next (c.f. Savic et al., 2022) and how this accumulation may change across childhood (c.f. Bishop et al., 2012; James et al., 2019; Savic et al., 2022; Smalle et al., 2018). We focused on developmental changes between the ages of four and six because it is a period of substantial changes in children's interconnected word knowledge (Crowe & Prescott, 2003; Storm, 1980; Unger, Fisher, Nugent, Ventura, & MacLellan, 2016) and abilities to form connections from a single bout of exposure to regularities (Miller-Goldwater et al., 2021; Savic et al., 2022; Schlichting et al., 2021).

From previous studies of learning from a single bout of exposure, we expected that the very first session would reveal an early-emerging ability to form connections from direct cooccurrences that improves with age and is gradually supplemented by an ability to integrate connections from shared co-occurrence. Critically, our design allowed us to investigate developmental changes in building and strengthening these connections with repeated exposure over time. Such developmental changes would point to the maturation of semantic learning systems responsible for extracting knowledge from everyday regularities.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 4, 5, and 6-year-olds recruited in the US via social media posts. Following exclusions based on performance on "control" trials assessing attentiveness and task com-prehension (see below), final sample sizes were: 27 4-year-olds (range = 4.01 -4.92, M = 4.48, SD = 0.26), 29 5-year-olds (range = 5.00 -5.97, M = 5.549, SD = 0.28), and 32 6-year-olds (range = 6.03 -6.96, M = 6.46, SD = 0.31). The proportion of children who failed to meet the criteria for attentiveness and task comprehension was higher for younger children, such that the numbers of participants excluded were 16 four-year-olds, 8 five-year-olds and 2 six-yearolds. The demographic makeup was 74% White, 16% Multiracial, 6% Black, 2% Asian and 2% unspecified, of whom 57% were female, 41% male and 2% other or unspecified.

Materials

Triads. The primary materials were two triads of words (Figure 2 A.) Each triad contained a novel pseudoadjective (e.g., "foobly") paired with a familiar noun (e.g., "apple") and a novel pseudonoun (e.g., "mipp"). Thus, each triad contained two pairs of words embedded in a short story as described below, for a total of four word pairs.

Story. Word pairs from triads were embedded within sentences that were combined to form a short story (Figure 2 B). Sentences were uninformative about the meanings of novel pseudowords, such as "Yesterday I saw a foobly apple". Thus, the only information about the con-nections between triad pseudowords and their meanings came from the regularities with which triad words were used in sentences. The four pairs were each embedded within 10 different sentences (40 sentences in total).

Pilot work suggested that children in the youngest age group (age 4) might struggle to learn even the simpler connections based on direct co-occurrence when all four word pairs were mingled throughout the story. The simplified variant of the task that most consistently led to above-floor performance in 4-year-old pilot participants presented the story in two non-counterbalanced parts. Part 1 presented 20 sentences containing the pairs in which the pseudoadjective directly co-occurred with the familiar noun, and Part 2 presented 20 sentences containing pairs in which the pseudoadjective directly co-occurred with the pseudonoun. All sentences were recorded by a female native English speaker.

Muffled Test Sentences.

We developed an additional set of sentences for use in a "Muffled Test" designed to test whether children formed connections based on direct co-occurrence (see Procedure and Figure 3). These sentences followed the logic of commonly used sentence completion tasks, in which one word in a sentence is blank, and participants infer the missing word (e.g., Bloom & Fischler, 1980). Muffled Test sentences were similar to the story sentences, but in each sentence, one of the words in a pair was masked by a static sound, as in "I'd love to see a [STATIC] mipp". As described in the Procedure, children were asked to choose the masked word from two options. We developed four sentences to test the formation of connections between each pair (16 sentences in total), in which half masked the pseudoadjective and half masked the familiar or pseudonoun.

Label Test Pictures.

The "Label Test" was designed to test whether participants connected the familiar noun to both (a) the pseu-doadjective in the same triad based on direct co-occurrence, and (b) the pseudonoun in the same triad based on shared co-occurrence. We used four pictures of each familiar noun from the triads (i.e., apple and horse). As described below and in Figure 3, each picture was presented with one prompt to assess connections based on direct co-occurrence, and another prompt to assess connections based on shared co-occurrence.

Control Items. Importantly, we developed a set of "control" items for both the Muffled and Labeled tests to check that participants were attentive and understood the tasks. Recall that each test assessed whether children formed connections between a pseudoadjective and a noun in the same triad, or a pseudonoun and a noun in the same triad. To match this structure, control items consisted of a familiar adjective and two familiar nouns that are already likely to be connected in young children's vocabularies, such as "furry"-"dog" and "cat"-"dog". Items were high-frequency words that reliably co-occur in children's everyday language input (see Supplemental Materials). As shown in Figure 4, control items for Muffled Test were presented during the Muffled Test Warmup, whereas control items for the Label Test were interleaved with test items during testing.

Procedure

Participants completed three sessions on separate days within one month (with an average gap of 6 days between sessions). Sessions followed the sequence shown in Figure 4, in which children were exposed to regularities in language and their formation of connections was assessed. Sessions were hosted on the online Gorilla platform, took place on Zoom and were guided by an experimenter.

Introduction and Muffled Test Warmup. Participants were introduced to Izzy, a cartoon alien who tells travel stories. Participants then completed a warmup version of the Muffled Test that was subsequently used to assess children's formation of connections based on direct co-occurrence (see below). The warmup consisted entirely of control trials made up of familiar words. Children were first told that Izzy was going to say some things over the radio in her spaceship, but that the radio sometimes makes a funny sound. The experimenter would give the child two word choices, and the child would pick the one that Izzy probably said when the radio made the funny sound. The experimenter then played an example sentence, in which Izzy said "My mom saw a [STATIC] butterfly", and the experimenter explained that if the word choices were "pretty" and "sharp", the best answer would be "pretty", because "pretty butterfly" makes sense. Children then completed four trials of this warmup version of the Muffled Task using Control Item familiar words, such as hearing "I'd really love a [STATIC] cookie" and being asked whether the muffled word was "yummy" or "wet".

Story and Muffled Test. Participants were told that Izzy would tell them a travel story using some fun alien words. Participants then completed this phase in two parts. The Part 1 Story and Muffled Test presented and assessed connections between the pseudoadjective and familiar noun from the two triads, and Part 2 presented and assessed connections between the pseudoadjective and pseudonoun from the two triads. Parts 1 and 2 had the same format. The experimenter first played audio clips of the pseudowords used in that part, and prompted the child to repeat them. Next, participants heard the story and completed the Muffled Test. Each part of Muffled Test contained 8 trials in the same format as the warmup (see Figure 3). In each trial, children heard one sentence in which an audible word from a triad was accompanied by an audio clip of static. Children were prompted to choose between a word from the same triad as the audible word, and a word from a different triad. For example, when the familiar noun was audible, the choices were the pseudoadjective from the same versus different triad. Accordingly, the Muffled Test assessed connections based on direct co-occurrence of words from the same triad.

Label Test. For the label test, the experimenter explained that Izzy had taken pictures on her travels, and asked the child to help Izzy describe them. On each trial, participants saw a picture and were asked to label it with one of two word options: either one of the two pseudoadjectives, or one of the two pseudonouns. Trials in which the two options were pseudoadjectives measured the formation of connections based on direct co-occurrence from the accuracy with which participants label pictures of a familiar noun (e.g., apple) using the pseudoadjective with which it directly co-occurred (e.g., "foobly"). The same logic applies for measuring the formation of connections based on shared cooccurrence from accuracy on pseudonoun trials (e.g., using "mipp" to label apples). To ensure that children were asked to use the pseudowords as labels in a way that is grammatical in English, for pseudoadjectives, children were asked, "Is this one [pseudoadjective 1] or [pseudoadjective 2]?", and for pseudonouns, children were asked, "Is this one like a [pseudonoun 1] or a [pseudonoun 2]?". Control trials used the same format and prompted children to label pictures with familiar adjectives and nouns, such as labeling a dog as "furry" versus "yummy", and like a "cat" versus "ice cream". Participants completed 8 trials measuring connections based on direct and shared co-occurrence, and 6 control trials.

Accumulating Connections Over Time

A primary goal of the present study was to investigate the development of accumulating knowledge from exposure to co-occurrence regularities over time. We therefore examined the effects of Age (4-, 5-, or 6-year-old) and Session (1, 2, or 3) and their interaction for each of the measures of connections that could be formed based on co-occurrence (with control trial performance included as a covariate). The study included four such measures, depicted schematically in Figure 2, right panel. Figure 5 depicts performance on these measures across sessions in each age group. For each measure, interactions between Age and Session were of particular interest because they would reveal that the accumulation of knowledge changes with development. All interactions below also emerged when age was treated as a continuous variable (see Supplemental Materials and code on OSF). As shown in Table 1, for three of the four measurements, the analyses revealed significant main effects of Age, Session, and their interaction. The exception was the measurement of direct co-occurrence connections between the pseudoadjective and familiar noun in the Label Test, in which accuracy improved with Age and marginally improved across Session. Inspection of Figure 5 suggests that this exception occurred because 6-year-olds were already near ceiling from Session 1 on this measurement, so did not have room to improve across Sessions.

For the three out of four measurements that yielded interactions between Age and Session, we conducted follow-up tests to evaluate whether the strength of connections improved across Sessions within each Age. Building Shared Co-Occurrence Connections

As described in the Introduction, forming connections based on direct co-occurrence is a prerequisite for forming connections based on shared co-occurrence. However, findings from single-session studies suggest that forming connections based on shared co-occurrence further depends on developing abilities to integrate across direct co-occurrence connections.

The present study allowed us to gain further insight into the prerequisites for forming connections based on shared co-occurrence. Here, shared co-occurrence connections could be formed based on the direct co-occurrence of a pseudoadjective with both a familiar noun and a pseudonoun (Figure 3). We thus tested whether the strength of shared co-occurrence connections was predicted by: (1) the strength of direct co-occurrence connections measured in the same session, (2) repeated exposure to regularities across sessions, and (3) age. This model was specified as: shared ∼ direct pseudo-familiar + direct pseudo-pseudo + session + age

The strength of shared co-occurrence connections was predicted by all factors (ps < .05) except direct co-occurrence connections between the pseudoadjective and familiar noun (p = .24). Follow-up holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated that the effect of age was driven by a significant improvement in shared co-occurrence connections from age 5 to 6 (p < .001), whereas ages 4 and 5 did not significantly differ (p = .996; see Supplemental Materials for further exploratory analyses of change with age). Thus, shared co-occurrence connections may emerge from the conjunction of direct co-occurrence connections, repeated exposure, and developmental improvements in abilities to integrate across direct co-occurrence.

General Discussion

Human word knowledge is fundamentally interconnected. The remarkable successes of language models have shown that in principle, much of the richly interconnected word knowledge that humans develop can be derived from co-occurrence regularities abundant in everyday language (Landauer & Dumais, 1997; Petroni et al., 2019; Webb et al., 2023). Therefore, like language models, children might also build richly interconnected word knowledge from regularities in everyday language. To capitalize on these regularities, children must retain what they have learned from the language they experience on one day and build upon it from the language they experience on another (McClelland & Rogers, 2003). For the first time, we show that abilities to build up interconnected word knowledge with experience change substantially over childhood.

Children in the youngest age group did form some connections starting from their first exposure, but showed no evidence of building upon these connections with repeated exposures. With age, children formed increasingly strong connections from one exposure to the next. Although younger children might accumulate stronger connections with the many hours of language they experience daily over weeks, months and years, the current findings indicate that this accumulation becomes faster and more robust with age. Therefore, the increasingly interconnected word knowledge that emerges in development may come in part from the interplay between repeated exposures to regularities of word use and developmental improvements in abilities to capitalize on these exposures.

Accumulating Knowledge

The present findings shed new light on the development of learning processes that build knowledge over time. The interconnected word knowledge studied here is part of the stable base of semantic knowledge about the world that is amassed over the course of childhood and into adulthood (McClelland & Rogers, 2003; Tulving, 1972). Acquiring this knowledge is commonly attributed to a learning system that is inherently gradual. For example, the popular Complementary Learning Systems theory (Ku-maran, Hassabis, & McClelland, 2016; McClelland, McNaughton, & O'Reilly, 1995) proposes that experiences are processed in two learning systems: a fast, episodic system that rapidly stores individual experiences, and a slow semantic system that gradually picks up on regularities that are consistent across experiences. The semantic system is characterized as having particular features that allow it to accumulate a stable knowledge base rather than memories for idiosyncratic experiences, including a slow learning rate and a dependence on consolidation in memory between one experience and the next.

When it comes to development, there are multiple proposals that the semantic system emerges and matures relatively early in childhood (Gómez & Edgin, 2016; Ngo et al., 2021). This idea is grounded in evidence that even infants and toddlers are sensitive to some kinds of statistical regularities (e.g., Saffran et al., 1996; Wojcik & Saffran, 2015) and begin to accumulate some semantic knowledge (e.g., learn some words and semantic connections between them, Arias-Trejo & Plunkett, 2009; Bergelson & Aslin, 2017; Bergelson & Swingley, 2012). However, although semantic learning processes might begin to emerge early on, prior evidence does not illuminate its development. Prior evidence is particularly uninformative about the development of semantic learning that unfolds over real-world timescales, such as learning regularities that are repeatedly experienced from one day to another. Thus, the present study provides new evidence that slow semantic learning systems continue to develop substantially across childhood, even after the age of four.

From Direct to Shared Co-Occurrence

As in single-session studies of both language and other domains (Bauer & San Souci, 2010; Benton, 2026; Benton et al., 2021; Miller-Goldwater et al., 2021; Savic et al., 2022; Schlichting et al., 2021 Schlichting et al., , 2017;; Yermolayeva & Rakison, 2016), the present findings revealed an early-emerging sensitivity to direct co-occurrence (learning A-B and A-C) that improves and is supplemented by sensitivity to shared co-occurrence with age (learning A-C). Although the exact ages when these sensitivities emerge and become robust may vary across domains (either due to real domain differences or more superficial differences between paradigms, such as number of connections to be learned), evidence consistently points to a later-emerging ability to form shared co-occurrence connections for which direct co-occurrence connections are necessary but insufficient. However, the mechanisms underlying this later-emerging ability remain unclear. Literature across domains has posited mechanisms of two broad types: (1) forming similar mental representations of items that share co-occurrence, and (2) chaining together direct co-occurrence connections that share an overlapping item (e.g., Banino, Koster, Hassabis, & Kumaran, 2016; Benton, 2026; Hall, Mitchell, Graham, & Lavis, 2003; Kumaran et al., 2016; McClelland et al., 1995; Schapiro, Turk-Browne, Botvinick, & Norman, 2017; Zeithamova & Bowman, 2020). Alternatively, in the present study, integration could also involve a more language-specific ability to first infer meanings for the pseudoadjective (e.g., that "foobly" is a characteristic of fruits similar to "sweet" and "juicy" based on its direct cooccurrence with "apple") and then infer meanings for the pseudonouns (e.g., that "mipp" is a fruit based on its direct co-occurrence with "foobly" that has taken on a fruit-characteristic meaning) (e.g., Lany & Saffran, 2010). Illuminating the mechanisms underpinning shared co-occurrence connections and whether they are the same or different across domains therefore represents a key target for future research.

Development of Interconnected Knowledge

The developmental changes in learning over time observed in the present study may help explain the way interconnected word knowledge evolves over childhood. Early on, semantic connections often link words that directly co-occur, including "thematically" related words different in meaning that are associated with each other such as "dog" and "bone", and many "taxonomically" related words similar in meaning such as "dog" and "cat" (Blaye & Bonthoux, 2001; Fenson, Vella, & Kennedy, 1989; Fisher, 2010; Lin & Murphy, 2001; Lucariello, Kyratzis, & Nelson, 1992; Lucariello & Nelson, 1985; Matlen, Fisher, & Godwin, 2015; Sloutsky, Yim, Yao, & Dennis, 2017; Unger, Savic, & Sloutsky, 2020; Unger, Vales, & Fisher, 2020; Unger et al., 2023). With age, these connections are supplemented by taxonomic connections between words that may not directly co-occur with each other, but are likely to share co-occurrence with other words (Lucariello et al., 1992; Unger, Savic, & Sloutsky, 2020; Wojcik & Kandhadai, 2019). There is a clear alignment between this pattern in semantic connections and the direct-before-shared pattern in semantic learning processes observed in the present study. Thus, the developmental changes in learning processes observed here may in part drive the typical development of interconnected word knowledge.

Conclusion

Everyday experience is rich in regularities that can support learning meaningful connections between words. To benefit from these regularities, a child must retain some trace of past experiences and build upon these traces with subsequent experiences. The present findings reveal that such semantic learning develops substantially across childhood. Thus, richly interconnected word knowledge may emerge from the interplay between repeated opportunities for learning and developing abilities to capitalize on these opportunities.
