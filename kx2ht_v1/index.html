What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Abstract

Diet can impact on physical and mental health, but to what extent do people believe this? Health psychology theory proposes that beliefs about the impact diet has on health likely shape behaviour. Yet, diet-health beliefs have not been systematically studied and methodology used in existing research is limited.

This two-part study aimed to explore UK adults' beliefs about the impact diet has on short-term and long-term physical and mental health conditions, and to conduct initial development and validation of a scale to measure diet-health beliefs.

An initial 20-item scale was completed by a general population sample of 399 UK adults online. Results show that participants had inconsistent beliefs about the impact diet has on long-term and short-term physical health outcomes. Participants tended not to believe that diet impacts mental health.

Next, exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis of survey responses indicated a 15-item two-factor (physical health, mental health) scale provided the most statistically appropriate measure of diet-health beliefs. The scale was found to be reliable at both full (ω = ₜ 0.93) and subscale level (factor 1: physical health, ωₜ = 0.90, factor 2: mental health, ωₜ = 0.93). Convergent, divergent and test-retest validity assessments confirmed the scale had acceptable validity. The scale could be used to investigate whether beliefs about the impact of diet on health shape dietary behaviour and investigate the effectiveness of interventions designed to change diet-health beliefs.

1. Introduction

A strong body of evidence has long supported the fundamental role that diet plays in maintaining and promoting physical health. Eating a healthy diet can help prevent future health problems (Cena & Calder, 2020). Conversely, eating an unhealthy diet is a major risk factor for developing short-term health conditions and long-term non-communicable diseases (NCDs) (World Health Organization, 2026). In the short-term, poor diet can impact digestive, metabolic and immune function, energy levels, mood and cognitive performance (Kandel, 2019; Loizidou et al., 2025; Semnani-Azad et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2021). In the long-term, poor diet increases the likelihood of developing cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, overweight or obesity (Kandel, 2019). Having obesity is also a risk factor for several cancers, kidney disease, and respiratory and musculoskeletal disorders (Ansari et al., 2020; Firth et al., 2019).

Emerging evidence shows that the connection between mental health and diet is bi-directional. Mental health conditions have been found to negatively impact dietary quality (Firth et al., 2018; Hill et al., 2022), and evidence on the impact of diet on mental health has also emerged over recent years with systematic reviews and meta-analyses finding associations between poor diet and mental health conditions including depression, anxiety, distress and schizophrenia (Chen et al., 2021; Selvaraj et al., 2022). Evidence from systematic reviews shows that following a healthy diet, particularly a diet containing fruit and vegetables, can lower the risk of developing depressive symptoms or clinical depression (Selvaraj et al., 2022). Having obesity is also a risk factor for developing anxiety and depression (Ansari et al., 2020). As a result, public health guidance provided to the general public now outlines how diet affects both physical and mental health (Mental Health America, 2026; Mental Health Foundation, 2026; NHS Professionals, 2026).

Beliefs are distinct to nutrition knowledge and literacy

Nutrition knowledge refers to the understanding of dietary guidelines, the nutrient content of food and how these impact health, and is primarily based on information acquisition (Fry & Anderson, 2025). Research suggests a small positive association between nutrition knowledge and healthier food intake (Spronk et al., 2014), particularly fruit and vegetable intake (Spronk et al., 2014; Wardle et al., 2000). Nutritional literacy, the ability to understand, use and apply nutritional knowledge, has also been examined as a factor influencing dietary choices. Systematic reviews suggest that nutritional literacy positively impacts dietary behaviour (Carmona et al., 2026; Steinke et al., 2026).

Although the psychological concepts of nutrition knowledge, literacy and beliefs are inter-related, they are different constructs. Beliefs can be thought of as opinions about diet and the strength of conviction in these opinions. As such, beliefs affect perceptions about the world, influence conceptual change, and alongside other factors can be influenced by knowledge (Boldrin & Mason, 2009). People with greater nutritional knowledge may therefore have stronger beliefs about the impact of diet on health. However, people with stronger beliefs in the diet-health connection may also be more likely to seek out nutrition knowledge and literacy skills.

Beliefs are a fundamental element of several health psychology theories (Wardle & Steptoe, 1991). The Common Sense Model proposes that people form perceptions of illness through five domains including beliefs about the cause of the condition, the potential timeline of the condition and whether the condition can be controlled (Leventhal et al., 2016). The Health Belief Model (HBM) and Protection Motivation Theory propose that the likelihood of engaging in health promoting behaviours depends not only on cues to action (e.g. advice from a doctor), but also beliefs about health conditions (Walker, 1999). Consistent with this, a study in US adults (N = 607) investigating beliefs in the association between diet and cancer found that those who believed in a strong association between diet and cancer decreased their daily consumption of dietary fat (-1.20%) and increased their daily fibre intake (+0.69g), compared to people with little or no belief in the association (fat -0.21%, fibre -0.57g, p<.05) (Patterson et al., 1996).

Limitations of existing research

Previous related research has tended to focus on measuring knowledge about diet and health, as opposed to measuring personal beliefs about the impact diet has on health. For example, a diet-health awareness test assessed knowledge of 27 diet-disease relationships by asking participants to link specific nutrients to the development of specific diseases (Sapp & Jensen, 1997), but did not examine how strongly participants believe these nutrients affects disease risk. Similarly, a more recent study examining the association between the six constructs of the HBM and the likelihood of eating nutrientrich foods measured diet-health 'beliefs' through nutritional knowledge questions (Glick et al., 2024), as opposed to personal beliefs. Whilst such studies are informative, a focus solely on knowledge of scientific associations between diet and health does not allow for consideration of how strongly people believe that diet may impact health.

Few validated scales measure diet-health beliefs comprehensively. In studies to date, this construct is commonly measured using single items and/or, by encompassing multiple health conditions within the same question (Anthony et al., 2012; Deshpande et al., 2009) and/or using limited dichotomised response formats (e.g. 'yes' vs. 'no'), without validation. Single item measures are not recommended in psychometric testing (Irwing & Hughes, 2018) and measurement of the strength of belief (as opposed to mere awareness of a diethealth relationship) is of importance (Bosnjak et al., 2020), which most research to date has not considered.

The most widely used scale relating to dietary knowledge and beliefs is the General Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire for Adults (Parmenter & Wardle, 1999). This now dated measurement tool includes a subscale on diet-disease relationships which is largely drawn from items measuring knowledge about diet-disease relationships, as opposed to personal beliefs about the impact diet has on health. Furthermore, the subscale focuses on knowledge relating to a limited number of long-term health conditions (e.g. cancer) and does not consider shorter-term physical health or mental health outcomes.

To our knowledge, no research has examined beliefs about the impact of diet on both short-term and long-term health outcomes. Some research suggests that the extent to which people prioritise future over immediate outcomes predicts diet quality, which suggests that measuring beliefs about both shortterm and long-term impacts of diet on health would be valuable, especially as many diet-related health impacts are asymptomatic in the short-term (Garza et al., 2013). Furthermore, although more recent research on diet-health beliefs has included limited measures of mental health (Huang et al., 2025), no validated scales have examined or compared beliefs about the impact of diet on both physical vs. mental health outcomes. Given there is a growing body of evidence on the impact of diet on mental health (Chen et al., 2021; Firth et al., 2019), it will now be important to understand whether beliefs align with this.

In the present research, we collected and used new survey data to investigate the extent to which UK adults believe diet impacts a range of common shortterm and longer-term physical and mental health outcomes. Next, we used this survey data to conduct initial development and validation of a psychometric scale to measure beliefs about the impact of diet on health.

2.1.1 Diet-health belief survey: item generation

Potential survey items were collated from existing measures assessing dietdisease knowledge (Glick et al., 2024; Guan & Chen, 2024; Sapp & Jensen, 1997; Sapp & Weng, 2007; Smith et al., 2000). Collated items were assessed for relevance to short-term and long-term diet-related health conditions by three researchers at the University of Liverpool. Twenty items were included in the initial survey (see online supplementary materials, Table 1), assessing beliefs about long-term health impacts of diet (on physical health n=8, e.g. heart disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer, dementia, and on mental health n=3, e.g. anxiety, depression) and short-term health impacts of diet (on physical health n=5, e.g. cholesterol, blood pressure, digestive health and on mental health n=4, e.g. mood, stress, self-esteem). We defined long-term outcomes as those which would typically take 'multiple years or decades' to develop. Numbers of items reflected recommendations for 3-5 items per potential questionnaire variables/factors of interest (Swanson & Holton, 2005), as we reasoned that the scale may yield up to a four factor solution: mental vs. physical health outcomes crossed with short-term vs. longer-term health outcomes.

2.1.2 Diet-health belief survey: response scale

Participants were asked to rate their level of agreement with each of the items (e.g. 'The food a person eats influences their likelihood of developing heart disease in the long-term') using a seven-point Likert scale. The scale points were: strongly agree (7), agree (6), somewhat agree (5), not sure (4), somewhat disagree (3), disagree (2) and strongly disagree (1). A seven-point scale was used to be comparable to similar measures (Smith et al., 2000), and is considered preferable to a five-point scale when rating two opposing options (Boateng et al., 2018; Kyriazos & Stalikas, 2018). We measured beliefs about 'a person', as opposed to 'yourself' to reduce the length and complexity of item wording, and also to avoid confusion for participants who may have to consider both exacerbation of pre-existing health conditions alongside development of new conditions (Stefana et al., 2025).

We interpreted responses options of 'strongly agree' (7) and 'agree' (6) as participants believing with a high degree of certainty that a given health outcome is affected by diet. Similarly, for response options of 'strongly disagree' (1) and 'disagree' (2), we interpreted as participants believing with a high degree of certainty that a health outcome was not affected by diet.

2.2 Participants: recruitment and eligibility

The target sample was 400 participants, reflecting guidance of 5-10 participants per survey item for scale development (Boateng et al., 2018).

Participants were recruited via the data gathering platform Prolific (Prolific, 2025), and randomly selected from an existing sample of 1000 participants who had previously completed an online study 10 weeks earlier (Finlay, 2026), so that earlier collected participant information could be linked with survey responses.

The survey was administered as a questionnaire via the survey administration platform Qualtrics (Qualtrics, 2025). UK resident adults aged 18 years were ≥ eligible to take part. Pre-screening questions were used to exclude participants with previous or current eating disorders as they were not eligible to take part due to ethical considerations and potential differences to the general population. In the previously completed online study, participants were asked to report their height and weight (for calculation of BMI), gender, age, and education level as defined by the National Qualifications Framework (NQF), whereby NQF1= no formal qualifications, NQF2= GCSEs or equivalent, NQF3=

The target sample aimed to be representative of the UK population: approximately 50% male, 50% female (Gov.UK, 2023), and 40% with NQF4 or above (Office for National Statistics, 2023). Participants were reimbursed for their time through the Prolific platform at the recommended rate of £9 per hour.

4.1 Content validity assessment

The content validity of the scale items was assessed by an independent panel of five psychology researchers with experience in scale development (Boateng et al., 2018; Sireci, 1998). Panel members were asked to rate how relevant each of the 20 items was as part of a scale to measure beliefs about long-term and short-term impacts of diet on physical and mental health, and also how clear the wording of each item was, i.e. how easy it would be for participants to follow.

For relevance, validators were asked to score each item on a five-point scale, with the options 'not relevant', 'somewhat relevant', 'quite relevant', 'relevant' and 'highly relevant'. For clarity, validators were asked to score each item on a five-point scale, with the options 'not clear', 'somewhat clear', 'quite clear', 'clear' and 'very clear'. Item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated for each scale item, where the number of validators rating each item as relevant (by selecting 'quite relevant', 'relevant' or 'very relevant') was divided by the total number of validators. Scale-level content validity index was assessed by calculating average I-CVI, whereby the number of items considered relevant by all experts (rated as 'quite relevant', 'relevant' or 'highly relevant') was divided by the total number of items (Almanasreh et al., 2022).

As all items scored 0.78 for I-CVI and were deemed relevant, average I-≥ CVI for the diet-health belief scale was 1.0 ( 0.90 considered excellent) ≥ (Almanasreh et al., 2022). See online supplementary materials for full details.

4.2 Data Analysis

Data were analysed using the 'psych', 'REdaS' and 'lavaan' packages in R version 4.3.1. (R Core Team, 2021).

4.3 Data preparation

Sampling adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin statistic to determine if correlations among variables were compact enough to produce reliable factors, with >0.7 considered acceptable (Howard, 2016). Bartlett's Test for Sphericity was used to assess whether correlations between different variables were sufficient to run factor analysis, i.e. if correlations were significantly different from zero (p<0.01). The dataset was randomly divided into two groups for factor analysis, 50% of the responses were used for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and the remaining 50% were used for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

4.4 Exploratory factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis was used to determine the latent structure of the scale. Visual scree plot analysis was used alongside parallel analysis to determine the number of factors for extraction (Howard, 2016). EFA with oblique rotation was conducted. Satisfactory items loaded onto the primary factor above 0.40 and to alternative factors below 0.30 (Howard, 2016).

4.6 Internal reliability

McDonald's Omega was used to assess internal reliability, with values >0.7 considered acceptable (McNeish & Wolf, 2023).

4.7 Test-retest reliability

The diet-health belief scale was administered twice, two weeks apart, to a randomly selected 10% (n=40) of participants. The test-retest sample was randomly selected to avoid self-selection bias and to adhere with guidance for a test-retest sample sufficiently large enough to estimate intra-class correlation coefficient (Belzak & Lockwood, 2024). The intra-class correlation coefficient was used to compare initial and retest scores, with values of 0.60 considered ≥ good test-retest reliability (Koo & Li, 2016).

4.8 Convergent and discriminant validity assessments

Convergent validity was assessed using the health influences ranking task. We predicted that participants scoring higher on the diet-health belief scale should also rank 'the food you eat' as a more important influence on their own longterm health than other factors in the health influences ranking task. Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) was used to compare diet-health belief scale scores with health ranking task scores, with r 0.50 indicating a high positive ≥ correlation (Cohen, 2013).

To inform discriminant validity, a health fatalism assessment scale was also administered to participants (Shen et al., 2009). Fatalism is defined as the belief that death is inevitable when serious disease occurs (Powe & Finnie, 2003), feeling powerless or hopeless over life events, or 'passively denying personal control' (Neff & Hoppe, 1993). Although health fatalism and beliefs about diet and health are likely related, we assumed that the two are distinct constructs. Therefore, to confirm discriminant validity it was hypothesised that participants with more fatalistic views should have weaker beliefs about the impact of diet on health, but the size of this relationship would not be strong.

Discriminant validity was also assessed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which states that a construct should share more variance with its own indicators (Average Variance Extracted (AVE)) than it shares with any other construct (Hair et al., 2019).

5.3 Confirmatory factor analysis

Evaluation of modification indices suggested that two pairs of items (L9 and L10, S16 and S18) exhibited notable residual covariance (modification indices >17). Consistent with recommended practice for addressing localised model misfit, and as the item pairs overlapped conceptually, we allowed the error terms to correlate (i.e. their corresponding error covariances were freed) resulting in improved overall model fit (Saris et al., 2009). Confirmatory factor analysis model fit statistics indicated the 15-item model was a good (robust CFI = 0.962; robust TLI = 0.954; SRMR = 0.065) and acceptable fit (robust RMSEA = 0.06) (McNeish & Wolf, 2023). Standardised factor loadings were all significant (p<0.001) indicating that each item was an appropriate reflection of the relevant latent variable (Figure 1).

To enable calculation of strength of belief, a total diet-health belief score was calculated for each participant by totalling scores for all statements, and separate scores were calculated for both physical and mental health subscales. 5.4 Internal reliability

The scale demonstrated good reliability, McDonald's Omega total (ωₜ) for the full scale was above the acceptable value of 0.7 (ω = ₜ 0.93). Reliability statistics for each of the subscales were also above acceptable values, factor 1 (physical health) ωₜ = 0.91 and factor 2 (mental health) ωₜ = 0.93, suggesting that diethealth belief scale scores are reliable at both subscale and full-scale level.

5.5 Test-retest reliability

The diet-health belief scale was re-administered to a randomly selected 10% of the total sample (n = 40 participants), two weeks after initial completion. Testretest reliability was found to be good for the full scale (ICC = 0.67), and moderate for factor 1 (ICC = 0.56) and factor 2 (ICC = 0.56) (Koo & Li, 2016).

5.6 Convergent validity

Diet-health belief scale scores were compared with how strongly participants ranked diet as an influence on their own health in the 15-item ranking task.

There was a small positive correlation between full scale score and impact of diet on health ranking score, r(398) = 0.24, p <.001, (95% CI = 0.14 to 0.33). Both subscales showed similar patterns; factor 1 (physical health) demonstrated a small positive correlation, r(395) = 0.13, p = 0.007, (95% CI = 0.04 to 0.23) as did factor 2 (mental health), r(395) = 0.22, p = <.001, (95% CI = 0.13 to 0.32). These findings indicate that individuals who hold stronger diet-health beliefs also tend to rank diet as more influential for health outcomes than other factors, supporting convergent validity.

5.7 Discriminant validity

Diet-health belief scale scores were compared with health fatalism scores.

There was a negligible negative correlation between full scale score and fatalism score, r(398) -0.04, p = 0.39, (95% CI = -0.14 to 0.06). Factor 1 (physical health) demonstrated a small but significant negative correlation, r(395) = -0.11, p = 0.04, (95% CI = -0.20 to -0.01), indicating that stronger diet-health beliefs about impacts on physical health are modestly associated with lower fatalistic attitudes. Factor 2 (mental health) showed a negligible association, r(395) = 0.01, p = 0.85, (95% CI = -0.09 to 0.11). Findings for diet-physical health beliefs were therefore consistent with divergent validity predictions; individuals who believe diet influences physical health should less strongly endorse fatalistic views, supporting discriminant validity.

Discriminant validity was also evaluated using the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which states that a construct should share more variance with its own indicators (Average Variance Extracted (AVE)) than it shares with any other construct (Hair et al., 2019). AVE values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.50 for both factors (factor1 = 0.502; factor2 = 0.577), indicating that each latent construct explained more than half of the variance in its indicators. The latent correlation between the two factors was modest (r = 0.344), with a shared variance of 0.118. As the shared variance was substantially lower than the AVE for each factor, this suggests that although the two constructs are related, they are empirically distinct.

5.8 Beliefs by type and participant demographics

Consistent with our narrative description of findings in section 1, scores on the physical health subscale (M = 6.09 (±1.01)), were significantly higher than the mental health subscale (M = 4.51 (±1.38)), indicating that participants believed more strongly that diet affects physical than mental health (t(395) = 29.04, p < .001, (95% CI 1.48 to 1.69).

Scores from the total 15-item scale and two subscales were compared across demographic groups. There were no significant differences in diet-health belief scores by gender, ethnicity, level of education or BMI. Diet-health belief scores correlated significantly with age; younger participants demonstrated higher total diet-health belief scores than older participants, and diet-health belief scores reduced as each age category increased (r(394) = -0.12, p = 0.02 (95% CI -0.21 to -0.02)). This trend was replicated for mental health; scores on the mental health subscale (factor 2) also correlated significantly with age, with younger participants holding stronger beliefs about the impact of diet on mental health than older participants (r(394) = -0.20, p<.001 (95% CI -0.29 to -0.10). 6. Discussion

In the first part of the present research, we created and administered a 20item scale to examine beliefs about the impact of diet on multiple aspects of short-term and longer-term physical and mental health in a sample of UK adults. Participants tended to believe with a high degree of certainty that diet affects some longer-term physical health conditions such as heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity. Beliefs about diet's influence on day-to-day physical health (e.g. cholesterol, blood sugar, digestive health) were similarly strong. In contrast, beliefs about the impact of diet on short-term and longer-term mental health outcomes were far more variable and a large proportion of participants did not believe with any certainty that diet affects long-term mental health, particularly anxiety and depression.

Although participants tended to believe with certainty that diet affects physical health outcomes, there was significant variability across different outcomes. For example, fewer than 1 in 2 surveyed UK adults believed with a high degree of certainty that diet affects the likelihood of developing cancer.

Similarly, most participants did not believe with certainty that diet affects life expectancy. This potentially highlights a lack of understanding of how diet can impact cancer risk and lead to early mortality (Key et al., 2020; Steck & Murphy, 2020). Further, the lack of certainty regarding the impact of diet on longevity is an interesting contradiction to the certainty in beliefs about the impact of diet on multiple life-limiting physical health conditions such as heart disease and type 2 diabetes. This could be partly explained by the human tendency to be present-biased (Liu et al., 2014), faith in medical treatment, or a disconnect in knowledge that long-term physical health conditions lead to early mortality.

Participant beliefs about the impact of diet on mental health outcomes were less certain than those about physical health. For example, just over 1 in 4 adults believed with certainty that diet impacts longer-term mental health, with even fewer believing with certainty that diet impacts anxiety (12%), depression (17%) and stress (23%). Conversely, recent systematic reviews show associations between poor diet and mental health conditions (Chen et al., 2021; Firth et al., 2019) and mental health guidance provided to the general public highlights the importance of diet (Mental Health Foundation, 2026; NHS Professionals, 2026). These findings potentially highlight a lack of understanding and a need for further information or education on the role of diet on mental health (Chen et al., 2021; Firth et al., 2019).

Belief about the impact of diet on dementia were very mixed, with most participants (35%) unsure whether diet impacts the likelihood of developing dementia, and the number of people believing with certainty that diet impacts dementia risk almost exactly matching those disbelieving diet affects dementia risk (15% and 16% respectively). These findings highlight a lack of understanding of the likely role that diet plays in dementia risk (Arora et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2020), and are similar to findings of an Alzheimer's Research UK survey showing only 25% of adults listed diet as a risk factor for developing dementia (Alzheimer's Research UK, 2026). These findings may also reflect the differentiation in beliefs that participants have about diet's impact on physical vs. mental health. Whilst dementia was categorised as a long-term physical health impact during scale creation, factor analysis using participant survey responses showed that dementia clearly loaded onto the subscale relating to mental health. Therefore, participants may less strongly believe diet affects dementia because it is not typically thought of as a physical health condition.

Participants also completed a task in which they ranked diet alongside other influences on health. Although participants ranked diet quite highly (54% ranked it in the top five factors), they believed that smoking was a far greater influence on health (80% ranked it in the top five factors). Comparison of responses to beliefs about the impact of smoking on physical health to a similar question about diet produced similar results. This is a notable finding, given that recent evidence suggests that at population level, poor diet has overtaken smoking as the leading cause of preventable death, notably death attributable to cardiovascular disease (Hull et al., 2025; Norouzzadeh et al., 2024).

Use of illegal drugs and substances (60% of participants ranked it in the top five factors) and alcohol (59% of participants ranked it in the top five factors) were also considered similar or more important influences on health than diet.

Methodology used in previous research means direct comparison of specific diet-health beliefs is difficult. Nonetheless, questions previously used to assess diet-health awareness show similarly variable results to the findings presented here, although potentially highlight improvements over time. In assessments used during development of the General Nutritional Knowledge Questionnaire, knowledge of diet-disease relationships was below average in a group of students without specific dietetics qualifications (M = 6.2 / 20) (Parmenter & Wardle, 1999). A study in 2006 found diet-health awareness was average (M = 5.4 / 12) whilst perceived importance of eating a healthy diet was relatively high (M = 3.44 /4) (Sapp & Weng, 2007). A more recent study in a US adult population found responses to the question 'what I eat does not really affect my health' were moderate (M = 2.11, ±1.17 (whereby1 = strongly disagree and5 = strongly agree) (Glick et al., 2024).

The present research developed and validated a new 15-item scale to measure diet-health beliefs. Factor analysis did not support our original hypothesis of a four-factor structure (i.e. physical vs. mental health, long term vs. short term health impacts) as factor loadings did not differentiate between short-term and long-term health conditions. Exploratory factor analysis indicated that a two-factor solution (factor 1: physical health, factor 2: mental health) provided the most accurate representation of the data, with strong primary loadings and minimal cross-loading after the removal of five items. Confirmatory factor analysis further supported this structure, demonstrating good model fit and significant standardised loadings for all retained items. These findings suggest that diet-health beliefs are multidimensional, with individuals differentiating between physical and mental aspects of health when evaluating the impact of diet, but not necessarily shorter-vs. longer-term outcomes. The 15-item scale demonstrated excellent internal reliability, at full scale and subscale level. Evidence supported convergent validity; diet-health belief scores were positively associated with other ratings of how strongly diet influences health, aligning with theoretical expectations that individuals who recognise the health consequences of diet also tend to recognise other established physical health risks. Discriminant validity was also supported, with negligible or small correlations with health fatalism and Average Variance Extracted values exceeding shared variance between factors, indicating that the two constructs are empirically distinct. Test-retest reliability was good for the total scale, but only moderate for the individual sub-scales (ICCs = 0.56). This was lower than anticipated and may be explained by relative strength of diet-health beliefs being prone to some fluctuations over time. Further research examining test-retest reliability of the scale would now be informative. These initial development and validation results indicate that the diet-health belief scale could be used in future research to examine population variability in diet-health beliefs, factors that shape diet-health beliefs, the underlying reasons why diet-health beliefs vary from person-to-person and/or testing of interventions designed to change diet-health beliefs. Although a proportion of participants in the present study tended to agree that diet affects a range of health outcomes, there was considerable variability within and between individual health outcomes. It is therefore feasible that a significant subset of the population does not believe that diet affects health or does not strongly believe that diet has a meaningful impact on health. The scale could therefore also be used to examine whether individual differences in diet-health beliefs impact on behaviour and diet quality. Importantly, diet-health beliefs did not differ significantly across gender, ethnicity, and education level. Further research could explore whether stronger diet health-beliefs in younger age groups and higher BMI categories are replicated in larger samples. Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the sample varied by education level, age and gender, the majority of participants identified as white. Despite this being representative of the UK population, it limits generalisability to more ethnically diverse populations. The sample was randomly selected from participants that had previously completed an online study examining the impact of nutrition information labels on hypothetical food selection, and thus participants may have been more interested in diet and health than the general population. Because our focus was on scale development in the general population, we did not stratify recruitment by current physical or mental health condition. Whilst we would anticipate prevalence of individual physical and mental health conditions would be relatively low in the sampled population, we did not measure diagnosis of physical or mental health conditions in participants. Future work could address these limitations by purposefully sampling participants with different health conditions, in order to be able to formally compare whether beliefs about diet and health differ by health status. Further validation of this newly developed scale outside of the general population and in samples characterised by having existing physical and/or mental health conditions would also be of value.

The cross-sectional design used in the present research limits causal inference, preventing conclusions about the potential directionality of associations between diet-health beliefs and related constructs. Dependent on scale purpose, some psychometric scale development methodology recommends the use of first-person language (Stefana et al., 2025). It would therefore be informative to understand whether first vs. third-person anchoring affects responses for diet-health beliefs. Based on typical first vs. third-person effects (Fontaine & Smith, 1995), we would hypothesise that participants may be even less likely to believe diet impacts on health when reporting about one's own diet and health, as opposed to others (as in the present study).

These limitations highlight directions for future research. Given that most people in the UK do not meet national nutritional recommendations (Office for Health Improvements and Disparities, 2025) investigating how diet-health beliefs correlate with dietary choices could now be informative. Thus, an important next step would be to examine the scale's predictive validity, particularly in relation to real-world food purchasing, habitual dietary intake, eating out-of-home and responses to interventions designed to promote healthier eating such as nutrition labelling. As scale wording was assessed for clarity by psychology researchers, think-aloud work may also help to clarify that scale wording was understood as intended by participants, especially as terms such as 'mental wellbeing' may be open to individual interpretation (Padilla & Leighton, 2017).

Longitudinal research could explore how diet-health beliefs evolve over time and whether they shift in response to public health messaging, interventions, educational campaigns or changes in personal health status. Given the variability in beliefs about diet and mental health, qualitative work may help clarify how individuals conceptualise the relationship between diet and psychological wellbeing. Validation studies outside of the UK would help determine whether the two-factor structure is stable across different cultural and dietary contexts. Conclusion This study provides evidence that the newly developed Diet-Health Belief Scale is a reliable, valid, and conceptually coherent measure of beliefs about the influence of diet on physical and mental health. Participants tended to believe with some certainty that diet impacts multiple aspects of physical health, but did not tend to strongly believe that diet affects longevity and most mental health outcomes. Together, these findings indicate that the scale is well suited for use in future research and may serve as a valuable tool for understanding diet-health beliefs.
