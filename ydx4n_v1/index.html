To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 13 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

Whose deposit is each link? It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Whether metaphorical language is processed differently from literal language remains an open question: lesion and imaging studies have pointed to partly distinct neural substrates, whereas reading-time studies have found comparable costs when context is supportive. This study examines the question in speakers of Brazilian Portuguese with Alzheimer's disease (AD), a condition that affects both semantic and visuospatial abilities, and asks whether the conceptual content of a sentence, spatial or non-spatial, modulates processing cost independently of metaphoricity. Forty-two participants (14 with AD, 14 healthy elderly and 14 healthy young adults) read twelve sentences in a 2 × 2 design (metaphorical or literal; spatial or non-spatial) on a tablet and judged an inference about each of them. Accuracy, reading times and response times were recorded. The AD group was less accurate and slower overall. Spatial sentences were read more slowly by all groups. Metaphoricity interacted with group in reading times: only the AD group read metaphors more slowly than literal sentences, and this cost was concentrated in metaphors with a spatial source domain. Spatiality interacted with group in response times, although comparisons within groups were not significant. The results indicate that the cost attributed to metaphor depends on the population tested, on the stage of processing that is measured and on the conceptual domain recruited by the sentence. They do not support a general processing disadvantage for metaphorical language.

1 Introduction

Speakers routinely talk about one thing in terms of another. They fall into a crisis, face an opponent in court or carry a heavy heart, and they rarely notice that they are doing so. Within Cognitive Linguistics, expressions of this kind are treated as the linguistic surface of conceptual mappings between a source domain, usually closer to bodily experience, and a target domain that is more abstract (Lakoff; Johnson, 1980). If such mappings are part of ordinary conceptual structure, an empirical question follows. Are literal and metaphorical language comprehended differently? This question has generated a variety of answers that get to the heart of what it means for something to be metaphorical.

The question has practical weight in the study of ageing and dementia. Difficulty with figurative language has been documented in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and, more recently, in mild neurocognitive disorders (NCDs)foot_0 , and has been proposed as a possible early sign of cognitive decline (Chakrabarty et al., 2023; Rapp; Wild, 2011). The findings are not uniform, however. Some studies report preserved comprehension of conventional metaphors in the early stage of the disease (Amanzio et al., 2008; Papagno, 2001), while others report losses that precede those observed for literal language (Fujimoto et al., 2019). AD also compromises visuospatial abilities from early on (Coughlan et al., 2018; Possin, 2010), and space is one of the most productive source domains for metaphor (Lakoff; Johnson, 1980). The literature on metaphor in AD has seldom controlled for the conceptual content of the sentences it tests.

This article reports a reading study with speakers of Brazilian Portuguese that distinguishes two factors usually confounded in that literature: whether a sentence is metaphorical or literal, and whether its content is spatial or non-spatial. Three groups took part: people with AD, healthy elderly adults and healthy young adults. Section 2 reviews the main processing models. Section 3 examines the neuropsychological and behavioural evidence on which they rest. Section 4 presents what is known about figurative language and spatial cognition in AD and states the research questions. Sections 5 to 7 present the method, the results and their discussion, and Section 8 closes the article.

2 Processing models of metaphor comprehension

Early theoretical models of language processing have focused on the time course of language comprehension, asking whether the literal meaning of an utterance must be assessed before metaphorical processing can begin. For example, the "standard pragmatic" three stage model has argued that non-literal meanings are only accessed once literal ones have been rejected (Grice, 1975; Searle, 1979). According to this model, in the first stage of processing the literal interpretation is determined. In the second stage, this interpretation is evaluated against the context: for Grice (1975), a metaphor taken literally violates the maxim of Quality, since the speaker says something patently false; for Searle (1979), the literal reading is "defective" in the situation of utterance, and, if warranted, in the third stage a non-literal interpretation is determined. The model therefore predicts that metaphors should take longer to understand than comparable literal sentences, and that a metaphorical reading should be sought only when the literal one fails.

An alternative to this model, the parallel processing model (Glucksberg; Gildea; Bookin, 1982) suggests that both the literal and the figurative meanings of an utterance are processed simultaneously, much like the parallel processing of multiple meanings of polysemous words. As with an ambiguous word, both readings become available and the one that is more familiar to the comprehender, or better supported by the context, prevails. Blasko and Connine (1993), for instance, found that the figurative meaning of familiar and apt metaphors was available as soon as the metaphor was heard, whereas for unfamiliar metaphors it was available only when they were apt. What the model leaves unspecified is how the comprehender settles on one reading when the two are incongruent, or when the context supports neither clearly.

A proposal on this point comes from Gibbs (1984). In his direct access view, a figurative meaning can be reached without a complete analysis of the literal meaning of the sentence, provided that the context supports it. The evidence comes from reading-time studies in which metaphors embedded in a sufficiently long context were read as fast as literal controls (Gibbs; Gerrig, 1989; Ortony et al., 1978). On this account, ambiguity is resolved by the context before or as the sentence is read, not by a later stage of repair.

The case for context dependency is reviewed by other authors, such as Giora (1997), who argues that the various possible meanings of an utterance are not all equally salient. In her graded salience hypothesis, a meaning is salient when it is conventional, frequent, familiar or prototypical, and salient meanings are accessed first whether they are literal or figurative. Context acts afterwards, strengthening the meaning it supports and inhibiting the others. The relevant contrast is thus between salient and non-salient meanings, not between literal and figurative ones. In a conventional expression such as "he had a heavy heart", the figurative meaning (sadness) is the salient one and is retrieved directly; in a novel metaphor, the literal meanings of the words are more salient, are accessed first and must be revised, which takes additional time. Event-related potential data are compatible with an early contribution of literal meaning to the comprehension of metaphors (Weiland; Bambini; Schumacher, 2014).

3 Is metaphor like literal language?

One question that arises from these models is whether metaphorical meaning is in fact computed only after a literal meaning has been computed and rejected. Two bodies of evidence bear on it. Neuropsychological studies ask whether the two kinds of language can be dissociated in the brain; behavioural studies ask when each meaning becomes available.

3.1 Neuropsychological evidence

A growing body of neuropsychological studies suggests that metaphorical and literal language may not in fact be processed in the same way. For example, Winner and Gardner (1977) asked whether the two hemispheres of the brain play different roles in the processing of metaphorical language. To find out, they asked people with lesions in either the left hemisphere (LHD) or the right hemisphere (RHD) to complete a picture-matching task in which they had to choose between pictures depicting literal and non-literal interpretations of a sentence. For example, faced with a sentence containing the expression "a heavy heart", participants had to choose among pictures that included one showing the metaphorical meaning (such as a person crying) and one showing the literal meaning (such as a person carrying a very large heart). RHD patients chose the literal picture more often than LHD patients did. The same RHD patients, however, were able to explain the metaphors verbally, so that the deficit depended on the task. Ianni et al. (2014) note that picture matching may therefore introduce a visuospatial confound for RHD patients. With a similar sentence-to-picture task, Van Lancker and Kempler (1987) found that LHD patients understood familiar idiomatic phrases better than RHD patients, while the reverse held for novel literal sentences.

At the word level, Brownell et al. (1984) and Brownell et al. (1990) used sorting tasks in which patients grouped words by similarity of meaning. In the 1990 study, 19 LHD and 15 RHD patients had to respond on the basis of the less frequent meaning of polysemous adjectives with a metaphorical alternative (e.g. warm, "loving") and of polysemous nouns with a nonmetaphorical alternative (e.g. pen). Both groups performed below controls, but LHD patients showed a relatively spared appreciation of the metaphorical meanings. With word-triad and word-pair tasks, Gagnon et al. (2003) did not replicate the dissociation: both patient groups had difficulty and they did not differ from each other.

Visual half-field studies with healthy adults suggest that while metaphoric meanings are initially activated in both hemispheres, they are only sustained in the right hemisphere (Anaki; Faust; Kravetz, 1998). In that study, target words related literally (e.g. stinging-mosquito) or metaphorically (stinging-insult) to a prime were presented to the left or to the right visual field. At a 200 ms interval, metaphorical targets were primed in both visual fields; at 800 ms, only in the left visual field, that is, the right hemisphere. This evidence is corroborated by functional neuroimaging of healthy adults that has revealed increased activation of right frontal and temporal areas during metaphorical comprehension (Bottini et al., 1994).

Taken together, these studies suggest that metaphorical language is predominantly processed in the right hemisphere, in contrast with prior findings regarding literal language, whose core phonological, lexical and syntactic operations depend mainly on left perisylvian cortex (Hickok; Poeppel, 2007). Later work has qualified this picture. Reviewing the field, Schmidt et al. (2010) observed that the right-hemisphere hypothesis is not confirmed consistently and that many early studies used few items and did not match metaphorical and literal conditions for difficulty. With controlled sentence materials, Ianni et al. (2014) described selective metaphor deficits after left-hemisphere lesions, and Cardillo, McQuire and Chatterjee (2018), testing 20 LHD patients, 20 RHD patients and 20 controls, found that both patient groups were worse on metaphorical than on literal sentences and did not differ from each other. However, whereas this evidence suggests that metaphorical and literal language can be dissociated after brain damage, it gives us very few insights into the relative timing of metaphor and literal interpretation, a factor which figures critically in the theoretical proposals regarding comprehension of the two types of language.

3.2 Behavioural evidence

When, then, are literal and metaphorical meanings computed? Are they computed serially, as proposed by the three-stage model, or in tandem, as proposed by the parallel processing model? Or, indeed, are metaphorical meanings sometimes computed before literal ones, as suggested by the direct access model?

In an experiment designed to answer a related question, Glucksberg, Gildea and Bookin (1982) asked participants to make judgements about the literal truth of sentences of the form "All/Some X are Y", exemplified in (1): (1) a.

Some jobs are jails. b. Some jobs are snakes. Participants took significantly longer to judge the truth of sentences which had a reasonable metaphorical meaning (1a) than those which did not (1b). Nevertheless, since the task only required participants to make judgements based on literal meaning, the results suggest that metaphor comprehension is an automatic process; i.e., metaphorical interpretations are computed even when literal meaning is intended and may thus interfere with processing of literal meanings. This result is incompatible with a strictly serial model, in which a figurative reading is sought only after the literal one fails. It does not, by itself, decide between the parallel and the direct access models, since both predict that an available figurative meaning will be computed without being required by the task.

Subsequent studies point in the same direction. Giora (1997, p. 184) summarises the position they support in two claims: literal language has no priority over non-literal language, and the two are understood by the same processes and with the same contextual information (Gibbs; Gerrig, 1989; Gildea; Glucksberg, 1983; Glucksberg, 1989; Glucksberg; Gildea; Bookin, 1982). Three findings are representative. Ortony et al. (1978) showed that a target sentence was read as quickly in its metaphorical as in its literal sense when it followed a long context, and more slowly only when the context was short. Keysar (1989) placed sentences in contexts that made their literal and metaphorical readings independently true or false, and found that verification was slowed whenever the two readings diverged, including the cases in which the literal reading was perfectly plausible. A metaphorical interpretation is thus computed without any triggering violation. McElree and Nordlie (1999), using a speed-accuracy tradeoff procedure, estimated that literal and figurative interpretations of simple propositions become available at the same time. These studies are again compatible with both the parallel and the direct access models; what they exclude is the obligatory priority of the literal meaning.

The fate of the literal meaning is less clear. Some reports indicate that it is not active after a non-literal expression has been understood (see the studies of idioms reviewed by Gibbs, 1984), but other studies find that literal meaning does contribute to early processing (Blasko; Connine, 1993; Weiland; Bambini; Schumacher, 2014).

3.3 Making sense of the debate

The results reviewed above do not point to a uniformly higher cost for metaphorical than for literal language. Rather, we have behavioural evidence suggesting that metaphor and literal language are processed in parallel, and neuropsychological evidence suggesting that this processing can be selectively disrupted. These two types of evidence are not incompatible. A function may depend on partly different neural resources and still be carried out, in the intact brain, in the same amount of time.

Arguments for and against a higher processing cost of metaphor based on such things as hemisphere deficits may be difficult to support since processing in different hemispheres does not imply different cognitive costs. On the other hand, the distinction between semantic and pragmatic processing, and the idea that some meanings are accessed before others in the mental lexicon, are useful but have been applied in different ways from one study to the next, so that they do not offer a single account of how metaphor is processed.

Methodologically, the studies also differ: some do not distinguish metaphor from other kinds of figurative language, and the conventionality of the items varies from one study to another. Some studies even suggest that metaphor comprehension poses a greater challenge than that posed by literal language of equivalent syntactic complexity: in the patient studies reviewed in Section 3.1, performance on metaphorical items was lower than on literal items matched for structure (Cardillo; McQuire; Chatterjee, 2018). However, is this challenge caused by the nature of metaphor itself?

There are reasons to doubt it. Glucksberg et al.'s (1982) finding that conventional metaphorical meanings are computed even when expressions are intended to be taken literally suggests that the contrast between literal and metaphorical language does not by itself determine how comprehension proceeds. Bowdle and Gentner (2005) found evidence that conventional metaphors are processed more as an operation of category inclusion, while novel metaphors are processed more like an operation of comparison. Such results imply that metaphors are not all the same and that we cannot presuppose this sameness when analysing metaphorical language processing.

We therefore ask whether the contrast between metaphorical and literal language is enough to account for differences in processing cost, or whether other factors, such as the type of metaphor, also contribute. Two variables have received little attention. The first is the population tested: most reading-time evidence comes from university students, whose cognitive resources may absorb small costs. The second is the conceptual content of the sentences, which is rarely controlled. In this study, we bring new data to bear on the issue of metaphorical language processing of people with Alzheimer's disease. This group is relevant here because AD is often associated with semantic and executive impairments that affect the comprehension of figurative language (Amanzio et al., 2008; Papagno et al., 2003; Rapp; Wild, 2011) and with early changes in spatial cognition (Possin, 2010; Serino; Riva, 2014; Tu et al., 2015), a frequent source domain for metaphor. This makes it possible to ask whether differences in processing reflect only the time required at different stages or also the neurocognitive and conceptual resources that the task recruits.

4 Alzheimer's disease and spatial language comprehension

The studies reviewed so far compared metaphorical and literal language without attending to two variables: the population tested and the conceptual content of the stimuli. This section takes up both, examining figurative language and spatial cognition in Alzheimer's disease.

4.1 Figurative language in Alzheimer's disease

Studies of metaphor in AD are few and their results diverge. Papagno (2001) examined 39 patients with probable early AD and concluded that the decline of figurative language is not an early symptom and can occur independently of the impairment of literal language. Amanzio et al. (2008) compared 20 patients with probable AD and 20 controls: the patients understood conventional metaphors and idioms as well as controls but were impaired with novel metaphors, and this difficulty was related to executive measures. Roncero and de Almeida (2014) found that the quality of patients' interpretations depended on the aptness of the metaphor more than on its familiarity. In contrast, Fujimoto et al. (2019) reported that patients with very mild AD were already impaired on a simile comprehension test while still performing normally on a test of literal comprehension. In amnestic mild cognitive impairment, Yang and Huang (2023) observed lower accuracy in judging metaphorical sentences. In idiom comprehension, patients with mild AD tend to choose the literal interpretation, which Papagno et al. (2003) attribute to a failure to inhibit the literal meaning, not to loss of the idiomatic one.

Two points follow for the present study. First, whether a cost for metaphor is observed in AD depends on properties of the stimuli, such as conventionality and aptness, and on executive resources. Second, almost all of this evidence comes from offline measures (explanation, multiple choice, picture matching), which show whether comprehension succeeds but not when difficulty arises. Measures of reading and response time have rarely been applied to this population.

Considering that mild Alzheimer's disease tends to produce more left-sided than rightsided neuropsychological impairment (Capitani; Della Sala; Spinnler, 1990), we are led to assume that semantic impairment poses a greater challenge to literal comprehension. However, pragmatic abilities, often attributed to the right hemisphere, are also affected early in the disease (Chakrabarty et al., 2023), which would predict a deficit for metaphor. Since metaphor comprehension itself draws on both hemispheres (Section 3.1), lateralisation offers no clear prediction. A more useful notion is compensation. Individuals with greater cognitive reserve can maintain performance in the presence of brain changes by recruiting additional resources (Stern, 2009). In young university students, small differences between conditions may be absorbed in this way and go undetected; in people with AD, whose capacity for compensation is reduced, the same differences may surface as measurable costs.

4.2 Embodiment and spatial source domains

Work on embodied cognition proposes that sensorimotor domains, such as space, give structure to abstract concepts and to the metaphors that express them. Gallese and Lakoff (2005) argue that the concept of grasping, including its metaphorical use in grasping an idea, is represented in the sensorimotor circuits that control the action itself. On a strong embodiment view of cognition, sensorimotor source domains for metaphors (e.g., physical sensation of warmth) are activated in parallel with more abstract target domains, so as to provide structure and semantic content for understanding metaphorical expressions (as in "warm smile"). In other words, processing of both conceptual domains should occur at the same time. Coulson and Van Petten (2002) recorded event-related potentials while 18 adults read sentences ending in a word used literally, metaphorically or in an intermediate "literal mapping" condition. The amplitude of the N400 increased from literal to literal mapping to metaphorical endings, a graded pattern that the authors attribute to the demands of conceptual integration, which affect literal and metaphorical language alike. Coulson and Matlock (2001) propose a related account, in which understanding a metaphor consists in building a blend from mental spaces set up by the source and the target domains.

As with literal language, the semantic integration of a metaphor depends on contextual support and on the demands of mapping between domains. The literature still debates whether the sensorimotor activity observed during the comprehension of metaphors is part of their meaning or a by-product of it (Casasanto; Gijssels, 2015). Whether action metaphors recruit brain areas underlying comprehension of actions, or space metaphors recruit brain areas underlying spatial cognition, our concern is how to measure costs of this processing when the source domain itself is compromised. If understanding a spatial expression involves the representations used to perceive and reason about space, then a population with visuospatial impairment should have more difficulty with spatial sentences than with non-spatial ones, whether the spatial expression is used literally or metaphorically.

4.3 Spatial cognition in Alzheimer's disease

Spatial reasoning is another function early affected by AD. Visuospatial impairment is often among the first symptoms of neurodegenerative disease, and in AD it accompanies the degeneration of medial temporal and parietal regions (Possin, 2010). Spatial disorientation is a prominent feature of the early stage and has been linked to the retrosplenial cortex (Tu et al., 2015). Serino and Riva (2014) relate the early decline of episodic memory to a failure in synchronising two allocentric representations of space, one independent of and one dependent on the viewpoint, and Coughlan et al. (2018) propose navigation deficits as a marker for preclinical AD.

These changes extend to spatial language. In a pilot study with speakers of Brazilian Portuguese, Leite, Feist and Toscano (2021) compared people with AD, healthy elderly adults and young adults in tasks that required decisions about locative descriptions. The groups preferred the same frames of reference, but the AD group needed more time to respond. Leite (2023) reports related data on the description of object location. The present study builds on these findings: if locative expressions are costly for people with AD when used literally, the same may hold when they provide the source domain of a metaphor.

If most of our fundamental concepts are organised in terms of one or more spatialisation metaphors, as suggested by Lakoff and Johnson (1980), processing spatial language should bring no extra complexity to understanding of metaphor for healthy speakers, for whom spatial schemas are well entrenched. The prediction changes for speakers whose spatial cognition is impaired. This assumption opens a question as to whether processing cost might vary when different conceptual domains are recruited as a source domain of a metaphor.

4.4 Research questions

The study addresses three questions: (i) does metaphoricity affect the time needed to read a sentence and to judge an inference drawn from it, and does this effect differ among people with AD, healthy elderly adults and healthy young adults? (ii) does spatial content affect the same measures, and does its effect differ among the groups? (iii) do the two factors interact? If metaphor carries a general cost, as the standard pragmatic model holds, metaphorical sentences should be read more slowly by all groups. If the cost depends on available cognitive resources, it should appear only, or mainly, in the AD group. Given the visuospatial impairment associated with AD, spatial sentences should be more costly for this group than for the others. The experiment was originally designed to test related hypotheses (Sousa, 2017): that the comprehension of spatial metaphors would be impaired in AD, that spatial metaphors would require more reading and response time in the AD group than in the control groups, and that accuracy and times for spatial metaphors would differ from those for structural metaphors and for literal spatial sentences. The crossing of metaphoricity and spatiality examined here is a reanalysis of those data.

5 Method

The data come from the experiment designed and run by Sousa (2017) for his doctoral research at the Federal University of Paraíba (UFPB) under Leite's supervision, from which the information on participants, materials and procedure given below is taken. For this article the data were reorganised in a 2 × 2 scheme and reanalysed.

5.1 Participants

Forty-two people participated in this study. Fourteen elderly individuals with AD (mean age 74.1 years) were recruited from a group of patients receiving transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), followed by researchers of the Graduate Programme in Cognitive Neuroscience and Behaviour (PPGNeC) at UFPB, who also applied the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) (Morris, 1993). The tests reported here were conducted prior to TMS sessions. Thirteen participants were rated CDR 1 (mild dementia) and one CDR 2 (moderate dementia). Fourteen healthy elderly (HE) participants (mean age 68.6 years) were recruited at a community centre for older adults in João Pessoa (Clube do Idoso) and through personal contacts (Sousa, 2017). The fourteen healthy young adults (HY; mean age 26.4 years) were students of UFPB and of the State University of Paraíba, or personal contacts.

Inclusion criteria were age of 60 or more for the two elderly groups and between 18 and 40 for the young group, completed primary education (ensino fundamental) and reading ability sufficient for the task; for the AD group, a diagnosis of AD in the mild or moderate stage was also required (Sousa, 2017). The CDR was preferred to the Mini-Mental State Examination because it is less sensitive to schooling, which varied in the sample. Forty-eight people were tested and six (three HE and three HY) were excluded from the analyses for not completing the entire test. Table 1 describes the final sample. 5.2 Materials

Participants read and reasoned about sentences varying along two dimensions: metaphorical/literal and spatial/non-spatial. The language sample included metaphorical sentences with spatial and non-spatial source domains and literal sentences with spatial and non-spatial content. Spatial content was restricted to locative phrases, and non-spatial sentences contained none.

The material consisted of twelve sentences, each followed by an inference statement (Table 2). Four sentences contained an orientational metaphor with a vertical source domain, two instantiating HAPPY IS UP and two SAD IS DOWN; two contained the structural metaphor ARGUMENT IS WAR (Lakoff; Johnson, 1980); two were literal sentences with a locative phrase; and four were literal sentences without spatial content. In the original design the last four served as distractors and were not analysed (Sousa, 2017); in the present analysis they provide the literal non-spatial condition. The 2 × 2 scheme therefore has four, two, two and four sentences per cell.

All sentences had an animate subject, a verb in the simple past (pretérito perfeito) and between 11 and 14 syllables. Each inference statement repeated the subject of its sentence, and half of the inferences in each condition were adequate (expected answer YES) and half inadequate (NO). In the two metaphorical conditions and in the literal spatial condition the inference was built with a copular verb and a state (e.g. O sócio esteve desolado, "The partner was devastated"); in the literal non-spatial condition it contained a locative (e.g. Mariana esteve na frutaria, "Mariana was at the fruit shop"). Although controlled for syllables, the spatial sentences were on average one word longer than the non-spatial ones (6.5 and 5.5 words).

The metaphors were chosen after a pilot test with healthy adults that included three orientational and three structural metaphors; the one with the most homogeneous accuracy in each type was kept. The sentences were then selected from a set of 48 candidates rated for plausibility on a five-point scale by 102 adults (aged 18 to 63) in an online questionnaire. Table 2 gives, for each sentence, the percentage of ratings in the two highest points of the scale. Plausibility was above 83% for all sentences except the two SAD IS DOWN items, which were rated as plausible by about 59% of the judges.

5.3 Procedure

The experiment was a self-paced reading task run on the Qualtrics online platform and presented on a tablet with a 10-inch touchscreen. Participants were tested individually. After signing the consent form they completed a one-minute manual speed test, in which they drew a stroke in each square of a grid as fast as possible, and filled in a form with personal data. They then read the instructions and practised with three sentence-inference pairs before the twelve experimental pairs, which were presented in random order (Sousa, 2017).

Each trial had two phases (Figure 1). First, a sentence appeared on the screen; the participant read it at his or her own pace and touched the screen to continue. Then the inference statement appeared and the participant judged whether it was adequate by choosing YES or NO. The platform recorded the time spent on each screen and the answer given. We measured time at two points in processing: time to read the sentence and time to respond to an inferencing question. Reading time is taken here as an index of the earlier phase of comprehension, and response time as an index of a later phase, in which the content of the sentence is used to reason. 5.4 Design and analysis

The design was a 3 × 2 × 2 mixed factorial, with group (AD, HE, HY) as a betweenparticipants factor and metaphoricity (metaphorical, literal) and spatiality (spatial, non-spatial) as within-participants factors. For each participant, reading times and response times were averaged over the sentences of each cell, including trials with incorrect answers. The means, in seconds, were submitted to repeated-measures analyses of variance in IBM SPSS Statistics 23 (general linear model), with Bonferroni-corrected comparisons between groups and partial eta squared (η²p) as the measure of effect size. Interactions with group were followed up with paired comparisons between the levels of the within-participants factor in each group, Bonferroni-corrected for the three groups. Accuracy was computed as the percentage of correct answers per participant and compared across groups with a one-way analysis of variance, Tukey comparisons and, given the coarse scale of the measure, a Kruskal-Wallis test. The paired comparisons within groups and the analyses of accuracy were computed for this article from the spreadsheets of the original study, in Python (SciPy and Pingouin); they reproduce the SPSS output for all effects available there.

Thus, for AD, metaphoricity only impacted earlier processing, while the effects of spatial language were present in reading for all groups and, descriptively, in the inference judgment.

7 Discussion

In light of these results we ask whether and how metaphoricity affects language processing. The results point to an early effect of metaphoricity in people with Alzheimer's disease. The healthy groups read metaphorical and literal sentences in similar times; participants with AD were slower in both conditions and, in addition, slower with metaphorical than with literal sentences.

The absence of a metaphor cost in the two healthy groups agrees with the reading-time literature reviewed in Section 3.2 and is what the parallel, direct access and graded salience models predict for familiar metaphors; it is not what the standard pragmatic model predicts. The cost observed in the AD group shows that this equivalence is not unconditional. It is compatible with the view that understanding a metaphor draws on executive and semantic resources that are reduced in AD (Amanzio et al., 2008; Papagno et al., 2003), and with the idea that healthy readers compensate for demands that become visible only when resources are limited (Stern, 2009). The cost appeared in reading and not in the inference judgment, where the AD group, although less accurate overall, was not slower or less accurate after metaphorical than after literal sentences. Once a sentence had been read, its metaphorical origin no longer weighed measurably on the reasoning based on it.

The cost was not the same for the two kinds of metaphor. In the AD group it was carried by the orientational metaphors with a vertical source domain, while sentences based on ARGUMENT IS WAR were read as fast as literal ones. This is the pattern anticipated by Sousa (2017), who expected spatial metaphors to be the most demanding for this population, and it fits the proposal that a metaphor is harder to understand when its source domain is itself affected (Section 4.2). Two reservations apply. The three-way interaction was not significant, so the difference between the two kinds of metaphor is not statistically established. And the two SAD IS DOWN sentences had the lowest plausibility ratings of the set, about 59%, so that part of the difficulty may come from the sentences and not from their spatial source domain.

Spatial content affected reading in all groups to a similar extent. This result must be read with caution: although the sentences were matched for number of syllables, the spatial ones contained a locative phrase and were on average one word longer, which may account for part of the effect. At the inference stage, spatial content affected processing costs differently across groups. The two elderly groups went in opposite directions: while people with AD responded slower after spatial inputs than after non-spatial language, healthy elderly individuals responded slower after non-spatial than after spatial language. Because none of the differences within groups was significant, this pattern is a lead for further work and not an established finding. Its direction in the AD group agrees with the slower responses to locative descriptions reported for this population (Leite; Feist; Toscano, 2021) and with the hypothesis that an impaired spatial system makes spatial content harder to reason about. The reversed pattern of the healthy elderly group has a possible explanation in the materials: the inference statements that followed the non-spatial literal sentences contained a locative, unlike those of the other conditions (Section 5.2), so that in this condition spatial content was moved from the sentence to the inference and the contrast between spatial and non-spatial trials is not clean at the response stage.

The results also show why the population tested matters. The metaphor cost appeared only in the group with AD: neither the healthy elderly nor the young adults read metaphors more slowly than literal sentences. Conclusions about the cost of metaphor drawn only from young, healthy adults may therefore underestimate differences that exist in other populations.

We now return to our main question: are literal and metaphorical language comprehended differently? Metaphorical and literal language do differ from one another, but speakers may compensate during processing for difficulties encountered earlier. As for metaphor contents, our results suggest that the type of source domain (e.g. space) can alter the cost of processing, implying that not all metaphors may be processed equally. Results, however, do not support a claim for a general difference in processing in terms of what is literal and what is metaphorical. Differences of the kind analysed here may become evident only among populations with cognitive impairment. Cognitive costs for language are not all alike: processing difficulties can arise at different stages in processing and can involve different conceptual material.

The study has limitations that restrict what can be concluded. The number of items was small, two or four per cell, and the literal non-spatial condition was formed by sentences that had been designed as distractors. The groups were also small (14 participants each), the two interactions were at the conventional threshold of significance and the analyses did not treat items as a random factor. The groups were not matched for sex or schooling (Table 1), and the AD group was on average five years older than the healthy elderly group. Times were recorded by an online platform on a touchscreen, and the AD group was markedly slower in the manual speed test, so that part of the group difference in response time is motor and not linguistic; this does not affect the comparisons between conditions within a group. The conventionality of the metaphors was not manipulated, although it is the variable that most clearly separates preserved from impaired performance in AD (Amanzio et al., 2008). Some spatial metaphorical sentences contain a second, non-spatial metaphor in the verb, as amargou (literally "tasted as bitter"), and a verb such as enfrentar has a spatial origin of its own, so that the two dimensions are not fully independent. Future studies should use a larger set of sentences per condition, matched for length and plausibility, cross conventionality with spatial content and relate reading measures to individual scores on visuospatial tests.

8 Final remarks

This study separated metaphoricity from spatial content in sentences read by people with Alzheimer's disease and by healthy elderly and young adults. Metaphorical sentences were read more slowly than literal ones only by the group with AD, and within this group the cost was concentrated in metaphors with a spatial source domain. Spatial content showed effects of its own, in reading for all groups and, descriptively, in later reasoning for the group with AD. For the debate on whether metaphor is processed like literal language, the data suggest that the answer varies with the reader, with the stage of comprehension that is measured and with the conceptual domain involved. For research on language in dementia, they indicate that timebased measures can detect difficulties that tests of final comprehension may miss, and that the content of the sentences used in such tests needs to be controlled.
