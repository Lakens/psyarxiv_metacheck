Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 51 papers with 87 statements, this module correctly identified 38 statements (true positives) and incorrectly flagged 22 statements (false positives). It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate). This validation was made with an earlier, looser pattern; the pattern now requires “significant” or “significance” to follow the trigger word closely (#695).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Personalized information environments have been proposed to contribute to political polarization, yet empirical findings remain mixed. The present work aimed to clarify these inconsistencies by distinguishing between different polarization outcomes and considering individual characteristics as potential moderators. In an online experiment, we tested how attitude-congruency of information environments affects issue and affective polarization regarding unconditional basic income (UBI) in Germany, and whether Intellectual Humility (IH) and Need for Cognitive Closure (NfCC) moderate these effects. N = 756 participants were exposed to one of three information environments containing predominantly attitudecongruent, attitude-incongruent, or neutral statements about UBI. Affective polarization was assessed separately as the divide in negative affective evaluations (stronger negative feelings toward the outgroup than ingroup; NAD) and the divide in positive affective evaluations (PAD; stronger positive feelings toward the ingroup than outgroup) between issue-based groups. Compared with neutral exposure, attitude-congruent information increased issue polarization, NAD, and PAD. Attitude-incongruent information decreased issue polarization but had no signficant effect on either affective divide. IH was positively associated with and moderated effects on PAD. NfCC showed no effects. Findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between polarization outcomes and suggest that positive and negative affective intergroup divides may show different associations with individual characteristics.

Introduction

Rising political polarization is a concern in many societies including countries in the European Union: Perceptions of a broken society (Ipsos, 2025) and an increasing divide in opinions (Wissenschaft im Dialog gGmbH, 2025) are widespread in public opinion according to recent surveys. Similarly, the support for more extreme political parties seems to have increased (Casal Bértoa & Rama, 2021; Müller & Schnabl, 2021). In the psychology and political science literature on political polarization in the general public -i.e., mass polarization (Fiorina & Abrams, 2008; Jost et al., 2022)-research has devoted considerable attention to the underlying reasons. Among the factors examined as potential drivers of political polarization is the widespread use of social media and other online platforms for news and political information, including prevalent personalization systems on such platforms (Eg et al., 2023; Kubin & Von Sikorski, 2021; Ludwig & Müller, 2022). In line with this, researchers have extensively investigated the relations between personalized information environments and political polarization (Kubin & Von Sikorski, 2021; Ludwig & Müller, 2022), often based on the personalization-polarization hypothesis (Keijzer & Mäs, 2022). This hypothesis constitutes that information environments that predominantly align with individuals' political views (attitude-congruent) are related to higher political polarization, while counter-attitudinal (attitude-incongruent) information environments are related to lower political polarization or even political moderation. However, empirical research in this area has yielded mixed evidence for both the effects of attitude-congruent (Ludwig & Müller, 2022) and counter-attitudinal (attitude-incongruent) information environments (Kubin & Von Sikorski, 2021; Ludwig & Müller, 2022). This heterogeneity in findings may reflect, at least in part, variation in how political polarization is conceptualized and operationalized across studies (Hanfstingl et al., 2024; Kubin & Von Sikorski, 2021).

Additionally, the heterogeneity in findings points toward the necessity to examine potential moderators, such as individual differences.

Against this background, the present study examines the causal impact of attitudecongruent and -incongruent information environments on political polarization in the context of a currently discussed policy in Germany: the unconditional basic income (UBI). To compare effects on varying polarization outcomes, on the one hand, we examine effects on issue polarization regarding the UBI. On the other hand, we investigate the impacts on affective polarization measured between issue-based groups (Kohler et al., 2025; Segovia, 2025), i.e., supporters and opponents of the UBI. Building on research on ambivalent attitudes (Conner & Sparks, 2002) and the evaluative space model (Cacioppo et al., 2012) affective polarization is further assessed separately for positive and negative feelings towards both groups. Finally, intellectual humility and need for cognitive closure are examined as potential moderators of the relationship between the two information environments and political polarization outcomes.

Exposure to Attitude-Congruent and Counter-Attitudinal Information and Political Polarization

Theoretical accounts such as those by Pariser (2012) suggested that exposure to predominantly attitude-congruent information in online environments may foster political polarization, alongside other detrimental outcomes in the socio-political domain. Empirical findings on the relationship between attitude-congruent information exposure, however, are not unequivocal. Some studies suggest that exposure to attitude-congruent information or reduced exposure to attitude-incongruent information increases issue polarization, i.e., the extremity of individuals' views on a specific issue (Kelm et al., 2023; Ludwig et al., 2025), or affective polarization, i.e., increased differences in feelings toward the political in-versus outgroup, (Garrett et al., 2014; Kelm et al., 2023). Conversely, other studies have reported limited or null effects of exposure to attitude-congruent information on issue polarization (Levy, 2021; Ludwig et al., 2023) and affective polarization (Guess et al., 2023). As for the effects of exposure to attitude-incongruent information on polarization, findings are even less straightforward. Some studies suggest that attitude-incongruent information is associated with reduced issue polarization or greater political moderation (Barberá, 2015; Bogado et al., 2024) and reduced affective polarization (Garrett et al., 2014). Others report a backlash effect with increasing issue polarization (Bail et al., 2018; Kim, 2019), or find little evidence that counter-attitudinal information reliably produces backlash effects (Guess & Coppock, 2020).

Yet, others find no decrease in either polarization outcome (Nyhan et al., 2023).

This mixed pattern of findings may reflect not only differences in substantive effects or methodology across studies, but also the importance of differentiating between polarization outcomes and examining potential moderators when investigating the personalization-polarization relations.

Hypotheses and Research Questions on Information Environments and Polarization Outcomes

Against the background of the aforementioned literature and conceptual considerations, the present study aims to establish a clearer link between attitude-congruent and attitude-incongruent information environments and both issue and affective polarization, where we divide affective polarization into a positive and a negative affective facet. To do so, we compare information environments containing attitude-congruent statements, attitudeincongruent statements, or neutral statements about a political issue: the introduction of an unconditional basic income (UBI) in Germany.

Intellectual humility is in general defined as the tendency to acknowledge the limitations of one's knowledge and intellectual abilities, as well as to appreciate others' intellectual capabilities (Porter & Schumann, 2018). These include acknowledging one's intellectual fallibility and ignorance (self-focused, internal), admitting and remedying these limitations (self-focused, expressed), acknowledging other's intellectual capabilities (other-focused, internal), and being receptive to other's ideas and being open to other's feedback (other-focused, expressed). Method Transparency and Openness

The hypotheses, research questions, sample size rationale and power analyses, data exclusions, study design including measures, and statistical analyses of the present study have been pre-registered before data collection. Deviations from the pre-registration are reported transparently. The pre-registration, data, analysis code, and stimuli used in this study are available at the OSF repositoryfoot_0.

Participants

An initial sample of N = 792 participants was recruited by the ISO-certified panel provider Bilendi GmbH (https://www.bilendi.de/). Participants who did not meet the preregistered inclusion criteria were excluded prior to the determination of the initial analytic sample. Exclusion criteria were: being younger than 18 years; reporting a German language proficiency below C2; failing all attention-check items; completing the study in less than 7 minutes; reporting a neutral attitude toward the UBI (i.e., a score of 5.00).

Additionally, all pre-registered careless respondersfoot_1 in the intellectual humility scale and the need for cognitive closure scale were excluded (n = 36) from the sample, resulting in a final sample size of N = 756 (female: n =390; male: n = 366; Mage = 50.44; SDage = 16.10).

Procedure

Prior to participation, participants received information about the study and provided informed consent electronically. The present study was approved by the local ethics committee at [BLINDED FOR REVIEW]. After providing demographic information, participants reported their political orientation and completed an attention-check item. Next, they read an explanation of the UBI which specified parameters for the policy as understood in the context of the present study. Afterward, participants answered two additional attentioncheck items assessing comprehension of the UBI description. Participants subsequently reported their baseline attitudes toward the UBI as well as their positive and negative feelings toward supporters and opponents of the UBI, respectively. They were then randomly assigned to one of three information environments, each containing 30 statements. The statements were mostly either in favor of, against, or neutral toward the UBI, depending on the experimental condition. Afterwards, participants again reported their attitudes toward the UBI and their feelings toward UBI supporters and opponents. They then completed questionnaires assessing intellectual humility and NfCC. Finally, participants estimated the percentage of the population in Germany that shares their attitude toward the UBI and reported the personal relevance of the UBI to them.

Information Environments and UBI Statements

Participants were exposed to one of three information environments. In the "pro UBI" environment, participants were presented with 24 statements in favor of the UBI and six neutral statements. In the "contra UBI" information environment, participants saw 24 statements against the UBI and the six neutral statements. Finally, in the neutral information environment, participants were exposed to 30 neutral statements, including the same six neutral statements as for the two other environments. Assignment to the three information environments was randomized, with quotas ensuring a comparable distribution of UBI supporters and opponents across information environments.

The valenced statements were selected from a pool of pre-tested UBI statements so that, based on pre-test data, the set of statements in favor of the UBI and the set of statements against the UBI did not differ significantly in terms of absolute valence, importance, plausibility, familiarity, and comprehensibility ratings, as well as reading time. Further information on the creation of the pool of statements as well as the selection method can be taken from the Supplementary Materials. The neutral set of statements was taken from Jalbert et al.'s (2019) trivia claim statement pool and translated into German. All statements had a length between nine and 14 words.

UBI Attitudes and Issue Polarization

Participants' attitudes toward the UBI were assessed at baseline as well as after the experimental manipulation using a 4-item scale. The scale contained three items that measure attitudes toward the expected impact of the UBI on the economy, culture and life quality, as well as one item assessing overall support/opposition for the UBI. Participants responded to each item on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (indicating a very negative evaluation) to 10 (indicating a very positive evaluation). Consistent with previous findings (study currently under review), the UBI scale showed an internal consistency estimate of Cronbach's ⍺ = .92 in the present sample. Details on the development and validation of the UBI scale can be taken from the Supplementary Materials.

The measure of (state) issue polarization was then calculated separately for preexperiment (baseline measure) and post-experiment assessments as the absolute distance of the aggregated UBI attitude score from the theoretical midpoint of the scale (5.00).

Affective Polarization

Participants were classified as UBI supporters or UBI opponents based on their baseline UBI attitude score: scores above 5.00 indicated supporters and scores below 5.00 indicated opponents (participants scoring exactly 5 were excluded; see exclusion criteria). For each participant, the issue-based ingroup was defined as the attitude group matching their own stance (supporters rating supporters; opponents rating opponents), and the issue-based outgroup as the opposing stance (supporters rating opponents; opponents rating supporters).

Before and after the experimental manipulation, participants reported their negative feelings towards supporters and opponents of the UBI on 101-point scales ranging from 0 ("Gar nicht negativ"; not negative at all) to 100 ("Sehr negativ"; very negative), rating supporters and opponents separately. The CIHS scale showed an internal consistency estimate of Cronbach's ⍺ = .89.

Participants' need for cognitive closure (NfCC) was assessed using the German short scale developed by Schlink and Walther (2007). Each of the 16 items was rated on a 6-point scale ranging from1 = "Stimme gar nicht zu" (do not agree at all) to6 = "Stimme völlig zu" (completely agree). For each participant, an NfCC score was computed as the mean of all items after reverse-coding the three negatively worded items. The reliability estimate of the scale was Cronbach's ⍺ = .77.

Statistical Analyses

For each measure of political polarization -issue polarization, NAD, PAD, three linear regression models were fitted: The first model included the control variables age, gender, income, and baseline polarization measurefoot_2 as predictors (block 1). The second model included information environment as two dummy coded variables to contrast the attitudecongruent and -incongruent information environment to the neutral one. Main effects of intellectual humility and NfCC were also included. Lastly, we fitted a third model with added interaction terms. Prior to analyses all non-categorical variables were z-standardized and gender was dummy coded with female as the reference category. For models predicting PAD, where heteroskedasticity was a concern, we report HC3 heteroskedasticity consistent standard errors (Hayes & Cai, 2007) and Wald F-test for overall model fit. We note discrepancies from conclusions based on conventional OLS SEs. For all other models, conventional OLS standard errors are presented. Discussion

The present study examined whether experimentally constructed attitude-congruent and attitude-incongruent information environments shape political polarization in the context of attitudes toward the introduction of a UBI. More specifically, it investigated effects on issue polarization as well as on the negative and positive evaluative components of affective polarization, separately. In addition, it examined whether these effects are moderated by intellectual humility and NfCC.

Our results revealed four main findings. First, exposure to attitude-congruent information increased issue polarization as well as the negative and positive affective divides in intergroup evaluations. Thus, the findings largely support the expectation that information environments dominated by information congruent with an individual's pre-existing attitudes can foster political polarization (hypothesis H1a-c). This pattern is in line with theoretical accounts (Levendusky, 2013a; Pariser, 2012; Sunstein, 2007) and empirical work (Kelm et al., 2023; Kubin & von Sikorski, 2021; Levendusky, 2013a; Ludwig et al., 2023) linking personalized information environments to political polarization. Regarding RQ1, the effect of attitude-congruent information was descriptively largest for issue polarization, followed by PAD and smallest on NAD.

Second, our results indicate a depolarizing effect of attitude-incongruent information on issue polarization. However, they provide no evidence of such an effect on either affective polarization measure. Although the estimated effects on NAD and PAD were negative, they did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, our findings are consistent with accounts suggesting that exposure to cross-cutting content can reduce polarization (Barberá, 2015) but provide no evidence of the backlash effects suggested by accounts of motivated cognition (Levendusky, 2013b). These results should, however, be qualified insofar as the information environment to which participants were exposed to did not contain any attitude-congruent information. This limits comparability with interventions that present more balanced media diets or increase attitude-incongruent information without excluding congruent information entirely. It is possible that whether attitude-incongruent information leads to depolarization or backlash depends on the ratio between attitude-congruent and -incongruent information. For instance, responses to incongruent information may shift from defensive processing toward attitude revision after tipping point is reached and accuracy motivations take over (Redlawsk et al., 2010). Nonetheless, the effect of exclusively attitude-incongruent information provides an informative benchmark test.

Third, regarding moderators, our results suggest an association between intellectual humility and PAD. However, this relationship is not straightforward. On the one hand, higher intellectual humility was related to higher PAD in the neutral information environment, contrasting with previous findings that suggest a negative relationship between intellectual humility and affective polarization (Knöchelmann & Cohrs, 2024). On the other hand, the polarizing effect of attitude-congruent information on PAD was attenuated for individuals higher in intellectual humility, in line with its proposed buffering effect (Bowes et al., 2020).

However, considering that the inclusion of the interaction terms did not significantly improve overall model fit and that conclusions regarding both the main effect of intellectual humility on PAD and its interaction with the congruent information environment were sensitive to the type of SEs used in the analyses, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further evidence is needed to more conclusively determine the role of intellectual humility in the context of congruent information exposure.

Regarding NfCC, we found no evidence of an association with any polarization outcome, nor evidence that NfCC moderated the effects of attitude-congruent or attitude-incongruent information exposure. Thus, the results do not support the expectation that individuals high in NfCC would be especially susceptible to reinforcement under congruent exposure or backlash under incongruent exposure. One possible explanation is that participants may not yet have held sufficiently strong or crystallized attitudes toward the UBI. Kruglanski and Webster (1996) argue that a key boundary condition separates "seizing" from "freezing" at the point where a belief "crystallizes", i.e., becomes "subjectively firm" (p.

226). Before that point high NfCC should foster "seizing", making individuals more susceptible to persuasive arguments in their search for closure. After crystallization, by contrast, high NfCC should foster "freezing", including resistance to persuasion and disconfirming information that threatens existing closure. Under this logic, heightened polarization and backlash effects among individuals high in NfCC may be more likely for issues they have firmer beliefs on.

Fourth, the present findings illustrate the value of separating polarization facets, including the differentiation of affective polarization into positive and negative components.

In particular, the finding that intellectual humility moderated the effect of attitude-congruent exposure on PAD, whereas no corresponding moderation effect was observed for NAD. Had affective polarization been assessed only with a traditional bipolar difference score, these findings may have been harder to detect and interpret. In line with bivariate accounts of affect such as the Evaluative Space Model (Cacioppo et al., 2012; Cacioppo & Berntson, 1994; Norris et al., 2010) and research on ambivalent attitudes (Conner & Sparks, 2002), this divergence supports the conceptual argument that positive and negative evaluations should not simply be treated as opposite poles of a single continuum. Bipolar measures such as feeling thermometers, though convenient, may therefore obscure distinct components of group evaluation and impose an assumption of reciprocity that is conceptually contestable.

This distinction is also relevant to Röllicke's (2023) conceptualization of affective polarization, which distinguishes between increases in affective polarization (affective polarization as process) driven by growing ingroup positivity, growing outgroup negativity, or both. Importantly, these processes need not occur simultaneously. Accordingly, the present finding that intellectual humility moderated the effect of attitude-congruent exposure on PAD, but not NAD, suggests that positive and negative intergroup differentiation may respond differently to individual characteristics.

Implications

This study provides a controlled test of the effect of exposure to attitude-congruent and attitude-incongruent information. Much prior work has examined polarization in platform environments in which exposure may not only be shaped by content congruence exposure alone but also by self-selection, recommendation mechanisms, and concurrent exposure to additional information (Barberá, 2020; Levy, 2021; Ludwig & Müller, 2022). The present study cannot reproduce that ecological complexity, but it helps clarify one part of the puzzle by isolating the attitudinal congruence of exposure itself. The finding that attitude-congruent information exposure increased all three polarization outcomes under these controlled conditions suggests that attitude congruence itself can contribute to polarization, independently of additional processes such as self-selection and platform characteristics.

A further implication of the present findings concerns the conceptualization and operationalization of affective polarization. Affective polarization is often conceptualized in relational terms, as an evaluative gap between a political ingroup and an outgroup (Iyengar & Westwood, 2015; Röllicke, 2023; Wagner, 2021). Accordingly, the presence of positive ingroup or negative outgroup evaluations alone does not necessarily indicate a divide in group evaluations. For instance, individuals may hold similarly strong positive and negative evaluations of both in-and outgroups in which case no intergroup differentiation or gap would be present. By assessing ingroup-outgroup gaps separately for positive and negative evaluations, the present operationalization preserves the relational conceptualization of affective polarization while distinguishing evaluative dimensions that are not necessarily reciprocal. Beyond allowing for a more nuanced examination of affective polarization, this distinction has broader conceptual implications. As Cacioppo and Berntson (1994) argue, measurement instruments shape what can be observed and how phenomena are theorized.

Assessing affective polarization only with bipolar scales may therefore favor interpretations in terms of reciprocal ingroup love and outgroup hate, even when this pattern is partly imposed by the measure itself and the underlying evaluative processes are more differentiated.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The experimental manipulation relied on constructed information environments around a single political issue, UBI, and therefore cannot speak to whether the same effects generalize across issue domains. The design also prioritizes internal validity over ecological validity. Real-world exposure usually involves more heterogeneous, socially embedded, and dynamically updated information streams than those presented here (Kubin & Von Sikorski, 2021; Sindermann et al., 2024; Winter et al., 2015). Moreover, although the issue-based operationalization of affective polarization addresses limitations of party-based measures in multiparty settings, such measures remain less established in the literature. Replication with other issues, populations, and measurement strategies is therefore necessary.

Further, while the separate assessment of positive and negative affective divides allows the disentanglement of both dimensions, the use of difference scores also has interpretative limitations. Particularly, changes in these divides cannot establish whether they are driven by changes in ingroup evaluations, outgroup evaluations, or both. Thus, although this operationalization preserves the relational conceptualization of affective polarization, it does not fully distinguish the underlying evaluative processes. The choice between either preserving the relational aspect of affective polarization and further differentiation into singular group evaluations may depend on the question a researcher is interested in.

Conclusion

Overall, the present findings suggest that attitude-congruent information environments can increase both issue polarization and positive and negative affective intergroup divides, whereas attitude-incongruent environments may attenuate some but not all forms of polarization. The findings further illustrate the value of assessing positive and negative affective components of affective polarization separately as it can reveal patterns that may have remained obscured in traditional bipolar measures of affective polarization.

Taken together, the results support a more differentiated understanding of the emergence of political polarization as a multifaceted phenomenon that depends both on the structure of the information environment and on how polarization itself is conceptualized and measured.
