Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

The prefrontal cortex (PFC) transforms what we know about the world into intelligent, adaptive behaviour. Existing theories have traditionally explained this function through cross-circuit interactions, where PFC neurons control sensory-action mappings elsewhere in the brain. However, recent findings reveal a remarkable organisation of neuronal activity that enables flexible computation within the PFC itself. These findings motivate a framework of duplicated and structured representations (DSRs), where the abstract structure of the world is embedded locally in connections between prefrontal neurons encoding conjunctions of action and context. 1-Introduction

Our rich repertoire of behaviours, and ability to adjust them when necessary, is central to our success in a changing world (Miller et al., 1960). This flexibility requires us to turn knowledge about the world into organised actions that serve our current goals, a process that critically involves the Prefrontal cortex (PFC; see Supplementary Box 1 for a discussion of PFC definitions). Indeed, PFC damage often spares knowledge of the world while impairing the ability to use it to guide behaviour (Duncan et al., 1996; Shallice, 1982; Shallice and Burgess, 1991). Moreover, the dramatic expansion of the primate PFC has been linked to our unique capacity to devise new ways of organising complex behaviours (Klein-Flügge et al., 2022; Passingham, 2021), such as those that enabled the invention of farming in ancient Mesopotamia (Smith, 1995; Zeder, 2011). Such a revolutionary invention necessitated inferring how our actions, and those of others, would change under a multitude of different scenarios -like changing weather conditions, market demands and family dynamics. The PFC is strongly implicated in such inferences about ourselves and others (Klein-Flügge et al., 2022; Mahmoodi and Rushworth, 2026). Yet this diversity of functions poses a challenge for any attempt at a unified mechanistic understanding.

An emerging body of work suggests an additional type of representation that coexists with factorised representations of abstract context. These findings reveal that the PFC itself computes possible actions through two key representational features. First, dedicated neurons explicitly represent possible actions, and these representations are duplicated across different contexts -different neurons represent the same action depending on its context. Second, structured knowledge is embedded in the connectivity between these duplicated representations, allowing knowledge about the outside world or internal states to shape which combinations of actions can be encoded. Together, duplication and structured connectivity allow the PFC to encode "local copies" of possible actions whose organisation is constrained by synaptic connectivity that embeds abstract structure (Figure 1b). These two features therefore allow the PFC itself to compute over possible actions -it no longer acts merely as a mediator between stimulus and response in other circuits. Instead, the burden of behavioural control is shifted inwards, to local network dynamics within the PFC.

In this review, we highlight a growing body of empirical findings that can be understood through this framework of duplicated and structured representations (DSRs) in the PFC. We first explain the representation and its algorithmic implications. We then outline how it provides a mechanistic framework that explains emerging empirical data across diverse PFC-dependent tasks, from stimulus-response mappings through value learning to sequence working memory and planning. Finally, we discuss how DSRs align with the diverse functions of different PFC subregions, shedding new light on the evolutionary expansion of PFC and its role in hierarchical reasoning and social inference. For simplicity, we visually illustrate DSRs using single neurons tuned to conjunctions of actions and contexts. However, the same computational principles apply when such conjunctions are encoded at the population level (Supplementary Box 2).

2-Local computation in the PFC uses duplication and structure

2.1 Duplicated and Structured Representations (DSRs) of possible actions

How does the PFC translate abstract knowledge into flexible behaviour? Consider Hassan, an experienced farmer on the modern-day Euphrates who reorganises his actions depending on the weather. In dry conditions he fertilizes before sowing, but plants first if rain is expected (Figure 1c). Crucially, he can also imagine how his actions, and those of others, would change if the world was different. When his daughter finally gets the tractor she has always dreamed of, Hassan will immediately know how this will reshape both their daily schedules. A wealth of evidence places the PFC at the top of an action selection hierarchy critical for mediating such complex inferences (Fine and Hayden, 2021; Fuster, 2000; Koechlin and Summerfield, 2007; Miller et al., 1960). Here, actions encompass a broad set of volitional choices about states that can be planned over, from concrete limb movements to more abstract decisions like navigating to a particular goal. Under this view, PFC neurons represent possible ways of actively organising future actions rather than passively predicting sensory outcomes (Fine and Hayden, 2021; Miller et al., 1960). We propose that this function depends critically on a pervasive representational principle. The activity of PFC neurons explicitly represents possible actions, and their connectivity embeds structured knowledge that organises these actions. These representations have two key features. They are: 1-Duplicated: The PFC uses different neurons, or different population activity patterns (Supplementary Box 2), to represent the same action depending on its context.

For example, representations can be duplicated for different ordinal positions; different expected times; different levels of preference; or different task rules. In sequential behaviours, representations are duplicated according to ordinal position. Different populations in Hassan's PFC represent the same 'sowing' action depending on whether it is planned for today, tomorrow, or the day after (Figure 1c). When actions are ranked by preference, representations of the same action are instead duplicated according to their rank. The same plan to 'plant rice' is represented by different neurons depending on whether it is the first, second, or third priority (Figure 1d). Action representations can also be duplicated according to the sensorimotor mappings specified by different 'rules'. For example, one set of neurons might encode that "Hassan should water tomorrow if it is dry", while another would encode that "Hassan should water tomorrow if his niece comes over to help". Crucially, such tuning generalises across different action combinations -the same neurons encode a particular conjunction of action and context regardless of which actions occur in other contexts. For example, a neuron encoding 'plant oats at position 2' fires whenever this action occupies that position, regardless of which actions fill the remaining positions (Figure 1d). DSRs are therefore 'combinatorial': new combinations -or "assemblies" -of neurons within the local network can represent any of the many possible action combinations, including hypothetical ones that have never been executed before. The result is a highly expressive network brimming with possibilities.

2-Structured: Our actions are structured by knowledge of the world and ourselves. This knowledge renders some action combinations more probable than others. Such 'action statistics' can reflect the structure of a particular environment, mirror the abstract rules of a particular task, or emerge from internally generated constraints. We propose that the vast combinatorial landscape of duplicated representations is sculpted by the statistical relationships between actions. For instance, knowledge of seasonal food preferences will constrain connectivity between crop representations in Hassan's PFC to determine the order of his planting cycles. In other words, action statistics should constrain the connectivity between the local action copies. Neurons encoding more probable ways of organising actions excite each other, and those encoding improbable action patterns are mutually inhibitory. When this connectivity is persistently active (Box 1), it stabilises neuronal assemblies representing highly probable action combinations (Figure 1e). These assemblies become "attractor" states of the network dynamics, which are more likely to be expressed in the network than assemblies representing alternative action combinations (Figure 1e). Structured connectivity can also be gated (Box 1). In this case, it only mediates information transfer after a control signal is received. This could be an external stimulus, but it could also reflect internal signals indicating execution of an action or a change in internal state. Such gated connectivity allows the PFC to perform various operations over duplicated representations (Figure 1f). Crucially, structured connectivity between duplicated representations does not need to be monosynaptic. 2.2 What do DSRs offer?

Duplicated and structured representations (DSRs) allow the PFC to organise behaviours through local dynamics. Dedicated neurons are tuned to different possible actions, and the relationships between actions are constrained by the network connectivity. This allows recurrent network dynamics to translate structured knowledge into flexible processing of complex, multi-action behaviours.

2.2.1 -Duplicated representations enable efficient readout

Under DSRs, the PFC represents several relevant actions simultaneously in the activity of different neuronal populations. Multiple actions can now be processed concurrently -an idea postulated by Lashley as early as the 1950s (Lashley, 1951). The challenge of organising actions, such as forming extended plans, is reduced to simply selecting among different assemblies within the local network. DSRs therefore differ from factorised representations, where the organisation of actions is encoded in the temporal pattern of neuronal activity (Figure 1g). A prominent example of this temporal coding is hippocampal replay, where several place cells fire one after another to encode a sequence of locations (Skaggs and McNaughton, 1996). In this case, the encoded action sequence can only be deciphered by integrating information over time, which is substantially more complicated than reading static assemblies (Branco et al., 2010; Buzsáki, 2010). By representing action combinations with static assemblies, duplication facilitates efficient "linear" readout by other sensory, motor, and memory systems. This simplified neural readout is similar in spirit to that offered by random non-linear mixed selectivity described in the PFC and wider neocortex (Rigotti et al., 2013; Tye et al., 2024). Both theories rely on high-dimensional representations to simplify downstream readout of conjunctive information, like actions and their contexts. However, they also have important differences. First, the duplication in DSRs is a special case of non-linear mixed selectivity, where the tuning of neurons to conjunctions of actions and contexts generalises across different action combinations. This enables flexible combinatorial coding of new action combinations (Figure 1c,d). Second, DSRs are not random projections of actions and contexts. Instead, they are connected in a way that embeds structured knowledge about the world (Figure 1b,e,f). This not only enables efficient readout, but also allows powerful computations over future actions within the local PFC network.

2.2.2 -Structured representations enable efficient computation

DSRs are poised for efficient computation. Inference happens locally through simple attractor dynamics that are constrained by the structure of the world and internal goals. By pre-configuring such structure into connectivity between action copies, network activity is steered towards stable assemblies that encode action sequences. Under DSRs, planning therefore becomes equivalent to recognition. Just as visual cortical activity is shaped by the statistics of visual scenes (Iacaruso et al., 2017; Lee and Nguyen, 2001; Marr, 2010) (Box 1), we propose that PFC activity is shaped by the statistical patterns of action transitions. This allows inference of future actions instead of objects and minimises the need for costly sequential planning at decision time. DSRs therefore unify our understanding of the PFC with other cortical regions that embed statistical structure in synaptic connections to enable efficient neural computation (Marr, 2010). Crucially, when this structured connectivity is gated, it enables abstract operations over multiple actions. Swaps, reversals and comparisons can now be computed locally, simultaneously, and on demand. Together, these features endow DSRs with remarkable flexibility and efficiency in organising our actions to solve new problems.

At the core of this efficiency is the combinatorial nature of DSR duplication. This is distinct from the "cloning" in clone-structured cognitive graphs (CSCGs; George et al., 2021). Whereas CSCG states are split based on sequential history to de-alias similar observations, DSR actions are systematically copied across contexts to span all possible action combinations. Notably, the rich de-aliased representations built by CSCGs, such as place cells, can themselves serve as inputs to DSR duplication in the PFC, building a combinatorial code of possible future locations (e.g. see Section 3.1.2).

2.2.3 -DSRs balance computational and neuronal efficiency

DSRs are computationally efficient, but this efficiency requires large neuronal populations. To encode all possible action combinations, every conjunction of action (A) and context (N) must be represented by a different pattern of neural activity. This differs from the 'factorised' representations proposed in the switchboard view of the PFC, where actions and contexts are encoded in separate neurons and combined via gating or synaptic plasticity at decision time (Behrens et al., 2018; Burgess and Hitch, 1999; Eichenbaum, 2004; Whittington et al., 2020) (Figure 1a,g,h). DSRs therefore require as many neurons as the product of actions and contexts (AxN), while the factorised solution only needs the sum (A+N; Figure 1h). Indeed, RNNs need many more neurons to learn DSRs than factorised representations, especially when A and N are large (Jensen et al., 2026; Whittington et al., 2025). However, this neuronal cost of DSRs is justified for two reasons. First, DSRs are more computationally flexible and efficient than factorised solutions (Section 2.2.1,2.2.2). Second, DSRs can be more neuronally efficient than alternative solutions that encode each combination of actions in a different neuron. This depends on how much flexibility is needed (Dorrell et al., 2026). If Hassan always plants his crops in the same order, a single neuron suffices to encode this stereotyped "planting" sequence. If he instead wants to consider all possible sequences, assigning one neuron per sequence uses more neurons (A N ) than the combinatorial DSR regime (Figure 1h).

To summarise, we propose that the PFC leverages large neuronal resources to build expressive representations that allow efficient readout and inference. By duplicating action representations across different contexts and then connecting these duplicates to reflect structured action statistics, DSRs allow generalisation and efficient inference across a broad swathe of different behaviours. We next explore a growing body of empirical findings that can be understood through DSRs.

3-Understanding PFC function through DSRs

Recent decades have seen a surge in high-yield neurophysiological data (Jun et al., 2017; Pachitariu et al., 2016; Zong et al., 2022) obtained during increasingly more sophisticated tasks. These advances have revealed rich representations and dynamics within the PFC itself that go far beyond simple contextual gating of activity in other brain regions. In this section, we show that the DSR framework provides a unifying lens for understanding such rich PFC data across paradigms. We start with sequential tasks that range from simple sequence working memory to complex planning. We then consider tasks where actions are organised by stimulus-response rules or ranked by preference. Across these paradigms, DSRs provide a framework that explains how neuronal dynamics can implement canonical PFC-dependent computations.

3.1-Sequential behaviours

We begin with action sequences. Patients with PFC lesions are profoundly impaired in flexible (re)sequencing of their actions to achieve new goals (Petrides and Milner, 1982). Thanks to improved neuronal recordings, the field has made substantial strides in understanding the underlying neural computations.

3.1.1 -Sequence working memory uses duplicated and structured representations

One way to generate a sequence is to simply retrieve it. In such sequence working memory (SWM) tasks, subjects hold a cued chain of actions in memory and execute it in the correct order after a short delay. The DSR framework suggests that different actions are represented by different neurons depending on their relative order in the sequence. Consistent with this idea, early studies in macaques performing SWM tasks described frontal neurons tuned to conjunctions of action and ordinal position (Barone and Joseph, 1989; Botvinick and Watanabe, 2007; Funahashi et al., 1997; Inoue and Mikami, 2006; Ninokura et al., 2004 Ninokura et al., , 2003;; Rigotti et al., 2013; Tanji, 2001). More recently, Xie et al. (Xie et al., 2022) took advantage of higher recording yields to comprehensively characterise SWM representations (Figure 2a). They found that monkeys represented each element of the remembered sequence in near-orthogonal subspaces of the dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC) during the delay phase (Figure 2b). This provides direct evidence that the representation of each action is duplicated across different ordinal positions (Chen et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2022). Intriguingly, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) trained on SWM tasks learn a representation that similarly duplicates actions across different subspaces -or slots -according to ordinal position (Botvinick and Plaut, 2006; Wang et al., 2025; Whittington et al., 2025) (Figure 2c). Normative theory is beginning to explain why: Dorrell et al. (2026) showed that the observed nearorthogonal duplicated representations are optimal when many different action sequences are encountered (Dorrell et al., 2026) -that is, when all action-context combinations are possible.

The DSR framework posits that connections between action copies in SWM tasks incorporate the linear order of actions, ensuring the first action is retrieved first, then the second, and so on. If the memory subspaces encode relative sequence position (now, next, next +1) instead of absolute position (steps 1, 2 and 3), gated connectivity can map each action in a given subspace (e.g. 'next') to the corresponding action in the preceding subspace (e.g. 'now'; Figure 2d). Activating these connections after executing an action advances the representation one step: it "moves" the action from subspace N+1 into subspace N. This gives rise to a 'conveyor belt' mechanism, gradually shifting actions from subspaces representing the distant future to the 'now' subspace, where an action can be read out and executed (Figure 2e). Whether the PFC uses relative or absolute position coding, and thus whether a conveyor belt mechanism operates, can be resolved by tracking representations through execution, not just the delay period. In the simplest case, this conveyor belt provides an elegant mechanism for retrieving a sequence from memory (Botvinick and Plaut, 2006; Jensen et al., 2026; Whittington et al., 2025). Intriguingly, this principle can also extend to inference of action sequences. Action representations are duplicated according to ordinal position, and gated connections map action representations from the "next" position to the "now" position, and from "next + 1" to "next", etc. (e) DSRs predict that, at execution time, conveyor belt dynamics read out the sequence one element at a time. As an action is executed, the remaining action representations are shifted by one position: a new action is moved to the "now" position then executed, leading to a further shift along the conveyor belt.

3.1.2 -Duplicated and structured representations support inference

The same duplicated representations that support SWM can, when their connectivity embeds knowledge of the world, do more than retrieve sequences from memory. They can infer new ones. Evidence for such inference through DSRs is emerging. In a recent study, El-Gaby et al. (El-Gaby et al., 2024) trained mice on the "ABCD" task. Animals learned to navigate multiple sequences comprising four reward locations that repeated according to an abstract loop structure (... ABCDABCD…; Figure 3a). Animals used this structure to make novel inferences -on the first trial of a new goal sequence, they navigated directly from goal D to goal A (Figure 3b). Neurons in the prelimbic cortex (area 32) encoded duplicated representations of different spatial goals and subgoals during task execution, with some neurons firing when the goal/subgoal was visited "now", while others were tuned to the same goal/subgoal at a specific lag in the future (Figure 3c). Similar duplication is found in the PFC of humans performing on-screen variants of the ABCD task -both in single units and fMRI (Küchenhoff et al., 2025). Crucially, offline correlations between mouse prelimbic cells reflected the abstract task structure. The coactivity between action duplicates during sleep mirrored their proximity on the 4-goal loop -neighbouring positions showed stronger correlations than distant ones (Figure 3d). This is consistent with connections between action duplicates reflecting the abstract task structure. Embedding this abstract loop structure in gated connections allows a conveyor belt mechanism to both execute each action from memory and to infer the D→A step that completes the ABCD loop (Figure 3b). 3.1.3 -DSRs enable efficient planning

We plan in rich ways by leveraging our internal world model to generate new action sequences. The PFC is critical for this ability to override habitual responses in favour of adaptive behaviour (Doohan et al., 2026; Miller and Cohen, 2001). In section 3.1.2, we saw that connecting duplicated representations of the same action at different ordinal positions supports simple inferences like loop closure. However, DSRs can also generalise structured connectivity to enable richer inferences. This requires structured information to be embedded in connections between duplicated representations of different actions across ordinal positions. Such connectivity should follow a simple principle: neurons are mutually excitatory if they represent compatible actions, and mutually inhibitory if they do not. For example, if Hassan has to avoid manual labor on the farm after a surgery, the representation of a scheduled surgery tomorrow should inhibit the representation of harvesting the day after (Figure 4a). Recent in silico simulations lend support to these intuitions, showing that DSRs can implement planning by embedding such structured knowledge (Jensen et al., 2026). The resulting 'spacetime attractor' network steers representations towards plausible action sequences conditioned on a distant goal. Planning takes the form of attractor dynamics that simultaneously refine the plan backwards from the goal and forwards from the current state to converge on an entire action sequence, which can then be executed through the conveyorbelt mechanism discussed before (Botvinick and Plaut, 2006; El-Gaby et al., 2024; Jensen et al., 2026; Whittington et al., 2025). This contrasts with common planning algorithms that use sequential search to evaluate actions one by one. Recurrent neural networks trained to solve challenging planning tasks with minimal energy expenditure learn a solution that precisely recapitulates a DSR -in representation, in dynamics, and in connectivity (Jensen et al., 2026). DSRs may therefore constitute an efficient solution to planning through local dynamics.

Several studies of PFC representations during planning can be understood through the DSR framework. PFC neurons represent actions multiple steps into the future after monkeys plan action sequences (Averbeck et al., 2002; Chiang and Wallis, 2018; Mushiake et al., 2006; Saito et al., 2005; Sakamoto et al., 2020). In particular, Chiang and Wallis found future representations to be stronger than representations of the past in the dlPFC, in line with the view that PFC is poised to infer future actions rather than merely remember the past (Figure 4b). In addition to this direct support, a broad set of findings are consistent with duplicated action representations during planning. For example, Manakov et al. (Manakov et al., 2025) recorded from ACC (area 24) in rats performing rewarded action sequences and found neurons tuned to conjunctions of actions and ordinal position within a specific sequence. This mixed selectivity rules out factorised ordinal representations. However, it could rule in DSRs if neurons represent the same conjunctions across different sequences, which remains to be tested. Separately, human neuroimaging has identified PFC BOLD responses that instantaneously represent entire sequences during planning (Schuck et al., 2016; Schwartenbeck et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023; Wittmann et al., 2025). Such concurrent coding of extended sequences is consistent with DSRs but could also reflect a rapid sequential roll-out. This distinction can be resolved in fMRI by testing whether representations are conjunctive for action and ordinal position, as DSRs predict, or encode action alone, as sequential rollout would predict. Finally, DSRs propose that plans can be computed by prefrontal attractor dynamics. Consistent with this, Saito et al. (Saito et al., 2005) found that PFC representations of later actions often emerge before representations of early actions during planning. This could result from backward step-by-step planning from a goal. It would also naturally arise if plans were inferred within a DSR itself, where representations gradually propagate away from both the current location and distant goals to infer intermediate actions. Together, these results suggest that duplicated representations extend from working memory to internally generated action plans. However, direct characterisation of the structured connectivity underlying planning remains an important open question. Optical imaging and holographic stimulation offer promising tools to address this (Daie et al., 2021; Vinograd et al., 2024).

3.1.4 -DSRs enable flexible computations over sequences

We have so far presented examples that require us to plan once and then act. However, real-world behaviour is rarely so straightforward. We often revise and update previously planned sequences: we reorder, skip, or substitute actions as our goals or environment change. If Hassan's niece shows up earlier than expected, he will reorder his plan: store the grain first and pen the animals later, so she can ride the donkey. DSRs simplify such 'computations over sequences' because the entire future is represented concurrently, which facilitates simultaneous operations over multiple actions. In one demonstration of this, Tian et al. (Tian et al., 2024) trained macaques to remember sequences of 2 or 3 locations on a screen. Subjects were then cued to either repeat the sequence as shown or produce it in reverse (Figure 4c). Neurons in the dlPFC again duplicated action representations by their ordinal position (Figure 4d). Intriguingly, representations in the first and last subspaces were swapped on reversal trials by passing actions through two temporary subspaces (Figure 4d,e). Such a mechanism may help explain earlier fMRI findings, which showed greater dlPFC activation when subjects reversed a sequence than when they recalled it in order (Sakai and Passingham, 2003) -perhaps reflecting the additional activation of gated temporary subspaces. It also reveals remarkable flexibility in PFC representations, where information can be moved between different ordinal positions depending on control signals from the environment. Dedicated temporary subspaces allow common transformations, like reversals, to be carried out efficiently without the need to re-infer the updated sequence de novo. Other abstract operations can similarly be implemented through such 'gated' subspaces that conjunctively code for an operation and the variables it operates over. These flexible operations over concurrent representations bear a striking resemblance to the transformer architecture that has revolutionised machine learning. Transformers represent extended sequences concurrently, which allows an 'attention mechanism' to integrate information across time (Vaswani et al., 2023). This approach outperforms previous methods that process sequence elements one by one. The PFC may have arrived at the same solution: parallelising representations to enable powerful inferences and abstract operations through network dynamics.

3.2-Contextual behaviours beyond sequences

We now turn to prefrontal computations in non-sequential settings. Under DSRs, flexible processing of parallel action representations provides insights into a wide range of PFC-dependent contextual behaviours that require comparisons and integration of information about multiple possible actions. What unites these behaviours is a common principle: structured knowledge is used to organise actions flexibly when demands change. We highlight two especially prominent cases, where actions are either organised by the rules that govern them or by their changing priorities.

3.2.1 -Organising actions by rules

A hallmark of PFC-dependent flexibility is the ability to switch between different stimulus-response mappings depending on the context. In such 'set-shifting' tasks, identical stimuli call for different actions depending on which 'rule' is currently active (Birrell and Brown, 2000; Broschard et al., 2021). This can be achieved by duplicating representations, such that the same action is represented by different neurons for different rules. In a seminal study, Panichello and Buschman (2021) first presented monkeys with two colour cues positioned at the top and bottom of a screen (Panichello and Buschman, 2021). A subsequent cue indicated whether to attend to the top or bottom colour, and animals then selected their response on a colour wheel (Figure 5a). Colour representations were duplicated in three orthogonal subspaces: one encoded the top colour, one encoded the bottom colour, and a "choice" subspace was active at decision time for both rules (Figure 5b). This duplication allows any combination of top and bottom colours to coexist in the neural activity of PFC. Under DSRs, rule information selectively gates connections that move colour information from the appropriate input subspace into the choice subspace (Figure 5c). Similar duplication of choices across rules has also been found in mouse ACC (Tissot et al., 2025; area 24) and monkey dlPFC (Bernardi et al., 2020; area 46). Together, these results provide cross-species evidence for rule-dependent duplication of actions. DSRs also suggest structured connectivity between the rule subspaces, which could be used to infer actions that are not explicitly cued. Structured connectivity could impose specific dependencies between available choices: e.g. if the top colour is red, the bottom colour cannot be blue. The same inferential machinery used for sequential behaviour in Section 3.1 could therefore operate over 'rule subspaces' to infer actions that were never explicitly cued.

3.2.2 -Ranking actions by preference

Our environment is constantly changing. To adapt, we must continually reassess which actions are most valuable. The importance of the PFC for this flexibility is evidenced by its well-established role in reversal learning tasks (Dias et al., 1996). Such value learning has traditionally been explained by reinforcement learning (RL), where choice values are learned via dopamine-dependent synaptic plasticity at cortico-striatal synapses (Reynolds et al., 2001; Schultz et al., 1997). However, recent computational and experimental work has motivated an alternative meta-reinforcement learning framework for reward-guided behaviour (Hattori et al., 2023; Jensen, 2024; Wang et al., 2018). In this view, the PFC meta-learns the abstract task structure through slow synaptic plasticity across many tasks. Once learned, this structure allows rapid adaptation to structurally similar tasks via recurrent dynamics rather than further synaptic change. Such 'learning through dynamics' echoes a key hallmark of DSRs, where connections between action copies embed the task structure to support rapid inference in new tasks via neural dynamics. Consistent with this idea, recent theoretical work shows that neural networks can implement meta-RL by duplicating action representations according to their preference (Warren et al., 2026). In recurrent neural networks trained on multi-armed bandit tasks with drifting rewards, duplicated representations emerged for each action. One subspace encoded the identity of the preferred action, an orthogonal subspace encoded the second-best action, and so on (Figure 5d). Learning in a new task then involves reorganising the appropriate actions across these rank subspaces. Crucially, this reordering is governed by a separate representation of the value of each rank, independent of action identity. For example, when the value of the second option exceeds the preferred option, a gated connection is activated to swap the corresponding action representations (Figure 5d). DSRs may also encode structured dependencies between action preferences. For example, planting may consistently have low priority whenever irrigation has high priority. Such correlations could be embedded in persistent connectivity between representations of actions at different ranks. This would allow inference of action preferences in an analogous way to how structured connectivity inferred action sequences in Section 3.1.2.

The DSR-based account of reward-driven learning makes three main predictions. First, PFC neurons should duplicate action representations according to their preference. Consistent with this, Li et al. recorded from the lateral PFC of macaques solving an economic decision-making task (Li et al., 2026). Subjects were first shown the identity and then value of option 1, and then the identity and value of option 2. Before 'value 2' was shown, the representations of option 1 and option 2 were orthogonal. However, once value 2 was presented, a consistent subspace encoded the preferred option (Figure 5e). Prefrontal representations were therefore duplicated by preference when subjects had the information necessary to rank the options. A related literature has described non-linear mixed selectivity for actions and value in the PFC (Abe and Lee, 2011; Hayden and Platt, 2010; Johnston et al., 2024). Within a DSR framework, such mixed selectivity arises because value correlates with rank, and different subspaces represent actions at different ranks. Second, the PFC should encode the value associated with each rank independently of the corresponding action identity. This idea is consistent with the widely reported finding that the PFC separately represents the value of "chosen" (preferred) and "unchosen" (non-preferred) options in binary decision tasks (Boorman et al., 2011 (Boorman et al., , 2009;; Daw et al., 2006; Fouragnan et al., 2019; Hayden et al., 2009; Padoa-Schioppa and Assad, 2008, 2006), and the value of all ranks in tasks with three options (Boorman et al., 2011; Knudsen and Wallis, 2021; Zhang et al., 2026) (Figure 5f). Third, DSRs predict structured connectivity between subspaces that represent actions with different preferences. Value representations should control gated connections that swap the content of different ranked subspaces (Warren et al., 2026). Persistent connectivity could also impose correlations between actions at different ranks and allow inference to complement reward-driven learning. Although recent findings suggest that structure and value coding coexist in the PFC (Jahn et al., 2024; Veselic et al., 2025b), structured connections across subspaces for different preferences remain to be explored. Overall, the DSR framework offers a mechanistic account that could unify previously separate literatures on canonical PFC-dependent behaviours such as sequence working memory, planning, set-shifting and value-based decision making. In all of these behaviours, PFC recordings reveal duplication of action representations across ordinal position, rule or rank. Functional connectivity between these action duplicates embeds both concrete and abstract structures -allowing not only memory retrieval but also inferences and flexible computations over actions. In the following section, we move beyond the classically well-studied PFC-dependent behaviours to explore how DSRs may help us understand broader, more complex roles of the PFC.

4-Why is the PFC so big?

So far, we have reviewed a large literature spanning normative theories (section 2) and neural data (section 3). To bridge the theory-data divide, the field has looked to a well-established tradition of modelling the PFC with recurrent neural networks (RNNs; Botvinick and Plaut, 2006; Wang et al., 2018; Whittington et al., 2025; Zipser et al., 1993). We have learned from a growing body of simulations that DSRs emerge when RNNs are trained on PFC-like tasks (Botvinick and Plaut, 2006; Dorrell et al., 2026; Jensen et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025; Warren et al., 2026; Whittington et al., 2025). However, there is an elephant in the room. While RNNs typically use tens to hundreds of units, the human PFC boasts billions of neurons, organised into a rich landscape of anatomical and functional gradients (Passingham, 2021) (Figure 6a). This ten-million-fold disparity suggests an intriguing possibility: perhaps the core principle motivating DSRs -trading neuronal resources for computational efficiency -extends further. An expanded PFC could allow dedicated neuronal resources to be allocated to behaviours with distinct transition statistics, achieving efficiency not only within tasks but also across them.

Over his lifetime, Hassan will engage in a wide range of behaviours that entail distinct actions governed by unique statistics. His hand movements when operating the tractor have different statistics to his spatial movements across the field, and both differ from his negotiations with the cattle merchant. If you challenge a small network with enough different tasks, it will eventually reconfigure the same neurons and synapses to encode new actions and their transition statistics (Jensen et al., 2026). With a billion neurons, the PFC can afford to do something different. It can allocate dedicated neural real estate to each class of behaviour, with connectivity tailored to its unique statistics. This minimizes the need for synaptic plasticity when switching between different types of behaviour. It also allows the PFC to process multiple action types in parallel, and to integrate information across these processes. We now explore how this perspective bears on representational heterogeneity in the PFCspanning different action modalities, levels of abstraction, and even different agents. We end by discussing how these different action statistics may emerge, through both evolution and experience.

4.1-Organising different action modalities

We have known since the 1970s that different subdivisions of the PFC process different actions in line with their anatomical inputs and outputs (Goldman and Nauta, 1977; Goldman-Rakic, 1984). For example, the dorsolateral PFC has strong interactions with parietal circuits that bias it towards sensorimotor information (Passingham, 2021; Petrides and Pandya, 1999). In contrast, medial PFC is biased towards allocentric representations that reflect its interactions with the hippocampal formation (Eichenbaum, 2017; Passingham, 2021). These differences map directly onto DSRs recorded from different subregions (Figure 6b). For example, DSRs in the dorsolateral PFC organise sequences of eye movements (Xie et al., 2022), while those in the medial PFC represent sequences of allocentric locations (El-Gaby et al., 2024; Küchenhoff et al., 2025). Finer subdivisions have also been reported. For instance, area 8/46 is biased towards occulomotor control due to its preferential inputs from parietal eye areas and outputs to the frontal eye fields (Cavada and Goldman-Rakic, 1989), while area 9/46 generalises across action modalities (Dum and Strick, 2005; Lu et al., 1994). This is reflected in DSRs recorded across these subdivisions. While Xie et al. report DSRs for eye movements in 8/46 neurons (Xie et al., 2022), Chiang and Wallis find DSR-consistent representations of hand movements that span areas 9 and 9/46 (Chiang and Wallis, 2018). These results suggest a basic idea: PFC subregions contain duplicated representations of the action modality they process. Under the DSR framework, such duplicated representations should be connected according to the statistics of the relevant modality. This may shed new light on action domains where mechanistic understanding remains limited, such as speech production in the inferior frontal gyrus. For example, recent single cell recordings from human PFC reveal simultaneous representations of past, present and future phonemes, syllables and words (Gadonneix et al., 2026). Connections between duplicated phoneme representations could reflect the transition probabilities driving the organisation of speech using a DSR-like mechanism. Recent advances in human single cell recordings (e.g. Khanna et al., (Khanna et al., 2024)) could shed new light on this and other examples of structured representations across modalities.

4.2-Representational Hierarchies

As well as different modalities, the PFC also represents actions at multiple levels of abstraction along a posterior to anterior gradient (Badre and Nee, 2018; Collins and Koechlin, 2012; Koechlin et al., 2003). Both single neurons and human neuroimaging have identified more abstract representations in anterior than posterior PFC (Badre and Nee, 2018; Collins and Koechlin, 2012; Koechlin et al., 2003), and anterior prefrontal lesions lead to deficits specifically when sequencing multiple abstract subtasks (Chau et al., 2025; Shallice and Burgess, 1991). If such hierarchical representations are combined with DSRs, exciting new computational possibilities emerge. A series of DSRs at different levels of abstraction could implement hierarchical planning -a hallmark of human behaviour (Eckstein and Collins, 2020). Critically, the use of different DSRs enables simultaneous planning across multiple levels of abstraction. This allows the different DSRs to interact, such that actions at one level of abstraction can constrain those at another. For example, an abstract 'task-level' DSR might infer that Hassan should harvest before his surgery. This abstract plan could in turn constrain activity in an intermediate DSR that infers the need to refuel the tractor before beginning the harvest. Finally, a low-level DSR would infer the path to the petrol station (Jensen et al., 2026) (Figure 6c). This suggests a role for structured connections not just within one level of abstraction, but also across levels. Although these additional levels of abstraction require more investment of neural resources, they enable planning over long time horizons with fewer neurons than non-hierarchical alternatives. Instead of resource-intensive duplication of low-level actions throughout an entire plan, the distant future is represented only at a high level of abstraction. Plans can therefore unfold efficiently, with transitions between higher levels defining higher goals and lower levels filling in the details.

4.3-Social computations

Alongside its role in planning, the PFC is strongly implicated in social computations (Mahmoodi and Rushworth, 2026; Wittmann et al., 2025; Mitchell et al., 2005; Amodio and Frith, 2006; Tamir and Mitchell, 2010; Behrens et al., 2008; Nicolle et al., 2012; Wittmann et al., 2016; Yoo et al., 2020; Wittmann et al., 2021; Isoda, 2021). Parts of the cingulate gyrus and dorsal area 9 are functionally and anatomically specialised to process social information (Isoda, 2021; Mahmoodi and Rushworth, 2026). Famously, they are activated in human tasks that involve simulating the actions and minds of others (Amodio and Frith, 2006; Jamali et al., 2021; Tamir and Mitchell, 2010; Wittmann et al., 2016). If these representations were combined with the computational principles of DSRs, they would allow inference of the actions of other agents in the same way that DSRs enabled planning as an inference over our own actions in Section 3. Indeed, representations of self and others appear to be anatomically distinct. Human neuroimaging suggests that more ventral areas (24/32) encode our own actions, while more dorsal areas (9) encode the imagined actions of others (Tamir and Mitchell, 2010; Wittmann et al., 2016). Similarly, single unit recordings in the primate PFC have identified separate representations of the future actions of self and others (Chericoni et al., 2025; Falcone et al., 2017; Haroush and Williams, 2015). Extending DSRs along a social dimension is consistent with PFC size scaling positively with social group size, both within (Sallet et al., 2011; Powell et al., 2012) and across species (Bouret et al., 2024). While it remains to be tested, such a self-other separation of DSRs would allow knowledge of others' action statistics to be embedded in dedicated synaptic connections, enabling efficient inference of their future actions (Figure 6d). It would also enable computation of multi-agent interactions. Encoding the statistics of interactions in synaptic connectivity between DSRs for self and other would allow inference of their combined future. For example, if Hassan cooks a big breakfast, his daughter will be more energized to help on the farm. Hassan's PFC could therefore contain a system of two DSRs that jointly settle into a representation of their mutually constrained future actions, akin to planning dynamics in a single DSR (Jensen et al., 2026) (Figure 6d). It is an intriguing prospect that such complex social interactions may become mechanistically tractable if we combine DSRs with known social specialisations across PFC subregions.

4.4-How does structured knowledge emerge in the PFC?

When functional specialisation in the PFC is combined with DSRs, domain-specific knowledge can be embedded in the synaptic connectivity of each PFC subregion to reflect the statistics of different actions. These preconfigured circuits allow network dynamics to rapidly infer actions at decision time, without the need for further plasticity. The burden of plasticity is instead shifted back to a time where the circuits are sculpted. How and when this happens remains unclear.

On the one hand, functional subdivisions suggest the possibility of region-specific connectivity that is genetically hardwired into the circuits, similar to the hardwired encoding of attractor networks for navigation (Guardamagna et al., 2026). This would reflect evolutionary constraints on domain-specific action statistics. For example, to process sequences, the dlPFC might benefit from hardwired connections that link representations of the same action across adjacent ordinal positions. This would implement conveyor belt dynamics that can recall and generate behavioural sequences (Section 3.1). Indeed, monkeys passively viewing images had semiorthogonal dlPFC subspaces for current and past images despite not solving an explicit task (Bellet et al., 2024; Pu et al., 2024). This suggests an a priori task-agnostic neural algorithm that shifts stimuli from a 'current' to a 'previous' subspace in dlPFC. mPFC dynamics also prominently track progress towards goals across species and tasks (Basu et al., 2021; El-Gaby et al., 2024; Guidera et al., 2026; Holton et al., 2024). These goal progress sequences may provide an innate representational scaffold for pacing DSRs dynamics.

Learning must also play a part, to encode structures our ancestors never encountered. Classical theories of complementary learning systems (CLS; McClelland et al., 1995) could provide a mechanistic basis for such learning. Domain-specific structured knowledge may first arise in the hippocampus, which encodes individual episodes, and then gradually consolidate into the PFC with repeated task experience (McClelland et al., 1995; Scoville and Milner, 1957; Spens and Burgess, 2024). Intriguingly, hippocampal replay is known to reflect the statistics of experience (Ólafsdóttir et al., 2015; Pfeiffer and Foster, 2013) and entrain cortex (Swanson et al., 2020). It is therefore ideally suited to catalyse the transfer of the transition structure of hippocampal memories into synaptic connections between prefrontal action copies. CLS theory posits that this transfer happens slowly after repeated experience of a particular environment (McClelland et al., 1995). This could provide a mechanism for allocating neuronal resources to DSRs only when they are useful (Sun et al., 2023). Hassan's PFC will have DSRs for farming while chess DSRs in a professional chess player's PFC will allow faster inference in a timepressured game. Future studies that track and manipulate activity in early postnatal development and during adult learning could help characterise both preconfigured structure and experience-dependent learning mechanisms.

Overall, extending the logic of DSRs places our mechanistic understanding within the broader context of the rich and extensive anatomy of the PFC. Dedicating different patches of PFC to different action modalities, levels of abstraction and agents may allow embedding modality, level and agent-specific structure -allowing complex and parallel computations across different action types. This in turn has implications for how structured representations in the PFC may emerge during development or learning.

5-Outlook

How do we translate structured knowledge into adaptive behaviour? Classical work has focused on factorised representations, where abstract knowledge and concrete actions are encoded by separate circuits and flexibly combined at decision time (Behrens et al., 2018; Burgess and Hitch, 1999; Eichenbaum, 2017; Miller and Cohen, 2001; Whittington et al., 2020). We have reviewed a growing literature that highlights an alternative strategy, where abstract knowledge and concrete actions are represented conjunctively in the same PFC neurons. This shifts some of the computational burden to local circuits, emphasising the role of internal prefrontal dynamics in flexibly structuring our actions. The result is a representational regime with all the flexibility offered by world models but with computational speed and efficiency rivalling habitual mechanisms. DSRs in the PFC might therefore mark proficiency, reflecting an investment of neural resources to translate knowledge into efficient behaviour in domains relevant to the individual.

The DSR framework motivates future analyses of structure between local action representations. However, elucidating structure in the PFC is challenging not only because of its size, but also because function is not easily mapped onto neuron identity. This is in stark contrast to simpler circuits like the fruit fly brain, where it suffices to build a connectome of a single brain to map the structured connectivity between known representations. Instead, we need to simultaneously measure prefrontal representations and connectivity de novo in each individual animal. Proxy measures of structure based on offline neuronal coactivity can allow progress (El-Gaby et al., 2024). Excitingly, more direct measures of structure are also becoming feasible. It is now possible to measure both representations and connectivity in the same neurons with optical holography (Yang and Yuste, 2018), and these tools can even be combined with freely moving neuronal imaging (Zong et al., 2022). These technologies may uncover how local connectivity implements flexible computations in a heterogeneous circuit like the PFC.

An important future direction will be to situate prefrontal DSRs within the broader literature on "cognitive maps", which finds structured knowledge representations throughout the brain (Behrens et al., 2018; Eichenbaum, 2004; Hafting et al., 2005; O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971). A defining feature of DSRs is that they encode structured knowledge in a highly actionable form. Because such knowledge is embedded into connections between action representations, it constrains efficient local computations over actions. This avoids the more expensive cross-circuit control and online search used by other representations (Section 2). It may also explain why PFC lesions spare semantic knowledge while disrupting the flexible organisation of behaviour it supports (Duncan et al., 1996; Shallice, 1982; Shallice and Burgess, 1991). Hassan may know that smoking causes cancer and yet make no effort to quit until his PFC embeds this relational knowledge into representations of his daily routine. The PFC could learn this structure from other circuits that encode abstract cognitive maps, in temporal (Section 4.4) and parietal (Bottini and Doeller, 2020) areas. It is an intriguing prospect that we can now move towards an integrated mechanistic understanding of how different cognitive maps interact to enable flexible behaviour. By combining precise theories with emerging experimental tools, our field is poised for a revolution in its mechanistic understanding of cognitive functions.

Glossary terms

Actions Volitional choices about states the animal can plan over. These can be low level actions, like moving a particular limb. They can also be more abstract actions, like choosing to pursue a degree. This definition of actions treats the firing of PFC neurons for e.g. spatial locations as encoding the decision to move to such location.

Context

Any variable that determines which action is appropriate in a given situation -including task rules, ordinal position within a sequence, preference rank, or the identity of an agent. In DSRs, context is encoded conjunctively with action -the same action is represented by different neurons depending on its context.

Action statistics

The statistical regularities governing how actions relate to one another. This includes the periodicities by which actions repeat and how frequently certain action transitions occur. In the DSR framework, action statistics are embedded in the synaptic connectivity between duplicated action representations, allowing the network to favour more probable action combinations and suppress improbable ones.

Attractor dynamics

Network activity that evolves towards stable representations known as 'attractor states'. The stability of different representations is determined by the pattern of synaptic connections. In DSRs, structured connectivity between action representations gives rise to attractor states that encode more probable action combinations. This allows planning and inference to emerge from dynamic relaxation rather than sequential search.

Factorised representations

A representational strategy in which different task variables, such as abstract context and concrete actions, are encoded by separate neural populations and combined only at decision time. This is in contrast to DSRs, where context and action are encoded conjunctively by the same neurons.

Mixed selectivity

A phenomenon where neurons respond to conjunctions of multiple task variables, such as an action and its context, rather than a single variable in isolation.

Readout

The process by which downstream circuits extract information from a neural population. DSRs represent action combinations in static population activity patterns, which allows a linear readout mechanism to decode the relevant information without needing to integrate activity over time.

Neural subspace

A low-dimensional subset of the high-dimensional space of all possible population activity patterns. In DSRs, different copies of the same action are represented in non-overlapping subspaces, each dedicated to a given context. This allows simultaneous encoding of multiple action-context combinations in population activity.

Meta-reinforcement learning

A framework in which the PFC learns the abstract statistical structure of a class of tasks through slow synaptic plasticity across many experiences. Once this structure is embedded in connectivity, the PFC can rapidly adapt to new tasks of the same class through fast recurrent dynamics alone, without further synaptic changes.

Box 1 |Two types of structured connectivity

DSRs can embed two classes of structured connectivity. The first is 'persistent' connectivity, which is always active and determines the stable activity patterns, or "fixed points", of a neural network. The second is 'gated' connectivity, which is activated by additional context signals to move neural activity between fixed points.

Persistent connectivity

This connectivity between duplicated representations reflects ubiquitous action statistics. For example, a representation of 'sowing' in the morning might excite a representation of 'watering' in the afternoon and inhibit representations of 'harvesting' in the near future. This idea has close parallels in visual cortex, where neurons with distant receptive fields are more frequently connected if they have similar orientation selectivity (Iacaruso et al., 2017) (Figure a: Persistent connectivity in visual cortex reflects the prevalence of straight lines in natural environments). This imposes structural constraints on visual representations and permits inference of global information from partial or noisy stimuli (Lee and Nguyen, 2001) (Figure b). We will see that PFC uses the same principle to infer representations of future actions, which enables efficient planning.

Gated connectivity

This connectivity implements structured operations over neural representations, such as swapping two sequence elements. Like persistent connectivity, gated connectivity reflects action statistics. Unlike persistent connectivity, gated connections are selectively engaged by additional context signals. Gated connections are well known from Drosophila, where a single head direction (HD) cell preferentially excites its 'clockwise' (CW) neighbor when the fly is turning clockwise and vice versa. This control of effective connectivity is achieved by two auxiliary 'turn' populations that implement the CW and CCW operations. When (de)activating the turn populations, HD cells change their effective connectivity to implement the appropriate rotation (Figure c: Gated 'left' and 'right' connections in the fruit fly head direction circuit implement angular velocity integration). PFC similarly controls the effective connectivity between duplicated representations by gating auxiliary neural populations that implement abstract computations. Such gating can be implemented through neurons that are conjunctively tuned to an operation and its substrate, similar to conjunctive cells that update spatial representations (Sargolini et al., 2006; Turner-Evans et al., 2017 , 2020).

Supplementary Box 1 | What is the PFC?

Classical definitions of the PFC rely on cytoarchitectonic (granular layer IV (Walker, 1940)), connectivity (mediodorsal thalamic input (Guldin et al., 2008; Leonard, 1969; Rose and Woolsey, 1948)), and functional (cognitive control (Fuster, 2001; Miller and Cohen, 2001)) criteria. These definitions disagree at the margins, with the cytoarchitectonic definition notably excluding agranular areas 32/24 in rodents. We focus instead on a functional definition of the PFC that side-steps these controversies. This widely adopted definition includes both granular (e.g. Walker area 9,10,45,46,47) and agranular (rodent Walker area 24, 32) subdivisions (Klein-Flügge et al., 2022; Passingham, 2021). Most data covered in this review comes from areas that are encompassed by all definitions. More broadly, however, the DSR framework aims to shed light across frontal areas that translate structured knowledge about the world into flexible behaviour.

Exact delineations of subdivisions across the PFC have also been challenged, and recent studies emphasise that there is no one-to-one mapping between neuronal representations and anatomical subdivisions (Hayden et al., 2026). Here, we provide a brief overview of classical subdivisions as a reference for the reviewed studies, which often record from only a small part of the broader prefrontal cortex.

Medial PFC

In primates, 'medial PFC' refers to cingulate and medial areas. This includes Walker areas [24, 25, 32], and the medial part of areas [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In rodents, the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), prelimbic (PL) and infralimbic (IL) areas are homologous to Walker areas 24, 32, and 25 respectively. However, other definitions have area 32 being homologous to cingulate areas in primates (Preuss, 1995). Across species, medial PFC has strong reciprocal connections with the hippocampal formation (ventral CA1/subiculum in rodents and anterior hippocampus in primates).

Lateral PFC This is a primate-specific region that is not found in rodents. It includes Walker areas [8, 9, 10 (lateral), 44, 45, 46, 47]. Major inputs include parietal cortex, and major outputs include premotor and motor areas. This enables top-down control over sensorimotor mappings.

Orbitofrontal/Ventral PFC In primates, this includes Walker areas [10 (orbital), 11, 12, 13, 14, and 47/12 (ventral)]. Rodent medial OFC, ventral OFC, and lateral OFC are homologous to Walker areas 11, 12 and 13 respectively.

Supplementary Box 2 | DSRs under the neuron and population doctrines

Duplication works similarly regardless of whether action-context conjunctions are encoded by single neurons or population patterns. As such, the distinction between DSRs and factorised representations is distinct from that between the neuron and population doctrines (Figure a). Under the "neuron doctrine", different conjunctions of context and action are encoded by separate neurons. In contrast the "population doctrine" (Ebitz and Hayden, 2021) has orthogonal directions in activity space encoding the different context-action combinations. In other words, unique combinations of neuronal activity patterns encode context-action conjunctions. This has the same effect on network capacity and the same arguments about DSRs being "neuron hungry" but "computationally efficient" apply (section 2). This is because the dimensionality of the network, the number of orthogonal axes that the network can harbour, is limited by the number of neurons. While there is evidence in the literature for all 4 types of codes (Ebitz and Hayden, 2021; El-Gaby et al., 2024; Shima et al., 2007; Veselic et al., 2025a; Wallis et al., 2001; Xie et al., 2022), the current review focuses on DSRs, both when they are encoded by individual neurons or by population patterns (shaded green area). For simplicity, we use the single neuron notations in diagrams to illustrate the properties of DSRs more intuitively.
