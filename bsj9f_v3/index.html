Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

What we found

We found 17 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Giving empathy is good, but providing emotional support is hard work. Empathic AI gives cheap and easy support, but may be ethically costly. When AI provides empathy, people may feel less compelled to support others. Four studies provide evidence for "empathy offloading": when people see AI as capable of empathy, they become less willing to empathize with the suffering of others, even without realizing it. When AI seems more capable of empathy, people-both with correlations (Study 1) and causal experiments (Study 2)-report more empathy offloading. When people learn that someone has received emotional support from large language models (LLMs), they engage less with others' mental states (Study 3) and offer fewer empathic responses and more social judgment (Study 4). Empathic AI might provide emotional support at any time, but it may weaken our ethical and emotional commitments to fellow human beings. The implications of offloading emotional support may include empathic devaluing and atrophy, moral deskilling, and societal fraying.

Introduction

Providing others with empathy is virtuous. It is also hard work. One good thing about artificial intelligence (AI) is that it reduces hard work, but it may also reduce the virtuous behavior of providing others with empathy. Like many other time-saving inventions (e.g., calculators, computers), large language models (LLMs) are agents of replacement (Gray et al., 2025). They can replace cognitive labor-analyzing complex equations and collating data-but they can also replace emotional labor. They can listen carefully to people's problems and then sympathize with them to foster emotional support (Ovsyannikova et al., 2025). Because LLMs can provide empathic behaviors, people may outsource those behaviors to AI. Once we know that someone is going to AI, we may "offload empathy," perhaps giving less effort, attention, or concern to others who need emotional support. Offloading empathy can change how we interact with each other, and may reduce our ethical commitments to fellow humans.

What do we owe each other? This is perhaps the most foundational question of ethics (Rawls, 2003; Scanlon, 2000). How much do we have a duty to care for others versus to pursue our own self-interest (Batson, 2014; Nagel, 2016; Singer, 2016)? Our genes are inherently selfish, and yet humans can perform incredible acts of compassion, like donating organs to strangers and sacrificing lives to protect comrades and countries. Most of us will never have the chance to act heroically, but we still express the ethical behaviors of care and compassion by providing empathy to each other (Davis, 1983; Tangney et al., 2007; Zagefka et al., 2011). Both philosophers and everyday people agree that providing emotional support to others is morally good (Algoe et al., 2008; Gable et al., 2006) and believe that those who consistently provide compassion to others deserve the label of "hero" (Walker & Frimer, 2007).

One reason human empathy is ethically celebrated (but see Bloom, 2017) is that it is personally costly. Providing empathy is effortful. It can be emotionally exhausting, uncomfortable, financially taxing, and can lead to burnout and compassion fatigue (Cameron et al., 2019; Hodges & Klein, 2001; Lockwood et al., 2017; Robertson et al., 2024). But the work required to provide empathy is a powerful reason why we praise those who emotionally support others (Inzlicht et al., 2018)-humans generally praise effort (Bigman & Tamir, 2016).

Providing empathy is often experienced as an ethical duty grounded in one's relationship with someone. People still feel some obligation to provide empathy to others, even after learning that they have already received some support (Aquino et al., 2011; Hong et al., 2022; Jordan et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2017). But even in the face of ethical duty, we feel entitled to some emotional self-preservation. Scholars argue that people should protect their "emotional boundaries" by not providing empathy (Bloom, 2017; Brown, 2021; Sheldon et al., 2010).

Whether this self-concern is selfish or ultimately ethical (you can't help others if you're too exhausted) is open to debate (Maslach & Jackson, 1981; Neff, 2003), but it is clear that psychologically, people trade off costs to the self against benefits to others (Cameron & Payne, 2011).

The tension between fulfilling ethical duties versus being emotionally self-protective (or lazy) means that every chance to provide empathy is a trade-off: expend effort and be a virtuous person, or conserve effort and be less virtuous. This choice is complicated by empathic AI, which can provide empathy to other people without them having to do it, helping save people emotional labor and time.

Humans are usually happy to offload labor. Research on cognitive offloading, social loafing, compassion fatigue, and the bystander effect (Fischer et al., 2011; Risko & Gilbert, If we feel comfortable shirking our ethical obligations to others, might that ultimately further fray the social fabric of society (Morelli et al., 2017)? When AI replaces humans as a source of empathy, will empathy itself be seen as less virtuous, and will that make humanity itself less ethical? When someone can go cry to AI, why bother getting splashed with their tears?

Four studies test whether people from the U.S. offload empathy to AI (specifically, ChatGPT). Study 1 explores the correlation between self-reported willingness to offload empathy and perceptions of AI as capable of empathy. Study 2 manipulates whether AI is capable of empathy and tests whether that increases self-reported empathy offloading. Study 3 tests whether learning that someone has already sought support from AI reduces quasi-behavioral empathic engagement. Study 4 extends this by comparing empathy offloading across AI, friends, and therapist conditions, exploring whether people's reported moral duty to empathize aligns with their actual empathic responses.

Study 1: Perceived Capacity of AI and Empathy Offloading

If people see AI as capable of providing empathy, would they feel less need to provide empathy themselves? In this study, people rated 1) how much they believe ChatGPT is capable of providing empathic support, and 2) their willingness to offload empathy to ChatGPT. We predicted a positive correlation between these ratings.

Method

Participants. We recruited 251 participants from CloudResearch Connect to participate in an online study. We excluded one participant for choosing not to have their data be a part of the analysis and one participant for being younger than 18, leaving a remainder of 249 participants (M age = 38.07; SD age = 12.22; 52.61% Men, 46.59% Women, .80% Other; 68.27% White).

Perceived ChatGPT Capacity for Empathy.

Participants first rated their agreement with three randomly ordered items measuring ChatGPT's capacity to provide empathy ("GPT is capable of providing empathy/being compassionate/understanding pain") from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (7). These items were adapted from Gray et al. (2007) and were averaged to make a composite score of capacity (α = .90).

Empathy Offloading.

Participants then rated their agreement with four randomly ordered items (α = .93) measuring willingness to offload empathy to ChatGPT ("I'd rather GPT provide emotional support to someone I know than have them rely on me; If someone seeks empathy from me, I'd prefer they turn to GPT instead; GPT can take over my role in offering emotional support to others; I would offload my empathic responsibilities to GPT") from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (7).

Trait Empathy.

Different people may have different baseline tendencies to provide empathy, so we measured individual differences in empathy as a covariate. Participants completed seven randomly ordered items (α = .88) from the Empathic Concern subscale (sample item: "I often have tender, concerned feelings for people less fortunate than me") of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI; Davis, 2011). Items were rated on a scale from Does not describe me well (A) to Describes me very well (E).

ChatGPT Use for Empathy.

Those who already use AI for empathy might have a different perspective, so we measured how often participants use LLMs like ChatGPT for emotional support from Not at all often (1) to Extremely often (5) as a covariate.

Results

Main Finding: Empathy Offloading.

As predicted, perceiving ChatGPT as capable of providing empathy was positively correlated with offloading empathy to ChatGPT, b = 0.44, SE = 0.05, t(247) = 9.64, p < .001, 95% CI [0.35, 0.53], R² = .27 (see Figure 1). This correlation remained significant even after controlling for trait empathy and experience using ChatGPT for empathy, b = 0.31, SE = 0.04, t(245) = 7.38, p < .001, 95% CI [0.23, 0.40], R² = .47. People saw ChatGPT as somewhat incapable of empathy, scoring below the scale midpoint of 4 ("neither agree nor disagree") on the 7-point scale but well above the floor of 1 (M = 3.24, SD = 1.64). to offload empathy.

Discussion

The results show that people who view ChatGPT as capable of providing empathy are more willing to offload empathy to it. Although perceptions of ChatGPT's empathic capacity were modest, many participants still viewed ChatGPT as a viable source of empathy, and these beliefs predicted offloading. We also found that using ChatGPT more for empathic support predicted offloading, and that trait empathy was negatively correlated with offloading. Of course, this study was only correlational, a limitation we now address.

Study 2: Manipulating AI's Empathic Capacity

Study 2 experimentally manipulated perceived empathic capacity of ChatGPT using a naturalistic manipulation-real newspaper articles describing the rise of AI being used for empathy-compared to control articles about AI generating images. We predicted that participants exposed to articles about empathic AI would view ChatGPT as more capable of providing empathy and, in turn, report greater willingness to offload empathic responsibilities to AI. We also tested whether perceived capacity of ChatGPT for empathy mediated the link between condition and willingness to offload.

Method

Participants. An a priori power analysis suggested we needed a minimum sample size of 175 per condition to achieve 80% power to detect a small effect size, specified as Cohen's d = .30. We aimed to get 250 participants per condition to account for possible exclusions. We recruited 502 participants from CloudResearch Connect to participate in a between-groups study.

We excluded six participants for choosing not to have their data be a part of the analysis, one participant for not consenting, and one participant for being an outlier, leaving a remainder of 494 participants (M age = 37.4; SD age = 12.5; 45.95% Men, 51.82% Women, 2.22% Other; 64.98% White).

Manipulation. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions manipulating perceived ChatGPT capacity for empathy. In the "AI Image Generation" condition (n = 246), participants viewed news headlines and snippets about AI generating images. In the "AI Empathy" condition (n = 248), participants viewed headlines and snippets about AI being used for empathy. All articles were published between January 2024 and April 2025 and were drawn from high-traffic U.S. news outlets (The New York Times, CBS News, The Wall Street Journal, MSN, and Forbes; Similarweb, 2025), presented in random order. Screenshots and article selection details are provided in the online supplemental materials (Figures S1-S10).

Measures. After viewing the five newspaper headlines, participants rated ChatGPT's capacity for empathy with the same measure as in Study 1 (α = .89), which served as a manipulation check here. They also rated their willingness to offload empathy with a single item from Study 1-selected because it was the most face-valid among items ("I would offload my empathic responsibilities to GPT"). Participants then completed the Empathic Concern subscale from the IRI (α = .87).

Main Finding: Empathy Offloading.

Confirming our manipulation, participants in the AI Empathy condition rated ChatGPT as more capable of empathy (M = 4.30, SD = 1.69) than in the AI Image Generation condition (M = 3.23, SD = 1.72), t(492) = -7.02, p < .001, Cohen's d = -.63.

As predicted, people in the AI Empathy condition reported more empathy offloading (M = 3.27, SD = 1.93) than those in the AI Image Generation condition (M = 2.43, SD = 1.74), t(487.28) = -5.02, p < .001, Cohen's d = -.45. Trait empathy did not differ between conditions, t(492) = -0.12, p = .90, Cohen's d = -.01. The results show that reading news articles about empathic AI increased empathy offloading, and this effect was explained by changes in perceived capacity. The next study extends empathy offloading to two key measurement domains: explicit ratings of moral obligation and more quasi-behavioral measures.

Study 3: Testing Offloading Empathy with Quasi-Behavioral Proxies

Study 3 was an experiment that compared empathy offloading after someone learned that a sad acquaintance had already received empathy from ChatGPT, a friend, or no one (the control condition). We predicted more empathy offloading for ChatGPT versus the control condition, but were unsure about the relative offloading between someone receiving support from ChatGPT or another person. On one hand, people are generally seen as more capable of providing empathy, and our first two studies show that perceived capacity can drive offloading. On the other hand, when other people provide empathy, it sets some social norms for empathy that others might follow (Algoe et al., 2008).

We also explored whether empathy offloading might extend beyond self-reports to explicit ratings of moral obligation and to more quasi-behavioral measures. Given that empathic behavior often involves asking questions (Nils & Rimé, 2012) and engaging with others' expressions and mental states (Bensalah et al., 2016; Decety & Jackson, 2004; Malle & Hodges, 2007; Preston & de Waal, 2002), we included two quasi-behavioral measures: a question-writing task and the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001). Finally, we measured ratings of emotional pain to test for an alternative hypothesis about empathy offloading: that people infer other people have less pain when they go to AI for empathy.

Method

Participants. An a priori power analysis suggested we needed a minimum sample size of 223 per condition to achieve 80% power to detect a small effect, specified as η² = .014 or Cohen's f = .12. We aimed to get 250 participants per condition to account for possible exclusions. We recruited 750 participants from CloudResearch Connect to participate in a between-groups study. We excluded seven participants for failing attention checks and/or choosing not to have their data be a part of the analysis, for a remainder of 743 participants (M age = 38.06, SD age = 11.57; 47.24% Men, 50.74% Women, 2.02% Other; 70.70% White).

Manipulation. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions manipulating prior emotional support. In the "No Support" condition (control; n = 248), participants read the following vignette: "Imagine someone you know, J.S., told you that they have been having a really tough time lately. It seemed like they want to talk to you." In the "ChatGPT Support" condition (n = 249), participants read the same vignette but were also told that J.S. "has been going to ChatGPT for emotional support over the past week." In the "Human Support" condition (n = 246), participants read the same vignette as the "No Support" condition but were also told that J.S. "has been going to another person for emotional support over the past week." Participants were aware that J.S. was a hypothetical person in all three conditions.

Measures

We used the same face-valid measures of perceived ChatGPT empathic capacity (α = .90), empathy offloading, and trait empathy (α = .87) as in Studies 1 and 2. We also included a more explicit measure of personal responsibility, and two quasi-behavioral proxies for empathy.

The first was asking questions to J.S., and the second was the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test to measure engagement with mental states. We also included perceptions of J.S.'s emotional pain.

Personal Responsibility.

Participants rated their agreement with three randomly ordered items (α = .83) measuring perceived personal responsibility to help J.S. out ("I feel obligated to provide emotional support to J.S.; I feel personally responsible to help J.S.; I feel compelled to talk to J.S.") from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (7).

Question Asking.

Participants asked up to five questions to J.S. about their sad situation.

We were especially interested in questions about J.S.'s "emotional situation," reflecting participants' interest in providing empathy. Three undergraduate research assistants independently coded these questions into two subcategories: questions about J.S.'s emotional situation in general (e.g., "What is bothering you?" ; α = .94) and, given J.S.'s situation, questions about how participants themselves could help out J.S. (e.g., "Do you want me to just sit and listen?" ; α = .89). Fewer questions indicated less emotional effort and more offloading.

Engagement with Mental States. Participants completed the Reading the Mind in the

Eyes Test (RMET), which asks people to guess the emotions of different people who are represented by only their eyes. Originally a task developed for autism research, the RMET reflects variation in people's capacity for emotional mental state reading, a skill related to empathy (Decety & Jackson, 2004; Yalçın & DiPaola, 2020). While the RMET is traditionally seen as a measure of emotion recognition ability (and we originally preregistered the RMET as a perspective-taking measure), performance on the task also requires attention to and engagement with others' mental states. We therefore included the RMET to assess whether situational factors, such as prior AI support, might influence how attentive people are to others' mental states. Prior work has shown that experimentally induced states can shift RMET performance (Harkness et al., 2005 (Harkness et al., , 2010;; Nolte et al., 2013), suggesting the task is sensitive to contextual influences and not solely a fixed trait measure. We reasoned that the RMET could also predict differences in engagement across conditions, especially because we selected the 12 most difficult stimuli from the original set of 36 eyes (defined as those with the lowest mean accuracy in emotion identification across both the general population and student samples from the original paper). The correct emotions for the 12 selected items were: uneasy, cautious, anticipating, doubtful, decisive, tentative, interested, cautious, interested, confident, serious, and concerned.

For each set of eyes, participants were told the following: "Imagine this is J.S., who told you they have been having a tough time lately." In the "ChatGPT Support" and "Human Support" conditions, participants were additionally reminded that J.S. had "been going to [GPT/another person] for emotional support over the past week." All participants were then asked to guess what emotion J.S. was feeling (see Figure 3). Fewer correct emotions guessed indicated less engagement and-we inferred-more empathy offloading.

Ratings of Emotional Pain.

Participants rated how much emotional pain J.S. was in using two randomly ordered items ("How much emotional pain is J.S. experiencing? ; How much is J.S. suffering?" ; α = .90) from No pain/suffering at all (1) to Extreme pain/suffering (5).

Results

Perceived Capacity, Empathy Offloading, and Trait Empathy.

Across conditions, participants did not differ in ChatGPT's perceived capacity for empathy, F(2, 740) = 2.01, p = .14, η² = .005, or trait empathy, F(2, 740) = 1.73, p = .18, η² = .005. But as predicted, participants significantly differed in empathy offloading across conditions, F(2, 740) = 3.25, p = .04, η² = .009. Post-hoc analyses using Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) revealed that offloading was highest in the ChatGPT Support condition (M = 2.79, SD = 1.77) compared to the No Support condition (M = 2.43, SD = 1.60), p = .04. The Human Support condition was between the other two conditions and was not significantly different from either, ps > .14. Full descriptive statistics of variables and correlations are in Tables S3-S4 in the online supplemental materials.

Mediation.

Given the results of the ANOVA above, we focused the mediation analyses on comparing empathy offloading between the ChatGPT Support condition and the No Support condition (see the online supplemental materials for mediations with the Human Support condition). Compared to the No Support condition, the ChatGPT Support condition significantly increased perceived ChatGPT capacity, b = 0.35, β = .10, SE = 0.16, z = 2.21, p = .03, 95% bootstrap CI [0.04, 0.65]. Perceived ChatGPT capacity, in turn, strongly predicted willingness to offload empathy, b = 0.58, β = .59, SE = 0.03, z = 16.94, p < .001, 95% bootstrap CI [0.51, 0.65].

As in Study 2, the indirect effect of ChatGPT support (vs. no support) on empathy offloading through perceived capacity was significant, b = 0.20, β = .06, SE = 0.09, z = 2.19, p = .03, 95% bootstrap CI [0.02, 0.38].

The model demonstrated acceptable fit based on commonly used criteria (Lai & Green, 2016; Shi et al., 2022): χ²(6) = 87.57, p < .001; CFI = .96; TLI = .90; SRMR = .04. Although the RMSEA was somewhat elevated (.14, 90% CI: [.11, .16]), the CFI and SRMR met conventional thresholds for good fit, suggesting the model was reasonably specified overall.

Personal Responsibility.

Interestingly, there were no significant differences in explicit ratings of personal obligation or responsibility to help J.S., F(2, 740) = 1.32, p = .27, η² = .004.

Discussion

Consistent with predictions, participants showed more empathy offloading after learning that an acquaintance went to AI for support versus not receiving any previous empathic support.

These results were found with self-reports of empathy offloading, with question asking, and with the RMET. Notably, the RMET emotions span a range of affective states from positive to neutral-cognitive to negative. Because affective states were not uniformly negative, we reduced the likelihood that the "tough time" framing inflated accuracy through base-rate guessing of negative emotions. All three conditions received the same "tough time" framing, so any inflation in accuracy would apply equally across conditions and cannot account for the significant differences we observed.

Evidence for differences between AI and human support was limited. While some measures showed small differences favoring greater offloading in the AI condition, others showed no reliable differences. Interestingly, participants' explicit ratings of personal responsibility (or perceived emotional pain) were not significantly different across conditions, suggesting that people feel the same ethical responsibility to help someone regardless of any previous support they received. This mismatch between self-reports and quasi-behavioral outcomes suggests that offloading may happen more implicitly: even if people feel ethically compelled to help someone after they go to empathic AI, their actions showcase less empathy.

Study 4: Differences in Empathy Offloading Among Friends, Therapists, and AI

The previous studies found evidence for empathy offloading, but still, open questions remain. First, would we find the same effects for someone less hypothetical? In Study 3, people were asked to imagine someone who needed empathy, but in this study, participants were given the initials of a real student at the Ohio State University who was experiencing emotional distress (although we did include a fictional manipulation about who/what they received empathy from).

Second, how does empathy offloading for AI compare to other sources of empathy? To test this, this study included four conditions: an AI empathy condition, a control/no support condition, and conditions where the student received empathy from either friends or a therapist.

We again assessed self-reports of empathy offloading and responsibility. This study also included a new quasi-behavioral measure of empathy offloading: language selection. Participants were presented with a cloud of words-some empathic (e.g., understanding), some un-empathic (e.g., indifferent)-that they clicked to represent how they would act or feel toward K.D. We predicted that people would select the fewest empathic words (and the most un-empathic words) in the AI Empathy condition.

We also broadened our focus on empathic AI, discussing AI chat platforms in general instead of ChatGPT specifically, to provide more generalizability.

Method

Participants. An a priori power analysis suggested we needed a minimum sample size of 134 per condition to achieve 80% power to detect a small effect, specified as Cohen's f = .14.

We aimed to get at least 150 analyzable participants per condition after exclusions. We recruited 800 participants from CloudResearch Connect to participate in a between-groups study. Due to general increases in bot farming, we imposed stricter exclusion criteria. We excluded 59 participants for failing attention checks (i.e., choosing inaccurate scale items to the statement "Choose Somewhat disagree for this statement," choosing an irrelevant answer to the question "In the beginning of the survey, you were asked to read a prompt about K.D. What did we mention about K.D.?" and/or failing the Müller-Lyer illusion bot-detection checks). We excluded seven other participants for choosing not to have their data be a part of the analyses and/or failing data quality checks (i.e., completion times that were less than one-third of the median or values exceeding 3.29 standard deviations from the mean). We had a remainder of 737 participants (M age = 40.28, SD age = 13.97; 42.88% Men, 54.14% Women, 2.98% Other; 68.25% White).

Discussion

Discussion and what they actually do.

Study 4 again revealed a dissociation between what people say about providing empathy

People said they were less willing to offload in the AI Empathy condition compared to the Friends and Therapist Empathy conditions. In the language selection task, however, participants in the AI Empathy condition selected fewer empathy words and more indifference words than all other conditions. Consistent with all our previous studies, AI's perceived capacity for empathy also strongly predicted willingness to offload to it. Even though average perceptions of AI's capacity were modest, variation in perceived capacity reliably tracked offloading.

Interestingly, participants selected the most empathy words (and the fewest indifference words) when the student went to friends for support, consistent with the power of social norms to personally provide empathy even if others already have (Algoe et al., 2008; House, 2018).

Furthermore, moral duty to provide empathy was highest when the student received support from friends, compared to AI, therapists, and no support, which did not differ from each other. This pattern further highlights that friends set a social norm that heightens perceived relational obligation to provide support.

Another important pattern is the difference between the AI and Therapist conditions, where people may be reducing their empathy giving for different reasons. Participants reported greater willingness to offload to therapists and perceived them as more capable, yet showed the most indifference when K.D. went to AI. Offloading to therapists may reflect a rational reduction in empathy to a trusted, socially accepted source. But the increased selection of indifference words after K.D. went to AI may not only reflect offloading to save empathic effort. People may also be signaling social judgment, disapproval, or psychological distancing toward the person who sought support from AI (Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Williams, 2007), consistent with participants also rating K.D. as more weird, antisocial, and desperate than in the Friends and Therapist Empathy conditions (see the online supplemental materials). Offloading and social distancing may therefore be operating simultaneously, meaning that the person who turns to AI for support may end up doubly worse off, receiving even less empathy for how they chose to get support.

General Discussion

Philosophers mostly agree that it is ethically good to provide empathy. It also seems clear that it is a (moral) good for people to receive empathy if they need it. In other words, when it comes to the question of what we owe each other (Rawls, 2003; Scanlon, 2000; Singer, 2016), we do seem to owe each other some kind of emotional support. However, this sense of empathic obligation seems to reduce when we learn that someone has gone to AI for empathic support.

To be sure, there is good in empathic AI, because it allows people to feel supported at all hours of the day with little cost, but our research reveals support for the idea of empathy offloading. Four studies show that, when AI is seen as capable of providing empathy, it reduces people's willingness to emotionally support people who could go-or who have gone-to AI for empathy.

Study 1 found that people who see AI as more capable of providing empathy are also more willing to offload interpersonal empathy to it. Study 2 manipulated the perceived capacity of AI and found the predicted effect: when AI was described as more capable of empathy, people offloaded interpersonal empathic support to it. Studies 3 and 4 provided evidence beyond simple self-report that learning someone sought AI support reduces empathy given to them, including asking fewer questions and engaging less with others' mental states (Study 3), and selecting fewer empathy words and more indifference words in a language selection task (Study 4).

Interestingly, empathy offloading occurred while people explicitly reported having the moral responsibility to provide interpersonal empathy, highlighting a disconnect between what people say and do. This suggests that empathy offloading may occur more implicitly and intuitively than deliberatively, consistent with models of moral judgment as an intuitive process (Greene, 2014).

Implications AI and Empathy.

Scholars disagree about whether AI can authentically provide empathy (Kleinert et al., 2026; Perry, 2023; Rubin et al., 2025; Yin et al., 2024), and science has largely focused on how recipients react to AI empathy: do people trust and benefit from it, does the "AI" label matter to people, and how does AI empathy compare to receiving real human empathy (Inzlicht et al., 2024; Laban & Cross, 2024; Ovsyannikova et al., 2025; Pelau et al., 2021; Wenger et al., 2026; Yam et al., 2023).

Far less attention has been paid to the downstream consequences of empathic AI-how the rise of AI impacts human connection. Some work examines how people can feel less empathy when writing AI-assisted empathic messages (Lee et al., 2025), and other work shows that AI can corrupt ethical behavior, such as by increasing dishonesty (Köbis et al., 2021 (Köbis et al., , 2025)).

Our work suggests that AI can shift ethical behavior between people. Even if empathic AI is good for recipients in the moment, it may quietly weaken our empathic behaviors without us recognizing it.

Ethical Change.

Our findings suggest at least two ways empathic AI can reduce human empathy, each with distinct ethical implications: offloading and social judgment.

Studies 1-3 reveal that when people believe that AI can provide empathy, they may put less effort into providing empathy themselves. Over time, this behavior may lead people to devalue empathy as a morally important emotion. People intuitively associate value with effort and intention (Inzlicht et al., 2018), and we often devalue the tasks done by AI (Böhm et al., 2023; Lubars & Tan, 2019; Zhang & Gosline, 2023). So, when AI provides empathy automatically and effortlessly, people may see offering empathy as cheap, trivial, and ultimately, a less ethically meaningful resource.

The devaluation of empathy may eventually lead to moral deskilling-the gradual loss of human ability to exercise moral judgment and cultivate moral skills, especially empathy. This stems from increased reliance on technology to perform labor on humans' behalf, paralleling how automation devalues skilled labor in the workforce (Vallor, 2015). If people increasingly offload their empathy-giving behaviors to AI, they may no longer appreciate all the hard work and kindness that goes into giving and receiving empathy.

Alternatively, when people see others going to AI for empathy, they may also express disapproval for this choice and withdraw empathy as a form of social punishment. Study 4 revealed that participants in the AI Empathy condition actively selected more indifference words and rated K.D. as more weird, antisocial, and desperate (see the online supplemental materials).

Rather than saving the laborious effort of giving empathy, people may be penalizing the struggling person because they view AI as an unsuitable source of support, and-consequentlywithdrawing their support. If AI-seeking remains stigmatized, recipients may hide their emotional struggles out of fear of social disapproval, leading to more general loneliness, less help-seeking, and perhaps other social ills.

Societal Fraying. Society is not a single "thing" but instead a web of connections between people. These connections are built by social interactions and mutual exchanges of kindness and support (Morelli et al., 2017). Yet offloading empathy may hinder one of the main ways we maintain social connections: noticing distress and emotionally responding to show that we matter to each other.

If social connection is severed by empathic AI, we may stop feeling like we owe each other love, care, and support. We may deprive ourselves of emotional intimacy, shared understanding, and feelings of vulnerability that make us human (George et al., 2023; Krapp et al., 2024; Laban & Cross, 2024). We may also grow distant and isolated from each other, feeding into the loneliness epidemic (Office of the Surgeon General, 2023; Twenge et al., 2021). As empathy offloading becomes normalized, social norms around relational responsibility may weaken, lowering the ethical commitments we make to each other.

Empathy Atrophying.

Aristotle argued that moral virtue is developed through repeated practice (Aristotle, 1999, Book II). Empathy may not be a muscle in the traditional sense, but it certainly strengthens with repeated practice and atrophies with decreased use (Shastry et al., 2025). If AI replaces empathy, people in all kinds of relationships may lose the ability to practice genuine human empathy.

Likewise, the rise of empathic AI may change how children learn to live. Some are already worrying that by offloading emotional labor to AI, like writing break-up notes, kids may skip out on the difficult but essential parts of social development (Shirky, 2026). When the default response to any distress is "go cry to AI," generations may not learn how to connect with others. Friendships and romantic relationships may also wane if people struggle to open up emotionally and instead run to AI (Ho et al., 2025). Without practicing empathy, we may end up losing our virtues of compassion and care.

Study Limitations

It is important to note that our conclusions come with a number of caveats.

Overall Low Perceived Empathic Capacity of AI. Empathic AI is in its infancy, and accordingly, people gave relatively low ratings for AI's capacity to provide empathy (see Figure S11 in the online supplemental materials for histograms of AI's capacity for empathy across conditions). These low AI empathy ratings likely explained the relatively low explicit endorsement of empathy offloading after learning that someone went to AI for emotional support, versus a friend or therapist. Of course, all four studies revealed support for the crucial thesis-that the more people saw AI as capable of empathy, the more they offloaded empathybut we acknowledge these low ratings. Importantly, this paper attempts to get ahead of the curve of social change by examining the social and ethical phenomenon of offloading empathy to AI before its widespread use.

Consistent with lower perceptions of AI's capacity for empathy, the average amount of empathy offloading was relatively low across Studies 1-3 (M = 2.10-3.27 on a 7-point scale), which could raise concerns about floor effects and violations of normality assumptions underlying parametric tests. To assess this, we examined skewness and kurtosis values.

Following Curran et al. (1996), skewness values below 2 and kurtosis values below 7 are considered acceptable for parametric testing. Our values fell within these ranges across all studies and all primary measures. To further confirm robustness for the word cloud task in Study 4, we ran Kruskal-Wallis tests on the equal-weights indices, and all primary findings held. The low means therefore reflect genuine variation around a low baseline, consistent with the fact that AI empathy is still an emerging phenomenon and people's baseline willingness to offload remains modest.

Interpreting Expressed Indifference.

As discussed in Study 4, the number of indifference word selections in the AI Empathy condition may reflect negative social judgment toward AI-seekers. However, exploratory analyses of our data suggest the existence of more authentic empathy offloading. Regression analyses find that offloading was predicted by both perceiving AI as setting a higher empathic standard and by people wanting to put in less effort (see Table S6), consistent with cognitive offloading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016) and growing evidence that AI can match or exceed human empathic responses (Ovsyannikova et al., 2025; Wenger et al., 2026). Thus, offloading and disapproval may not be mutually exclusive and may even operate simultaneously, but they call for meaningfully different interventions: one focused on restoring felt responsibility, the other on reducing stigma toward AI-seekers.

We also acknowledge a broader limitation of the Language Selection Task: visual search does not directly operationalize interpersonal emotional labor, and the task reflects symbolic effort with minimal real-world consequences. We also note that empathic expression was the predominant selection of words across all conditions, consistent with the idea of a general ethical obligation to endorse empathy. As our methods stated, we examined the relative difference between indices across conditions rather than absolute levels. The between-condition differences should be interpreted as shifts in how much empathy people expressed rather than a wholesale abandonment of empathic engagement. Future work should use measures that more directly capture empathic labor to establish stronger construct validity and to cleanly isolate mechanisms.

Study Measures.

Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test (RMET).

The RMET, a task where participants identify emotions of people shown only by their eyes, is typically treated as a measure of stable individual differences in emotion recognition ability rather than momentary engagement. While we still observed significant differences in engagement with others' mental states and were able to shift empathic engagement toward an acquaintance who has gone to AI for empathy, the RMET is still an imperfect proxy for the active, interpersonal engagement involved in real-world empathy. Future work should build on these findings using more targeted measures of empathic engagement and perspective-taking.

Study 4 Manipulation Imbalance.

In Study 4, where we described a student receiving empathy from AI, we note that this was the only condition where we included an institutional endorsement-a reference to Harvard research identifying therapy and companionship as the top use of generative AI. This institutional endorsement may have independently and asymmetrically influenced AI's perceived capacity for empathy.

We intentionally designed the vignette in this way to ensure our manipulation was strong enough to test whether capacity leads to offloading, because people's baseline perceptions of AI's empathic capacity were low (Study 1 mean = 3.24/7; Yin et al., 2024). We prioritized experimental validity (creating conditions under which offloading could plausibly occur) over perfect symmetry across conditions. Of course, this tradeoff is a limitation that means that we cannot fully rule out whether the institutional framing contributed to the effects we observed, potentially creating some demand characteristics. Future work should continue to test offloading effects among participants who already believe that AI can provide empathy with more symmetrically framed vignettes to isolate the effect of support source from the effect of institutional framing.

A Scale Change.

Participants rated perceived capacity for empathy and empathy offloading from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (7) in Studies 1-3, and from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (6) in Study 4, which may impact interpretability of mean values.

We removed the "Neither agree nor disagree" (4) scale point in Study 4 to encourage participants to choose one side of agreement, since Study 2 had a mean of 4.30 for AI's perceived capacity when participants read newspaper headlines about AI and empathy.

Contextualizing Effect Sizes.

Behavioral science has long debated which effect sizes are meaningful. Many well-replicated findings in social psychology have small to medium effect sizes (Richard et al., 2003), and some of our effects fall within this range (e.g., d = .45-.63 for Study 2, η² = .005-.009 in Study 3 for perceived capacity and empathy offloading), with not all comparisons reaching significance in Studies 3 and 4. While a small effect might suggest that any single encounter with someone who sought AI empathy likely only has a modest impact on the empathy they receive, small effects are not necessarily unimportant. If people start to learn about struggling others using AI for empathy more often, over time many small independent empathy reductions can sum to a meaningful amount of empathy withheld (Funder & Ozer, 2019). Our studies estimate the per-encounter effect, but they do not test how often this situation actually occurs in everyday life, though recent work suggests it is becoming more common (Huang et al., 2026; Zao-Sanders, 2025). Future work should test whether such effects accumulate over repeated exposures, both within-person and across populations.

Future Directions

The current research poses many questions, understandably given this paper is among the first to examine the interpersonal consequences of empathic AI.

One obvious future direction is to use more behavioral measures, which is always important for establishing the real-world importance-and robustness-of psychological findings. Our studies used both self-report ratings and quasi-behavioral measures, but we acknowledge that we did not explore real-time embodied social interactions. This is only an initial exploration of empathy offloading, and so we encourage other work that explores these effects in more real-world contexts.

Real-world empathy also unfolds between specific people with specific relationships, and likely hinges on who is giving support, who is seeking support, and what they are seeking it for.

Differences in Empathy Givers.

Not all observers will respond to someone seeking AI emotional support in the same way. Observers who are averse to AI or who have less in common with AI-seekers are likely to judge them more harshly, while those who personally value AI support may not show disapproval (Meyers et al., 2026). Our own exploratory data suggest that people who found AI empathy helpful expressed greater willingness to offload, while those who found friends or therapists more helpful showed greater indifference (see Table S5).

Younger people may also be more receptive to people seeking AI empathy. Young people appear more open about discussing their mental health struggles and broadcasting how they seek support (Rüsch & Kösters, 2021), and they may also be less concerned (and judgmental) about empathic AI use.

Differences in Empathy Seekers.

Who is seeking support also shapes how much empathy observers can give or offload to AI. Someone may not be worried about their acquaintance going to AI for empathy if they are technologically competent, while the same behavior in someone who is seen as less tech-savvy may elicit stronger disapproval (Meyers et al., 2026).

The closeness of the relationship between empathy givers and seekers also likely plays a pivotal role. Offloading empathy when a close friend is struggling may feel more like a betrayal of relational duty (Finkel et al., 2002) than the same behavior toward a casual acquaintance, where obligations to give empathy are weaker.

Additionally, people may choose to offload empathy (or communicate disapproval), depending on the reason why someone is seeking AI empathy. People turn to AI for concerns ranging from everyday anxiety to serious mental health struggles and abusive situations (Huang et al., 2026), and empathy givers may be less likely to offload if someone really needs help, such as in a serious crisis. But for less serious situations, like everyday stress or minor frustrations, people may be more willing to offload.

Differences Across Cultures.

We recruited U.S. participants, but different cultural contexts likely shape differences in empathy offloading and social judgment behaviors. Western countries tend to be more skeptical of AI while Eastern cultures tend to be more trusting and optimistic about AI (Gillespie et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), suggesting that perceived capacity for AI empathy-and consequently, empathy offloading-may be higher in Eastern cultures. At the same time, people in more collectivist cultures display higher empathic concern and stronger felt obligations toward others (Chopik et al., 2017; Markus & Kitayama, 1991), which could prevent offloading even when AI is trusted. Whether greater trust in AI or stronger felt obligation to provide human empathy dominates-and whether norms around support amplify or decrease social judgment toward AI-seekers-remains an open question. Future work should test empathy offloading effects beyond Western populations to understand when empathic AI leads to offloading behaviors.

Awareness in AI Empathy Use.

Before deciding to offload emotional support to AI, someone must first become aware that someone they know has sought AI empathy. While people often keep their empathy-seeking private (Gross & John, 2003; Vogel & Wade, 2009), some are publicly disclosing their use of empathic AI in online communities like Reddit (Huang et al., 2026), and perhaps they will soon disclose to friends and family. Our two study designs capture two potential routes through which such awareness likely spreads: Study 2 tests how people learn about empathic AI from the media, and Study 3 tests observers learning about another's AIseeking in a personal context. As AI emotional support becomes more visible and discussed, the situations we study here will only become more common, making the ethical questions they raise more urgent to explore.

Conclusion

AI is changing how we live with one another. LLMs already draft our emails and edit our prose. But, with the rise of empathic AI, machines replace not just human cognition, but human empathy. For most of human history, providing emotional support was an ethical behavior that bound people together. But now, we can offload this crucial interpersonal behavior to AI-and our data suggest that people are willing to let AI shoulder some of the burden of empathic support. As empathic AI advances, the answer to the question "What do we owe each other?" might be "less than before."
