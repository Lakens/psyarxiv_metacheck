What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Several theories aim to explain how people categorize entities as members of natural kinds, but no study has systematically tested their competing predictions. We present four experiments that pit purely feature-based, psychological essentialist, teleological essentialist, causal-model, and dual-character accounts of natural-kind categorization against one another using a common set of vignettes. Across these experiments, we independently manipulated internal composition, appearance, telos, and the perceived causal relationships among them.

We found that, while people tended not to treat any single feature as sufficient for category membership, internal composition was the strongest contributor to categorization judgments.

In addition, categorization judgments depended on whether an entity's composition stood in the appropriate causal relationships to its other features, rather than merely on whether those features were present. We also found little evidence for the dual-character view that naturalkind concepts involve distinct superficial and deeper criteria for category membership.

Together, these findings favor a composition-centered causal-relational account of naturalkind categorization over its leading alternatives.

1. Introduction

In recent decades, researchers have proposed competing theories of the knowledge structures underlying our natural kind concepts, such as WATER and TIGER. These include prototype theories (Del Pinal, 2016; Hampton, 2006; Haukioja et al., 2021; Rosch, 1978), psychological essentialism (Gelman, 2003 (Gelman, , 2004;; Keil, 1989; Leslie, 2013; Neufeld, 2022), teleological essentialism (Rose et al., 2022; Rose & Nichols, 2019 , 2020; Toorman, 2023), causal-model theories (Rehder, 2003a (Rehder, , 2003b (Rehder, , 2010 (Rehder, , 2017)), and dual-character theories (Del Pinal & Reuter, 2017; Reuter, 2019; Tobia et al., 2020). While a great many studies lend support to these different theories, no study has systematically examined their competing predictions within a common experimental framework. In this paper, we present a series of experiments that do exactly that.

1.1 Theoretical background

To map out the different approaches to natural-kind categorization and to distinguish their predictions, we begin by organizing them into two families: purely feature-based and causal-relational accounts. We then consider dual-character accounts separately.

The core difference between purely feature-based and causal-relational accounts concerns what determines categorization judgments. According to the former, people's judgments about whether an entity belongs to a particular natural kind only depend on which features they represent the entity as possessing, along with the relative importance they assign to each feature. By contrast, causal-relational accounts hold that such judgments also depend on the (perceived) causal relationships among the entity's features. Importantly, this distinction specifically concerns the factors that influence categorization judgments, not how people represent natural kinds more broadly. Someone might, for example, represent the characteristic features of a natural kind as causally related without those perceived relationships independently influencing their judgments about category membership.

To illustrate, on a purely feature-based account, people might categorize a creature as a tiger depending on whether it possesses features such as tiger DNA, stripes, claws, and a tendency to hunt prey, weighted according to their relative importance. What determines categorization is simply which features people represent the creature as possessing and how much weight they assign to each. Category membership does not additionally require that these features be produced in any particular way or stand in any causal relationships to one another.

A purely feature-based account could hold that people assign roughly equal weight to composition, telos, and appearance. Another possibility is a composition-privileging account, according to which an entity's internal composition carries greater weight in categorization judgments than its appearance or telos. Moreover, composition-privileging accounts may differ in how decisive people take composition to be for category membership, including whether people treat it as necessary or sufficient. For example, an account that treats composition as sufficient predicts that people will categorize a substance as water as long as it consists of H2O, regardless of whether it looks and tastes like typical water. Conversely, an account that treats composition as necessary predicts that people will not categorize a substance as water if it lacks H2O, even if it looks and tastes like typical water. 1Another possibility is a teleology-privileging account, according to which people assign greater weight to an entity's telos than to its composition or appearance. Again, proponents may differ on whether people treat the telos as necessary or sufficient for category membership. Rose and Nichols (2019, 2020), for example, present evidence that people represent natural kinds as having teleological essences-a view they call "teleological essentialism". Importantly, though, they do not take a stance on whether categorization depends solely on which features people represent a given entity as possessing or also on perceived causal relationships among them. Their view is therefore compatible with both purely feature-based and causal-relational approaches.

Purely feature-based accounts are compatible with different theories of how natural kinds are represented. Most obviously, they are compatible with prototype theories, according to which people represent categories, including natural kinds, via lists of weighted features that are common and distinctive to the given category (Gärdenfors, 2024; Hampton, 1979; Haukioja et al., 2021; Murphy, 2004; Rosch, 1978). However, in principle, purely featurebased accounts are also compatible with richer conceptual representations, including theories that incorporate causal information about natural kinds (see Machery, 2009). As explained earlier, what matters is whether categorization judgments depend solely on which features people perceive an entity as possessing and how much weight they assign to each, rather than on the causal relationships they perceive as holding among those features.

In contrast to purely feature-based accounts, causal-relational accounts hold that beliefs about causal relationships among features independently influence categorization judgments. One influential version of the causal-relational approach is causal-model theory (CMT). According to CMT, people make categorization decisions by estimating the likelihood that its features were generated in accordance with the causal graphical model associated with that category (Neufeld, 2022 (Neufeld, , 2024;; Rehder, 2003a Rehder, , 2003b Rehder, , 2010 Rehder, , 2017)).

CMT therefore predicts coherence effects: all else equal, a to-be-classified entity is judged as a more likely category member when two causally related features are either both present or both absent-that is, when they cohere (Marchant & Chaigneau, 2020; Murphy, 2004; Puebla & Chaigneau, 2014; Rehder, 2017; Rehder & Hastie, 2004; Rehder & Kim, 2010). In other words, on CMT, perceived features can make an interactive (in addition to an independent) contribution to categorization judgments, such that the contribution of one feature depends on whether another causally related feature is present.

Coherence effects are not the only way in which causal relationships among features might influence categorization judgments. One such alternative arises when causal relations allow the state of one feature to be inferred from another. Categorization decisions are often made in the presence of incomplete information, as when labeling a skunk as a "skunk" occurs even in the absence of information about the animal's DNA. Yet, causal-relational accounts allow for the possibility that perceptual features (smelliness, a white stripe) allow one to infer unobserved features (skunk DNA) on the basis of the causal relations that connect them, in the same way that a doctor might reason diagnostically from a disease's symptoms to its underlying cause (Neufeld, 2021; Rehder & Kim, 2009). Relatedly, an animal that is made to have the perceptual features of an alternative kind (e.g., a raccoon that is altered to be smelly and have a white stripe) is understood to not support an inference to skunk DNA, exactly because the explicit intervention on the raccoon severs the usual percept-to-composition inferential link (Gelman, 2003; Gelman & Wellman, 1991; Hampton et al., 2007; Johnson & Solomon, 1997; Keil, 1989; Opfer & Bulloch, 2007; Oppenheimer et al., 2013; Rehder & Kim, 2009; Rips, 1989). Evidence that categorizers draw these kinds of causal inferences when making categorization judgments favors a causal-relational account.

"Psychological essentialism" is often used to refer to some version of the causalrelational approach. At a minimum, proponents of psychological essentialism hold that people regard the members of natural kinds and certain other categories as possessing underlying "essences" that causally generate their characteristic features (Gelman, 2004; Medin & Ortony, 1989; Neufeld, 2022). However, while some proponents explicitly hold that representations of causal structure influence categorization judgments (e.g., Gelman, 2004; Rehder, 2007), others do not clearly specify whether perceived causal relationships exert an independent influence on such judgments (e.g., Prentice & Miller, 2007; Ahn et al., 2013; see also Newman & Knobe, 2019). Thus, some formulations of psychological essentialism may be compatible with purely feature-based accounts of categorization.

Just as purely feature-based accounts can differ in the relative importance they assign to different features, causal-relational accounts can differ in the importance they assign to both individual features and the causal relationships among them. In particular, they may differ in their predictions about which features people regard as playing a privileged causal role in generating other features, and how strongly these relationships influence categorization judgments. And as with purely feature-based accounts, causal-relational accounts may also differ in whether people treat certain features, or certain causal relationships among them, as necessary or sufficient for category membership. 2 What we term "composition-centered" causal-relational accounts hold that people's categorization judgments are particularly sensitive to whether they perceive the given entity's internal composition as causing its other characteristic features, such as appearance and telos. Psychological essentialism is an example of a composition-centered causal relational account.

Such accounts may treat internal composition as necessary or sufficient for category membership while still allowing perceived causal relationships to influence categorization judgments.

Alternatively, categorization judgments might be particularly sensitive to other causal relationships, including those involving an entity's telos or appearance. Such accounts might 2 Versions of the view that people regard a causally potent essence as highly important, but not sufficient for category membership, have been implicitly or explicitly endorsed in the literature on psychological essentialism (for discussion, see, e.g., Ahn et al., 2001 Ahn et al., , 2013;; Cain, 2025; Gelman, 2003; Machery, 2009; Medin & Ortony, 1989; Neufeld, 2022; Newman & Knobe, 2019; Rips, 2011; Sloman, 2005; Strevens, 2000).

Finally, the dual-character approach (Knobe et al., 2013; Newman & Knobe, 2019; Reuter, 2019; Tobia et al., 2020) addresses a different question: whether natural kind concepts involve a single criterion for category membership or two distinct criteria.

According to its proponents, natural kind concepts have two separable dimensions: a descriptive dimension, tied to a kind's observable features, and an essentialist dimension, tied to its underlying essence. These function as independent criteria for membership, so an entity can count as a member along one dimension but a non-member along the other. To illustrate, a creature could be a tiger in the descriptive sense-displaying stripes, claws, and predatory behavior-while not being a tiger in the deeper, essentialist sense, because it lacks, say, tiger DNA or tiger tele (see also Nichols et al., 2016). Conversely, something could be a tiger in the deeper, essentialist sense, but not in the descriptive sense. The dual character view thus predicts that people will often regard these creatures as tigers in one sense, but not in another. Tobia et al. (2020) further suggest that the two dimensions might involve distinct categorization processes, with the descriptive dimension relying on a prototype of superficial features and the essentialist dimension relying on a theory of deeper causal properties.

Key studies have lent support to each approach. However, no study has systematically pitted all of them against one another. What's more, several of these approaches face unresolved questions that the existing evidence cannot resolve. This is, at least in part, because the evidence comes from studies designed around a single theory, rather than studies that independently manipulate all the relevant aspects (internal composition, telos, appearance, causal structure) within a common experimental framework. Take, for example, the view that people regard an entity's internal composition as sufficient for category membership, rather than merely as one particularly important feature among several. Malt (1994) found that participants' estimates of the proportion of H2O in various liquids did not consistently track whether those liquids were categorized as water: tea was judged to contain 91% H2O, whereas pool water was judged to contain only 81%. Such findings raise questions about whether people treat a particular chemical composition as sufficient for membership in a chemical kind like water. One possibility is that other features, such as appearance or telos, also influence judgments about what qualifies as water.

However, without independently manipulating these features alongside internal composition, it is difficult to determine their relative contributions, including whether any particular feature, or combination of features, is treated as necessary or sufficient for membership. 3Similar difficulties arise when assessing evidence for the role of tele in categorization. Rose and Nichols (2019, 2020) found that changes to an entity's telos can substantially influence categorization judgments, which they interpret as evidence for teleological essentialism. However, as Neufeld (2021) argues, these findings are also compatible with the view that people use an entity's telos as diagnostic evidence of its underlying composition.

Thus, the influence of telos on categorization judgments does not establish that telos itself is privileged over internal composition. Distinguishing these possibilities requires independently manipulating telos, appearance, and internal composition and comparing their relative influence on categorization judgements.

Dual-character accounts of natural-kind categorization have also been subject to important criticism in the recent literature. Tobia et al. (2020) found that participants sometimes agreed both that an entity belonged to a natural kind in one sense and that, in a deeper sense, it did not really belong to that kind. They interpret this pattern as evidence that people employ distinct superficial and deeper criteria for category membership. However, Haukioja et al. (2023) failed to replicate this pattern across several variations in question wording. A further limitation is that Tobia et al. manipulated the given entity's "superficial" properties while holding its internal composition constant, rather than independently manipulating appearance, telos, and composition. A more systematic investigation would allow us to examine whether dual-character judgments arise across different combinations of these features, and thereby better assess the proposed distinction between superficial and deeper criteria for category membership.

Finally, another unresolved question concerns the role of perceived causal structure.

Although previous research has shown that people's categorization judgments are sensitive to perceived causal relationships among features (Rehder, 2017; Rehder & Hastie, 2004; Rehder & Kim, 2006), much of this evidence comes from studies in which participants were instructed about the relevant causal relationships. It also remains unclear how the relationships influence natural-kind categorization when internal composition, appearance, and telos are independently controlled. For example, the apparent importance of internal composition could reflect the weight people assign to composition itself, its perceived causal role in generating other characteristic features, or both. Independently manipulating causal relationships would allow us to distinguish these possibilities and investigate whether people privilege particular relationships, such as those in which composition generates appearance or telos, or assign comparable importance to different relationships.

To sum up, existing paradigms often manipulate one potential dimension of category membership without adequately controlling for others, making it difficult to test the competing predictions of different accounts. What is needed is an experimental framework that independently manipulates composition, appearance, telos, and the causal relationships among them, allowing these predictions to be evaluated side by side.

1.2 The present studies

Across four experiments, we examined competing theories of natural-kind categorization by systematically manipulating internal composition, appearance, telos, and causal structure.

Experiment 1 tested what we call the sufficiency thesis: the claim that people treat a single feature of a natural kind-whether internal composition, appearance, or telos-as sufficient for category membership. Versions of this thesis are associated with compositionprivileging and teleology-privileging accounts, as well as the dual-character approach.

Participants evaluated targets that matched an Earthly kind on exactly one of the three dimensions (i.e., composition, appearance, or telos), while differing on the other two.

Experiment 2 extended this idea by independently manipulating composition, appearance, and telos in a full-factorial design. This allowed us to assess the relative contribution of each factor to category judgments and to examine whether certain combinations of features influence categorization over and above their individual effects.

Experiment 3 provided a within-subject replication of Experiment 2. Participants evaluated multiple combinations of composition, appearance, and telos, allowing us to test whether direct comparison across cases would elicit the coherence effects predicted by causal-model theory-i.e., whether combinations of features influence category judgments beyond their individual contributions.

Experiment 4 directly manipulated whether an entity's composition was causally responsible for its appearance and telos, while holding all three features constant. 2. Experiment 1

The aim of Experiment 1 was to test what we call the sufficiency thesis: the claim that people treat some single feature of a natural kind-composition, telos, or appearance-as sufficient for category membership. Different versions of this thesis are associated with composition-privileging accounts, teleology-privileging accounts, and the dual-character approach.

To test the sufficiency thesis, we presented participants with vignettes in which explorers encounter an alien creature or substance that matches an Earthly kind on exactly one aspect-composition, telos, or appearance-while differing on the other two. Participants rated their agreement with two statements: one asserting that there is a sense in which the target is a member of the Earthly kind; the other asserting that there is a sense in which it is not. If a particular aspect is deemed sufficient for membership, preserving that aspect alone should yield agreement with the Member statement. For instance, the dual-character approach predicts that participants may simultaneously endorse both the Member and Non-Member statements when a target satisfies one criterion for membership but not another.

2.1 Methods

We recruited 950 participants through the Prolific platform (Mage = 31.31, SD = 8.66; 49.4% women, 48.6% men, non-binary 2%). We excluded 91 participants for failing comprehension or attention checks, resulting in a final sample of 859 participants.

No a priori power analysis was conducted; instead, we targeted approximately 50 participants per cell. The experiment employed a 6 × 3 × 2 mixed design. The betweensubjects factors were KIND (tigers, vultures, spiders, water, coal, iron) and ASPECT PRESERVED (appearance, composition, telos); the within-subjects factor was STATEMENT (member, non-member). The primary dependent variables were participants' agreement ratings with the member and non-member statements, measured on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree,7 = Strongly agree).

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the 18 conditions. In each condition, they read a vignette describing a scenario in which explorers encounter an alien entity that matches an Earthly kind (e.g., tigers) on one dimension (e.g., appearance) but differs on the other two. 5 The order of information about each dimension was counterbalanced across participants. Following the vignette, participants completed a comprehension check and then rated their agreement with two contrasting statements (presented in random order): one from a character (Bill) asserting that the entity is a member of the category (e.g., "These creatures are tigers"), and one from another character (Mark) asserting that it is not. Attributing the two statements to different speakers allowed participants to agree that there is a sense in which each is correct without treating the two as directly contradictory-important for assessing whether participants endorse both senses, as dual-character accounts predict. Moreover, the prompts were phrased in terms of whether there is a "sense" in which the entity does or does not belong to the category, rather than asking flatly whether it is or is not a member.

Responses were recorded on 7-point Likert scales (1 = Strongly disagree,7 = Strongly agree).

Lastly, participants completed an item concerning the characteristic tele of the category in question (e.g., "One of the main purposes of a tiger is to stalk its prey, using its stripes for camouflage"). The full set of items are included in the Supplementary Materials (S1.1).

To illustrate the experimental manipulation, we include a sample vignette below from the condition in which the kind was tigers and the preserved feature was appearance (with composition and telos altered): Suppose that in the future, humans can travel to other galaxies. 2.3 Discussion

These findings suggest that people tend not to regard any single aspect-composition, telos, or appearance-as sufficient for membership in a natural kind. Ratings for the Member statement never rose significantly above the midpoint for any combination of kind and aspect preserved, and fell significantly below it in every case but one: chemical kinds with composition preserved. This pattern is inconsistent with versions of composition-privileging and teleology-privileging accounts that treat the relevant aspect as sufficient for membership, and provides little support for the dual-character approach.

These results instead favor accounts on which composition, telos, and appearance contribute to membership judgments without being sufficient on their own. Experiment 2 tests these latter accounts by independently varying composition, appearance, and telos in a full-factorial design, allowing us to assess their relative contributions to categorization judgments.

3. Experiment 2

Whereas Experiment 1 preserved one aspect at a time, Experiment 2 used vignettes spanning all possible combinations. This served two purposes, the first of which was that it allowed us to address a potential confound in the first experiment. There we found that Member ratings were highest (and the Non-Member ratings were lowest) for items in which composition was the preserved aspect (with the caveat that this effect was stronger for the chemical kinds). On the face of it, this result suggests that composition is relatively more important to judgments of category membership. Yet, recall the coherence effects predicted by causal-model theory, discussed in the Introduction. All else being equal, items in which two causally related features are both present or both absent will be judged as better category members than items in which one is present and the other absent (Rehder, 2017). Thus, composition-preserved items may have been judged better category members than telospreserved items in Experiment 1 because the former were judged as more coherent than the latter. This might have arisen if, for example, subjects viewed the causal connections between telos and appearance as stronger than the ones between composition and appearance (and thus telos-preserved items were more incoherent than the composition-preserved items).

Thus, Experiment 2 asks if the relative importance of features observed in Experiment 1 replicates, using a more reliable assessment of the relative importance of features, one that controls for interactions between features.

A second advantage of the full-factorial design is that it allows a direct assessment of whether coherence effects are in fact present for our materials. If they are, this will be reflected in strong two-way interaction effects between the features. For example, if coherence between composition and appearance is an important factor influencing participants' judgments, a positive two-way interaction between those features will be observed.

As in Experiment 1, participants read vignettes about explorers who encounter an animal species or chemical substance on another planet, described as matching or differing from an Earthly counterpart along three dimensions-composition, telos, and appearance.

Participants then rated the same Member and Non-Member statements used in Experiment 1.

3.1 Methods

We recruited 900 participants through the Prolific platform (Mage = 30.61, SD = 8.61; 50.5% women, 46.2% men, non-binary 3.3%). We excluded 77 participants for failing comprehension or attention checks, resulting in a final sample of 823 participants. As in Experiment 1, we targeted at least 50 participants per cell.

The experiment employed a 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 mixed design. Given the full-factorial design, we only used one representative kind from each domain-tigers and water-rather than the full set from Experiment 1.

3.3 Discussion

In Experiment 2, we investigated participants' category judgements when target entities were described as differing from their Earthly counterparts along no, one, two, or all of the three dimensions: composition, tele, and appearance. The regression analysis showed that composition was by far the strongest determinant of membership judgements. This dominance of composition over tele thus challenges teleological essentialism, whether construed in purely feature-based or causal-relational terms. At the same time, even when composition alone was preserved, membership ratings sat only at the scale midpoint: composition was the most important cue, but not a sufficient one, as some compositionprivileging accounts predict.

Experiment 2's design also allowed us to test for coherence effects-whether membership judgments depend not only on which features are present but on whether particular combinations cohere with a kind's inter-feature causal relations (Rehder, 2017). It also addressed an alternative reading of Experiment 1: that the low single-feature ratings reflected not the weakness of those features but the incoherence of items in which one feature was present and two absent. The two-and three-way interactions among features were close to zero, indicating both that categorization here was not governed by coherence and that incoherence did not substantially drive the results of Experiment 1. This near-absence of coherence effects is theoretically important in its own right, and we return to it in the General Discussion.

We also expanded the number of trial types from 8 to 20 by including 12 trials in which one feature was described as unkown, with the other two presented in all four combinations of present or absent. One goal was to further establish the relative importance of features: If composition is most important when the states of appearance and telos are known, it should remain so when one of those features is unknown.

A second goal was to assess the use of inter-feature causal relations during categorization. Because the state of some features was left unspecified in this experiment, the known features could provide inferential support for their presence (or absence) via the causal relations among them. For example, if an animal looks like an Earthly tiger, that fact might be used to reason diagnostically to the presence of Earthly tiger DNA on those trials in which composition was left unspecified. If so, appearance should exert a greater influence on category-membership judgments when composition is unspecified than when its state is known, because appearance would then provide both direct evidence for category membership and indirect evidence via the inferred presence of the relevant composition.

4.3 Discussion

This experiment provides further evidence that people weigh internal composition more heavily than either tele or appearance when judging whether an entity belongs to a natural kind. Consistent with Experiment 2, however, there was little evidence of the coherence effects predicted by causal-model theory. These results therefore remain compatible with purely feature-based accounts that privilege composition. One possibility is that coherence effects failed to emerge because the causal relations among the target's features were not sufficiently salient to participants-an issue we return to in the General Discussion. Experiment 4 therefore adopts a different approach, directly manipulating whether an entity's internal composition stands in the appropriate causal relation to its appearance and tele, thereby allowing us to distinguish between purely feature-based and causal-relational accounts that privilege composition.

5. Experiment 4

Composition emerged as the dominant cue to membership across Experiments 1-3, but this finding does not distinguish between purely feature-based and causal-relational accounts that assign a privileged role to composition. Experiments 2 and 3 sought to distinguish these accounts by testing for the coherence effects predicted by causal-model theory (CMT), a particular version of the causal-relational approach. However, coherence effects were largely absent.

Experiment 4 therefore tested the causal-relational approach using a different strategy: rather than looking for coherence effects in the form of interactions between features, it directly manipulated the presence or absence of the relevant causal relation. Specifically, in contrast to Experiments 1-3, the target entity matched its Earthly counterpart on all three dimensions in every condition; what varied was whether the entity's internal composition was described as producing its appearance and tele, or whether those features arose from some other cause. Because all features are present in both conditions, purely feature-based accounts predict no difference between them. A composition-centered causal-relational account, by contrast, predicts that severing this causal relationship should lower agreement with the Member statement even when every feature is preserved.

The Introduction alluded to previous research showing that explicitly denying the presence of causal relations between category features will affect category membership judgments. For example, Oppenheimer et al. (2013) presented items with features typical of a category (e.g., that an animal has four legs, a tail, and is very smelly, as is typical of skunks).

But in one condition an alternative explanation was provided for one of the features, e.g., that the animal was smelly because it had been wading through a sewer. In this condition the animal was judged less likely to be a skunk as compared to a control condition in which no alternative explanation was provided, confirming that denying the usual cause of a feature (smelliness is usually a result of a skunk's DNA or metabolic processes) undermines the evidentiary weight that that feature has for category membership (see Rehder & Kim, 2009 for similar results). Providing an alternative causal attribution for familiar features is also central to studies employing the well-known transformation paradigm, which typically show that categorizers can reason diagnostically from observed features to unobserved but essential ones (Gelman, 2003; Gelman & Wellman, 1991; Hampton et al., 2007; Johnson & Solomon, 1997; Keil, 1989; Opfer & Bulloch, 2007; Rips, 1989). For our materials, denying that the other features were caused by composition undermines the coherence of the to-be-classified item, with the result that Member ratings should be lower (and Non-Member ratings should be higher) as compared to the condition where the inter-feature causal relations are explicitly affirmed.

We recruited 500 participants through CloudResearch's Connect platform (Mage = 38.96, SD = 12.71; 51.6% women, 44.0% men, non-binary 4.3%). 5.3 Discussion

Experiment 4 found that severing the causal relationship between an entity's internal composition and its telos and apppearance lowered participants' willingness to categorize it as a member of the given kind, even though composition, appearance, and telos were all preserved-that is, they all matched the Earthly counterpart (tigers or water). This is predicted by composition-centered versions of the causal-relational approach. By contrast, purely feature-based accounts predict no such difference: an entity is an equally good featurematch regardless of whether its composition produced those features.

Both manipulation checks reinforce this reading. The first confirmed that the manipulation shifted participants' causal beliefs as intended, with higher ratings of composition's causal responsibility for appearance and telos in the causal-relation-present condition (M = 6.32) than in the causal-relation-absent condition (M = 3.77). The fact that mean attribution in the causal-relation-absent condition did not significantly fall below the midpoint works against the effect rather than for it, since participants on whom the manipulation failed should respond like those in the causal-relation-present condition, thereby reducing the observed difference between conditions rather than producing it.

The second check provided converging, observational support: within the causalrelation-absent condition, participants who took composition to be the cause of the entity's other features endorsed membership, whereas those crediting the alien intervention did not.

This evidence for the causal-relational approach does not conflict with the absence of coherence effects in Experiments 2 and 3. Those experiments tested whether particular combinations of features produced the interaction effects predicted by CMT. Experiment 4, by contrast, tested whether the causal relationship between composition and other features independently influences categorization, even when those features are held constant. Thus, the experiments examined different ways in which causal coherence might influence categorization. We develop this distinction in the General Discussion.

6. General discussion

Across four experiments, we compared leading theories of natural-kind categorization by investigating how composition, appearance, tele, and the causal relationships among these features influence category membership judgments. We asked two main questions. The first concerned the relative importance of different features in determining category membership.

The results were clear-cut: Across Experiments 1-3, whether a target shared the internal composition of its Earthly counterpart had a substantially greater influence on membership judgments than whether it shared its appearance or telos. Nevertheless, composition was not treated as sufficient for category membership. When a target shared only the composition of its Earthly counterpart, membership ratings did not rise significantly above the scale midpoint. Moreover, membership ratings were higher when the target also shared its Earthly counterpart's appearance or telos.

In addressing this first question, we also examined whether the relative importance of features varied between biological and chemical kinds. The results were somewhat mixed across experiments. In Experiment 1, composition was more strongly weighted than the other features for chemical kinds, whereas this advantage was much weaker for biological kinds. In Experiment 2, composition received greater weight for both kinds, although the effect was stronger for water than for tigers. In Experiment 3, however, the relative weighting of composition did not differ between tigers and water. Because Experiment 3 used a withinsubject design, allowing the same participants to evaluate different combinations of features, we suspect that it provides the most robust assessment of relative feature weights. On the other hand, Experiment 1 tested a wider range of biological and chemical kinds (but did not orthogonally vary the three features, a procedure we argue is necessary for a fully accurate assessment of feature weights). In sum, our results do not rule out the possibility that the advantage of composition might differ depending on whether the kind is biological or chemical. 9

The second main question concerned whether there was evidence of inter-feature causal relations affecting membership judgments. The answer depended on how those relations were assessed. One method tests for the presence of feature interactions in category membership judgments. Although we detected no such interactions, we observed large effects when the causal relations were explicitly manipulated in Experiment 4. 6.1 The role of composition in natural-kind categorization

Our first main finding raises the question: Why does composition receive greater weight than appearance or teleology? Many have argued that a natural kind's causal structure matters because it explains why its characteristic properties cluster together, thus supporting explanation, prediction, and induction concerning category members (Murphy & Medin, 1985; Rehder & Hastie, 2001 , 2004). And if these are among the central functions of naturalkind concepts, it is unsurprising that the information underlying those functions, including perceived causal structure, should inform categorization itself. But this does not explain why composition, rather than appearance or teleology, occupies a privileged position in the causal structures people represent.

One explanation draws on Cimpian and Salomon's (2014) proposed inherence heuristic: the early-emerging tendency to explain an entity's characteristic properties, including its appearance, by appealing to something inherent to it as opposed to extrinsic factors. As such, the heuristic does not specify which inherent features people invoke. But Cimpian and Salomon argue that there's a second, independent expectation already present in infancy: that an entity's insides are causally powerful (Gelman & Wellman, 1991; Newman et al., 2008). Such an expectation, they argue, turns the search inward, toward internal rather than surface features. Together, these tendencies might lead people to regard internal composition as more causally fundamental than appearance and teleology, helping to explain why our participants gave it greater weight in categorization. Of course, an early-emerging bias toward internal features does not preclude a further influence of scientific education on adult judgments, particularly for kinds such as water whose chemical composition is explicitly taught. Rather, the bias may provide a foundation that is subsequently reinforced by scientific education.

This privileged role for composition also bears on the intuition underlying Putnam's (1975) Twin Earth thought experiment. Putnam asks us to imagine a substance that resembles water and plays the same characteristic roles but has a different chemical composition (XYZ rather than H2O). He suggests that this substance is not in fact water. Our participants broadly shared this intuition, particularly for water: when appearance and telos were preserved but composition was altered, participants tended to deny category membership. Importantly, however, the converse did not hold. On average, preserving composition while altering appearance and telos was not sufficient to secure category membership. Thus, our findings support the privileged role of composition suggested by the Twin Earth intuition without supporting the stronger claim that having the relevant composition is deemed sufficient for category membership.

6.2 Sensitivity to relational structure

As mentioned above, our second main question concerned whether inter-feature causal relations influence category membership judgments-a core prediction of the causalrelational approach. Coherence effects are one specific signature of such influence. Recall that coherence effects arise when interactions between causally related features influence membership judgments above and beyond their individual contributions, such that ratings are higher when both features are present or both are absent than when only one is present.

Experiments 2 and 3 were able to test for the presence of such interactions because they orthogonally varied the state of internal composition, appearance, and teleology. However, robust coherence effects were not observed in either experiment. Experiment 3 presented a wide range of test items in which the state of some features was described as unknown, and so could test whether the presence of one feature might imply the presence of another (a kind's typical appearance being present might imply that its typical internal composition is too). But, again, there was no evidence of features providing this sort of inferential support for one another.

The absence of coherence effects here is surprising, given that they have been observed in prior research. Studies by Rehder and colleagues have taught participants novel categories and explicit causal relations between their features. For example, participants learned about a species called Kehoe Ants, whose iron sulfate-rich blood caused several other features, including thick blood and faster nest building. They were also given mechanistic explanations of how the iron sulfate-rich blood produced these effects. Coherence effects emerged over a wide range of experimental conditions and causal network topologies (see Rehder, 2017, for a review). Coherence effects have also been established in real-world categories. For example, Malt and Smith (1984) found that classifiers were sensitive to within-category inter-feature correlations. However, they observed the effect only when subjects explicitly rated the pairs of features as causally related (also see Ahn et al., 2002; Murphy & Wisniewski, 1989; Rehder & Ross, 2001; Wisniewski, 1995). Even children exhibit coherence effects. Hayes and Rehder (2012) taught 5-6 year olds novel categories with causally related features. For example, Rogos were described as animals that have big lungs, can stay underwater for a long time, and that the former feature was causally responsible for the latter. Holding the total number of features constant, an animal whose features exhibited coherence was more often chosen as the Rogo over one that did not (also see Barrett et al., 1993).

What distinguishes these previous studies from the current one? We suspect that two factors are especially important: whether the inter-feature causal relations are explicitly taught or emphasized during the experiment, and how concrete or familiar the features themselves are. In general, research has shown that not all the knowledge that people have about a category is automatically activated when the category's label is used (e.g., Murphy & Ross, 1999), a finding that potentially extends to inter-feature causal relations. Thus, interfeature causal relations are more likely to influence categorization judgments when they are explicitly taught or otherwise made salient (as in Hayes & Rehder, 2012, and the studies described in Rehder, 2017). Although our materials included concrete examples of the relevant features, they were organized around relatively broad dimensions-composition, appearance, and telos-and, crucially, Experiments 1-3 did not explicitly emphasize the causal relations among them. As a consequence, the relevant causal knowledge may have been less readily activated than in studies where such relations were made salient, either by explicitly teaching them or through other means. For example, even though Malt and Smith (1984) didn't use explicit instruction, the sort of feature pairs they examined (e.g., flying and having wings) may have made their causal relationships particularly accessible.

Importantly, Experiment 4 provides evidence that inter-feature causal relations can influence category membership judgments when those relations are made explicit. In that experiment, targets were described as possessing the same composition, appearance, and telos as their Earthly counterparts, but we explicitly manipulated whether the target's composition caused its appearance and telos. Participants were more likely to judge the target as a category member when this causal relation was present. Thus, when the relevant causal relation was made explicit, category judgments were sensitive not merely to which features the target possessed but also to how those features were causally related.

Taken together, our findings favor a composition-centered causal-relational account of natural-kind categorization. On this account, internal composition plays a privileged role in membership judgments, but other features also contribute, and the causal relationships among features can exert an independent influence. Although we found little evidence for the feature interactions predicted by CMT, this may reflect the failure of our experimental materials and procedures to activate the relevant causal knowledge. Thus, our findings do not rule out CMT as an account of natural-kind categorization. Crucially, however, Experiment 4 provides direct evidence that inter-feature causal relations can influence category membership judgments independently of the features themselves.

6.3 Assessing the dual-character account of natural-kind concepts

According to the dual-character account, people's natural-kind concepts involve two distinct criteria for category membership: a superficial criterion invoking observable properties and a deeper criterion invoking an underlying essence (Tobia et al., 2020). Here, we consider how our results bear on this account and whether findings previously marshalled in its support can instead be explained by the causal-relational account defended above.

The most direct evidence for the dual-character account comes from participants' willingness to endorse apparently conflicting category-membership judgments. Tobia et al.

(2020, Experiment 1b) presented participants with Twin Earth cases in which a target possessed the superficial properties of a natural kind but lacked its hidden, deeper properties.

Participants separately rated whether there was "a sense in which" the target was a category member and whether, considering "what it really means" to belong to the category, there was a sense in which it was "not truly" a member. Participants agreed with both statements at levels significantly above the scale midpoint. Tobia et al. interpreted this joint endorsement as evidence for distinct superficial and deeper criteria for category membership.

Our experiments produced a markedly different pattern. In Experiments 1 and 2, participants separately rated whether there was "a sense in which" the target was a category member and "a sense in which" it was not. Despite being given the opportunity to endorse both statements, joint endorsement was rare. Instead, the apparent conflict in judgments occurred primarily between participants.

One possible explanation for this difference concerns how readily available alternative senses of category membership are. For paradigmatic dual-character concepts such as artist or scientist, superficial and deeper properties may come apart sufficiently often in ordinary life such that the corresponding senses have become conventionalized and thus readily accessible (see Baumgartner, 2024). For example, it is not uncommon for people to accuse established artists as having "sold out": they remain artists in a superficial sense, while being seen as no longer exemplifying what it is to be a "true" artist. Natural kinds may differ in this respect. For kinds such as tigers and water, appearance, telos, and internal composition ordinarily covary, providing little occasion for distinct senses based on these features to become conventionalized. Consequently, simply asking whether there is "a sense"

in which an entity is a category member and "a sense" in which it is not, as we did, may not lead participants to spontaneously distinguish between superficial and deeper criteria for natural kinds. By contrast, wording that asks participants to consider what membership "really means" and whether the target is "truly" a member may supply a dimension along which to construct such a distinction, making it natural to spontaneously map observable properties onto a more superficial criterion and hidden properties, such as internal composition, onto a deeper one (Del Pinal, 2018). 10Importantly, this explanation does not challenge the claim that some concepts genuinely have a dual-character structure. Rather, it suggests that the evidence for such a structure may be weaker for natural-kind concepts, where the properties underlying the putative senses ordinarily covary. 11A second source of evidence for the dual-character account comes from contextual effects on natural-kind categorization. Tobia et al. (2020)found that participants were less inclined to categorize Twin Earth targets as members of the relevant kind in a scientific than a legal context, which they interpret as reflecting greater reliance on deeper properties in the former and superficial properties in the latter. On the dual-character account, context determines which of these distinct criteria is employed.

Context sensitivity of this sort, however, does not require two distinct criteria for category membership. It is perfectly compatible with causal-relational accounts that the relative weight of composition, appearance, and telos vary across contexts. For example, a scientific context might increase the weight of composition and its causal role, whereas a social or practical context might increase the weight of appearance or functional properties (Del Pinal, 2018). Importantly, this does not reduce the account to a purely feature-based one:

According to causal-relational accounts, category judgments depend both on which features people represent an entity as possessing, and on the perceived causal relations among them.

Contextual variation in feature weighting, then, is compatible with our causal-relational account.

A third source of evidence concerns the distribution of responses to natural-kind categorization tasks. Such responses are often bimodal, with some participants strongly endorsing category membership and others strongly rejecting it. On a dual-character interpretation, this disagreement arises because the superficial criterion is salient for some participants, whereas the deeper criterion is salient for others (Knobe, manuscript; Tobia et al., 2020). More recently, Knobe (manuscript) has proposed a more specific distributional signature of dual-character concepts: a strong negative association between Member and Non-Member ratings accompanied by pronounced heteroskedasticity.

Our results reproduced only part of this pattern. In Experiments 1 and 2, Member and Non-Member judgments were strongly negatively correlated, but their joint distributions did not exhibit pronounced heteroskedasticity (see Figures S2 and S3 in Supplementary Materials). Moreover, bimodality arose, to varying degrees, whether composition, appearance, or telos alone was preserved. Thus, although our experiments replicated the substantial disagreement that motivates the dual-character account, they did not exhibit its predicted distributional pattern.

If the disagreement between participants does not reflect the deployment of two distinct senses of category membership, what else might explain it? One speculation is that in everyday life, a range of weighting schemes will yield equivalent categorization judgments in familiar cases. And since for natural kinds, composition, appearance, and telos ordinarily covary, everyday categorization will rarely reveal the difference in different weighting schemes. Unusual cases like ours, however, will pull these features apart and produce disagreement. Thus, our cases may expose individual differences in feature weighting that ordinary categorization largely hides. Note that this explanation is compatible with composition nevertheless being privileged on average. As discussed in §6.1, a general tendency to regard internal properties as especially causally powerful (Cimpian & Salomon, 2014) could bias judgments toward composition without requiring everyone to assign it the same weight.

6.4 Limitations and future directions

Our experiments have several limitations. As was noted above, participants were never explicitly asked to predict an entity's features. This matters because prediction tasks naturally encourage participants to reason about the causal relations among an entity's properties. Experiment 3 came closest to encouraging prediction-based reasoning. Because one feature was left unspecified in some trials, participants could have arrived at a categorization judgment by considering what the specified features implied about the missing one. Even so, any such predictions remained implicit.

The absence of explicit prediction tasks may help explain the lack of robust coherence effects in Experiments 2 and 3 (see §6.2). More generally, our results may underestimate the importance of perceived causal structure, since prediction tasks make causal reasoning central to successful performance. Combining categorization judgments with explicit prediction tasks would therefore provide a stronger test of the role of perceived causal structure in naturalkind cognition.

A second limitation concerns the fact that we did not manipulate the explanatory context in which participants evaluated target entities. Lombrozo (2009) manipulated whether participants adopted a mechanistic or functional mode of explanation and found that doing so altered subsequent categorization judgments by changing which features were treated as most central. Our vignettes did not explicitly encourage participants to adopt either explanatory perspective. Future work could therefore manipulate explanatory goals. Participants asked to explain why a species possesses its characteristic traits or behaviors (e.g., why tigers hunt) might preferentially engage teleological explanations, leading them to place greater weight on teleological features during categorization than they did in the present experiments. By contrast, participants asked to explain how those traits or behaviors are produced (e.g., how tigers are capable of hunting) might instead favor mechanistic explanations that appeal to the target's internal composition, leading them to place even greater weight on composition and its causal relation to observable features.

A third limitation concerns the relevance of historical lineage. Our experiments did not manipulate whether biological targets shared an evolutionary history with their Earthly counterparts. However, some participants may have regarded shared ancestry as important for category membership. Presumably, ancestry is only a candidate criterion for biological kinds: two samples of a non-biological substance like water or iron count as the same kind whatever their respective histories, whereas two compositionally identical organisms may belong to different species if they lack shared ancestry.

Future work could therefore manipulate lineage orthogonally to the dimensions investigated here, testing whether biological categorization depends partly on a criterion unavailable for non-biological, chemical kinds.

Finally, it remains an open question whether a composition-centered causal-relational account extends beyond natural kinds to certain social kinds. Previous work suggests that some people essentialize categories such as race and gender (Gelman & Taylor, 2000; Haslam et al., 2000; Neufeld, 2019 Neufeld, , 2020) ) and that this predicts certain forms of prejudice (e.g., see Mandalaywala et al., 2018; Mandalaywala & Rhodes, 2014). However, existing studies have generally not distinguished between purely feature-based and causal-relational accounts of social categorization, nor have they systematically compared these with dualcharacter accounts. Judgments about humanness provide an interesting test case. Recent work has argued that dehumanization is partly driven by essentialist conceptions of humanness (Landry et al., 2022; Livingstone Smith, 2011 , 2021). At the same time, other research suggests that people possess a dual-character concept of being human (Phillips, 2022 (Phillips, , 2023).

Yet purely feature-based, causal-relational, teleological essentialist, and dual-character accounts have not been systematically compared as explanations of how people categorize others as human or subhuman.

More broadly, the distinctions drawn in this paper may help clarify which versions of purely feature-based or causal-relational accounts best explain how people think about race, gender, humanness, and other social concepts, and whether these different representations are associated with distinctive forms of prejudice.

6.5 Conclusion

Our findings favor a composition-centered causal-relational account of natural-kind categorization over the leading alternatives. Across four experiments, participants tended not to treat any single feature-appearance, telos, or internal composition-as sufficient for category membership. Nevertheless, composition exerted a substantially greater influence on membership judgments than either appearance or telos. Moreover, categorization was affected by whether the target's composition was perceived as standing in the appropriate causal relations to its appearance and telos. More broadly, our findings highlight the importance of independently manipulating appearance, telos, composition, and causal structure when adjudicating between theories of natural-kind categorization. The same approach may also help distinguish competing accounts of categorization for social categories that are often essentialized, such as race, gender, and humanness. In turn, this may help explain why different ways of thinking about social groups give rise to distinctive forms of prejudice.
