Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Rapid technological innovation and artificial intelligence (AI) have transformed not only the resolution at which the brain can be observed, but also the questions that can be asked, shifting the field from description toward prediction, regulation and generation. Yet progress has largely advanced along parallel trajectories, and findings at one level rarely inform or constrain the next. Here, we review recent advances in three exemplary domains and propose a futureoriented framework for their integration. First, molecular and cellular neurotechnology, including synaptic proteomics, cryo-electron tomography, super-resolution microscopy, optoacoustic and ultrasound imaging, sonogenetics and molecular MRI, resolves neural processes with unprecedented specificity. Second, AI-based decoding of human brain activity yields precise signatures of complex, subjective conscious experiences in naturalistic contexts and is beginning to inform diagnosis, drug evaluation and individualized neuromodulation in mental disorders. Third, large-scale neural recordings, mechanistic whole-brain simulations, neural foundation models and AI surrogate brains are converging toward individualized digital twin brains. We argue that the next level of progress requires integrating these domains into a closed loop of reciprocal constraints, in which molecular measurements inform model parameters, human signatures define and evaluate what models must reproduce, and model predictions prioritize perturbations that can be tested across domains.

Introduction

Neuroscience has re-entered a phase of exceptional momentum. Over the past decade, the convergence of advanced neuroimaging, molecular and electrophysiological neurotechnology, and artificial intelligence (AI) has dramatically changed the resolution at which the brain can be observed. Consequently, it has also changed the kind of question that can be asked: from describing pathways and localizing isolated functions, to building precise, individualized -and in part generative -models of how neural systems produce and regulate behavior, cognition and subjective experience. Considerable advances have been made in elucidating how neural circuits give rise to behavior, how emerging technologies can read out, regulate or computationally model these circuits, and how their dysregulation contributes to devastating neurological and mental conditions. Progress now spans biological and computational scales that were long treated in isolation, from ion channels and synaptic signaling in defined cell types and circuits, through the precise decoding of conscious cognitive and affective processes in humans, to in silico whole-brain generative simulations and AI-powered digital twin brains. Across these fields, the ambition has shifted rapidly from description to prediction, observation to regulation, simulation to generation, ultimately paving the way to accelerate translation and application.

Two parallel global developments make this shift urgent and extend well beyond scientific interest alone. The first is the rising health and socio-economic burden of brain-based conditions, as also emphasized in the World Health Organization (WHO) position paper on brain health [1].

Worldwide, neurological and mental disorders have become a leading cause of disability, and their combined health-economic burden exceeds that of other disease groups, including cancer, cardiovascular disease and diabetes [2, 3]. Demographic aging across most societies is further intensifying this pressure: dementia, stroke and age-related neurodegeneration are expanding fast in aging populations [4]. With respect to mental disorders, depression and anxiety show high and rising prevalence rates, 332.4 million and 359.2 million cases globally in 2021 and projected increases to over 466 million and 515 million cases by 2040 [5, 6]. Critically, the dominant cost driver for these conditions is not direct medical intervention, but lost labor-force participation or long-term social care. This reflects that the currently available interventions lack sufficient efficacy or accessibility for substantial proportion of those affected.

Molecular and imaging findings do not fully describe the brain models and interfaces that are built, while the brain models rarely return molecular measures or interpretable cognitive or subjective experience levels.

Moreover, molecular mechanisms seldom constrain how imaging of human subjective experience and cognition is interpreted, while it remains an ongoing debate about whether AI-based simulations can generate subjective experience. In this Perspective we examine three rapidly moving domains in neuroscience and argue for the need for integration across research traditions, measurement scales and strategies. These domains are: (1) molecular and cellular neuroimaging and neurotechnology, including recent progress in engineered ion channels, molecular magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or ultrasound imaging, which supply unprecedented mechanistic detail that higher-level models otherwise merely assume; (2) human neuroimaging, combined with computational and translational strategies, which has begun to deliver comprehensive predictive whole-brain models of mental processes in humans and to translate these into novel brain models and treatment evaluation and may as such have the potential to integrate neuroscience and brain modelling across scales; and (3) datadriven and AI-inspired brain modelling, including whole-brain simulation, digital twin brains, and recent developments in BCIs, which can overcome recent constraints and allow generative brain models and interventions.

Advances in Molecular Neuroscience and Neuroimaging

Understanding brain function requires detailed knowledge of its physiological and pathological processes at the molecular level, as a foundation for interpreting their consequences on connectivity, cognition, and behavior. Modern advances in neurotechnology now provide unprecedented access to these processes, enabling direct investigation of complex molecular systems, such as ion channels that govern neuronal signaling, as well as the development of molecular imaging techniques capable of capturing activity across multiple spatiotemporal scales.

These complementary approaches span molecular and synaptic, cellular, mesoscopic, and macroscopic levels of brain organization and function (Figure 1). In this section, we briefly outline state-of-the-art approaches that generate these new insights, highlighting both their unique advantages and their current limitations. The synapse is the fundamental structural and functional unit that connects neurons and forms precise neural circuits throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems. Synaptic proteomics has revealed approximately 1,800 synapse-enriched proteins that define 18 distinct neuronal synapse subtypes [15], as well as diverse presynaptic neurotransmitter vesicles and postsynaptic neurotransmitter receptors (ion channels and G-protein-coupled receptors, in most cases). However, our understanding of how synaptic proteins assemble in their native environments and whether distinct cell types or neural functions utilize specialized synaptic components, remains limited. To achieve the next level of resolution in understanding synapse formation and function, at least three major questions need to be addressed. First, how can presynaptic components, including neurotransmitter vesicles and their associated molecular partners, be selectively isolated and analyzed (e.g. by means of mass spectrometry) at cell-typespecific resolution? Second, how can postsynaptic receptors be directly visualized and identified in vivo with cell-type-specific precision? Third, how can differences in synaptic composition and properties be linked to specific neural circuit functions and behaviors? For instance, a recent study demonstrated that sleep-dependent synapse loss is regulated by sleep pressure at the level of the single neuron in zebrafish, providing direct evidence that synapse-level properties can be causally tied to behavioral state [16]. Addressing these questions requires integrating emerging technologies that bridge molecular, circuit, and functional scales, which have substantially advanced in recent years. To highlight this progress, we briefly review emerging methods that have provided crucial insights into the molecular assembly and function of synapses and their major components. Following this, we then further augment the overview with the state-of-theart neuroimaging methods, which help expand this knowledge by providing information across different scales, i.e. from studying molecular-level processes, to their implications on behavior and mental states.

Modern progress in investigating the synapses starts with patch-seq, a combination of patchclamp electrophysiology with single-cell ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequencing [17], which enables direct correlation of electrical properties with transcriptomic profiles in individual neurons and thus linking ion channel function to a given cell-type identity. Advances in multi-omics approaches, including spatial single-cell transcriptomics and single-cell proteomics, provide complementary views of the molecular composition of synapses across defined cell populations.

Cross-linking mass spectrometry (XL-MS) can further capture protein-protein interaction networks within native synaptic complexes [18]. Cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) resolves the native ultrastructure of synapses [19, 20], revealing the in situ arrangement of ion channels, vesicles, and postsynaptic density components at nanometer resolution, while correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM) further bridges the two modalities, using light microscopy to provide cell identity and electron microscopy to resolve high-resolution subcellular ultrastructures [21, 22]. In addition, MINFLUX super-resolution microscopy enables the visualization or tracking of individual ion channels and their clustering within the synaptic cleft with unprecedented spatial precision [23]. Together, these approaches offer a path toward a celltype-specific, native-context understanding of synaptic organization.

On the other hand, neuroimaging is entering a new phase in which the central challenge is to connect biological processes across scales, while preserving biological specificity, temporal fidelity and quantitative interpretability. At the cellular and subcellular scale, fluorescence imaging remains a cornerstone of fundamental neuroscience, with an expanding repertoire of genetically encoded sensors enabling cell-type-specific visualization of various metabolic, calcium-, voltage-, neurotransmitter-and neuromodulator-related processes, thus providing essential insights with high spatiotemporal resolution and molecular-level specificity, albeit over a limited field of view [24, 25]. Recent advances in multi-photon microscopy have pushed the limit to previously inaccessible deep cortical and subcortical neuron populations in the intact mouse brain [26, 27] and towards simultaneous recording of millisecond dynamics of voltage activity from hundreds of neurons and calcium activity from thousands of neurons in vivo [28]. These advances are beginning to bridge the gap between the activity of individual cells and the collective dynamics of neural populations. Optoacoustic, also known as photoacoustic, imaging provides a complementary route across microscopic to macroscopic scales at millimeter to centimeter penetration by translating optical molecular and functional contrast into deeply penetrating acoustic signals [29, 30]. Its ability to visualize endogenous chromophores and exogenous probes in three dimensions has enabled investigations ranging from microvascular organization and oxygen metabolism to molecularly targeted processes in living organs [31]. Recent high-density, ultrawideband arrays have further enabled rapid volumetric imaging of cerebral vasculature and hemodynamics over mesoscopic fields of view [32], while multimodal combinations with fluorescence imaging and MRI provide a route towards linking vascular and functional dynamics across scales and bridging cellular optical microscopy and whole-organ imaging [33] [34] [35] [36].

Ultrasound-based approaches are similarly expanding the functional reach of neuroimaging despite the limited molecular information. Ultrafast ultrasound provides highly sensitive measurements of cerebral blood-flow dynamics at mesoscopic level with exceptional temporal resolution, revealing rapid neurovascular responses that are difficult to capture with conventional imaging [37]. Ultrasound localization microscopy further pushes beyond the conventional resolution limit by tracking individual microbubbles, thus enabling super-resolved visualization of cerebral microvasculature and blood flow deep within the brain [38]. Beyond imaging, sonogenetics introduces the possibility of genetically defined, non-invasive neuromodulation, providing cell-selective control of neuronal activity through ultrasound and thus transforming it from a modality primarily anticipated for visualization into a versatile platform for mapping and manipulating brain function [39].

At the macroscopic scale, molecular MRI is broadening the role of MRI beyond anatomy and conventional hemodynamics. By implementing novel chemical exchange mechanisms, spectroscopy, quantitative multiparametric and hyperpolarized MR imaging methods, it seeks to visualize receptors, metabolism, inflammation and other biochemical processes non-invasively [40, 41]. Moreover, with the introduction of bioresponsive MRI probes, the molecular-level investigations that involve visualization and monitoring of essential functional markers like calcium and neurotransmitters open a new realm of possibilities to study neuronal processes at the whole brain scale through the subvariant known as molecular functional magnetic resonance imaging (molecular fMRI) [42] [43] [44]. Although the spatial and temporal resolution of MR-based methods remain lower than that of optical and ultrasound methods, their depth, coverage and translational potential make them indispensable for connecting mechanistic imaging in experimental models with human neuroscience and clinical research. In brief, the future of neurotechnologies, including neuroimaging, demands integration, rather than competition among modalities. Consequently, the most recent advances in this research field are moving toward a cross-scale framework in which molecules, cells, vessels, circuits and whole-brain networks are not treated as separate domains, but as interconnected levels of the same biological system.

From Imaging-based Precision Signatures of Conscious Experience to Applications in Mental Health

Over the past three decades, methodological innovation in animal and human neuroimaging, together with parallel theoretical advances, has transformed our understanding of the neural bases of cognitive, affective and social processes and of their dysregulation in mental disorders, driving a reconceptualization of these conditions as brain-based disorders. Population initiatives such as the UK Biobank have acquired imaging data from more than 100,000 individuals [45], and clinical consortia have pooled data for large-scale case-control studies. However, these gains in data scale, largely based on intrinsic (resting-state) brain activity and structural indices, have not yielded precise accounts of cognitive and emotional processes or clinically actionable imaging markers. Reproducible associations between intrinsic brain architecture and complex phenotypes require thousands of individuals [46], and the field continues to face limitations in reliability, specificity, ecological validity and personalization, as well as in clinical translation [47]. Against this background, the convergence of AI and machine learning (ML) with cognitive, affective and social neuroscience may move the field beyond incremental methodological refinement towards more comprehensive and precise brain models of human cognition and affect. Two developments are central to this shift: precise computational and neural models of complex, highly subjective conscious experience in humans, and the translation of these models into applications that measurably improve mental health (Figure 2). connectivity-guided neuromodulation, BCIs, and pharmacological evaluation. A first line of progress concerns the decoding of subjective and highly idiosyncratic experience.

Multivariate predictive models trained on distributed brain activity or connectivity now track momentary affective experiences with higher precision than conventional univariate approaches [48]. For instance, a brain affective arousal signature developed with naturalistic fMRI generalized across valence domains, mental processes and stimulation modalities, and was neurally separable from autonomic arousal and wakefulness across 24 studies [49]. Such signatures render experiences that were previously accessible only through verbal report measurable, and they enable researchers to address long-standing theoretical debates, such as whether conscious emotional experience and peripheral physiological reactivity share neural substrates [49, 50]. The same logic now extends to the individual level: using intensive longitudinal fMRI in two individuals with chronic pain, personalized decoders tracked fluctuations of spontaneous pain across sessions, runs and minutes [51]. These developments are critical for clinical translation, because dysregulated subjective experiences are core diagnostic features and the primary reason why patients seek treatment, yet this component remains difficult to capture in animal models [52].

A second line of progress moves decoding from static laboratory stimuli to dynamic, close-to-reallife contexts [53]. Naturalistic paradigms such as movies, narratives and immersive environments capture the temporal evolution and context dependence of experience, but also expose the limits of conventional models. Fear signatures derived from static images did not reliably track fear during dynamic movie viewing, whereas models incorporating functional connectivity captured these dynamic changes [54, 55]. Evolving subjective states thus appear to be implemented in distributed, interacting networks, in which core hubs such as the ventromedial prefrontal cortex integrate prior experience and current goals with ongoing input, which may explain why identical stimuli give rise to idiosyncratic experiences [56]. Inter-brain concordance during movie viewing offers another innovative approach to predict psychopathological symptoms in child-parent dyads [57]. In parallel, AI-assisted generative decoding, combining linear decoders with large language models, has produced coherent text descriptions of both viewed and recalled videos [58], underscoring the potential to decode meaningful internal content. Contrastive learning approaches that learn shared spatiotemporal electroencephalography (EEG) representations across individuals extend this logic to portable, naturalistic recordings [59]. By dissociating conscious experience from autonomic responses and other cognitive states, these approaches advance affective theory and provide biologically grounded targets for translation. These advances are beginning to translate into clinical applications. In treatment-free patients with depression and anxiety, personalized brain-circuit scores derived from task-free and taskevoked fMRI identified six biotypes that differed in symptoms, cognitive performance and treatment response [60]. Emotion-specific functional networks identified during naturalistic emotion induction improved the classification of major depressive and bipolar disorder relative to baseline connectivity models [61]. Multimodal models combining comparably cost-efficient EEG with clinical, cognitive and inflammation-related protein markers have reached high accuracy in distinguishing patients with depression from controls, although validation in larger independent cohorts remains essential [62]. Because fMRI is costly and immobile, portable recordings are key to scalable application: emerging EEG studies in naturalistic settings indicate that emotional states can be tracked under daily-life conditions, with direct relevance for affective BCIs and digital mental health technologies [63].

Imaging and computation are also beginning to inform treatment. Accelerated, functional connectivity-guided intermittent theta-burst stimulation produced considerably higher remission rates than sham stimulation in treatment-resistant depression [64], although adequately powered head-to-head trials are required before AI-guided individualization can be established as superior to standard protocols [65]. Moreover, neural signatures can serve as sensitive readouts for the rapid evaluation of novel pharmacological agents, as recently demonstrated for the effects of oxytocin on naturalistic fear [66].

Together, technological progress combined with cognitive, affective and computational neuroscience is moving the field from mapping emotions onto isolated brain regions toward precise, individualized and ecologically valid models of conscious experience, and from descriptive biomarkers toward markers that support diagnosis, drug development and neuromodulation.

Realizing this potential will require prospective validation, individual-level reliability and integration with the molecular mechanisms and generative brain models discussed in the adjacent sections.

Integrative Brain Modeling from Large-Scale Neural Recordings to AI-Augmented Digital Twin Brains

Building integrative models of brain function requires linking molecular and cellular mechanisms to distributed neural activity and individual behavior. Advances in electrophysiology, computational simulation, and AI provide complementary approaches to this problem. Largescale recordings constrain the dynamics that models must explain; mechanistic simulations specify how biological interactions generate these dynamics; and AI models learn transferable representations and predictive relationships from heterogeneous data. Their integration offers a path toward digital twin brains that capture individual characteristics and generate experimentally testable predictions. Here, we consider progress across these approaches and the challenges of connecting measurement, simulation, and intervention.

Recent electrophysiological advances broaden neural sampling across brain regions, species, and timescales. Mechanically robust ultraflexible arrays support recordings lasting up to 240 days in nonhuman primates, with some single units tracked for up to two months [67]. Intravascularly delivered ultraflexible electrodes have recorded cortical spiking activity in sheep following vesselwall penetration, offering an alternative implantation route whose accessibility depends on vascular anatomy [68]. Neuropixels probes adapted for nonhuman primates enable acute sampling across multiple brain regions through long shanks and programmable recording-site selection [69]. Ultraflexible arrays have also enabled stable single-unit detection during human neurosurgery, extending population-level measurements to awake patients [70]. The complementary strengths of these interfaces must be weighed against implantation requirements, sampling density, chronic stability, and behavioral conditions. Their suitability depends on the dynamics and behavior that the model is intended to explain.

Turning these measurements into modeling constraints requires explicit harmonization across modalities, subjects, and species. Spikes, local field potentials, calcium signals, and hemodynamic responses sample different processes; modality-specific observation models are therefore essential for relating these signals to latent neural dynamics. Quantitative behavioral characterization and analysis of population dynamics help connect distributed activity to computation [71]. Shared data standards, including the Brain Imaging Data Structure and Neurodata Without Borders, facilitate reproducible organization and reuse [72, 73]. Integration additionally requires aligned timing, anatomical registration, acquisition metadata, and quality control. Cross-species integration requires explicit assumptions about anatomical homology and behavioral comparability. Federated learning may support collaboration when pooling data is impractical. Harmonization should retain meaningful individual variation while accounting for acquisition-related differences. Biological priors and empirical observations jointly constrain mechanistic simulations that link local neuronal interactions to large-scale brain dynamics. Spiking networks represent neuronal and synaptic activity explicitly, whereas mean-field models summarize population dynamics at lower computational cost. Platforms integrating structural and diffusion MRI, estimates of synaptic density from positron emission tomography (PET), and laminar architectural priors have reproduced selected resting-state and task-related features [74]. Complementary advances in distributed computing have enabled spiking simulations with architectures scaling to 86 billion neurons [75] Individually calibrated simulations provide a framework for examining how local perturbations propagate through subject-specific networks. A recent preprint combined individual neuroanatomy and task dynamics with virtual changes in excitatory and inhibitory synaptic conductance, reporting heterogeneous network responses, prediction of longitudinal symptom trajectories, and agreement with independent pharmacological fMRI responses [76]. These findings motivate testable intervention hypotheses. An effective synaptic parameter does not, by itself, specify a molecular drug action or establish clinical benefit. Linking simulations to treatment requires correspondence between modeled perturbations and experimentally delivered interventions, alongside prospective evaluation of individual outcomes. Longitudinal updating could then refine parameter estimates and prediction uncertainty as new measurements become available.

Neural foundation models complement mechanistic approaches by learning transferable representations from large neural datasets. Self-supervised EEG models, exemplified by the Large Brain Model (LaBraM), support transfer across recording configurations and downstream tasks [77]. In fMRI, atlas-based models reduce dimensionality through regional aggregation, whereas atlas-free approaches preserve finer spatial information at greater computational cost. SLIM-Brain and Omni-fMRI make voxel-level representation learning more tractable through selective temporal processing and adaptive spatial patching, respectively [78, 79]. Representation transfer remains sensitive to acquisition differences, preprocessing, and population coverage. Coupling neural measurements to pretrained language models also supports semantic reconstruction [80].

Assessing the value of neural foundation models for individualized dynamical modeling requires evaluation of subject-specific fidelity and temporal prediction. AI surrogate brains address this dynamical objective by learning how observed brain activity evolves over time [81]. Neural Perturbational Inference trains a neural network to approximate large-scale dynamics and estimates effective connectivity by perturbing the surrogate and quantifying directed responses across regions [82]. Validation against generative systems with known connectivity and comparisons with cortico-cortical evoked potentials support the approach. Nevertheless, virtual perturbations probe the learned model, and their biological interpretation depends on the observation process, unmeasured influences, and generalization beyond the training distribution. The sign of an inferred regional effect reflects a model-level influence and need not identify the underlying neurotransmitter or cellular mechanism. Independent stimulation data, matched to the modeled perturbations, are therefore essential for evaluating intervention predictions.

Discussion

The present review focused on three exemplary domains (which align with our expertise) to illustrate the current momentum of technology-driven progress in neuroscience. At the same time the overview underscores the central problem outlined in the Introduction: each field has advanced rapidly along its own trajectory and excels where the others remain limited providing an opportunity for an integrated and more comprehensive perspective. Molecular and cellular neurotechnology resolves synaptic composition, receptor organization and neurochemical signaling with exceptional specificity, but how these properties relate to complex behavioral regulation remains incompletely understood (for an exception, see e.g. Suppermpool et al., 2024 [16]), in particular for complex mental processes in humans. Human neuroimaging now decodes complex and highly subjective mental states with high accuracy, but despite progress in prediction these neurofunctional signatures remain largely uninformative about the underlying neural and molecular mechanisms. Data-driven and AI-based brain models simulate, predict and increasingly generate brain dynamics, but their parameters are not necessarily biologically grounded, and different mechanisms may reproduce the same observations. We propose that the next level of progress will require integrating these domains into a dynamic and multi-directional closed loop of reciprocal constraints: molecular measurements that inform or fix model parameters, human signatures that define what models must reproduce and generate, and model predictions that prioritize molecular and neural perturbations for experimental testing (Figure 3). Calls for multilevel integration are long-standing, from Marr's levels of analysis [83] to large-scale initiatives such as the Human Brain Project [84] and recent proposals for integrative neuroscience [47]. Recent advances in computational modeling and AI may now make it feasible to implement a unified, multiscale framework of reciprocal constraints and iterative learning that links neural mechanisms to behavior and cognition [85]. From the bottom up, molecular measurements and molecular-scale architectures can inform assumptions in higher-level models. Whole-brain simulations have begun to incorporate macroscale estimates of brain architecture, for example from MRI [86], yet they do not specify architecture or action at the molecular and mechanistic level. Technology-supported advances in molecular neuroscience, including cell-type-specific synaptic proteomics, in situ ultrastructure, and receptor-, metabolism-and neurotransmitter-sensitive molecular indices could in combination with the dedicated computational implementation progressively anchor and constrain model parameters in biology.

From the top down, human neuroscience can define the outcomes against which models are evaluated and thus ensure their relevance for behavior and mental processes, given that precision alone does not guarantee relevance for behavior or mental health [87] [88] [89]. Current models are primarily validated against intrinsic functional connectivity and task-evoked activation patterns, whereas complex cognitive processes or also dysregulated subjective experiences for which patients seek treatment, such as fear, pain, arousal or depressed mood, are not yet a model output, although they may be approximated by neurofunctional signatures (e.g. [90]). Carefully validated and behaviorally relevant neurofunctional signatures of these mental states and from corresponding data use to train the model (e.g. fMRI or EEG data) could provide benchmark targets for models and digital twins to infer behavioral and cognitive relevance. This would allow simulated perturbations to be evaluated against changes in distinguishable experiences rather than in brain dynamics alone, with predictions that can in turn be tested, for example, by pharmacological neuroimaging in humans or focused brain stimulation experiments. Together these models can in turn close the loop and identify the circuit-level and neurochemical systems on which molecular approaches can focus to determine mechanisms that regulate behavior and cognition. In addition, animal research on internal emotion models offers a route to -potentially -link these models to mechanisms that remain inaccessible in humans, provided that crossspecies paradigms capture comparable computations [52].

The loop closes through intervention, which links targeted manipulations to predicted outcomes -ultimately behavioral and mental processes -and provides a common experimental currency across domains and scales. Sonogenetic cell-selective modulation, pharmacological crosschallenges, connectivity-guided brain stimulation and virtual perturbation of AI surrogate brains all converge on the same experimentally critical question of how a controlled intervention propagates through neural circuits and what changes it induces in the dynamic system at different levels in comparison to a well-defined control condition. Initial studies have begun to crossvalidate model predictions with empirical data. Simulated excitatory-inhibitory changes in individualized models have been supported by pharmacological fMRI responses [76]. Processspecific neurofunctional signatures have been used to capture potentially treatment-relevant effects of oxytocin on naturalistic fear [66] and, in closed-loop decoded neurofeedback, to reduce amygdala responses to phobic stimuli in patients with specific phobia [91]. A fully integrated framework, ideally a bidirectional closed loop, would require that interventions predicted in silico are constrained and tested by molecular imaging in experimental models and by neural signatures and behavioral or experience-based outcomes in humans, with discrepancies used to rapidly adapt the models.

Realizing this loop requires addressing challenges that are shared across the three domains and cannot be solved within any single one. First, prediction must be distinguished from explanation: decoders can track experience without revealing mechanism, and simulations can reproduce the same outcomes through multiple mechanisms. Second, reliability and specificity at the level of the individual remain limited, although personalization of models and approaches may be key to translation into mental health applications. Third, ecological validity, feasibility and scalability: findings acquired under controlled laboratory conditions need to translate into daily life and, ultimately, into improved diagnosis and treatment of individuals with brain-based disorders.

Finally, the growing weight of private investment makes shared standards and neuroethical governance an urgent rather than a future requirement.

While challenges remain within and across domains, integration across approaches and scales will be critical to advance our understanding of the brain and its disorders. For mental disorders in particular, which are still diagnosed largely on the basis of verbal report and for which translation from animal models has been limited, integration offers the opportunity to design, test and rapidly evaluate novel candidate drugs and innovative treatments before costly clinical trials, and to enable individualized diagnosis and intervention that is planned in a digital twin, delivered with novel neurotechnology and evaluated against changes in symptoms and experience-specific markers. Success will therefore be measured not only by decoding accuracy or simulation scale, but by whether integrated models improve diagnosis, treatment selection and outcomes for individuals. Achieving a mechanistic explanation of behavior and mental processes, and its translation into mental and brain health, will require that molecular, human and AI-based neuroscience constrain and support one another.
