Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Abstract

Objective: Research on student AI use typically asks who adopts these tools, treating use as a stable trait. Yet students face a fresh decision at every problem. We examined how much AI use reflects stable individual differences and how much varies within a student across tasks.

Method: Undergraduates (N = 398) at two institutions completed 30 quiz trials, choosing on each trial to answer independently, consult an AI chatbot, or rely on an AI-generated answer.

Students rated their topic expertise before each decision; cognitive abilities, AI attitudes, and demographics were also measured. Choices were analyzed with Bayesian multilevel multinomial logistic regression.

Results: With moderately reliable AI, about half the variance in AI use reflected stable individual differences (intraclass correlations of 41% for chatbot use, 48% for full reliance); the remainder varied within individuals across trials. Self-assessed expertise accounted for this within-person variation: students sought help primarily when they felt unknowledgeable. Fluid intelligence predicted more chatbot consultation but less full delegation. Male students and those with more favorable AI attitudes delegated more readily.

Conclusions: AI use is better described as adaptive help-seeking than as a fixed trait.

Interventions targeting metacognitive calibration may prove more effective than blanket policies assuming uniform use.

1. Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence systems into educational environments has accelerated dramatically in recent years (Kasneci et al., 2023; Zawacki-Richter et al., 2019). A global study of over 23,000 students across 109 countries found 71% had used ChatGPT (Ravšelj et al., 2025). But this high adoption rate masks substantial variation in how individuals actually engage with these tools. Emerging evidence reveals that even among adopters, trust in AI, which affects reliance, varies across systems and contexts (Glikson & Woolley, 2020). Each time a student encounters a problem, they face a choice: attempt it independently or request AI assistance. This framing draws on classic help-seeking theory, intelligent tutoring systems research, and recent work on generative AI, reviewed below.

1.1 Help-Seeking as a Self-Regulated Learning Strategy

Early research in help-seeking treated it as a sign of dependence or immaturity. Nelson-Le Gall (1985) reframed it as an adaptive learning strategy, describing a sequence of decisions a learner must make: recognizing that help is needed, deciding to seek it, identifying a suitable source, eliciting the help, and evaluating the results. Ineffective help-seeking could be caused by a failure at any of those steps. Nelson-Le Gall (1985) also distinguished two kinds of help-seeking based on the goal. In instrumental help-seeking, the learner seeks only as much help as is needed, aiming to acquire the understanding needed to solve the problem independently. In executive help-seeking, the learner has little interest in understanding the solution and aims to have the problem solved by someone else. Newman (2002) later described adaptive help-seeking as a component of selfregulated learning that depends on cognitive competencies, including knowing when help is needed, and on motivational resources such as a willingness to acknowledge difficulty. The first of these competencies is metacognitive: learners must monitor their own knowledge accurately to recognize when help is warranted.

Empirical work in this area has mainly focused on the level of person rather than decision. Karabenick (2003), for example, surveyed 883 college students and used cluster analysis to identify groups with distinct help-seeking profiles. Strategic, adaptive help-seekers, who relied on instructors were more motivated, held stronger mastery goals, and earned higher grades than students who either avoided help or sought it quickly. These person-focused findings show that help-seeking tendencies differ reliably between students, but not how the same student decides, problem-by-problem, whether help is worth seeking.

1.2 Help-Seeking in Intelligent Tutoring Systems and Generative AI

Intelligent tutoring systems made help-seeking behavior more easily observable, because each request is logged with the problem-solving step that prompted it. In a review of help use in interactive learning environments, Aleven et al. (2003) found that learners frequently used ondemand help ineffectively, avoiding it when it would be useful or overusing it when it is not needed. The review found appropriate help use likely to be an interaction between metacognitive skill, cognitive factors such as prior knowledge, and features of the system itself.

Regarding overuse, Baker et al. (2004) studied the most extreme form, termed gaming the system, or systematically exploiting a tutor's hints and feedback, for example by requesting hints in rapid succession until the answer is revealed. In classroom observations of a cognitive tutor, gaming was associated with reduced learning, and students who gamed frequently scored substantially lower on a post-test even after controlling for prior knowledge. However, effective help-seeking can also be taught. Roll et al. (2011) added a Help Tutor that gave immediate feedback on help-seeking errors. Across two classroom studies it improved students' help-seeking behavior, and in the second study, which added explicit help-seeking instruction and support for self-assessment, the improved help-seeking skills transferred to new material after the support had been removed.

Generative AI extends the student's help-seeking decision to almost every academic task. Sheese et al. (2024) analyzed more than 2,500 queries that students submitted to a programming assistant, built on a large language model and designed not to reveal complete solutions, and found that students who were more successful in the course tended to use the tool more often. Sung and Thomas (2025) showed that the classic help-seeking framework applies to generative AI: in a survey of university students, general academic help-seeking goals mediated the relationships between achievement goals and ChatGPT-specific help-seeking outcomes, including executive use of ChatGPT, perceived threat, and avoidance.

The distinction between instrumental and executive help maps onto the choice that generative AI now offers. A student can interactively consult a chatbot, reading and evaluating its explanation, and following up before committing to an answer, or can have the AI simply supply the answer. The first resembles instrumental help-seeking and the second executive helpseeking, and the present study offered both options on every trial. Across this literature, however, help-seeking has been characterized mainly at the person-level. Whether a given student's decision to use AI help reflects a stable disposition or a judgment made problem by problem has not been examined.

1.3 AI Use as a Within-Person Decision Process

Framing AI use as help-seeking implies that it should vary within individuals across tasks. Each use decision is made in response to a specific problem under specific conditions, making within-person variation central to understanding AI use behavior. As a result, classifying students simply as AI "users" or "non-users" provides limited insight into when assistance is actually sought.

Despite this implication, most empirical work on generative AI in education has operationalized AI use as a between-person phenomenon, focusing on overall adoption rates or average use frequency (Strzelecki, 2024). Such approaches conflate stable individual differences with situational influences and obscure the processes that govern moment-to-moment AI helpseeking. Without explicitly separating within-person and between-person sources of variance, it is unclear whether observed predictors reflect enduring propensities or context-sensitive responses.

Distinguishing these levels of analysis is essential for both theory and practice. If AI use is primarily trait-driven, interventions should target learner characteristics and attitudes; if it is primarily context-dependent, task design and instructional structure become the primary levers.

Addressing this distinction requires research designs that capture repeated AI use decisions within individuals and analytic approaches that explicitly partition variance across levels.

1.4 Limitations of Current Methodological Approaches

Despite growing interest in generative AI use in educational contexts, existing empirical approaches are poorly suited to examining AI use as a within-person decision process. Crosssectional surveys cannot capture how decisions to seek AI assistance vary across tasks, nor can between-person associations be used to infer within-person processes (Curran & Bauer, 2011).

Field and quasi-experimental studies improve ecological validity but typically manipulate access to AI assistance or assign AI use experimentally (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026; Noy & Zhang, 2023). These designs are well suited for estimating average treatment effects but eliminate learner choice, thereby obscuring the decision-making process central to help-seeking.

Research on intelligent tutoring systems and AI assistants has observed help-seeking behavior directly (Aleven et al., 2003; Sheese et al., 2024) but, as reviewed above, has primarily emphasized between-person differences rather than decomposing trial-level fluctuations within individuals. As a result, existing estimates of individual difference effects in AI use may be confounded with situational variation (Curran & Bauer, 2011).

Addressing these limitations requires a controlled experimental paradigm with repeated decision opportunities in which participants freely choose whether to seek AI assistance. Such designs sacrifice some ecological validity but enable decomposition of within-person and between-person variance while holding task characteristics and AI reliability constant. The present study adopted this approach to address two research questions. From an instructional standpoint, understanding when students seek AI assistance is critical because most AI policies and learning environment practices implicitly assume that AI use is stable across tasks, rather than dynamically regulated in response to task-specific demands.

1.5 Predictors of AI Use

Treating AI use as a dynamic help-seeking process, we now turn to the specific factors that may shape these decisions. Table 1 summarizes candidate predictors organized by their expected level of operation, though these assignments are tentative given the absence of prior multilevel research on generative AI use.

Several predictors likely operate primarily at the between-person level, creating stable propensities toward more or less AI use across contexts. Cognitive abilities present competing theoretical predictions: higher fluid intelligence, working memory, and attention control could either increase AI use (by enabling better evaluation and integration of AI outputs) or decrease it (by reducing perceived need for external assistance). Evidence from cognitive offloading research is mixed. Higher short-term memory capacity predicted less offloading in one study (Meyerhoff et al., 2021), whereas working memory capacity was unrelated to offloading in another (Morrison & Richmond, 2020). Gilbert et al. (2020) found that people with poorer unaided memory used reminders more, and that reminder use beyond the optimal level was predicted by underconfidence relative to actual ability. Although help-seeking theory suggests that ability may relate differently to instrumental AI help (consulting a chatbot) than to executive AI help (delegating the answer), none of the prior studies measured fluid intelligence.

Dispositional attitudes toward AI similarly create baseline adoption propensities, with constructs such as perceived usefulness, AI anxiety, and propensity to trust all linked to technology engagement (Davis, 1989; Grassini, 2023; Russo et al., 2025). Gender differences in AI adoption are well documented, with women showing lower adoption rates even when access is equalized (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025; Otis et al., 2024). Women also report higher AI anxiety and lower self-assessed AI knowledge (Russo et al., 2025), and self-assessed knowledge explained most of the gap in one large U.S. survey (Aldasoro et al., 2024).

Other predictors may drive within-person dynamics. Trial-level metacognitive judgments of self-expertise may trigger AI help-seeking on a moment-to-moment basis. When individuals perceive a knowledge gap, they are more likely to seek help (Aleven et al., 2003; Undorf et al., 2021). Thus, we expected more AI use on trials where students judge their expertise insufficient.

Still other factors may moderate within-person effects: dispositional trust might amplify or attenuate how strongly self-expertise judgments influence use decisions, with miscalibrated trust producing maladaptive patterns of automation bias or algorithm aversion (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

1.6 The Present Study

Given these uncertainties about the direction and level of operation of each predictor, and the absence of prior multilevel evidence in generative AI contexts, we adopted an exploratory approach and addressed two research questions. The first asked to what extent students' AI use was driven by stable individual differences versus factors that vary across tasks (RQ1). The second asked how trial-level contextual factors related to moment-to-moment use decisions after accounting for individual differences (RQ2).

To address these questions, we designed an experimental task in which participants completed 30 trials with free choice to use AI assistance (chatbot or automatic answer selection) or respond independently. We assessed both stable individual differences and trial-level psychological states across repeated decision contexts. This design enabled multilevel analysis of both stable individual differences reflecting adoption propensities and trial-level decisions reflecting contextual variation within persons.

2. Method

>Method

2.1 Participants

Three hundred and ninety eight students (ages 18-24 years) were recruited from the Clemson University (n = 260) and United States Military Academy (USMA; n = 138) research pools. All students received course credit for their participation. All participants were 18 years of age or older. Written informed consent to participate in the research was obtained electronically from every participant before any study procedure began; participants were informed of the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, their right to withdraw at any time without penalty, and that anonymized data could be reported in scientific publications. No individually identifiable participant information is reported in this article. Data from the two locations were pooled for analysis because the study design, task structure, and AI system behavior were identical across sites. Location was not treated as an analytic factor, as the study was not designed to examine site-specific differences, and all primary analyses focused on within-person variation in AI use.

2.2 Measures 2.2.1 Personality and AI Attitude Scales

Participants completed a measure of a personality trait likely to affect AI use, Need for Cognition (Cacioppo et al., 1984), and four scales assessing attitudes and perceptions toward automation and AI: the Artificial Intelligence Attitude Scale (Grassini, 2023), the Negative Attitudes toward Robots Scale adapted for AI (Nomura et al., 2006), the Perfect Automation Schema Scale (Merritt et al., 2015), and the Complacency Potential Rating Scale (Singh et al., 1993). See Table 2 for scale details. The 18-item Need for Cognition scale was rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (extremely uncharacteristic of me) to 5 (extremely characteristic of me; e.g., "I would prefer complex to simple problems") and scored as a total. The four-item Artificial Intelligence Attitude Scale (e.g., "I believe that AI will improve my life") was rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), modified from the original 10-point format, and scored as a total. The 14-item Negative Attitudes toward Robots Scale was adapted by replacing "robots" with "AI" in each item and was rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Its three subscales were scored separately as totals: negative attitudes toward situations of interaction with AI (6 items; e.g., "I would feel uneasy if I was given a job where I had to use AI"), social influence of AI (5 items; e.g., "I feel that if I depend on AI too much, something bad might happen"), and emotions in interaction with AI (3 items; e.g., "I would feel relaxed talking with AI," reverse scored). The 7-item Perfect Automation Schema Scale was rated on a 5-point agreement scale scored 0-4 and summed (e.g., "Automated systems rarely make mistakes"; "If an automated system makes an error, then it is completely useless"). The 16-item Complacency Potential Rating Scale was rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree; e.g., "Automated systems used in modern aircraft, such as the automatic landing system, have made air journeys safer") and scored as a total.

2.2.2 Cognitive Ability Measures

Three cognitive abilities were measured at the latent level using multiple indicators.

Attention control was assessed with the Stroop squared, Flanker squared, and Simon squared tasks (Burgoyne et al., 2023). Working memory was assessed with shortened Operation Span (Oswald et al., 2015) and Symmetry Span tasks (Unsworth et al., 2009). Fluid intelligence was assessed with the 18-item Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices (Kane et al., 2004; Raven et al., 1998) and Number Series (Thurstone, 1938). See Table 2 for a summary. These cognitive tasks were designed and validated for individual-differences research with published reliability estimates for each task reported in Table 2.

2.3 Design

The study used a quasi-experimental design in which response mode was self-selected on every trial. For each question, participants chose to consult an AI chatbot before answering (chatbot), have the AI answer automatically (rely), or answer without AI assistance (manual; Figure 1), and could vary their choice across the 30 trials. Person-level predictors (Table 2) were working memory, attention control, fluid intelligence, Need for Cognition, four AI and automation attitude measures (AI attitudes, negative attitudes toward AI, perfect automation schema, and complacency potential), and gender. The trial-level predictor was self-rated topic expertise, assessed before each choice. Ratings of chatbot responses, collected after chatbot trials, were not analyzed here.

2.4 Experimental Task

Building on Pak et al.'s (2025) simulated chatbot paradigm with controlled accuracy, we implemented a 30-item web-based quiz spanning six academic domains (physics, history, chemistry, biology, geography, and psychology), calibrated to undergraduate senior-level difficulty. In an earlier study (at the same research sites), students answering these questions without AI were 39% correct (Pak et al., 2025). Questions were derived from standardized test

2.5 Procedure

After providing informed consent via Qualtrics, participants completed attitude scales (20-30 min), then proceeded to a study dashboard for cognitive ability tests (55 min) and the experimental task (30-45 min), with self-paced breaks permitted. For the quiz, participants were instructed to maximize their on-screen grade and could choose to answer immediately, consult a chatbot (and rate its quality), or have the computer answer for them.

3.1 Data Preparation and Analytic Approach

Of 425 participants with complete ability and individual difference data, 13 were excluded due to software failures and 14 for ability scores 2.5 standard deviations below the sample mean (indicating insufficient effort), yielding 398 participants for analysis. Descriptive statistics are presented in Table 3.

We used the brms package (Bürkner, 2017) and the brm() function to understand the effect of each predictor on the odds of choosing to answer manually, use the chatbot, or rely on the AI. The brms function relies on a Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm and was set to the default of 4 chains of 2,000 iterations each, resulting in 8,000 posterior samples used for estimates. All models included 11,940 observations from 398 participants with no missing data.

All models included a random intercept for participant. All outcomes were modeled as categorical with a logit link, using manual choice as the reference category. All priors were the default priors implemented in the brms package for categorical outcomes and were weakly informative. Category-specific intercepts and all variance components were assigned the brms defaults of Student-t priors with 3 degrees of freedom, mean 0, and scale 2.5. This Bayesian approach produced 95% credible intervals (CrI) for each main effect. We used a model-building approach similar to multilevel modeling in frequentist approaches, where we first ran an unconditional (null) model to understand the general odds of each AI choice. We followed with a second model that included all within-participant predictors, a third model that included all between-person predictors, and a final model that combined within-and betweenperson predictors. As we built the final model, our criterion for further investigation of any predictor was that the 95% credible interval excluded zero for either comparison (Manual vs.

Chatbot or Manual vs. Rely). Only predictors meeting this criterion were included in subsequent models. 𝑅 " indicators for each predictor in the models presented were always 1.00-1.01, indicating good convergence. Meaningful predictors are discussed in the text and bolded in Table 5. For interpretation, negative estimates indicate lower odds of choosing that AI option relative to answering manually, while positive estimates indicate higher odds. Odds ratios below 1.0 correspond to negative estimates, and those above 1.0 correspond to positive estimates.

3.2 Understanding the Choice to Use AI Assistance

Because the outcome was a three-level categorical choice (manual, chatbot, or full reliance), we performed multilevel multinomial logistic regression. Level 1 predictors were trial number and self-rated topic expertise. Level 2 predictors included cognitive abilities (attention control, working memory, fluid intelligence), personality (Need for Cognition), and technology attitudes (AI attitudes, perfect automation schema, complacency potential, and negative attitudes toward AI). Correlations among predictors were examined and none met multicollinearity criteria (|r| > 0.60; Table 4), so all were retained as separate predictors. Ability scores were standardized and combined into latent construct composites; all other predictors were centered.

Self-expertise ratings, performed for each trial, were highly right skewed with many values at 0-1 (0.938). Thus, this variable was transformed prior to analysis into a binary variable representing either no/very little expertise (values 0-1) or some expertise (values 2-9, Figure 2).

3.2.1 Unconditional Model

The posterior mean of the log-odds intercept for Manual vs. Chatbot was -0.78 (95% CrI = -0.95, -0.61), and for Manual vs. Rely was -0.08 (95% CrI = -0.27, 0.10). These estimates showed baseline differences in choice propensities: answering manually was chosen more frequently than using the chatbot; however, there was no difference between propensity to rely on the automation or respond manually. To quantify the proportion of variance attributable to stable individual differences, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were computed on the latent scale. Approximately 41% of the variance in chatbot choice and 48% of the variance in reliance choice were attributable to between-participant differences.

3.2.2 Within-Person Predictors

Within-person predictors included trial number (Trial) to account for changing behavior as the task progressed and a binary indicator of self-reported expertise.

Manual vs Chatbot.

Compared to trials where participants reported no or very little expertise, participants were substantially less likely to choose the Chatbot option relative to Manual when they reported some expertise (Table 5). Trial number was also negatively associated with Chatbot choice, suggesting that participants increasingly shifted away from Chatbot use over time relative to Manual (Figure 3). The standard deviation of random intercepts across participants was 1.55 (95% CrI [1.41, 1.71]) for Chatbot vs. Manual meaning participants differed greatly between each other. These standard deviations quantify how much individuals varied from each other in their baseline tendencies; larger values indicate greater between-person variability.

Manual vs Rely.

Participants with some or high expertise were less likely to rely than to answer manually. Trial showed no association with Rely vs Manual choice. Again, large between-participant variability was observed for Rely vs. Manual (SD = 1.71, 95% CrI [1.56, 1.88]).

3.2.3 Between-Person Predictors

All between-person predictors were entered into the model as centered averages for each participant (Table 5).

Manual vs Chatbot.

Higher fluid intelligence was associated with an increased likelihood of choosing the Chatbot over Manual control. The standard deviation of random intercepts was 1.49 (95% CrI [1.35, 1.64]).

Manual vs Rely.

Higher fluid intelligence was associated with a lower likelihood of choosing Rely relative to Manual. Gender predicted choice to Rely rather than answer manually with male participants more likely than female participants to choose Rely. More favorable AI attitudes predicted a greater likelihood of reliance. The standard deviation of random intercepts was 1.60 (95% CrI [1.46, 1.76]).

3.2.4 Final Model

Retaining only predictors whose credible intervals excluded zero in previous models, we fit a final Bayesian multilevel multinomial logistic regression model combining both trial-level and person-level predictors. The within-person fixed effects were Trial and Expertise. The between-person fixed effects were fluid intelligence, gender, and AI attitudes. Because predictors compete, any meaningful predictors in this final model represent a unique effect, conditional on the other predictors.

Manual vs Chatbot.

Expertise was strongly associated with choosing Manual over Chatbot. Higher fluid intelligence was associated with an increased likelihood of choosing the Chatbot over Manual. Gender was not meaningfully related to Chatbot use. AI attitudes were not meaningfully related to Chatbot use. The standard deviation of random intercepts was 1.52 (95% CrI [1.38, 1.68]).

Manual vs Rely. Participants reporting expertise were much less likely to choose Rely over Manual. Fluid intelligence was also negatively associated with reliance; male participants were more likely than female participants to choose to rely. More favorable AI attitudes were associated with greater reliance. The standard deviation of random intercepts was 1.60 (95% CrI [1.46, 1.76]).

4. Discussion

Understanding when students seek AI help requires examining both who is predisposed to use these tools and what momentary factors trigger assistance-seeking. We designed an experimental paradigm to disentangle these levels of analysis. Given the exploratory nature of the present analyses, the patterns observed here should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

4.1 Variance Structure and Individual Differences (RQ1)

The unconditional model revealed that approximately half of the variance in AI use decisions resided at the between-person level (ICCs of 41% for chatbot choice, 48% for reliance). This finding suggests that AI use should be conceptualized as a dynamic process shaped by both the situation and trait-like characteristics.

Among between-person predictors, fluid intelligence showed opposing effects across AI use types: higher fluid intelligence predicted increased chatbot consultation but decreased full reliance. This dissociation parallels the distinction between instrumental and executive helpseeking (Nelson-Le Gall, 1985). Consulting the chatbot is most similar to instrumental help, since the student evaluates an explanation and still selects the answer while delegating resembles executive help. Students higher in fluid intelligence appear to favor the instrumental form, perhaps because they are better able to evaluate a chatbot's reasoning, a possibility our data cannot test directly.

Gender differences replicated prior findings (Humlum & Vestergaard, 2025; Otis et al., 2024), with male participants showing greater willingness to delegate to automation specifically, rather than AI use broadly. Favorable AI attitudes similarly predicted reliance but not chatbot use, consistent with technology adoption frameworks (Davis, 1989; Grassini, 2023). The specificity of this gender effect to full delegation rather than interactive use suggests that previously documented gaps may reflect comfort with ceding control rather than reluctance to engage with AI tools.

4.2 Trial-Level Dynamics (RQ2)

Trial-level self-assessed expertise was a strong predictor across all models. Participants were substantially less likely to seek AI assistance when they reported topic familiarity (58% reduction for chatbot, 70% for reliance). This finding supports conceptualizing AI use as helpseeking behavior (Karabenick, 2003; Nelson-Le Gall, 1985). Students appear to monitor their knowledge before each decision and seek help when they judge a gap, as students do in intelligent tutoring systems (Aleven et al., 2003; Undorf et al., 2021).

One caveat is that self-rated expertise likely also reflects actual knowledge; students who know more may simply answer manually more often without evaluating each question.

Separating the two would require controlling for objective knowledge (cf. Gilbert et al., 2020), which our design does not allow because accuracy was observed only on the subset of questions students chose to answer manually. Thus, the expertise effect shows that AI use tracks students' judgments of their own knowledge, whether those judgments reflect metacognitive monitoring, actual knowledge, or both. Designs with forced unaided trials could separate these accounts.

Chatbot use declined over trials while automation reliance remained stable. This asymmetry suggests that students may have learned to calibrate their interactive AI use based on experience with the system (Lee & See, 2004), while full reliance reflects a more stable orientation toward automation dependence. This pattern implies that interactive consultation and full reliance may invoke different trust and reliance processes (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

Alternatively, students may have grown less willing to invest the additional effort required by chatbot interaction, preferring instead to simply delegate. Our data cannot distinguish between these explanations, but the distinction has a practical implication: if chatbot decline reflects appropriate trust calibration, it represents adaptive learning; if it reflects effort minimization, it may signal disengagement. Future work tracking response quality across trials could distinguish these accounts. Alternatively, because questions appeared in the same order for all participants, trial position is confounded with item content, and the decline may partly reflect the questions presented later.

Several predictors did not emerge as meaningful in our models. Working memory, attention control, and Need for Cognition were unrelated to AI use. Deciding when to seek help may not be cognitively demanding, or these abilities may matter for how well AI is used once sought, which we did not measure. Negative attitudes toward AI and complacency potential also did not predict use. Positive attitudes toward AI mattered but general wariness did not.

4.3 Theoretical and Educational Implications

These findings extend help-seeking theory (Karabenick, 2003; Newman, 2002) to generative AI contexts. AI assistance removes many social barriers to help-seeking, yet metacognitive processes governing when assistance is needed still apply. Students did not use AI indiscriminately; instead, they sought it on questions they judged unfamiliar, consistent with metacognitively guided help-seeking. The dissociation between interactive chatbot use and full reliance also suggests that the instrumental and executive distinction extends from human helpers to AI. Early evidence suggests that generative AI can improve learning when it supports productive back-and-forth but may impair it when it substitutes for the student's own thinking (Weidlich et al., 2025). This pattern has another implication: students were most likely to seek AI help, and especially to delegate, on questions they judged unfamiliar, which are the questions on which they could least evaluate the AI's output. Pak et al. (2026) called this undetected delegation failure: errors go unnoticed because users lack the knowledge to verify (in contrast to traditional complacency, which arises from a lapse in vigilance). Seeking help when knowledge is insufficient is adaptive, but with a fallible AI it concentrates reliance where errors are least likely to be caught. The problem is worse for full delegation, which removes even the chance to evaluate the AI's reasoning. Thus, interventions should target not only when students seek help but also their ability to evaluate it.

The substantial within-person variance (52-59%) challenges the premise underlying many institutional AI policies. Blanket bans or universal permissions assume AI use is a stable student characteristic, yet our findings suggest the same student may appropriately avoid AI on familiar material and appropriately seek it on unfamiliar material within a single assignment.

The strong predictive role of self-expertise judgments points toward a specific intervention target: metacognitive calibration. Rather than focusing on whether students use AI, educators might focus on whether students accurately recognize when they need help.

Embedding low-stakes knowledge checks or confidence prompts before AI access could promote deliberate decisions rather than habitual use or avoidance. This approach treats AI as analogous to other learning resources where the goal is strategic deployment rather than abstinence.

Practically, this might involve prompting students to judge their own knowledge before accessing AI assistance, since inaccurate self-evaluations lead students to stop studying prematurely (Dunlosky & Rawson, 2012). Learning management systems could prompt students to rate their confidence before AI access is granted. Such interventions target the judgment of one's own knowledge that, in our data, most strongly predicted each use decision.

The dissociation between chatbot use and full delegation also suggests that these behaviors warrant different pedagogical responses. Interactive chatbot use, which declined over trials and was associated with higher cognitive ability, may represent a form of active learning amenable to instructional scaffolding. Full reliance, which remained stable and was predicted by attitudes rather than ability, may instead require interventions addressing students' beliefs about automation or their willingness to engage effortfully with difficult material. Policies that prohibit all AI use may sacrifice the learning benefits of strategic help-seeking, while policies that permit unrestricted access provide no guidance for students who default to delegation. Instead, policies could specify the times where AI consultation is permitted. For example, instructors might permit AI use during initial exploration of a topic but require independent work on summative assessments or allow chatbot interaction but not full delegation.

As AI tools become more capable, the question of when to seek assistance will only grow in importance. Developing students' capacity to make these judgments wisely may prove more durable than any specific policy governing a particular tool.

4.4 Limitations

Our controlled paradigm differs from naturalistic AI use, which involves open-ended assignments, iterative dialogue, variable reliability, and social dynamics around appropriate use.

However, laboratory studies aim to test whether theorized processes can occur under controlled conditions rather than generalize directly to real-world settings (Mook, 1983). Existing observational studies cannot determine when and why individuals freely choose AI assistance because they either assign AI use or confound task characteristics with use decisions. Our paradigm enabled the variance to be partitioned by holding difficulty, domain, and AI accuracy constant and allowing participants to choose the response mode on every trial. Naturalistic research examining whether these predictors operate similarly in graded coursework and extended projects represents the logical next step.

Additionally, our simulated chatbot with a fixed 70% accuracy rate may not capture how students interact with commercial AI systems whose reliability varies across queries and topical domains. We set accuracy at 70% because automation below roughly this level tends to impair rather than aid performance (Wickens & Dixon, 2007), and because moderate reliability likely reflects what students encounter. Chatbot accuracy varies widely by task: current models exceed 70% on standard multiple-choice benchmarks (OpenAI, 2023), yet GPT-4 fabricated 28.6% of references when replicating systematic review searches (Chelli et al., 2024), and more than half of ChatGPT's answers to programming questions contained errors (Kabir et al., 2024). Frontier models answered fewer than 40% of challenging factual questions correctly (Wei et al., 2024), and reliability is uneven across tasks in ways users cannot easily anticipate (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026). Even at 70%, the AI simulated in this study was far more accurate than unaided students on these questions (39%; Section 2.4), so deferring to AI was beneficial on average but not on questions a student knew. However, our use of 70% reliability places limits on the generalizability of our central result. A current model would likely answer our questions more accurately, and a near-ceiling AI gives little reason to weigh one's own knowledge on each trial, making use more uniform and trait-like. The roughly equal split between within-and betweenperson variance is thus specific to a moderately reliable AI (such as the one used here). Future research should vary AI reliability systematically and examine how students adjust their helpseeking as they learn a system's strengths and limitations (Lee & See, 2004).

Finally, the absence of real academic consequences may have also affected behavior; more motivated examinees, such as those facing consequences for their scores, outperform less motivated ones by about 0.6 SD (Wise & DeMars, 2005), and students in classes perceived to emphasize avoiding poor performance more often avoid help or seek expedient help (Karabenick, 2004), an emphasis that grades are likely to strengthen. capacity and attention control, and their relationship to higher-order cognition and real-world performance.
