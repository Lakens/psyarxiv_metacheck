Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Boredom proneness has been linked to a disrupted relationship with one's internal states, yet the mechanism underlying this disruption remains unclear. One possibility is that this disruption reflects a difficulty in translating interoceptive signals into meaningful predictions about internal states, rather than a basic failure to detect them. Across two studies, we examined whether this disrupted relationship reflects interpretive challenges, a deficit in cardiac interoceptive accuracy, or both. Study 1 (N = 124) was a direct replication confirming that highly boredom prone individuals report attending intensely to bodily sensations while struggling to interpret them -a pattern that held across a combined sample of 350 adults and yielded moderate-to-strong correlations with established measures of self-focused attention and interoceptive sensory confusion. Study 2 (N = 121) administered a heartbeat counting task to determine whether this disrupted relationship was due to deficient interoceptive processing or interpretive failure. Boredom proneness was unrelated to objective heartbeat counting accuracy but remained associated with self-reported interoceptive sensory confusion. Converging with Study 1, this pattern suggests that boredom proneness is best characterized by a difficulty in interpreting internal signals than by a basic interoceptive perceptual deficit. Together, these findings are consistent with predictive processing accounts of boredom proneness where those high in trait boredom struggle to generate meaningful predictions from interoceptive cues. Whether this dissociation between self-reported and objective cardiac interoception generalizes across different measurement approaches and populations remains an open question that future work could examine using more sensitive interoceptive tasks and trial-level prediction error metrics.

Interoceptive Processing in Boredom Proneness: Sensing Without Making Sense

Boredom is a negatively valenced experience born of the feeling of wanting but failing to engage successfully with the world (Goldberg et al., 2011; Van Tilburg & Igou, 2011). Those who experience the state more frequently and intensely are said to be boredom prone, which is associated with a wide range of generally negative consequences ranging from poor attention control (Carriere et al., 2008; Cheyne et al., 2006; Hunter & Eastwood, 2018; Malkovsky et al., 2012; Tze et al., 2016) to poor mental well-being (e.g., higher rates of depression and anxiety; Farmer & Sundberg, 1986; Goldberg et al., 2011; Mercer-Lynn et al., 2013). Behaviourally, boredom is cast as a self-regulatory mechanism, prompting exploration to find a more satisfying activity (Bench & Lench, 2013; Bieleke et al., 2023; Danckert, 2019; Danckert & Elpidorou, 2023; Tam et al., 2021; Westgate & Wilson, 2018; Wolff & Martarelli, 2020). Recent work has also characterized state boredom as a signal of deviation from optimal engagement (Trudel et al., 2025), a response that is both reactive and proactive to internal (e.g., bodily signals) and external (e.g., a monotonous context) exigencies.

Trait boredom proneness has been associated with a disrupted relationship with one's own internal states, though the mechanism underlying that disruption remains unclear. That is, highly boredom prone individuals report greater levels of alexithymia -difficulty labelling affective states (Eastwood et al., 2007) -and a pronounced self-directed focus coupled with poor selfknowledge (Bambrah et al., 2023). Consistent with this, the embodied representation of boredom varies considerably across individuals, suggesting that accurately representing the experienced sensation of boredom is quite challenging (Hartmann et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). This accords with a broader failure to identify a consistent physiological profile for state boredom (Merrifield & Danckert, 2014; Stempfer et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings suggest that boredom proneness is not merely a matter of how often or intensely boredom is experienced but reflects a more fundamental difficulty in reading and interpreting one's own internal states to effectively regulate goal pursuit.

In addition, neuroimaging work has highlighted the involvement of the anterior insular cortex in the experience of boredom (Dal Mas & Wittmann, 2017; Danckert & Merrifield, 2018; see Drody et al., 2024, for a review of the neuroimaging literature). The insular cortex is the primary substrate for interoception -the processing of internal bodily signals that are ultimately mapped onto subjective feeling states (Craig, 2009; Namkung et al., 2018). The insula is also part of the salience network, which determines which of the multitude of internal and external stimuli require further processing (Menon & Uddin, 2010). Previous studies indicate that both functions are involved in boredom. While Dal Mas and Wittmann (2017) found increased anterior insula activation during attempts to re-engage with the environment in response to being bored, Danckert and Merrifield (2018) observed anticorrelated insula activity when boredom was induced, perhaps indicative of unsuccessful attempts to engage with the boring situation.

Considered jointly, these findings suggest that the insula is upregulated when searching for a solution to boredom and downregulated during a boredom episode. Lesion work further demonstrates that anterior insula damage produces marked alexithymia, implicating this region specifically in the capacity to read and interpret affective states (Hogeveen et al., 2016).

Consistent with this neural evidence, recent work from our group found that highly boredom prone individuals attend inordinately to interoceptive sensations but struggle to make sense of them (Trudel et al., 2024) -a pattern that mirrors the interpretive failure associated with insula dysfunction. Understanding interoceptive signals may therefore be essential for maintaining both affective (Barrett et al., 2016) and cognitive (Trudel et al., 2025) homeostasis by informing self-regulatory processes and accurately predicting the outcomes of intended actions. Thus, a chronic failure to decode these inner cues may be a key contributor to trait boredom proneness.

Purpose of the Current Investigation

We report data from two studies that build on these previous findings. Study 1 is a direct replication of Trudel and colleagues (2024), examining the association between boredom proneness and two facets of interoceptive processing: heightened self-focused awareness and interoceptive sensory confusion. Replicating this pattern would strengthen the notion that boredom prone individuals are acutely oriented toward their own internal states yet struggle to make sense of them.

Study 2 examines the hypothesis that misinterpreting interoceptive cues may contribute to trait boredom proneness. There are two possible explanations for the interoceptive dysfunction reported by individuals prone to boredom. First, boredom proneness could stem from a deficit in interoceptive accuracy (i.e., an impaired ability to accurately perceive internal bodily signals).

Such a deficit would make it difficult for these individuals to assign meaning to their affective states. Second, boredom proneness may result from a challenge in interpreting interoceptive signals rather than a failure to accurately represent such signals. In this case, internal signals may be accurately detected but poorly translated into coherent emotional states. To adjudicate between these accounts, we administered a heartbeat counting task as an index of objective cardiac interoceptive accuracy (Schandry, 1981). If boredom proneness reflects a detection deficit, accuracy on the heartbeat task should decrease as boredom proneness increases. If instead, boredom proneness reflects an interpretive failure, it should be associated with greater interoceptive sensory confusion independent of heartbeat detection accuracy.

Measures

The study included the following eight surveys.

Shortened Boredom Proneness Scale (BPS)

The eight-item Short Boredom Proneness Scale (BPS-SF; Struk et al., 2017) measured boredom proneness-i.e., the propensity for an individual to desire, but fail, to engage in a satisfying activity (α = .89). This scale was developed as a shorter, single-factor version of Farmer and Sundberg's Boredom Proneness Scale (1986). Participants rated items such as 'I don't feel motivated by most things that I do' using a 5-point scale (1=strongly disagree to5=strongly agree), with a higher total score indicating greater boredom proneness.

Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20)

The 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale-20 (TAS-20; Bagby et al., 1994) measured the difficulty identifying and describing feelings (α = .87). Participants rated items such as 'I have feelings that I can't quite identify' using a 5-point scale (1=strongly disagree to5=strongly agree), with a higher total score indicating greater difficulties with identifying and describing one's feelings.

Brief Self-Control Scale (BSCS)

The 13-item Brief Self-Control Scale (BSCS, Tangney et al., 2004) measured behaviours that involve self-control such as restraint and self-discipline (α = .87). Participants rated items such as 'I wish I had more self-discipline' using a 5-point Likert scale (1=not at all like me to5=very much like me), with a higher score indicating higher levels of self-control. Negatively phrased items were reverse scored to maintain scoring consistency.

Body Awareness Questionnaire (BAQ)

The 18-item Body Awareness Questionnaire (BAQ; Shields et al., 1989) measured selfreported attentiveness to body processes such as sensitivity to body cycles and small changes in normal functioning (α = .86). Participants rated items such as 'I notice specific bodily reactions to being over-hungry' using a 7-point scale (1=not at all true of me to7=very true of me), with a higher score indicating higher attentiveness to normal body processes.

Interoceptive Accuracy Scale (IAS)

The 21-item Interoceptive Accuracy Scale (IAS; Murphy et al., 2019) measured perceived accuracy of representing internal states (α = .91). Participants rated items such as 'I can always accurately perceive when I am hungry' using a 5-point scale (1=strongly disagree to5=strongly agree), with a higher score indicating greater self-reported interoceptive accuracy.

Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA-2)

The 37-item Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA-2; to maintain scoring consistency. Item 29 was missing from the survey in this sample due to an input error on our part. We used the scores from the 28 remaining items and although this meant the scale was not deployed as intended, results demonstrated that it did not correlate with boredom proneness and is unlikely to do so with the addition of the final item.

Self-Awareness Questionnaire (SAQ)

The 28-item Self-Awareness Questionnaire (SAQ; Longarzo et al., 2015) measured interoceptive awareness for commonly felt bodily sensations (α = .93). In contrast to the Interoceptive Accuracy Scale, this scale asks direct questions about immediate experience (e.g., 'I feel sudden thirst pangs'), whereas the Interoceptive Accuracy Scale asks what could be considered prospective questions (i.e., anticipating accuracy of experiences; 'I can always accurately perceive when I am thirsty'). Participants rated how often they experience each statement using a 5-point scale (1=never;2=sometimes;3=often;4=very often;5=always), with higher scores indicating higher levels of self-awareness related to bodily sensations.

Interoceptive Sensory Questionnaire (ISQ)

The Interoceptive Sensory Questionnaire (ISQ; Fiene et al., 2018) is a 20-item self-report questionnaire intended to measure interoceptive challenges (i.e., confusion in rating or labelling interoceptive experiences) in autistic adults (α = .96). Participants rated items such as 'Sometimes I don't know how to interpret sensations I feel within my body' using a 7-point Likert scale (1=not true at all of me,7=very true of me), with higher scores indicating more difficulty registering or interpreting interoceptive sensations. Three items were reverse scored to maintain scoring consistency.

Study 2 -Method

To determine whether boredom proneness was associated with a decrease in objective cardiac interoceptive accuracy, we conducted a direct test using a well-established heartbeat counting task (Hickman et al., 2020; Schandry, 1981). Given the results of the prior work replicated here in Study 1, the hypothesis was that boredom prone individuals would struggle to make sense of their interoceptive cues despite being acutely aware of them. This hypothesis suggests two possibilities: (i) a signal-detection failure by which people who are prone to boredom have diminished accuracy in perceiving internal sensations, including their heartbeats; or (ii) an interpretive failure, which posits that the connection between boredom proneness and interoception involves meaning-making deficits rather than poor interoceptive perception.

Objective heartbeat accuracy provides a critical test of these accounts. Reduced heartbeat detection accuracy among the highly boredom prone would support the signal detection account.

Alternatively, if heartbeat detection accuracy is unrelated to boredom proneness despite selfreported interoceptive sensory confusion, the evidence would support the interpretive account.

Measures

The scales of interest reported in the replication above were completed online by the participants as a prerequisite to the in-person study: A baseline measure of heart rate was obtained by instructing participants to close their eyes for two minutes and open them again when they heard a beep. On-screen prompts then directed them to report the number of heartbeats perceived in a practice trial followed by three distinct time intervals (25, 35 and 45 seconds) presented in ascending order for all participants.

One beep indicated when to start counting their heartbeats, and another indicated when to stop counting. Participants were instructed to press the space bar to begin, triggering a three-second countdown prompt ("Start counting your heartbeats in 3, 2, 1") before the active trial prompt ("Count your heartbeats"). The actual number of heartbeats occurring during each trial was recorded and compared to the number reported by each participant as a measure of accuracy.

General Discussion

The results of Study 1 replicated prior work and supported the hypothesis that people who are highly prone to boredom tend to focus intently on their internal bodily sensations but struggle to make sense of them. When combined (i.e., data from Trudel et al., 2024 and the two samples tested here), the pooled adult sample (N = 471) yields moderate-to-strong positive correlations between trait boredom and an excessive focus on internal states coupled with the struggle to interpret those very states. Correlations with other measures (e.g., Body Awareness Questionnaire) used in the original work (see Supplementary Material S1) were not replicated in the current sample (Table 1). This inconsistency may suggest that these measures do not function as reliable indices of a single underlying construct but rather capture different facets of interoceptive processing. However, the Self-Awareness Questionnaire (SAQ) and Interoceptive Sensory Questionnaire (ISQ) target precisely the two components of our central argument: that boredom prone individuals struggle at the interpretive stage of interoception despite attending well, or perhaps excessively, to their bodily experiences. The SAQ taps into the sensing side with higher scores indicating a heightened, recurring tendency to direct attention inward. This is consistent with earlier research showing that elevated self-focus does not necessarily correspond to improved self-knowledge (Bambrah et al., 2023). In contrast, the ISQ addresses the lack of clarity, reflecting a particular challenge in making sense of inner experiences, with participants reporting confusion or overlap in their interpretation of bodily sensations. If boredom proneness is characterized by increased internal focus and confused interpretation rather than a general interoceptive perceptual deficit, then only measures capturing these specific features should consistently correlate with trait boredom across samples as they did here (see Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Material S1).

Results from Study 2 did not find evidence that boredom proneness was associated with reduced accuracy in perceiving interoceptive signals, at least in the domain of heartbeat counting.

Although highly boredom prone individuals reported struggling to make sense of their interoceptive cues despite attending to them -relations evident across three separate samples now -objective heartbeat estimates did not vary significantly as a function of boredom proneness. These results suggest that boredom proneness may be more closely tied to how individuals attend to and interpret internal signals, rather than detection fidelity.

In Trudel and colleagues (2024) study, interoceptive sensory confusion (ISQ) and selfawareness (SAQ) together explained an additional 5% of variance in boredom proneness after controlling for other factors known to correlate with the trait (i.e., alexithymia, self-control, and body awareness). Here, we chose a more conservative covariate set (age, sex, and cardiac interoceptive accuracy) as a neutral baseline model to provide a direct test of our hypothesis and observed a substantially larger increment (ΔR² = .23). Notably, cardiac interoceptive accuracy did not predict boredom proneness, indicating that the observed increment was specifically driven by self-reported interoceptive measures rather than objective performance. The dissociation between objective (i.e., heartbeat counting accuracy) and self-report measures of interoception aligns with a growing body of evidence suggesting these dimensions are only weakly related and may reflect distinct physiological and metacognitive processes (Forkmann et al., 2016; Garfinkel et al., 2015).

Study 2 aimed to measure the connections between trait boredom proneness and interoceptive processing given the latter is deemed important for maintaining a cognitive set point (Craig, 2009; Trudel et al., 2025; Tsakiris & Critchley, 2016). That is, accurately representing interoceptive signals likely plays an important role in affective regulation and in the anticipation of what may or may not constitute an engaging activity (Barrett, 2017; Danckert & Elpidorou, 2023). This approach was grounded in neuroimaging evidence linking insular activation to state boredom (Dal Mas & Wittmann, 2017; Danckert & Merrifield, 2018). Other neuroimaging work has linked the posterior insula to processing afferent interoceptive cues such as cardiac signals, and the anterior insula to the integration of these interoceptive signals with exteroceptive sensory information (Nguyen et al., 2016). Paired with the notion that the anterior insula is involved in the subjective experience of emotions (Craig, 2009), it seemed plausible that trait boredom proneness would be linked to differences in interoceptive abilities. This interpretation is consistent with predictive processing accounts of boredom, in which the subjective experience reflects a failure to generate meaningful predictions about one's internal and external environment, rather than a deficit in raw interoceptive input (Ahlich & Rancourt, 2022; Darling, 2023; Westgate & Wilson, 2018).

Constraints on Generality and Recommendations

The primary constructs of interest examined here were boredom proneness, self-awareness, and interoceptive sensory confusion. However, negative affect is a well-established correlate of symptom perception and self-report (Pennebaker, 1982; Watson & Pennebaker, 1989; Van den Bergh et al., 2017). It is possible that shared variance, rather than a specific relationship, underlies the observed associations between boredom proneness and the our measures of interoceptive processing. To account for this possibility, future work could include the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988) or the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scales (DASS-21; Henry & Crawford, 2005) to use as covariates to control for negative affect across the self-report measures.

In addition, the heartbeat counting task used here may lack the sensitivity to detect differences that are well captured by self-report. A parallel problem has been documented in self-control research, where work exploring the ontology of self-control showed no relation between self-report measures and task metrics (Eisenberg et al., 2019). Furthermore, when using either domain to predict real-world outcomes, it was only self-report measures that did a moderate job of prediction (Eisenberg et al., 2019). This may stem from several factors, most prominently the sensitivity of the metrics used. To address the possibility that the task used here was simply not sensitive enough to detect differences associated with boredom proneness, future work could use a heart rate phase adjustment task (Plans et al., 2021) or a heart rate discrimination task (Legrand et al., 2022). Though pragmatic, silent heartbeat counting is prone to response biases, such as prior knowledge of one's resting heart rate and a reliance on non-interoceptive strategies like time estimation, both of which contribute to a substantial portion of variance in heartbeat counting scores under standard task instructions (Desmedt et al., 2020). Without a dedicated time-estimation control task, it remains possible that individual differences in heartbeat counting performance partly reflect timing strategy rather than cardiac interoceptive accuracy specifically. Given that boredom may slow the experience of time (Wolff et al., 2025) and that highly boredom prone individuals tend to overestimate durations (Danckert & Allman, 2005), we would expect such strategy to produce either an overestimation or, at least, less underestimation of heart rate as a function of boredom proneness. However, our findings did not support this prediction. Nevertheless, the heart rate phase adjustment task may be more robust to both physiological and strategic confounds of this kind.

Relatedly, different cardiac interoception tasks may not be interchangeable. A metaanalysis of 22 studies found only a weak relationship between accuracy scores on heartbeat counting and heartbeat discrimination tasks, and no association with interoceptive awareness (Hickman et al., 2020). Recently, a direct comparison revealed that while heartbeat counting correlated moderately with the cardiovascular signal detection task, multi-interval discrimination was unrelated to either. Notably, only one of 73 participants was classified as a heartbeat perceiver by all three methods (Ferentzi et al., 2025). Together, these findings suggest that cardiac interoceptive measures index at least partly distinct abilities, raising the possibility that the present null findings are specific to the heartbeat counting task rather than to cardiac interoception more broadly.

One further constraint of these findings relates to the organ-specific nature of interoceptive processes. Although Herbert and colleagues (2012) linked cardiac awareness to gastric sensitivity, Garfinkel and colleagues (2016) reported a dissociation between cardiac and respiratory accuracy alongside a positive relationship between their awareness metrics. This suggests that task performance is domain-specific while metacognitive insight may generalize across domains.

Banellis and colleagues (2026) recently extended this pattern in a large-scale psychophysical study (N = 241). They reported a modest positive correlation for self-reported confidence but no crossmodality correlation for sensitivity, precision or metacognitive accuracy between cardiac and respiratory tasks. These findings raise the possibility that subjective interoceptive sensory confusion, assuming it behaves similarly to confidence and awareness measures, may be more domain general. In contrast, objective interoceptive accuracy may be task specific. Whether the dissociation between self-reported and objective cardiac interoception observed here in relation to trait boredom proneness holds across other interoceptive domains remains to be tested directly.

It is also plausible that there are distinct roles of the anterior and posterior insular cortex (Nguyen et al., 2016). That is, the heartbeat counting exercise may depend more on the posterior insula, given the role this region plays in primary interoceptive perception (Craig, 2009). In contrast, the subjective, conscious interpretation of interoceptive signals, and the integration of those signals with external contingencies, likely depend more on the anterior insular cortex (Craig, 2009). In other words, trait boredom proneness may stem from differences in how the brain integrates interoceptive signals to represent emotions such as boredom in order to predict the best way to alleviate the discomfort, rather than from differences in how those interoceptive cues are perceived per se.

Lastly, other compensatory mechanisms may be at play to offset the perceived struggle highly boredom prone individuals have in making sense of interoceptive cues. An alternative explanation of these results could be linked to previous research suggesting that cardiac interoceptive accuracy plays a role primarily in how intensely emotions are experienced (Wiens et al., 2000). That is, good heartbeat detectors reported feeling emotions more intensely. While the causal direction of this relationship remains unclear, feeling boredom more intensely than most (a cardinal sign of boredom proneness) coupled with heightened self-awareness, may boost heartbeat counting performance for individuals experiencing chronic boredom, even when they report interoceptive sensory confusion. Perhaps these features of chronic boredom -elevated emotional intensity and heightened self-focus -are sufficient to obscure group differences in objective interoceptive accuracy as measured here on the heartbeat counting task.

Future versions of the task could incorporate trial-by-trial confidence ratings, in which participants predict their own accuracy. The discrepancy between predicted and actual accuracy would yield an interoceptive prediction error score -a metric that could more directly index the interpretive failures implicated in boredom proneness (see Garfinkel et al., 2015 for an example of this approach). Building on this, further research could investigate whether highly boredom prone individuals differ from their low boredom prone counterparts in other aspects of how they generate and update predictions about their own interoceptive performance. More broadly, high boredom proneness may involve a general failure to reconcile prediction errors by updating engagement expectations based on feedback. Alternatively, it may reflect a narrower range of predictions, where the highly boredom prone report similarly confident estimates despite performance fluctuations. Either pattern would extend the predictive processing account introduced earlier to the specific case of interoceptive performance monitoring, indicating a failure to generate or update accurate predictions about one's own bodily signals rather than the external world. Since self-regulation relies on interoceptive signals to anticipate which activities will resolve an unsatisfying state, an interpretive failure-rather than a failure to detect those signals-may explain the inability of the highly boredom prone to translate bodily cues into effective exploratory actions.
