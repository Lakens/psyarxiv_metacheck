Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Full text

Abstract

Auditory environments can substantially influence wellbeing and quality of life for Autistic people. Autistic auditory profiles can include variations in sound sensitivity which can include difficulties filtering out background noise, and experiences that are not adequately captured by standard audiological testing. Clinical auditory assessments are traditionally designed to evaluate hearing function, with a particular emphasis on hearing thresholds, rather than considering the broader contextual factors that shape listening experiences. Additionally, little is known about how Autistic adults seek support for auditory challenges, experience auditory assessments, or how these experiences may inform more accessible hearing-health and auditory research practices.

To explore how Autistic adults experience auditory assessment processes across clinical settings and research contexts, identify barriers and facilitators to positive experiences and outcomes, and develop experience-informed recommendations to improve the accessibility of hearing health and auditory assessment practices.

Data were drawn from two existing studies examining auditory experiences among Autistic adults. Directed qualitative content analysis was conducted on open-text responses from 85 Autistic adults who had sought professional or therapeutic support for auditory challenges. A separate In-Person Research Participation Survey captured the experiences of eight Autistic adults who completed in-person audiology testing and a listening task, using quantitative ratings and open-text responses analysed using descriptive statistics and inductive content analysis.

Analysis of support-seeking accounts identified three broad domains: Barriers, Recommendations, and Independent Strategies. Participants described barriers to accessing and implementing support, recommendations from specialists that varied in their perceived helpfulness, and strategies they identified through community or developed themselves. For the In-Person Research Participation Survey; quantitative ratings were concentrated toward the upper end of the five-point agreement scale. Open-text responses identified three domains-Accessibility, Emotional Toll, and Experience Benefit-highlighting the importance of clear and consistent communication, sensory accessibility, opportunities for choice and control, and the demands associated with sustained listening and concentration.

Introduction

Autistic people frequently report everyday auditory environments as challenging despite "clinically normal hearing thresholds" (Ceponiene et al., 2003; Demopoulos & Lewine, 2016; Dunn et al., 2021; Rosenhall et al., 1999). This is because conventional audiological assessments primarily evaluate hearing sensitivity, that is, the quietest sound a person can detect across a range of frequencies. Such measures capture very little of how sound is experienced or processed in daily life, but they remain useful in detecting hearing loss that has been reported more often in Autistic people than in the non-autistic "general population" (Caballero-Borrego et al., 2025; Demopoulos & Lewine, 2016; Rosenhall et al., 1999), with whole-country population data indicating that one in seven Autistic adults reported hearing loss (Rydzewska et al., 2019). Beyond hearing thresholds, Autistic people experience substantial heterogeneity in auditory processing and responses to sound (Poulsen et al., 2024). These experiences can include diagnosable auditory conditions, such as hyperacusis, misophonia, phonophobia, and sound-induced pain (Danesh et al., 2025; Poulsen et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2021), as well as differences in listening and processing under complex auditory conditions, including environments with competing background noise (Alcántara et al., 2004; Li et al., 2026; Ruiz Callejo & Boets, 2023). The way auditory information is processed and experienced is shaped by characteristics of the acoustic environment (Hernández-Pérez et al., 2025; Traer & McDermott, 2016) and listening contexts (de Hoz & McAlpine, 2024; Hernández-Pérez et al., 2021), and for Autistic people this also includes the predictability and complexity of auditory information (Gómez et al., 2014; J.-J. Hsieh et al., 2022; Kanakri et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2021). Taken together, these auditory experiences can substantially affect everyday life and wellbeing (Poulsen et al., 2025) and may lead Autistic people to seek professional hearing health support. The way in which auditory assessments are conducted may also shape the experiences and outcomes of Autistic people, as clinical and healthcare settings may not be ideal for many Autistic people (Black et al., 2022; Rosas-Pérez et al., 2025; Strömberg et al., 2022). Healthcare settings more broadly are challenging for Autistic people to access, with Autistic adults reporting more and different barriers to healthcare than adults with different support needs (Raymaker et al., 2017). These include challenges making appointments by telephone, communicating with providers and reception staff, feeling misunderstood, and navigating services, as well as sensory challenges such as noise, crowding, and bright lights in waiting rooms (Doherty et al., 2022; Raymaker et al., 2017). Sensory unpredictability and limited control over the immediate environment, including sounds, odours, and wayfinding, can further impede communication in clinical settings (Strömberg et al., 2022). In hearing health and audiology, current clinical environments and assessment approaches may not adequately recognise, measure, or accommodate the heterogeneity of Autistic auditory experiences (DuBois et al., 2017). Clinicians have been encouraged to move beyond traditional test batteries focused mainly on "hearing thresholds" to document the listening challenges Autistic people report, including through questionnaires (Schafer et al., 2022). Behavioural assessments of 'hearing' can also place substantial demands on Autistic people, including remaining still for extended periods, wearing headphones that may be uncomfortable, following instructions delivered in a particular way, and sustaining responses to repeated tones. When these demands do not fit a person's sensory, attentional, or communication needs, they can become barriers that prevent an assessment from being completed, and may reflect the assessment format rather than the person's hearing or ability (Krempley & Schmidt, 2021; Marzi et al., 2025). Auditory assessments and hearing health practices therefore need to be responsive to diverse auditory profiles, and to the settings and procedures through which they are delivered. Audiological assessments and laboratory-based auditory research share many features, with both commonly requiring participants to complete structured listening tasks within standardized and highly controlled environments. While standardization is important for clinical and scientific rigour, these procedures and environments may not accommodate the diversity of sensory, communication, and information-processing needs among Autistic people, thus hindering their ability to receive comprehensive, individualised care. Features such as unfamiliar or unpredictable environments, time pressures, sustained listening demands, bright lighting and busy clinics, inflexible task or response requirements, and limited opportunities for clarification, breaks, or choice and control may create barriers to participation (Johnson et al., 2022; Mason et al., 2019; Stein Duker et al., 2025; Strömberg et al., 2022). Navigating these neuronormative environments and practices-a closely related concept, with neuronormativity referring to the set of standards, expectations and norms that determine whose ways of being are valued and whose get pathologized (Walker, 2021)-may contribute to sensory overload, stress, and cognitive fatigue (MacLennan et al., 2022 (MacLennan et al., , 2023;; Rapaport et al., 2024), particularly when responsibility is placed on the Autistic person themselves to communicate or advocate for their needs (de Marchena et al., 2025; Nicolaidis et al., 2015; Pellicano et al., 2022; Santhanam & Gurbuz, 2026). Understanding the accessibility of auditory assessments, whether in-lab or research-focused, requires consideration not only of whether assessments capture the auditory experiences relevant to Autistic people, but also of how the assessment process itself is experienced across both clinical and research contexts, and how this may influence accuracy. These accessibility considerations have implications within auditory research. Autistic and other neurodivergent people remain underrepresented in laboratory-based perceptual and sensory research (Elise et al., 2025) and have been historically disenfranchised in research contexts (Beresford et al., 2004; Cascio et al., 2020; Pellicano & Stears, 2011). Barriers within research environments and procedures may affect not only whether people choose or are able to participate, but also their experiences during participation (Ashworth et al., 2021; Lory, 2019), meaning the research is not capturing a true representation of their auditory profile (Bendo et al., 2024; Wright et al., 2026). Having inaccessible in-lab research environments may contribute to difficulties with recruitment and retention and, in turn, limit the representativeness of research samples (Le Cunff et al., 2024). Improving the accessibility of auditory research is therefore important both for the experiences of participants and for the validity, inclusivity, and broader relevance of the evidence generated.

To achieve this, the present study draws on two complementary datasets examining Autistic people's experiences across professional support-seeking and auditory research contexts. We asked two primary research questions: (1) How do Autistic adults describe their experiences of seeking professional support for auditory challenges, including what barriers were encountered, recommendations received, and strategies used to support their auditory needs? and (2) How do Autistic adults experience the accessibility and acceptability of participating in an in-person audiology assessment and auditory research task?

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Data were drawn from two independent surveys conducted as part of a broader program of research into the hearing health experiences of Autistic people. The first, the Auditory Environments (AEs) survey (Poulsen et al., 2025), included questions addressing the experiences of Autistic people seeking professional support for their hearing health, from which relevant items were selected for analysis here and are referred to hereafter as "Support Seeking for Hearing Challenges". The second, the Research Participation Feedback survey, was administered as a separate activity from the primary auditory research being performed (not part of this study) and was designed to gather feedback on participants' experiences of taking part in an in-person listening task. This included feedback about the experiment and information provided prior to and during the experimental session, as well as feedback on the session itself. These data are referred to hereafter as In-Person Research Participation. Both surveys were approved by the Macquarie University Human Research Ethics Committee (Ethics Application Number: 52024563055582).

Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges Questions.

Data were part of an online survey of 296 Autistic adults hosted on REDCap and conducted in 2022 (Poulsen et al., 2025). The survey collected demographic information and information about participants' hearing and auditory experiences, and experiences of seeking support for auditory challenges. For the present analysis, participants were identified based on their response to a survey item asking whether they had sought therapeutic or professional support for their auditory challenges. Of the 296 participants in the broader survey, 104 reported having sought support in some capacity. Participants were then given the opportunity to elaborate on their support-seeking experiences in an open-text response to the question: 1) When seeking support for your sound sensitivities or other auditory issues, what treatments, strategies, or advice did you find helpful?

2) What did you find unhelpful or potentially harmful? Of the 104 participants who reported seeking support, 85 used the open-text box to provide additional information about their experiences. These 85 responses comprised the qualitative data analysed in the present study. Participants were aged 18-72 (Mean = 37.66 years, SD = 10.76). Of the 104 participants, 85 (81.7%) reported a clinical autism diagnosis, 16 (15.4%) self-identified as Autistic without a clinical diagnosis, and three (2.9%) preferred not to report whether they were clinically diagnosed or self-identified. Self-identified Autistic participants were included in recognition of documented systemic barriers to accessing formal autism diagnosis, including gender-and socioeconomic-related inequities (Ardeleanu et al., 2025; Botha et al., 2020; English et al., 2021; Kapp et al., 2013). Further participant characteristics, including gender, ethnicity, and co-occurring conditions, are presented in Table 1.

In-Person Research Participation Survey

Participants were recruited on a voluntary basis to attend an in-person psychoacoustic experiment on the perception of natural sounds, between 2024 and 2026. Interest was expressed via email, following this each participant received an 'Information Pack' designed to ensure participants had details about the study procedures and auditory stimuli in advance, supporting informed consent and preparedness prior to attending the in-lab session (Khatiwada et al., 2026; Maun et al., 2024). The introductory email outlined the three stages of participation: (1) an Auditory Experiences (AE) survey, completed prior to the in-person experiment via a REDCap link, which collected background information about the participant and their auditory experiences (Poulsen et al., 2025); (2) an in-person audiology assessment and listening task conducted at the Australian Hearing Hub at Macquarie University; and (3) a brief bespoke post-In Person Research Participation survey to capture the participant's experience of the in-person session (data analysed here) also completed from home via a REDCap link.

As part of the 'Information Pack' participants then received an Experimental Instructions document (Supplemental Information1) via email. This document was structured to guide participants through each step of participation. It began by introducing the study as an Autisticled project, a project description and introduced the research team. Pre-session logistics were covered next, including how scheduling would be confirmed by email and when the In-Person Research Participation Survey (Supplemental Information_2) link would be provided. A dedicated section addressed arrival at the in-person session (in words and with photos), providing the Hearing Hub's location, parking instructions (including the visitor intercom process), public transport directions from the nearest Metro station, and a designated meeting point in the building lobby, with an alternative contact method (text message) if that location did not meet the participant's sensory and emotional needs. The document then described the testing room and consent process, noting that the room would remain unlocked throughout (allowing participants to leave at any time), that a camera would be used for researcher monitoring, and that a support person was welcome to attend. This was followed by a description of the hearing screening procedure (an otoscopic check and pure-tone audiometric testing, with a choice of response method: button press, hand raise, or verbal), and then the experimental listening task itself. The document concluded with information on the end of the session and compensation (AUD $20/hour, provided via a multi-retailer e-voucher link, as compensation for their time).

The 'Information Pack' also included samples of the sounds participants would hear in the inperson research participation session, allowing them to experience the stimuli beforehand. The listening task required participants to make same/different judgements about short fragments of natural sounds (bubbling water, crackling fire, and chirping insects), presented in three blocks, one per sound category. The task lasted approximately 30-40 minutes in total, with breaks offered between blocks (Hernandez-Perez et al., in prep).

The development of this 'Information Pack' reflects the adoption of a neuro-affirming approach to reduce uncertainty and sensory/social friction (Benton & Johnson, 2014), rather than an ethical disclosure of experimental requirements (Khatiwada et al., 2026). This approach was informed not only by the literature but also directly by the lived experience and input of the anonymous Autistic advisor. Seventeen participants completed the in-person session and were invited to complete the feedback survey. Nine participants chose to complete the survey. One response was excluded because the participant, who was not a member of the research team at the time of data collection, subsequently joined the research team and is an author of the present study. This response was excluded to avoid a potential conflict of interest, resulting in eight responses included in the analysis. As completion of the post-participation survey was not a requirement for reimbursement, it is possible that some participants chose not to complete it, or simply forgot to do so.

Following the in-person research participation session, participants were sent an email containing their reimbursement voucher along with a link to the In-Person Research Participation survey (REDCap link). Beyond this email, only one follow-up reminder regarding the In-Person Research Participation survey was sent, in order to minimise the risk of participant fatigue or the perception of coercion within the community. Participation in the feedback survey was presented as a separate activity from the primary research task, and it was made explicit that participants would not receive follow-up reminders and that their decision to complete (or not complete) the survey would not affect their relationship with the research team or the ongoing project. The survey was estimated to take 15-20 minutes and comprised predominantly Likert-style agreement items (5-point scale: Definitely Disagree to Definitely Agree), and open-ended free-text prompts allowing participants to elaborate on their responses. All open-text response items within the feedback survey were mandatory fields, meaning participants were required to provide a response in order to proceed through the survey; consequently, a number of these responses consisted of "N/A" or similar non-substantive entries where participants had no additional feedback to offer.

Data Analysis

The two datasets were analysed separately, reflecting their different research contexts and data structures. The Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges open-text responses were analysed using directed qualitative content analysis, while the In-Person Research Participation data were analysed using descriptive quantitative analysis and inductive qualitative content analysis. Qualitative analyses were conducted using NVivo (Version 15.6.0). The analytic procedures for each dataset are described below.

Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges Questions.

The 85 open-text responses to the Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges question were analysed using directed qualitative content analysis, consistent with the approach described by (H.-F. Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). An initial coding framework was developed by A.S.M. and R.P. prior to full coding which reflected the single item wording and consisted of four categories: (1) helpful recommendations, (2) unhelpful recommendations, (3) positive experiences, and (4) negative experiences. A.S.M. conducted the initial coding using this framework. Responses containing multiple distinct ideas could be assigned to more than one code. Consistent with a directed content analysis approach, coding was not constrained to the initial categories (Assarroudi et al., 2018; Hsieh & Shannon, 2005). Where content did not fit the existing framework, A.S.M. developed additional codes inductively and incorporated these into the evolving coding framework.

Following initial coding, A.S.M. examined code frequencies and grouped related codes into broader categories representing recurring patterns across participants' accounts. The developing analytic structure was reviewed and refined by H.H.P. and R.P., including reconsideration of the relationships among the original and inductively developed codes.

Coding was non-mutually exclusive, such that a participant response could contribute to more than one code or category when it contained multiple relevant ideas. Accordingly, frequencies may sum to more than 100% across categories. Participant quotations were used to illustrate the identified patterns and retain the context and intended meaning of participants' accounts.

Given the larger sample size, participants in the Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges dataset are anonymous and only identified by participant ID rather than pseudonyms.

In-Person Research Participation Survey. Data analysis

Quantitative survey responses were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 31.0.1.0). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all quantitative items. For the five-point agreement items, responses were coded from 1 (Definitely Disagree) to 5 (Definitely Agree) and means and standard deviations were calculated for each item. Responses to the three-point items assessing the amount of information provided were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Responses to the six open-text questions were analysed using inductive content analysis, informed by the analytic stages described by (Bengtsson, 2016). During decontextualization, A.S.M. familiarised themselves with the data and developed initial codes based sections of the survey questions:

1) Feedback on Participant Presentation; 2) Feedback on Information Document; 3) In-Person Session and 4) Overall Comments.

During recontextualization, A.S.M. reviewed the codes against the original responses to ensure that they accurately reflected participants' intended meaning. Related codes were then organised into broader categories, with the developing structure reviewed and refined by H.H.P. and R.P. During compilation, A.S.M., H.H.P., and R.P. reviewed the resulting analytic structure and supporting participant responses to develop the final interpretation and reporting of the findings.

Coding was non-mutually exclusive, such that responses could be assigned to more than one code or category when they contained multiple relevant ideas. Accordingly, frequencies may sum to more than 100% across categories. Responses provided to the open-text question inviting additional suggestions were coded according to their substantive content and incorporated into the relevant categories rather than retained as a separate "Additional Suggestions" category. Following coding, AI-generated pseudonyms (Kamelski & Olivos, 2025) were assigned to each participant based on their demographic information to preserve anonymity while retaining the individuality of participants' accounts (see Supplemental Information_3).

Community involvement statement

This study drew on two datasets developed with input from Autistic community members. For the AE survey, Autistic advisors contributed to research priorities, language, accessibility, item burden, recruitment, and listening task design contained in the survey. Their feedback informed the task instructions, affective response options, and presentation of study materials. For the In-Person Research Participation Survey, one anonymous Autistic advisor provided initial feedback and accessibility recommendations for research settings. The research team also includes members with lived experience of neurodivergence.

Results

Results are presented across the two complementary datasets. We first describe qualitative findings from the Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges dataset, examining participants' experiences of seeking professional support for auditory concerns. Then we provide quantitative and qualitative analysis from the In-Person Research Participation dataset to provide an overview of participants' evaluations of the research materials and in-person experience.

Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges Questions-Qualitative Findings

Analysis of participants' support-seeking accounts identified three broad domains (Figure 1): Barriers, Recommendations, and Independent Strategies. Participants' accounts also indicated relationships between these domains across the support-seeking experience. Barriers were described both in accessing professional support and in implementing recommendations once support had been obtained. Recommendations varied in their perceived helpfulness, while participants also described identifying or developing strategies themselves, either independently or alongside professional support. These patterns suggested that participants' experiences did not always follow a simple progression from seeking support to receiving a helpful outcome. The findings within each of the three domains are presented below. Please note that f refers to the frequency with which a code occurred within the analysis. Therefore, f may exceed the number of participants associated with a code if a participant contributed multiple quotes coded under that category.

Domain 1: Barriers

Once the need for support was identified, participants often experienced barriers to accessing and receiving the required support (f = 13). These barriers could prevent participants from engaging with available services; however, the most frequently discussed barrier was related to participants' ability to implement the recommended strategies. These implementation barriers suggest that even for Autistic people who were able to seek professional help for auditory concerns, the support provided was often generalised and not applicable to their individual needs: "Using earplugs or headphone[s] is difficult for me since I really don't like things touching my head/ears" (ID 337). Where recommended strategies were not feasible or could not be implemented, this may have necessitated further help-seeking to identify more appropriate strategies, or may have contributed to disengagement from further support-seeking altogether.

For others, barriers occurred at much earlier stages of the process, impacting their ability to access support before being able to visit a specialist (f = 4). Within this domain we captured discussions from participants who were actively seeking support at the time of the survey, those who were still waiting to see a specialist (f = 2), and others who were experiencing financial struggles to afford to access ongoing professional support (f = 2). For example, one participant explained: "... I have not been able to afford to go back to the specialist who ordered the test so am unsure what the outcome was..." (ID 100).

Understanding the individual's subjective experience is important when assessing their auditory needs and determining support with tangible benefits. Some participants described logistic obstacles (f = 2) or faced issues with inaccurate hearing tests (f = 3) including a lack of concern for noise interference. This was described by one participant who did not hear the tones played in the test due to the placement of the headphones over their hair: "I told the audiologist... she did not seem at all concerned, but I was because I thought the test would be inaccurate..." (ID 100). Other participants were given tests that were irrelevant for their circumstances: "I said I had difficulty hearing/understanding low frequencies... the only tests I was given [were] high pitch noises to check my hearing, and I could hear just fine" (ID 113). Such examples highlight the importance for professionals to listen to and validate all experiences, rather than assuming that the perceived intensity or impact of a sound is consistent across individuals, or for individuals across different contexts.

Domain 2: Recommendations

Analysis of the survey responses outlined that while some participants described being provided with genuinely beneficial suggestions to support their auditory concerns (f = 29), the recommendations provided to support Autistic people were mostly considered unhelpful (f = 35). Notably, a small number of participants considered some suggested strategies partially or contextually beneficial (f = 3), while the helpfulness of other strategies remained unclear due to limited detail in their responses (f = 8).

From the professional suggestions deemed unhelpful, the most prevalent was being told to: "... try listening harder..." (ID 146), "...ignore the sound..." (ID 326), or "... push trough it..." (ID 87) (f = 8).

The second most reported area deemed unhelpful was support that was largely generalised and lacking consideration for individual variability or age (f = 6), with one (adult) participant explaining that they received: "... a single sheet of paper that gave me a list of accommodations for young children in school. It was addressed to parents" (ID 186).

Other recommended strategies were highly inappropriate and described by participants as being extremely harmful or painful (f = 2), such as: "Sudden or forced exposure to triggering sounds without consent" (ID 304). Others encouraged the removal of current support strategies such as ear defenders (f = 4). On multiple occasions, participants shared that they received medical interventions including prescription of anti-psychotics or anti-depressant medications (f = 4). This was considered extremely harmful and linked by some participants as making their original conditions worse: "...they said I was depressed and gave me [an] antidepressant, and after a few weeks it triggered tinnitus. Even after [I] stop[ped] taking the medication, the tinnitus never ceased" (ID 97). Elements of going through purposeful exposure to, and controlling and avoiding challenging auditory environments emerged in the analysis as unhelpful professional suggestions, with both occurring at the same frequency (f = 4). Interestingly, there were no participants who shared that they found being told to avoid the sound helpful, though some did find exposure helpful (f = 3), with two participants outlining the benefits if performed in a slow and controlled way. Although less frequent, there was some discussion of professional strategies and advice that participants found beneficial (f = 29). These were largely related to sound-blocking devices (f = 18) and sound-amplifying devices (f = 6), which included Auditory Processing Disorders (APD)-specific ear filters, Cochlear Implants, and ear protection, as well as strategies involving using music or background noise to block unwanted sound. One participant described how they used recommended support for managing a noisy work environment: "We discussed using over the ear headphones when I was working in an office to tune out the other distracting noises" (ID 91). However, there was less evidence of professionals providing a broader toolkit of strategies to support participants' fluctuating capacity across different auditory environments. This was illustrated by one participant who stated: "Noise cancelling headphones changed everything for me but that's the only solution I have been given" (ID 112).

Other strategies were only somewhat helpful (f = 3), such as this example by a participant who has custom earplugs but did not find them particularly useful: "...they're not much better than much cheaper foam earplugs and they're not nearly as good as noise cancelling headphones, so while I still have them I barely use them" (ID 269).

Although access to helpful strategies was valued, participants also emphasised the importance of the interpersonal qualities and personality of the professional delivering this support.

Negative elements of professional interactions occurred more frequently (f = 25) than positive (f = 11) or neutral (f = 3) elements, indicating a greater prevalence of negative experiences overall. Negative experiences unrelated to specific recommendations or advice commonly involved participants describing feeling dismissed by audiology professionals (f = 19), "I wasn't taken seriously and told to just use ear protectors" (ID 6). Some participants reported being invalidated (f = 6) including by being told that their hypersensitive hearing was a positive attribute that they should appreciate, rather than an experience that could be painful or distressing. For example, one participant stated, "... I was told I have hearing in the top 1%... they saw that as a good thing, not something that may be painful for me" (ID 198). Similarly, another participant reported being told that their "'very good' hearing was a benefit, not a challenge" (ID 46). Participants also reported negative experiences in relation to not being provided with any new information (f = 5), and dealing with staff/patient communication challenges (f = 3) with professionals who may have limited understanding of Autistic communication styles. Two participants described seeking support prior to receiving an autism diagnosis (f = 2). One discussed receiving psychological support for their sound sensitivities and was encouraged to undertake exposure therapy which "... made it worse" (ID 43). The other was medicated for sound sensitivities that were labelled as depression. Their accounts point to two barriers to identifying and advocating for support: limited access to understanding their own neurodivergence, and specialists' narrow view of what contributes to sound sensitivities. For example, some helpful strategies were contextually helpful (f = 5), and not always successful due to fluctuating capacity or context-dependent needs: "I have hearing aids to help me in situations where I need to hear people talking. I wear these under my sound cancelling over the ear headphones. But I can't tolerate the hearing aids for very long due to sensitive and narrow ear canals" (ID 123).

For participants who had positive experiences, they often found the practitioner provided a holistic approach (f = 3), validated their concerns and issues (f = 5), and demonstrated awareness of potential contributing factors: "Recognition of potential auditory processing issues and sensory sensitivities was helpful--even just the expression that something might be atypical/worth looking at more in general, without over-focusing on specific potential diagnoses pre-testing..." (ID 119). Also helpful were practitioners who provided support for stress or burnout (f = 2), which was especially useful for one participant who explained that: "...burnout made me less able to handle stuff I'm hypersensitive to" (ID 14). The finding that holistic support unrelated to audiology was perceived as beneficial to some participants, suggests the potential value of a wider approach to addressing participants' needs. Rather than focusing solely on the auditory aspects of their experiences through generalised strategies that may only be beneficial under certain circumstances, support may be enhanced by considering the broader factors contributing to an individual's needs and experiences.

It is important to note that some of the positive experiences of professional support are related to non-audiology professionals, including Counsellors, Occupational Therapists, and Speech-Language Therapists (f = 7). As the survey question did not ask participants to specify the type of professional, all other responses were assumed to refer to audiology professionals unless otherwise stated. Our analysis suggests that when negative experiences occurred alongside advice perceived as unhelpful, it may contribute to participants seeking alternative support services, such as autism community groups, or lead them to research and develop their own approaches. However, this was not directly measured in the present analysis and therefore remains a possible interpretation rather than a demonstrated outcome.

Domain 3: Independent strategies

In addition to identifying professional recommendations, a number of participants discussed solutions came from independently identified strategies, or ideas suggested by community members, friends, or family (f = 22). Notably, the benefits of community-based or alternative support may not have been attributable solely to the practical strategies provided. The positive and supportive nature of these relationships may have been an important component of participants' experiences, highlighting the potential value of interpersonal support alongside the provision of practical strategies. For example, this participant explains that it was their Autistic friend who provided support recommendations after their doctor did not acknowledge their needs: "Literally no PCP [Primary Care Provider] or ear doctor acknowledged my hearing issues... I didn't know I had auditory sensitivities until an autistic friend advised me to wear earplugs to a loud/crowded fair" (ID 113).

The most common helpful independently identified strategy that participants found for themselves was ear protection (f = 10). One participant described using earplugs and avoidance as the most helpful approaches they had identified: "I have tried various 'things' but mainly earplugs and avoidance has been the best way forward for me" (ID 264). Other participants appeared to have learned about ear protection through independent research or communitybased sources rather than through professional support: "I found out about ear defenders and about Loops..." (ID 80). Following ear protection, the second most common independently identified strategy was to increase the understanding of their condition/s in order to be able to self-advocate and find successful support (f = 6). One participant explains that after not having their hearing issues acknowledged, they were left to do their own research: "I had to learn on my own that my brain doesn't habituate to sounds, and I hear too many sounds to decipher them separately" (ID 113).

Another beneficial strategy that participants identified reported was around avoidance (f = 4). This was often accompanied with attempts to maintain control through making changes the environment to make it more comfortable (f = 3): "I alter the environment when I am able. occasionally wear noise cancelling earplugs, or sometimes just leave an area with [too] much noise" (ID 96). Other times avoidance was used as a last resort when no other strategies worked: "[T]he earbuds and headphones help, or I exit the situation as soon as possible" (ID 96). Participants also discussed avoiding auditory input through blocking unwanted sound with background noise or music (f = 4). As explained by this participant: "... maintaining a level of background noise (rain, soft music, etc.) is the best technique I've found (personally) to manage sound sensitivity" (ID 55).

In-Person Research Participation Survey-Quantitative Analysis

Participants provided ratings of the materials and processes associated with the in-person listening task across three stages of participation: Email Communication, Information Document, and In-Person Session Experience. Ratings for the individual five-point agreement items are summarized in Figure 2, with full descriptive statistics provided in Supplemental Information_4. Across the individual agreement items, ratings were concentrated toward the upper end of the five-point scale. Four items received the maximum mean rating with no variability (M = 5.00, SD = 0.00): researchers making participants feel comfortable during the in-person session, clarity of the step-by-step instructions provided by the researchers, opportunities to ask questions or seek clarification, and clarity of instructions during the hearing assessment. The manageability of the listening task duration had the lowest mean rating and greatest variability (M = 4.25, SD = 1.04), indicating greater variation in participants' experiences of task length than for other aspects of participation. The suitability of response options also showed comparatively greater variability (M = 4.50, SD = 0.93). Ratings for clarity of parking and public transportation information (M = 4.75, SD = 0.46) and the usefulness of images and image descriptions in familiarizing participants with the study environment (M = 4.63, SD = 0.52) were also slightly lower than several of the other items. Participants additionally rated the amount of detail provided in the introductory email and information document using a three-point scale. All eight participants (100%) rated the amount of detail provided in both documents as adequate.

In-Person Research Participation Survey -Qualitative Findings

This survey was designed to gather feedback from participants who attended an in-person auditory experiment to inform future studies. As a result, many open-text responses were phrased as suggestions for improvement rather than as descriptions of past experience. These suggestions were analysed alongside participants' comments about their experience, and the themes below reflect both. Inductive content analysis identified three broad domains: Accessibility, Emotional Toll, and Experience Benefit. Within these domains, participants described aspects of communication and the sensory environment that shaped accessibility; experiences of confusion, uncertainty, and anxiety; and aspects of participation associated with enjoyment, engagement, and understanding (Figure 3). The findings within each domain are presented below. Two key quotes for each Domain are shown.

Domain 1: Accessibility

Open-text responses identified specific areas where the information could be clarified or expanded. These responses included feedback around the consistency and clarity of information across documents, including the directions for locating the research site, information about scheduling, and greater detail about what to expect during the listening task.

The first domain describes the conditional nature of participants' ability to access the experiment. As shown in Figure 3, Domain 1 refers to this level of accessibility and its impact on participants' approach to the task, and experience of the task itself. Participants were provided with a number of information documents to help them prepare for the task. Most participants reported a positive experience with the resources provided and had no further suggestions for resources (f = 11), with one participant describing them as: "... inclusive and accessible" (Fiona). These measures put in place by the researchers to improve accessibility of the experiment were generally found to be beneficial, and many participants reported having no further suggestions when asked to provide feedback on the accessibility of the in-lab task (f = 4). For example, one participant shared: "Honestly, as a neurodivergent person, it was easy to follow as is and [I] am stumped to find other improvements" (Matthew).

The most frequently mentioned benefit that participants described in terms of the accessibility of the experiment was the researchers' clear instructions (f = 3). As outlined by this participant: "My overall experience exceeded what I could imagine... [The researcher provided] clear instructions and [helped] me understand all documentation..." (Matthew). While these documents were provided in an attempt to make the experimental information more accessible to participants, some identified areas for improvement, including documents lacking in detail (f = 3). Others noted discrepancies between different documents that created uncertainty around the booking process (f = 2). This prompted the suggestion from this participant for the researchers to update all documents because: "... the information sometimes did not align..." (Nadia). The confusion that some participants describe suggests that although the resources were generally perceived positively, some participants experienced a lack of detail or clarity regarding what to expect prior to participation. This included a lack of information around being allowed to take a break during the task (f = 3) as well as concern around discrepancies in information between documents (f = 2). As this participant explains: "the people I referred weren't sure if they should let you contact them per the document or contact you as per the email!" (Ananya). Some participants also described a general sense of confusion when navigating the documents and accessing the laboratory (f = 2). They explained it was: "... a little confusing for me because I'm not very good with spatial stuff" (Deborah), and suggested providing a clearer map. Participants also provided feedback about the accessibility of the auditory experiment itself. While there were participants who discussed negative elements of the experiment and provided suggestions on how it might be improved in the future, some did acknowledge the benefit of being provided with accessibility measures that enhanced their comfort during the experiment. Specifically, two participants valued having the option to turn off the lights during testing (f = 2), with one participant describing this as: "wonderful" (Deborah), although they worried that other participants might have additional sensory needs that are not currently addressed, such as, "temperature, air flow, tactile surfaces, body positioning, smells etc" (Deborah).

Domain 2: Emotional toll

The second domain suggested that the experience of the in-lab task had an emotional toll on some participants. While some participants found the experiment enjoyable (f = 6): "I really enjoyed participating" (Rachel), other participants found aspects of experiment generally challenging (f = 4). This was found largely to be due to difficulties focusing for an extended period of time (f = 2), with some participants describing the experience as lengthy (f = 2), or tiring/draining (f = 2). One participant's feedback for future experiments was: "[T]he length of each sample task was long and involved a lot of concentration" (Fiona). This was echoed by another participant: "Definitely shorter listening times! focusing so hard for that long was a bit exhausting so more frequent breaks might be good!" (Ananya). As discussed in Domain 1, areas of the experiment that participants found challenging surrounded being given information with limited details (f = 3). This information gap was reflected in participants' accounts of factors that could have improved their experience: "it was interesting! i did not know i could turn down the volume or take my headphones off for the insect sound portion but thats on me" (Ananya). The lack of detail was coded in both the accessibility domain (1) and the emotional toll domain (2). In this domain, they are encapsulated by suggestions to include additional details during the preliminary stages of participation, such as being able to view potential timeslots for booking (f = 1). For example, one participant noted how not being able to see available appointments made scheduling difficult. The same participant also discussed that more information about the control over sound level and headphones use could have benefited their experience: "it was interesting! i did not know i could turn down the volume or take my headphones off for the insect sound portion but thats on me" (Ananya). Participants also mention emotional toll in the context of breaks during the experiment (f = 3) and confusion understanding information or instructions (f = 2). As reported above, the impact of not being able to take a break, or not knowing you were allowed to take a break during the experiment resulted in the experience being: "... a bit exhausting..." (Ananya). The confusion experienced by some participants was also found to take an emotional toll (f = 2). In particular, one participant described experienced stress, finding it difficult to comprehend the signage indicating the laboratory's location because it was oriented differently from the surrounding road layout, explaining: "I usually leave extra time for these sorts of things, so it didn't make a difference with getting there on time thankfully…" (Deborah).

Other areas that participants found challenging surrounded the sounds in the test. One participant discussed finding the volume of the sounds challenging, and needing to turn down each sound to make them tolerable, suggesting that trigger warnings be provided in future (f = 1). They also found the sounds caused psychological challenges, explaining that: "... many of the survey sample sounds evoked negative emotions and images. They were only mild for me but may be more disturbing for other ND people (e.g. classroom noise, busy shopping centres), especially if they start at loud volumes..." (Fiona).

Domain 3: Experience benefit

The third domain relates to the parts of the experiment that participants found enjoyable (f = 6), and described as "positive" (Francesca). Participants found the experiment enjoyable for a variety of reasons, however, most participants did not go into detail about the reasons as to why their experience was positive, though some explained their enjoyment also came from the accessibility of the tasks, rather than the experiment itself. As this participant explains, the experiment was: "Very enjoyable. Manageable tasks with clear contribution to research aims" (Avery), stating they: "Would participate again" (Avery). Others mirrored this, stating the experiment was: "... easy to understand and most importantly fun" (Matthew). Areas which were encapsulated in earlier domains that also relate to participants perspective of the experiment include clear instructions (f = 3), which played a role in enjoyment, and no suggestions for improving resources (f = 11), implying that the resources were useful as they were.

In relation to the elements of the experience prior to the experiment itself, one participant discussed the benefit of being provided with multiple booking options (f = 1), allowing them to schedule the experiment on the weekend which worked best for their needs. They explained that if they attended on a weekday: "... [the] traffic would have been more overwhelming, which would have impacted my ability to perform the listening activity" (Deborah). Participants also pointed out accessibility measures which were helpful and contributed to their positive experience. These centred on being allowed to do the experiment in a more sensoryfriendly environment by turning the lights off (f = 2). As explained by these participants: "Lights off helped me" (Francesca), which others also found: "Wonderful" (Deborah). While for some, the enjoyment changed over time or was conditional on other factors (f = 2): "It was alright but eventually, I was getting bored which made me distracted and not focused" (Nadia). However, this did not dampen their enjoyment overall: "but I enjoyed being part of this study" (Nadia). Another participant shared similar sentiments: "I really enjoyed participating...", even though parts of the task were challenging: "I definitely found the harshness of some of the sounds hard going but never to the point that I couldn't tolerate it" (Rachel). This suggests that participants' experiences were shaped not only by the experiment's design, but also by the information researchers provided, which influenced both their understanding of the tasks and their confidence in the experiment's accessibility.

Overall, the findings highlight the variety of Autistic auditory experiences, including challenges in managing sound sensitivities and accessing appropriate support. Participants' responses also highlighted the value of accessible research and testing practices, demonstrating the importance of being provided with clear information in shaping the experience of the experiment. Together, these findings provide insight into Autistic people's experiences within clinical and research settings, the variability within the support-seeking journey, and improving participation in auditory testing and research.

Discussion

This study examined Autistic adults' experiences of auditory assessment and support across two complementary contexts: experiences seeking professional and clinical support and experiences of audiological and in-lab testing within a research context. Across these contexts, the findings highlight that accessible auditory practice involves more than simply access to an assessment or the provision of a particular professional recommendation. Participants' experiences were shaped by whether their auditory needs were understood and whether information and expectations were clear and consistent. Experiences were also shaped by whether the sensory and procedural demands of the environment were manageable, and whether they had opportunities for choice and control. The findings also demonstrate that accessibility and positive experience are not necessarily synonymous with an absence of challenge. Participants in the in-person study evaluated the research experience very positively overall, while still identifying specific demands associated with sustained listening, concentration, sensory conditions, and aspects of the information provided. Together, the findings suggest that accessible auditory assessment requires attention both to what is being assessed or recommended and to how that assessment or support is experienced by the person.

Autistic People Need Individualised Hearing Health Care

Participants described barriers both to accessing professional support and to implementing recommendations once support had been obtained. Recommendations varied substantially in their perceived helpfulness. Strategies that may be appropriate or beneficial for one person or in one context were not necessarily tolerable, practical, or effective for another. This underscores the need for an individualised approach to accessibility in hearing health and research contexts (Cascio et al., 2020; Sturrock et al., 2026), rather than the application of fixed or standardised accommodations based on a presumed homogenous profile of neurodivergent needs (Crane et al., 2009; Poulsen et al., 2024). Such variability highlights the risk of stereotypical assumptions about what Autistic or neurodivergent people require (Strömberg et al., 2022; Treweek et al., 2019; Wanigasekera et al., 2025) -assumptions which, even when well-intentioned, can inadvertently limit rather than support genuine accessibility if applied prescriptively across a diverse population (Cascio et al., 2020; Davies et al., 2022) . It is therefore not surprising that some participants described experiences in which their auditory concerns were dismissed, generalised, or addressed without sufficient consideration of their broader sensory needs. These findings are particularly important given the substantial heterogeneity in Autistic auditory experiences (Poulsen et al., 2024 (Poulsen et al., , 2025). An approach based on a standardized recommendation for a particular auditory concern may therefore fail to account for the interaction between a person's auditory profile, other sensory needs, communication preferences, and the environments in which a strategy needs to be used, rendering these recommendations minimally or completely unhelpful. The difficulties participants described may also reflect differences in understanding between Autistic people and the professionals they encounter in clinical and research settings. Milton's (2012) double empathy problem challenges the assumption that communication difficulties arise primarily from Autistic people. Instead, misunderstandings may occur in both directions, as Autistic and non-autistic people may struggle to understand one another's perspectives (Milton, 2012). Shaw et al., (2024) extended this concept to healthcare, proposing a triple empathy problem that also considers differences in expectations and priorities between healthcare professionals and those seeking care. Their model describes how early barriers and communication mismatches can lead, through self-doubt, diagnostic overshadowing and fear, to healthcare avoidance and adverse health outcomes, underpinned by epistemic injustice. A hearing professional may have considerable expertise in assessing auditory function yet have limited understanding of what an Autistic person's auditory challenges are as well as their implications in everyday life. While the double empathy problem recognises that misunderstandings can be reciprocal (Milton, 2012), the responsibility for addressing them in professional settings rests primarily with the professional. Participants' accounts of having their concerns dismissed or receiving advice that did not meet their needs may therefore reflect not only differences in understanding, but also the greater authority afforded to professional knowledge over Autistic people's own accounts of their experiences.

Consistent with Sturrock et al. (2026), who found that Autistic adults rely mainly on selfdevised strategies to manage difficult listening situations, our participants independently identified strategies that suited their own auditory needs. These included using hearing protection, modifying or gaining greater control over auditory environments, using sound or music to mask unwanted noise, avoiding challenging situations, and building a better understanding of their own auditory experiences to support self-advocacy. Unlike Sturrock et al., who reported that nearly all strategies were identified without clinical support, our participants described these strategies not as alternatives to professional support but as used alongside professional recommendations and advice from community members and family. This suggests a valuable role for hearing professionals in working collaboratively with Autistic people to understand the strategies they already use, why those strategies work, and in which situations, so that professional knowledge complements rather than replaces existing expertise. This approach also needs to be relevant and suited to a particular person within their everyday environment (Masiero et al., 2025; Poulsen et al., 2025), shifting the focus from identifying a universally applicable strategy to individualised care. Considering participants' accounts across the three support-seeking domains also suggests that this process may not be adequately represented as a linear pathway from identifying a need, to accessing a professional, to receiving and implementing a recommendation (Figure 4). Although directionality between domains was not formally examined in the content analysis, barriers could arise at multiple points in participants' accounts, including before support was accessed and when recommendations could not be implemented or did not meet the person's needs. For example, recommending neither unwanted exposure to uncomfortable or painful sounds or environments, nor recommending complete avoidance of these experiences, was perceived as useful advice. One possible explanation is that these are strategies that Autistic people may have already considered or attempted independently. Consequently, having these approaches suggested by a paid professional may not be perceived as providing new or useful support, and may result in the participant continuing within the cycle of seeking support (Figure 4) due to unaddressed needs. Participants could also identify independent strategies with or without professional support. Given the complexity of this support-seeking pathway, we therefore propose a preliminary framework in which identifying a need, encountering barriers, receiving recommendations, and developing independent strategies can interact dynamically, with multiple pathways through the support-seeking process (Figure 4). Both professional recommendations and independently identified strategies may provide an exit from this process when the person's needs are adequately addressed. The exit pathways in our framework has parallels with research on chronic conditions, where people reach adequate management through a combination of professional recommendations and strategies they identify themselves (Chen, 2016; Crowe et al., 2010) and may return to active seeking when new needs arise (Chen, 2016). Our proposed framework's non-linear bidirectional structure is also consistent with models of access to care (Levesque et al., 2013) and help-seeking in hearing health (Laplante-Lévesque et al., 2013). Levesque et al. (2013) conceptualise access as a process running from identifying a healthcare need, through seeking, reaching and using services, to having the need fulfilled. The authors describe that barriers can arise at the transitions between steps and accumulate over an episode of care.

In hearing health, Laplante-Lévesque et al. (2013) applied the stages-of-change model to 153 adults seeking help for acquired hearing impairment for the first time. Their analyses identified four stages (precontemplation, contemplation, preparation and action), with most participants in the action stage, and four clusters of participants (active change, initiation, disengagement and ambivalence). The framework proposed in the present study is intended as an experienceinformed conceptualisation of the patterns identified in the present data rather than a tested model of hearing health support-seeking pathways. Its non-linear structure is consistent with Laplante-Lévesque et al.'s (2013) conclusion that change in hearing help-seeking may be better represented on a continuum than as movement from one step to the next, but whether the pathways and exit points shown here hold in other samples needs to be tested. An important point to highlight is that two participants described seeking support for their hearing concerns before receiving an autism diagnosis. Their accounts show how limited understanding of Autistic auditory experiences can shape the support people receive: without that context, Autistic people may be less able to make sense of their sound sensitivities. This was compounded by specialists' narrow views of what contributes to sound processing differences, as in the cases where sensitivities were treated as hearing loss, depression or addressed with exposure therapy that participants felt made things worse. These barriers are likely to be amplified in clinical settings where professionals have limited knowledge of Autistic communication styles, consistent with the staff/patient communication challenges reported more broadly here and elsewhere (Finch et al., 2022; Nicolaidis et al., 2015; Strömberg et al., 2022). Together, these accounts highlight that hearing health professionals may work with Autistic people who have not yet received an autism diagnosis. Greater awareness of the diversity of Autistic auditory experiences (Poulsen et al., 2024 (Poulsen et al., , 2025;; Williams et al., 2021), alongside the communication and environmental barriers that can shape their access to healthcare (Doherty et al., 2022; Raymaker et al., 2017; Stein Duker et al., 2025), may help clinicians develop a more comprehensive understanding of patients/clients' experiences beyond what is captured by an audiogram. This awareness can also support more responsive, individualised care, including adjustments to the clinical environment, communication approaches, and referral pathways where appropriate (Cabezas et al., 2026).

Reducing Uncertainty Before Auditory Assessments is Linked to Positive Experiences

The In-Person Research Participation findings provide a complementary perspective to the Support-Seeking for Hearing challenges dataset, by demonstrating how the process of auditory assessment itself can shape participants' experiences. Quantitative ratings were concentrated toward the positive end of the response scale, with participants particularly endorsing the clarity of researcher instructions, opportunities to ask questions, and feeling comfortable with the research team. These positive in-person ratings are likely linked, at least in part, to the groundwork laid prior to attendance: the development of the information pack reflected a deliberate, iterative process designed to anticipate and reduce the uncertainty commonly associated with unfamiliar research environments for Autistic and neurodivergent participants (Le Cunff et al., 2024; Nicolaidis et al., 2019). Rather than providing only the minimum disclosures required for informed consent, the pack combined written instructions with photographs of key locations (the building entrance, parking, the testing room) and concrete sensory and procedural detail -what the task would involve, how long each stage would take, and what response options would be available -so that participants could form an accurate mental model of the session before attending. This staged, multi-format approach to information delivery, and the broader emphasis on reducing sensory and social friction, is consistent with recommendations in the participatory design literature (Autism, 2022; Maun et al., 2024) for structuring predictable, low-uncertainty engagement with Autistic and neurodivergent participants. Participants identified the importance of consistent information across materials, knowing what to expect before attending, being able to locate and navigate the research environment, understanding available choices such as breaks or volume adjustment, and accommodating sensory aspects of the environment. Sustained listening and concentration were also described as demanding for some participants, consistent with the greater variability observed in quantitative ratings of task duration and response options, aligning with previous literature (Gartenberg et al., 2018; Hornsby, 2013) . Participant feedback also indicated that the acoustic stimuli themselves could be distressing, and that this distress was not explained by sound level alone, even though stimuli were calibrated to a 'comfortable level' of 70 dBZ. Sound levels were presented during a familiarisation phase before the experimental session and deemed comfortable by each participant. However, the experimental session lasted 30-40 min, and sustained exposure may have changed how the stimuli were experienced over time. This suggests that comfort judged at the outset does not guarantee comfort throughout, and is consistent with cautions that sound exposure in clinical and research contexts must account for individual and variable sensory profiles (i.e., hypo and hyper-sensitivities (de Vries, 2021); and be supported by ongoing informed consent (Botha et al., 2021)). Moreover, sounds carry associations and memories, and everyday environmental sounds can evoke negative emotions and mental images for Autistic (Poulsen et al., 2025; Rosas-Pérez et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2021) and potentially other neurodivergent people. This suggests that sound-based research should be treated carefully and should consider the emotional meaning of stimuli alongside their physical properties, and that features such as content warnings, advance descriptions of the sounds, and participant control over presentation level may help protect participants' wellbeing.

The quantitative and qualitative findings provide complementary perspectives on accessibility.

All eight participants rated the amount of information provided before the in-person listening task as adequate. At the same time, the open-text responses identified specific information that could have made participation easier or more predictable. An adequate amount of information, then, does not necessarily mean that all the information relevant to a particular participant has been provided. A similar pattern was evident in participants' accounts of the testing itself. Participants could enjoy taking part while also experiencing fatigue, concentration demands, sensory discomfort, or uncertainty at different points in the session. Accessibility is therefore more nuanced than whether an experience is simply accessible or inaccessible (Mack et al., 2025). A positive overall experience can still include barriers or demands, some of which may be addressed through relatively small changes to communication, environmental conditions, task procedures, or opportunities for choice (Ashworth et al., 2021; Le Cunff et al., 2024). This nuance extends beyond the research task used in the present study. Hearing health assessment and laboratory-based auditory research and testing have different purposes, but there is considerable overlap in what they ask of people, beyond the audiological test itself. Both research participation and clinical hearing assessment can involve navigating an unfamiliar environment, processing instructions, sustaining attention to auditory stimuli, giving repeated responses, and communicating with the person conducting the assessment. Several of these demands, particularly unfamiliar sensory environments, communication with staff, and healthcare navigation, are well-documented barriers for Autistic people (Doherty et al., 2022; Raymaker et al., 2017; Strömberg et al., 2022), and are recognised as areas for consideration in inclusive research practice (Cascio et al., 2020; Nicolaidis et al., 2019). Some practical considerations therefore apply across both settings. Information should be provided clearly and consistently in advance, including what to expect from the environment and procedures. Every participant should be informed of what accommodations to the procedure and the environment are available, such as breaks or sensory adjustments. For some Autistic people who may struggle with decision fatigue, a short list of suggestions they may wish to implement may be more accessible (Star, 2025). Being provided with a list of available accommodations as a starting point then removes the burden on the Autistic person to be the one to identify and advocate for the accommodations they may need.

Where the assessment allows, sensory preferences and testing response methods can also be adapted. For example, lighting could be dimmed for light-sensitive participants, and a choice of over-ear headphones or insert earphones could be provided for those bothered by pressure on or around the head with sound levels calibrated for each option (as implemented in our own study). Response method could likewise be selectable, such as a button press, verbal response, or touchscreen, provided this does not change what the measure is designed to capture. These adjustments should be available to all participants, regardless of whether they identify as Autistic or have received an autism diagnosis. A flexible, inclusive approach should offer participants a range of options to support sensory, communication, and listening differences and needs, while also inviting them to suggest additional accommodations that may be helpful for them (Cascio et al., 2020; Nicolaidis et al., 2019). Importantly, responsibility for identifying support needs should not rest with participants or patients/clients; having clinicians and researchers proactively present options (Ashworth et al., 2021; Le Cunff et al., 2024) can help make accommodations more accessible and reduce the burden of having to recognise, articulate, and advocate for individual needs. Many of these accessibility considerations are unlikely to be exclusive to Autistic people (Le Cunff et al., 2024). Clear communication, predictable procedures, manageable assessment demands, and opportunities to ask questions are likely to support people accessing hearing health services more broadly, including people with hearing loss (Coleman et al., 2018; Grenness et al., 2015; Parmar et al., 2022). However, the present findings highlight how these features may take on particular importance in the context of Autistic sensory, communication, and information-processing experiences. An unfamiliar environment, for example, may present not only a logistical challenge but additional sensory and cognitive demands (Doherty et al., 2022; Raymaker et al., 2017; Strömberg et al., 2022), while a recommendation that is appropriate for an auditory concern may itself be difficult to tolerate because of a person's sensory profile (Crane et al., 2009; Poulsen et al., 2024; Robertson & David R Simmons, 2015) or fluctuating capacity (Mantzalas et al., 2022; Raymaker et al., 2020). Accessible practices therefore do not require entirely separate assessment pathways for Autistic or neurodivergent people. Rather, principles of effective hearing-health access (Lim et al., 2025) and research practice may need to be implemented with sufficient flexibility and informed by livedexperiences to respond to different sources and combinations of access needs. Importantly, these considerations are not solely about improving comfort or satisfaction during an assessment. Barriers to accessing appropriate support, or recommendations that cannot be implemented within a person's sensory and everyday context, may leave auditory and more broadly health needs inadequately addressed, with potential consequences for everyday participation and wellbeing (Bradshaw et al., 2021; O'Nions et al., 2023). Barriers within an assessment environment may also influence whether a person can engage with or complete an assessment and the demands placed on them while doing so (Marzi et al., 2025). In research settings, inaccessible procedures may influence who is able to participate, which has implications on the diversity and representativeness of auditory research samples. Although the present study did not examine whether accessibility adjustments alter clinical or experimental measurement outcomes, future work should examine whether reducing procedural, sensory, and communication barriers influences not only assessment experiences, but also engagement, completion, and the reliability or interpretation of assessment outcomes.

Future Directions

A key direction for future research is to understand what makes participation enjoyable and positive for Autistic and neurodivergent people, and how to provide that experience without compromising the rigour of the research or clinical assessments. Participatory and coproduction approaches, in which Autistic and other neurodivergent people help to define, design, and evaluate research, or even audit clinical practices, offer a way to develop auditory assessment procedures that are accessible and engaging from the very beginning (Nicolaidis et al., 2019; Poulsen et al., 2022). Because participants described sustained listening and concentration as demanding, co-designed studies could examine how tasks can be made more enjoyable, for example through task aesthetics, progress indicators, varied or meaningful stimuli, and opportunities for choice and control. One recommendation of particular importance is giving participants the opportunity to experience the sounds before a research session or clinical appointment, for example through audio samples provided in advance (implemented by us for the In-Person Research Participation), because sounds can evoke negative experiences and reactions in Autistic and other neurodivergent people that are not predicted by their physical properties alone (Williams et al., 2021). Listening in advance would allow people to decide whether and how to take part, to prepare for or request changes to the sounds, and to give informed consent to the specific sensory content of an assessment, an issue raised by Autistic researchers (Botha et al., 2021). Importantly, these questions can be tested empirically: studies could compare versions of the same task that differ in how engaging they are, and examine participant experience alongside data quality, response consistency, and completion, to test whether enjoyment and methodological rigour can co-exist. There is some reason to expect this, since inclusive laboratory environments may contribute not only to the ethical treatment of participants but also to the accuracy and reliability of findings (Le Cunff et al., 2024). Future research could evaluate the effects of changes such as providing more detailed preparatory information, offering explicit choices around breaks and sensory conditions, increasing flexibility in response methods, and enabling greater participant control over aspects of the assessment where this does not compromise the purpose of the measure. It will also be important to examine how these principles apply across different forms of auditory and neurophysiological assessment, including procedures such as electro/magneto-encephalography (EEG/MEG) or functional near infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), which introduce additional sensory, physical, and procedural demands. Le Cunff et al. (2024) proposed a checklist for laboratory studies with neurodivergent participants that covers the stages before, during, and after a visit, emphasising physical comfort, participant agency over environmental adjustments, and clear communication. Future work could compare participants' expectations and concerns before adjustments are introduced with their comfort and wellbeing afterwards, to see which adjustments help which participants in which assessments.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Both datasets were originally collected for broader research purposes rather than to address the specific questions examined in this study. The Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges data provide retrospective accounts of support from various professionals, including audiologists, other health professionals, and counsellors, but lack the contextual detail needed to examine individual interactions and recommendations. In particular, although ear protection emerged as a helpful strategy, the survey wording required careful interpretation of whether strategies were recommended by others or explicitly identified independently by participants. The survey did not establish how independently identified strategies were developed, when they were implemented relative to professional support, or whether negative experiences with audiology influenced participants' support-seeking pathways. Therefore, it remains unclear whether independent strategy identification reflects limitations in professional support or the complementary use of personal strategies. The In-Person Research Participation Survey also involved a small sample of participants who completed the auditory task and provided feedback, limiting the extent to which their experiences may represent those of people who did not participate, could not complete the procedures, or chose not to provide feedback. Finally, as the findings reflect experiences within a single research setting and team, further research across diverse clinical and research environments is needed to assess their transferability and applicability.

Conclusion

Auditory assessment and support for Autistic people require attention not only to the auditory differences being assessed, but also to the conditions under which assessment and support occur. Across Support-Seeking for Hearing Challenges and In-Person Auditory Research, participants' accounts emphasised the importance of being understood, having clear and consistent information, being able to exercise choice and control, and having strategies and procedures that are workable within their individual sensory needs and everyday environments. Rather than requiring Autistic people to adapt to fixed assessment and support practices, these findings support greater flexibility and collaboration in how hearing health care and research are designed and delivered. The experience-informed considerations identified here provide a foundation for developing and evaluating more accessible and responsive approaches to auditory assessments and support across hearing health and auditory research.
