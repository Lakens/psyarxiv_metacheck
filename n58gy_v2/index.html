Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Despite growing interest in advanced meditation, the cognitive mechanisms underlying these capacities remain poorly understood. This paper develops a cognitive account of advanced meditation as a specialized form of metacognitive skill learning. Drawing on classic accounts of skill acquisition, we propose the automatization of metacognitive control as a mechanism through which meditative skill develops. Through sustained practice, practitioners become less reliant on explicit instructions as metacognitive control becomes increasingly automatic. This shift may allow meditative practice to become more effective, fluent, and less effortful. The resulting metacognitive control is directed toward well-defined phenomenological targets, including advanced absorption states and insight stages. By connecting cognitive mechanisms with neural findings and experiential descriptions, the account offers a testable framework for explaining the development of advanced meditative capacities.

1. Introduction

Meditation research has built a substantial body of evidence regarding the neural and experiential changes associated with meditative practice, as well as its beneficial effects on psychological well-being. While this research has identified many important components and outcomes of meditation, comparatively little is known about how meditative capacities are acquired, stabilized, and integrated (Grabovac et al., 2026). This question is becoming increasingly important as the current "third wave" of meditation research increasingly investigates distinctive states, stages, and endpoints of advanced meditation (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026; Sacchet et al., 2024; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026). While the diversity of meditative practices has made a unified cognitive account challenging, prior work has proposed that these practices can be classified in terms of intentional meditative activities unified by awareness of awareness (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022). Building on this foundation, this paper proposes that advanced meditation can be understood as a specialized domain of metacognitive skill. We draw on classic models of perceptual-motor skill and cognitive skill acquisition (Anderson, 1982; Fitts, 1964), as well as recent work on metacognitive skill and mindfulness development (Conway-Smith, 2025; Kee, 2019), to examine automatization as a candidate mechanism of meditative skill acquisition.

A cognitive account aims to explain a phenomenon in terms of the representations, operations, and information-processing mechanisms through which it is produced (Newell & Simon, 1972), while enabling testable predictions. We propose that meditative development involves a transition from metacognitive control guided by explicit declarative knowledge toward increasingly implicit procedural operations. Through repeated practice, deliberate mental actions become more effective, stable, and automatic while requiring less conscious effort.

Earlier cognitive accounts identified regulatory skills involved in meditation and described how their use changes with training. Lutz et al. (2008) characterized focused attention practice in terms of monitoring for distraction, disengaging from it, and redirecting attention, with extensive practice reducing the need for effortful regulation. Building on this account, we propose a learning mechanism through which instruction-guided regulation becomes increasingly automatic: repeated practice embeds explicit directions within condition-action procedures, reducing the need to retrieve and deliberately apply instructions. We examine how this transition may support the stabilization of advanced concentration and the integration of insight while preserving higher-level strategic control.

Our proposal builds on Sparby et al. (2026), who define advanced meditation in terms of states, stages, and endpoints arising through the maturation and mastery of awareness skills.

They characterize advanced meditative development as a shift from deliberate, repeated effort toward increasingly effortless and self-sustaining mental activity, combining greater intentional control with self-attenuation. Ehmann et al. (2025) complement this account by identifying training variables, including volume, intensity, frequency, and periodization, that may shape this development. Together, these frameworks specify the developmental transition and training conditions to which the proposed skill learning mechanism applies. This mechanism may contribute to the development and stabilization of several advanced meditative capacities, including those cultivated through advanced concentrative absorption meditation (ACAM; Sparby & Sacchet, 2024) and advanced investigative insight meditation (AIIM; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025b), as well as progression through the stages of the Insight Development Process (IDP; Grabovac et al., 2026). Metacognitive skill learning may also help explain culminating events and persistent changes associated with meditative endpoints (MEND; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a).

The account proposed here does not endorse a strong automaticity view of meditative or metacognitive skill. A comprehensive skill theory must also explain characteristics of expertise such as higher-level strategic control, affect regulation, metacontrol, and flexibility. As, to our knowledge, this is the first attempt to integrate research on metacognitive skill with advanced meditation, we will focus on a particular learning mechanism rather than offering a complete theory of meditative expertise. Automatization therefore provides a starting point for this account, while we also consider how it interacts with higher-level control and flexibility.

Because this account connects advanced meditative experience with the cognitive mechanisms through which it develops, it spans multiple levels of analysis. Marr (1982) developed a multilevel framework for understanding biological and artificial cognitive systems, distinguishing between the implementation, algorithmic/representational, and computational levels of explanation. Inspired by this framework, Figure 1 organizes the proposed account across three complementary levels. The neural level concerns the biological mechanisms through which meditative processes are implemented, the cognitive level concerns the representations and information-processing operations involved, and the experiential level (or phenomenological level) concerns the structure and qualities of conscious experience. These levels connect the neural and cognitive mechanisms of automatization to changes in meditative experience.

By grounding advanced meditation in theories of metacognition and skill acquisition and connecting these processes to well-defined experiential targets, this paper offers a testable cognitive framework for understanding how meditative training produces stable, transformative states and capacities. The relationship between advanced meditation and metacognitive skill may in turn be mutually informative. Metacognitive skill acquisition offers a framework for explaining aspects of meditative development, while meditation provides a particularly rich domain for investigating how metacognitive skills are acquired. More broadly, this work provides a theoretical basis for understanding how human cognition can skillfully regulate its own processes in ways that support psychological well-being. 2. Metacognition

Metacognition refers to the broad capacity of human cognition to monitor and control its own processes (Flavell, 1979). Nelson and Narens (1990) helped formalize this distinction by separating the object-level, where cognitive activity occurs, from the meta-level, which monitors and controls that activity. This capacity encompasses the monitoring and control of processes such as emotion, reasoning, memory, and attention (Fleming & Dolan, 2012; Proust, 2013).

Metacognition can include processes that operate through implicit, non-conceptual signals such as feelings of knowing, fluency, and confidence (Koriat, 2000). It can also involve conceptually-driven processes such as explicit instructions that guide the deliberate regulation of mental actions, such as in explicit reasoning or attention training strategies (Jahn et al., 2023; Proust, 2013). The present account focuses on explicit, conceptually guided mental actions as they provide the starting point for the process of automatization examined below.

Metacognitive monitoring refers to detecting, representing, and evaluating features of one's cognitive and affective states and processes, including their relation to current goals, such as attentional stability, emotional intensity, or memory accuracy. Metacognitive control refers to regulating these states and processes by deploying mental actions directed toward metacognitive goals. Mental actions are operations intended to alter or maintain mental activity, such as directing focus, regulating an emotional response, or adopting a particular mode of observation such as transience. Monitoring and control form a reciprocal loop: monitoring can identify discrepancies between current and intended mental states (e.g., distraction), while control regulates mental activity to reduce those discrepancies (Nelson & Narens, 1990).

Metacognitive control is distinguished from cognitive control by the content of the goal toward which control processes are directed (Lange, 2025). On this view, cognitive control regulates mental activity instrumentally in pursuit of a further, usually external goal, such as directing one's focus toward the road while driving. Metacognitive control takes as its goal some internal mental state or cognitive process, such as stabilizing attention or developing a subtle perception of transience. While metacognitive goals may also contribute to broader meditative or therapeutic aims, the agent's immediate control processes are directed toward them as proximal goals to be attained in their own right. Many researchers consider the fundamental psychological capacity that is exercised in meditative practices such as mindfulness to be a form of metacognitive control (Dorjee, 2016; Jankowski & Holas, 2014; Lange, 2025; Repetti, 2022).

The relevant goal need not be consciously represented each time a learned procedure is executed.

On the present account, a procedure remains metacognitive insofar as it regulates mental activity toward that goal, even when its execution no longer requires explicit instruction retrieval.

Metacognitive skill refers to the learned capacity to monitor and regulate mental processes reliably and flexibly across contexts in pursuit of metacognitive goals. Examples include regulating one's own learning (Veenman et al., 2006), deliberately applying formal rules in scientific reasoning (Kuhn, 2000) as well as therapeutic strategies such as cognitive reframing and decentering (Kraft et al., 2026; Zawidzki, 2019). During meditation, metacognitive control is directed toward a specialized subset of metacognitive goals such as concentration, equanimity, compassion, and perceptual modes including transience, selflessness, and incongruence. On this view, advanced meditation is a specialized domain of metacognitive skill, where metacognitive control processes are trained to reliably and flexibly achieve these mental capacities and experiential outcomes. Section 3 examines a skill-acquisition mechanism through which these control processes improve with practice.

3. Skill Acquisition

Skills are learned capacities for reliably and flexibly achieving valuable outcomes across varying conditions (Christensen et al., 2019; Fridland, 2019). They encompass a wide range of activities, from everyday language use, reading, and driving to mathematics, chess, and musical performance. Early theories of skill learning focused on a candidate unifying principle-the gradual automatization of performance through practice. Since then, skill theorists have broadened their accounts of expert performance, arguing that some aspects of skill never fully automatize and emphasizing a greater role for cognitive control and metacognition (Christensen et al., 2016; Pacherie & Mylopoulos, 2021). Given the nascent state of cognitive accounts of meditative skill acquisition, a productive starting point is to examine the knowledge representations and processes of automatization emphasized in classic accounts of skill learning.

These foundational accounts offer a starting point for a broader explanation of meditative expertise that includes flexibility and higher-level control.

A prominent framework characterizes skill automatization as a three-stage progression from deliberate, effortful, instruction-guided action to increasingly fluent and automatic performance. This stage-based framework was popularized by Fitts and Posner's (1967) theory of perceptual-motor skill and extended by Anderson (1982) to cognitive skill. Anderson explains this transition through knowledge compilation, which involves composition and proceduralization. Composition combines sequences of operations into more unified procedures, while proceduralization embeds declarative information within those procedures, reducing reliance on explicit instructions. Together, these processes make performance more fluent and efficient. In this paper, we use automatization as a broader term for this change. This general account has since been applied to motor, cognitive, and metacognitive skills, with preliminary applications to mindfulness development (Ford et al., 2005; Conway-Smith, 2025; Kee, 2019).

We apply this skill-based framework here to propose a cognitive account of how advanced meditative capacities develop and stabilize. Specifically, we propose that the automatization of metacognitive control helps to explain increasingly effortless and self-sustaining concentration, the development of integrated insight, and the stable capacities associated with meditative endpoints. This account centers on key information-types used by basic and advanced meditators, specifically explicit, declarative knowledge and implicit, procedural knowledge.

Declarative knowledge is propositionally-structured and consists of consciously accessible facts, rules, and instructions that guide performance. It is supported by a declarative memory system that depends largely on medial temporal lobe structures, particularly the hippocampus (Squire et al., 2004; Stocco et al., 2024). However, while declarative knowledge may hold instructions for goal-directed action, it does not by itself execute performance.

Instructions are acted out by procedural knowledge, which deploys the specific, goal-directed actions. In Anderson's framework, procedural knowledge is formatted as condition-action statements that prescribe actions in response to associated cues. Through repeated practice, procedural knowledge is built, refined, and stored within procedural memory. Procedural learning relies more heavily on cortico-striatal circuits involving the basal ganglia (Anderson et al., 2008; Doyon & Benali, 2005).

Early in learning, agents rely on declarative knowledge, including facts and instructions that guide the appropriate actions toward a goal (e.g., driving a vehicle, playing chess, or directing attention). Repeated practice builds and refines procedural associations between task conditions and actions, which increasingly guide performance over time. Broadly speaking, this transition unfolds across three stages of skill acquisition: Basic: Actions are initially guided by declarative knowledge (instructions, facts, and strategies).

Intermediate: Through practice, declarative knowledge is gradually converted into implicit procedural routines that are refined and increasingly automatized.

Advanced: Procedurally guided actions become relatively effortless, fast, effective, and automatic.

One important consequence of automatization is that it reduces demand on working memory (Haith & Krakauer, 2018; Masters, 1992). Because practitioners no longer need to consciously recall explicit instructions, working memory can be reinvested into higherlevel control functions. These resources can then be redirected toward the flexible control characteristic of expert skill. These include monitoring progress, detecting errors, and adapting to changes in internal or external conditions.

In metacognitive skill, this process of automatization concerns the regulation of cognition itself, rather than externally directed behavior. A basic practitioner may initially rely on explicit knowledge about when and how to monitor or control reasoning, emotion, memory, attention, or other cognitive processes. Initially, these mental actions are slow, error prone, and effortful. With repeated practice, metacognitive monitoring and control become increasingly automatized, allowing internal conditions to cue the appropriate metacognitive responses more directly and with less reliance on deliberate instructions.

Through this process, metacognitive control becomes increasingly effective, effortless, and automatically deployed. Related empirical findings include increasingly efficient emotion regulation following structured practice (Christou-Champi et al., 2015), evidence consistent with automatization of memory retrieval (Racsmány et al., 2018), and improved reasoning following repeated training (Franiatte et al., 2024). These findings support the broader plausibility of practice-related gains in efficiency and automaticity, although they do not all directly test the proceduralization of metacognitive control. Of particular relevance to meditation, Ramamurthy and Blaser (2017) describe how practice can give rise to "procedural attention," in which learned rules of attentional selection operate automatically, much as procedural memory supports motor skill.

The same developmental progression has been explored in relation to mindfulness practice. Kee (2019) applies Fitts and Posner's (1967) three-stage framework to mindfulness development, describing a cognitive stage guided by explicit instructions, an associative stage marked by reduced reliance on instructions and greater application across contexts, and an autonomous stage in which mindful attention and nonjudgmental awareness become relatively automatic and effortless. However, the proposed transition from instruction-guided to increasingly automatic metacognitive control in meditation remains to be directly tested.

Mindfulness has also been described as a de-automatizing practice that interrupts habitual cognitive and emotional reactions (Kang et al., 2013). This is compatible with the present account because habitual reactions and the metacognitive skills that regulate them can change in different ways. Habitual emotional reactions can develop unintentionally, without deliberate training, and persist even when maladaptive. In contrast, the metacognitive skills considered here are deliberately cultivated to improve regulation, becoming increasingly automatic while remaining responsive to conscious adjustment.

Automatization of these self-regulatory skills could therefore help practitioners interrupt habitual maladaptive reactions and respond more flexibly.

4. Metacognitive Skill Acquisition in Meditation

4.1 Advanced Meditation

Advanced meditation practices are directed toward specific phenomenological targets.

Here, we focus on two complementary pathways: ACAM and AIIM. As summarized in Table 1, these pathways cultivate different experiential capacities while contributing to the broader development of meditative states, stages, and endpoints.

In AIIM and the Insight Development Process, targets include ways of perceiving experience in terms of transience, selflessness, and incongruence with expectations. These "perceptual modes" may develop from momentary experiences into increasingly integrated insight (Grabovac et al., 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025b). Within the present account, metacognitive monitoring and control are organized toward attaining these phenomenological targets.

A phenomenological target should be distinguished from both the object of practice and the proximal metacognitive goals used to attain it. For instance, the breath, a visual point, or a bodily sensation may serve as an object of attention. Sustaining attention on that object, detecting distraction, and returning attention when it wanders are proximal metacognitive goals. Through practice, more stable attention makes it easier to monitor ongoing mental activity, while metacognitive monitoring and control help sustain attention and restore it when it wanders.

Repeatedly attaining these goals may, in turn, support the development and stabilization of advanced concentrative states. Similarly, insight practices direct metacognitive activity toward ways of attending to experience that may initially produce momentary and subsequently more integrated perceptions of transience, selflessness, or incongruence.

For example, an explicit instruction such as "when you notice your attention has wandered, disengage from the distraction and return to the breath" identifies both an internal condition and the mental action to be performed. During early meditation practice, instructions are typically delivered verbally and held in working memory, so performance depends on explicit guidance and deliberate control. Through repeated action, the execution of instructions becomes encoded in increasingly task-specific procedures connecting internal conditions with the appropriate mental actions. For example, detecting a distraction may begin to automatically cue attentional refocusing without needing the instructions to be consciously retrieved. We propose that, as these explicit strategies become increasingly automatic, meditative control becomes more stable, fluent, and relatively effortless, thereby supporting the development of advanced meditative capacities. 4.2 Three Stages of Meditative Development

The development of meditative skill can be organized into three stages corresponding to the general progression described in Section 3. Whereas the skill-acquisition literature commonly uses the terms novice, intermediate, and expert, we follow the advanced meditation literature in referring to the corresponding stages as basic, intermediate, and advanced. These labels describe points along a gradual progression rather than sharply distinct phases.

Basic. Practice initially depends on explicit instructions that specify how attention and other cognitive processes should be monitored and controlled. Practitioners must retrieve and interpret these instructions while applying them to a meditation object or experiential target.

Performance is therefore relatively slow, effortful, and dependent on working memory.

Intermediate. With repeated practice, mental actions guided by instructions become increasingly associated with task-specific conditions, allowing them to be acted out more directly. This transition marks the beginning of automatization. Relevant internal conditions, such as detecting distraction, begin to cue the appropriate procedural knowledge, which deploys mental actions more rapidly. This reduces the reliance on explicit instructions and allows meditative control to become faster, more reliable, and less effortful. The mental actions supporting specific phenomenological targets may begin to arise spontaneously.

Advanced.

Through extensive practice, procedural knowledge can be cued and deployed with minimal conscious deliberation. The relevant conditions increasingly elicit appropriate monitoring and control operations without requiring the practitioner to retrieve the original instruction. Meditative performance consequently becomes more fluent, stable, and relatively effortless, while remaining available for strategic adjustment when conditions change.

4.3 Advanced Meditative Targets

This account of automatization can be applied to two complementary pathways of advanced meditation: the stabilization of advanced concentrative absorption states and the integration of insight. Although these pathways involve different phenomenological targets, we propose that both draw on metacognitive strategies that become more reliable, fluent, and less reliant on deliberate control.

In advanced concentrative absorption meditation (ACAM; Sparby & Sacchet, 2024), practitioners repeatedly perform actions such as sustaining attention, detecting distraction, disengaging from competing content, and regulating the intensity of effort. As these actions become increasingly automatic, distractions may be detected and corrected more quickly, with less need to retrieve and consciously apply explicit instructions. This may allow concentrative states to become increasingly stable, effortless, and self-sustaining, supporting progressively deeper absorption. Importantly, greater stability need not be accompanied by greater subjective intensity. Jhāna-type advanced concentrative absorption meditation (ACAM-J) comprises eight absorption states, referred to here as J1-J8. With experience, earlier states of ACAM-J such as J1 and J2 may become more stable and effortless even as their intensity or salience diminishes, a pattern consistent with increasing efficiency and automaticity rather than reduced skill. In later ACAM-J states, automatization may also support the stability of increasingly subtle contents of consciousness, including minimal expansive spatiality (J5), minimal expansive consciousness (J6), absence perception (J7), and liminality perception (J8).

In advanced investigative insight meditation (AIIM; Grabovac et al., 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025b), practice repeatedly directs attention toward specific features of experience and cultivates distinct perceptual modes, including transience, in which phenomena are experienced as arising and passing. Through repetition, strategies for accessing such perceptual modes, disengaging from conceptual rumination, and maintaining nonreactive awareness may become increasingly accessible and stable. Improved perceptual discrimination and increasingly automatic regulation may make complementary contributions to this development. Conway-Smith and West (2024) propose a computational mechanism linking them: faster and simpler monitoring procedures may increase the likelihood of detecting fleeting or intermittent changes in internal states. Applied to investigative insight, this could help practitioners notice affective fluctuations that previously went undetected, making their arising and passing more perceptually accessible. Automatization may also make disengagement from conceptual elaboration and sustained observation less dependent on explicit instructions. This proposed link between procedural learning and perceptual access remains to be empirically tested, including whether it contributes to the IDP's perceptual modes of selflessness and incongruence. By enabling more reliable and efficient deployment of the supporting operations, automatization may help initially momentary insights become more integrated into ongoing perception and cognition, and easier to access intentionally even when the corresponding modes are not continuously active (Grabovac et al., 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025b).

Automatization therefore does not determine the content of advanced states or insights.

Instead, it provides a learning mechanism through which the metacognitive operations that support them become sufficiently developed to sustain advanced concentration and integrated insight.

4.4 Integration and Flexibility

Automatization may support advanced meditation not only by improving individual skills but also by reducing their working-memory demands. At the basic stage, practitioners must consciously retrieve instructions, monitor performance, and deploy appropriate mental actions.

As these operations become increasingly automatic, they require less deliberate attention, preserving cognitive resources for higher-level monitoring, strategic regulation, and engagement with subtler aspects of experience. This is particularly important because expertise typically involves a repertoire of actions rather than mastery of a single action (Shepherd, 2021). Reduced working-memory demands may help practitioners coordinate several well-practiced skills concurrently, consistent with evidence that extensive practice can reduce interference between simultaneously performed tasks (Schumacher et al., 2001). In the emotion-regulation literature, the concurrent use of multiple regulatory strategies is referred to as polyregulation (Ford et al., 2019). This may help explain how advanced meditation practitioners can simultaneously coordinate several meditative skills, such as attentional stability, equanimity, effort regulation, and meta-awareness (explicit awareness of one's ongoing mental activity). When these metacognitive abilities become sufficiently practiced, they may function together rather than as separate strategies that must each be consciously managed. This may also help explain how advanced practitioners coordinate multiple forms of metacognitive control while maintaining a relatively effortless mode of practice.

Importantly, automatization need not imply rigid or inflexible control. Skilled procedures remain responsive to context and can be modified as the conditions change. An experienced practitioner may increase effort when their attention becomes unstable, reduce effort when control becomes excessive, or shift strategies such as increasing equanimity during a surge of emotion. Greater automatic control may therefore support meditative flexibility by making wellpracticed procedures available while preserving cognitive resources for higher-level monitoring and strategic adjustment. On this account, meditative expertise involves neither complete conscious control nor entirely automatic performance. Instead, it involves the flexible integration of increasingly automatic capacities that remain responsive to conscious control. This account may help explain the combination of spontaneous and consciously guided control that is characteristic of advanced meditation.

5. Phenomenology of Metacognitive Skill

The cognitive and experiential changes described previously during skill learning should also be reflected in meditative development. Our concern here is not the full phenomenology of advanced meditation or the particular contents of advanced states and insights. Rather, it is how the experience of metacognitive actions changes as those actions become increasingly automatized. We propose that this development is associated with three broad experiential shifts: from explicit instruction toward fluent engagement; from effortful regulation toward greater stability and integration; and from deliberate control toward a less salient sense of authorship.

5.1 From Explicit Instruction to Fluent Performance

Skill acquisition is described by Fitts (1964) and Anderson (1982) primarily in terms of changes in performance and knowledge representation. Dreyfus and Dreyfus (1986) offer a complementary phenomenological account of how the transition toward expertise is experienced.

Their model describes a progression from explicit, context-independent rule-following toward increasingly fluent, intuitive, and context-sensitive engagement. Although the authors do not specify the cognitive mechanism responsible for this development, they provide a useful phenomenological account of skill learning through various stages. This progression captures only part of the phenomenology of expertise, as deliberate monitoring and cognitive control continue to play important roles (Christensen et al., 2016). However, we focus on the experiential changes associated with automatization, in keeping with the scope of this paper.

Basic meditation may be experienced as a sequence of deliberate and partially separate operations. Instructions must be recalled, the relevant features of experience identified, and the appropriate mental actions selected. A practitioner might notice that their attention has wandered, recall the instruction to disengage, and then deliberately return attention to the object of meditation. Because these operations are not yet well coordinated, practice may feel effortful, intermittent, and unstable.

With practice, internal conditions begin to cue the appropriate responses more directly.

For example, a distraction may increasingly cue attentional refocus, while rumination may cue disengagement, without the practitioner needing to recall the full instruction consciously.

Practice therefore becomes less conceptually dependent and more automatic and fluent. During the transition from basic to advanced meditation, instances of fluency will alternate with occasions when instructions need to be recalled.

At more advanced levels, monitoring and control may occur quickly and with little conscious deliberation. Rather than experiencing meditation as separate instances of corrective actions, the practitioner may experience a more continuous and integrated engagement. The relevant features of one's internal experience become more perceivable, and the appropriate mental actions may arise more rapidly in response to them. In this way, the intuitive and contextsensitive responses described by Dreyfus and Dreyfus reflect a greater connection between internal conditions and metacognitive actions.

5.2 Effort, Stability, and Integration

The reduction of effort is one of the clearest experiential results of skill automatization. A basic meditator must divide their attention between the object of meditation, the instructions for practice, the monitoring of performance, and corrective actions. As these operations become automatized, they place fewer demands on working memory, and metacognitive control becomes more automatic and spontaneous. This may help explain the increasing stability and self-sustaining quality of ACAM.

When sustaining one's attention, detecting distraction, and making corrections no longer require constant effort, concentration may be experienced less as something that must be repeatedly restored and more as stable and self-sustaining (Sparby & Sacchet, 2024). This account may also help to explain the integration of insight. Early experiences of transience, selflessness, or incongruence may be brief and intermittent. Their episodic quality may partly reflect the need to deliberately and effortfully initiate a particular mode of perception.

As the supporting metacognitive operations become more fluent, access to these perceptual modes becomes easier, more frequent, and more continuous. Importantly, automatization concerns changes in the metacognitive actions that support insight, rather than the insights themselves. Consequently, this account helps to explain the cognitive mechanisms supporting momentary insights and how they become increasingly stable (Grabovac et al., 2026).

The reduction of cognitive demands also helps account for how several meditative capacities may operate together. Attentional stability, awareness, equanimity, effort regulation, and compassion may be coordinated and deployed in tandem. The phenomenology of advanced meditation practice may therefore not only involve a sense of fluency, but also the experience of multiple capacities operating in an integrated way.

5.3 Agency and Strategic Control

Automatization might suggest an apparent paradox: that practitioners may acquire greater control over mental activity while experiencing a less pronounced sense of initiating those actions. Motor-learning findings suggest that practice can alter agency, although the direction depends on the task and measure. Dempsey-Jones et al. (2023) found reduced intentional binding, an indirect measure associated with agency, following motor practice, whereas van der Wel et al. (2012) found that the sense of agency increased with better performance. Applied to meditation, the present account proposes that practiced mental actions may require less deliberate initiation, without implying a general reduction in the sense of control. In contrast, a basic meditator experiences themselves as deliberately directing attention, refocusing from distraction, or altering their mode of perception. With practice, these actions may occur more spontaneously in response to internal conditions. Attention returns, effort readjusts, or rumination subsides without being preceded by a conscious intention. This may help explain how advanced meditation can involve the combination of greater self-regulation abilities along with a sense of effortlessness, spontaneous awareness, and reduced self-related processing (i.e., self-attenuation).

A decreased sense of authorship should not be understood as a lack of conscious awareness or control. Research on skilled action suggests that expertise can involve remaining continuously sensitive to changing conditions, goals, and the quality of one's performance (Terrien et al., 2024). Advanced practitioners can deliberately redirect attention, modify a strategy, or increase effort when internal or external conditions change. For instance, a skilled tennis or chess player can fluidly modify their strategies based on the performance of their opponent, or their own internal states such as fatigue. Similarly, an advanced meditator may fluidly adapt their attentional control processes in response to distractions or emotional fluctuations.

Hence, advanced meditative skill is experienced neither as continuous deliberate control nor as entirely unconscious automaticity. It involves increasingly fluent and relatively effortless metacognitive activity that remains responsive to intentions and changing conditions. These experiential changes provide a complementary phenomenological account of the automatization of metacognitive control.

6. Training Conditions and Developmental Trajectories

The present account describes a learning mechanism through which meditative skill may develop, yet it does not imply that long-term practice alone is sufficient. The same is true in other skill domains: someone may spend many years writing novels or playing an instrument without attaining literary or musical mastery (Ehmann et al., 2025). Similarly, advanced meditative skill requires more than the automaticity that may develop through long-term repetition (Galante et al., 2023; Sparby et al., 2026). An important distinction between long-term and advanced practice may therefore lie in how training is structured, since training conditions shape how effectively metacognitive procedures are acquired and refined.

6.1 Structured Training

Drawing on exercise science, Ehmann et al. (2025) provide a framework for describing contemplative training in terms of variables such as volume, duration, frequency, density, and intensity. These variables characterize the amount, temporal distribution, and intensity of practice, while programming and periodization concern how training is organized over shorter and longer periods. Importantly, the framework distinguishes the length of a practitioner's training history from the volume and density of their actual practice. Practitioners with similar years of experience may therefore have substantially different training histories.

These training variables should be distinguished from the cognitive mechanism proposed here. While automatization involves changes in the representations governing performance, training variables describe the conditions under which those changes occur. Greater practice volume may strengthen associative connections between internal conditions and the appropriate mental actions. However, equivalent amounts of practice may produce different effects depending on how regularly and intensively the actions are performed. Meditative development should therefore not be understood simply as a function of accumulated practice hours.

Beyond the amount and distribution of practice, feedback may also shape which metacognitive actions are learned and selected. Work on metacognitive reinforcement learning suggests that actions followed by better outcomes become more likely to be selected again in similar situations (Krueger et al., 2017). Comparator mechanisms may support metacognitive monitoring by detecting discrepancies between current cognitive states and metacognitive goals, thereby informing corrective control (Proust, 2013). In meditation, for example, a practitioner may aim to sustain attention on an object, notice when the mind has wandered, and redirect attention toward the intended object. Across repeated practice, actions that more successfully achieve the goal may become more likely to be selected. Reinforcement learning could complement automatization by influencing which operations are learned and selected, while automatization makes those operations more fluent and less effortful.

Consistent with the principle of training specificity, the effects of meditative training should depend on the particular mental actions practiced and the conditions under which they are performed. For example, repeatedly observing transience, disengaging from conceptual rumination, and maintaining nonreactive awareness may refine the skills needed for investigative insight. The sequence in which these skills are learned may also matter. Developing stable attention, for example, may provide a foundation for subtler practices such as cultivating a perception of transience. Learning these skills separately may make them easier to coordinate later, which could help explain why many meditative systems develop attentional stability before more advanced forms of insight.

6.2 Developmental Trajectories

Meditative skill should not be expected to develop smoothly or uniformly. Research on cognitive skill acquisition suggests that gradual improvement can be interrupted when learners adopt a new strategy (Tenison & Anderson, 2016). Applied to meditation, proficiency in a technique may increase gradually, whereas a change in technique, target, or mode of control may temporarily reduce effectiveness.

Development may consequently include periods of improvement, consolidation, plateau, and temporary regression. When learning a new technique or pursuing a more demanding target, practitioners may return to explicit and effortful control, drawing on skills that are already well established. An increase in difficulty or perceived effort does not necessarily indicate a loss of skill. It may reflect the development of a more advanced procedure, or the coordination of multiple capacities.

Meditative development is also likely to be multidimensional (Galante et al., 2023; Murray et al., 2026). Changes in different capacities need not occur together. For instance, a practitioner may gain momentary access to a perceptual mode such as transience before being able to sustain it. Practitioners may also proceed through separate stages of skill acquisition for different capacities. Basic, intermediate, and advanced stages should therefore be considered as broad functional descriptions rather than a fixed timetable.

On this account, there is neither a simple relationship between practice hours and expertise nor an identical developmental trajectory for meditative practitioners. Instead, meditative development entails a complex interaction among the procedural operations being trained, the organization of practice, and the learner's capabilities. Longitudinal research could test this proposal by investigating how training conditions are associated with changes in fluency, perceived effort, stability, and transfer.

7. Predictions and Empirical Measures

The metacognitive skill acquisition account presented here generates testable predictions that can be examined across neural, behavioral, and phenomenological levels. No single measure would likely establish the automatization of meditative skill on its own. Instead, a convergence of evidence is required across several factors, including changes in performance, perceived effort, resistance to interference, and neural recruitment over the course of meditative training. These predictions can be evaluated by using established methods from skill-acquisition research.

7.1 Neural Correlates of Skill Acquisition

Skill acquisition is associated with well-established changes in the neural systems supporting performance. Research on motor-skill learning shows that practice reorganizes activity within cortical, striatal, and cerebellar systems, with their contributions varying across learning stages and task types (Doyon & Benali, 2005; Ungerleider et al., 2002). In a perceptuallearning task, learning to read mirror-reversed text was associated with increased striatal activity and decreased left hippocampal activity (Poldrack & Gabrieli, 2001). Practice on cognitive tasks has also been associated with reduced recruitment of prefrontal and parietal control regions (Chein & Schneider, 2005). Basal ganglia circuits are not limited to motor control. They are also thought to chunk cognitive action sequences into performance units (Graybiel, 1998). Internally cued mental actions, such as returning attention to the breath after noticing mind-wandering, could therefore plausibly be chunked in the same way.

If advanced meditation involves the automatization of metacognitive instructions, meditative practice may reveal a parallel neural shift. Early practice should rely more heavily on neural systems supporting explicit instruction and deliberate control, while skilled practice should involve more efficient and specialized patterns of activity supporting highly stable performance. Drawing on traditional descriptions of effortless concentration, Lutz et al. (2008) similarly predicted reduced recruitment of attention-regulation systems with extensive practice, interpreting the inverted-U pattern reported by Brefczynski-Lewis et al. (2007) as reflecting neural changes typical of skill acquisition in other domains.

Longitudinal meditation research provides evidence for changes in attentional stability and its neural correlates. Following a three-month intensive meditation retreat, Lutz et al. (2009) found a greater reduction in reaction-time variability during an auditory attention task in retreat participants than in a novice comparison group. This improvement was associated with increased trial-to-trial consistency of neural responses to attended tones. These findings support the trainability of attentional stability, although the nonrandomized design limits causal inference and the study did not directly assess dependence on explicit instructions.

Complementary evidence has linked distinct regulatory operations during meditation to neural activity. Hasenkamp et al. (2012) examined breath-focused meditation using practitioners' button presses to identify awareness of mind wandering and estimate surrounding periods of distraction, attentional shifting, and sustained attention. Awareness of mind wandering was associated with activity in salience-network regions, whereas shifting and sustaining attention recruited elements of the executive network. These findings provide neural correlates of the monitoring and control operations central to the present account, but do not establish how those operations become learned or automatized.

7.2 Behavioral Measures and Learning Signatures

Several measures from the skill-acquisition literature could be adapted to investigate this transition. Dual-task paradigms could assess how strongly the execution of a meditative skill interferes with a secondary task. The reduction of interference with training would suggest that the skill requires fewer cognitive resources. Resistance to disruption could be examined by testing whether capacities such as attentional stability or equanimity remain effective under different conditions such as emotional challenge, fatigue, or environmental distraction.

Subjective workload measures, including the NASA Task Load Index, could provide a complementary measure of reduced cognitive effort.

Repeated measurements across training could also reveal characteristic learning curves.

The proposed account predicts that observable components of meditative performance should improve rapidly during early training and more gradually as skill develops, potentially following the power-law pattern reported in perceptual-motor, cognitive, and attentional skill acquisition (Anderson, 1982; Logan, 1988; Newell & Rosenbloom, 1981; Shin et al., 2015). However, the form and rate of these learning curves may vary across meditative practices depending on the particular skills being trained (e.g., attentional stability, awareness, equanimity, effort regulation, compassion). Relevant outcomes might include the speed and reliability with which practitioners detect distraction, restore attention, regulate effort, or enter a specific experiential mode. These improvements should occur alongside reductions in perceived effort and working-memory dependence. Longitudinal designs would be especially useful because they could distinguish changes produced by training from pre-existing differences between basic and advanced practitioners.

The same developmental pattern may also extend to meta-awareness, with practice enabling practitioners to notice ongoing thoughts as mental events with less effort and less reliance on deliberate monitoring. This development could be tested by measuring reductions in subjective effort and dual-task interference, while assessing whether the ability to notice ongoing thoughts is maintained or improved.

Improved performance alone would not establish proceduralization. If practitioners become more proficient at a particular meditative operation but remain equally dependent on explicit instructions and equally susceptible to interference with maintaining those instructions, this would weigh against proceduralization as the explanation for that improvement. Such tests should compare the same practiced operation under comparable demands, since adopting a new technique or pursuing a more demanding target may renew the need for deliberate control.

For example, longitudinal research could test whether attentional restoration becomes less dependent on maintaining explicit instructions. This would require establishing that an interference manipulation disrupts instruction maintenance beyond its general attentional demands, and assessing whether any reduction in interference with training exceeds improvement attributable to practice on the secondary task itself. Researchers should distinguish how often distractions occur from the speed and reliability of restoring attention, while assessing secondary-task performance to account for changes in task priority. A training-related reduction in instruction-related interference, alongside maintained or improved restoration performance, would support reduced instruction dependence. Fewer distractions without this change would instead support improved attentional stability as an explanation for reduced effort. Reduced instruction dependence would be consistent with proceduralization, although it would not uniquely establish knowledge compilation over other learning mechanisms.

To test the proposed contribution to advanced meditation specifically, changes in instruction dependence should be examined alongside independently assessed attainment and stabilization of a specified meditative target, such as a particular absorption state or perceptual mode. Criteria for that target should be specified separately from the predicted learning signatures, so that reduced effort or automaticity is not itself treated as evidence of advanced attainment.

7.3 Phenomenological and Neurophenomenological Measures

Behavioral and neural measures can be further complemented by carefully structured first-person reports. Neurophenomenological approaches examine the dynamic relationships among reported experience, behavior, and neural activity rather than treating any one level as sufficient (Lutz, 2002). Advanced meditators may be especially informative participants because contemplative training can cultivate greater sensitivity to experience and the capacity to reliably enter and sustain specific experiential states across repeated investigations (Berkovich-Ohana et al., 2020; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026).

Reports of effort, fluency, stability, verbal mediation, and felt authorship could be collected along with behavioral and neural measures. The account predicts that increasing skill will be associated with less explicit and effortful regulation, greater experiential continuity, and more spontaneous initiation of metacognitive actions. A convergence among these first-person changes, behavioral learning signatures, and neural indicators would provide the strongest evidence for the proposed mechanism.

8. Future Directions

Future research could systematically test this framework longitudinally by examining whether meditative development reliably exhibits the predicted signatures of metacognitive skill acquisition. These include increasing automaticity, reduced cognitive effort, characteristic learning curves, and measurable changes in neural recruitment. Research could also determine whether similar patterns occur across the broader meditative activities and experiential domains identified by Sparby et al. (2026), or whether different practices develop through qualitatively distinct learning pathways.

Automatization may operate alongside other mechanisms of meditative development.

Laukkonen and Slagter (2021), for example, propose that meditation reduces habitual predictive processing, including predictions concerning the self and experiences beyond the present moment. Active inference models have also formalized meta-awareness and attentional control (Sandved-Smith et al., 2021). Reviewing applications of active inference to advanced meditation, Tal et al. (2026) identify an increasing emphasis on layer-specific precision re-weighting. The present account complements these approaches by proposing how practice may transform explicitly instructed metacognitive operations into increasingly fluent and automatic procedures.

For example, operations such as disengaging from conceptual elaboration and sustaining nonreactive observation could become increasingly automatic, helping practitioners loosen habitual predictive patterns with less deliberate effort. On this view, automatizing the regulation of mental activity could support a reduction in habitual processing. Whether these mechanisms develop together, and whether changes in metacognitive skill contribute to the changes described by predictive-processing accounts, remain questions for empirical investigation.

Future work could further examine meditative skill acquisition in individuals with psychiatric conditions involving altered metacognitive functioning, such as obsessivecompulsive disorder (OCD), and test whether individual differences in confidence, error monitoring, and control affect the observed rate or developmental trajectory of meditative development (Hoven et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2021).

Another question concerns how metacognitive skills become coordinated with increasing expertise. A basic meditator may need to perform monitoring, attentional control, and perceptual operations sequentially, whereas advanced practitioners may be able to coordinate several capacities simultaneously or in rapid succession. Research could examine whether there are limits on coordination and whether some combinations of skills are more seamlessly integrated. This framework may also help explain the persistent effects associated with meditative endpoints (MEND; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). Studies could investigate whether acquired metacognitive skills contribute to or mediate lasting changes in self-referential processing, affect, cognition, behavior, and well-being following MEND events. Together, these directions provide a testable research program for investigating how meditative training may produce increasingly stable, integrated, and enduring capacities.

9. Conclusion

This paper proposes that advanced meditation can be understood, in part, as a specialized domain of metacognitive skill involving the mastery of attentional and perceptual capacities.

Through practice, initially explicit, instruction-guided strategies become increasingly automatic, supporting more efficient, stable, and integrated metacognitive control. This transition may help explain the stabilization of advanced concentrative states and the integration of insight while preserving flexibility and higher-level strategic control. Automatization is therefore proposed as a candidate mechanism contributing to meditative development, rather than a complete account of meditative expertise. By connecting established principles of skill acquisition with neural findings and experiential descriptions, the framework offers a testable cognitive account of how advanced meditative capacities develop. Clarifying these mechanisms may contribute not only to a deeper scientific understanding of the mind and its capacity for transformation, but also to the development of educational and therapeutic practices that reduce suffering and support human flourishing.
