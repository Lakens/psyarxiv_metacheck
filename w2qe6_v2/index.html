Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Full text

Abstract

Dreams provide a unique window into human cognition, as their content may reflect individual and collective experiences. Here, we use language models to analyze 21,607 dream narratives, representing each report as a vector in a high-dimensional semantic space that enables continuous comparison without predefined categories. We ask whether different sources of information are organized within the same representational space and across multiple levels of semantic representation; we also tested how dream representational geometries evolve over time. We find that dreams produced by different individuals, as well as by individuals of different genders, occupy distinct regions of this space, particularly in abstract semantic representations. Similarity among descriptions of dreamers also predicts similarity in their dream content. Finally, we show how the semantic geometry of dream content evolves over time, with its trajectory shaped by the cumulative history of past reports. Together, this study provides evidence for the semantic structure of dream content and its evolution over time.

Introduction

Dreaming is a ubiquitous yet still poorly understood component of human cognition (Tononi et al., 2024). Across cultures and historical periods, dreams have been interpreted as meaningful reflections of internal states, memory processes and latent concerns (Freud, 1900; Domhoff, 2018; Tedlock, 1987). Within contemporary cognitive science, dreaming is increasingly viewed as a form of internally generated experience, in which perceptual, emotional and conceptual representations unfold in the absence of external input (Windt, 2015). Decades of research on dream content have indeed shown that dreams exhibit reliable patterns of similarity and difference across individuals, genders and contexts (Domhoff & Schneider, 2008a; Hall & Van de Castle, 1966). These findings suggest that dream content reflects both enduring characteristics of individual experience and variation across successive reports. An important question that follows is how these two aspects are related: how can dreams retain individual-specific patterns while incorporating different elements from one report to the next? Evidence that dream reports often incorporate elements of waking experiences, whereas complete episodic replay is rare, suggests that dream may arise through the retrieval and recombination of previously encountered content (Fosse et al., 2003).

From a theoretical perspective, traditional approaches to dream research have aimed to identify specific dimensions of dream content. Manual coding schemes (e.g., Hall & Van de Castle, 1966) have provided systematic measures of social interactions, emotions, activities and other features of dreams. These approaches have revealed both commonalities and reliable individual differences in dream content (Domhoff & Schneider, 2008a) and have been extended through large-scale digital approaches that allow dream reports to be analyzed more efficiently and objectively (Bulkeley, 2014).

More recently, computational approaches have applied natural language processing techniques to quantify semantic dimensions and lexical features of dream reports (Bulkeley & Graves, 2018; Elce et al., 2021; Elce et al., 2026). Large-scale automated analyses have further shown that dream reports contain systematic information about characteristics of the dreamers and their waking lives (Fogli et al., 2020). However, these approaches typically focus on predefined content dimensions, lexical features or specific aspects of dream reports, leaving open whether individual and collective experiences are also reflected in the overall semantic organization of the whole narratives. An approach based on distributed semantic representations offers a complementary perspective by testing whether dream content can be studied as a continuous space rather than as a set of predefined categories. In such a space, dreams can be compared according to graded semantic similarity, allowing the organization of dream content to be examined continuously rather than along a limited number of predefined dimensions. This perspective also connects the study of dream content to approaches used to characterize other forms of memory and mental representation, in which similarity and distance between representations can be examined within a continuous representational space (Günther et al., 2019; Piantadosi et al., 2024).

Representation-learning approaches provide an opportunity to examine this possibility without specifying in advance which features of dream content should account for similarities between reports. Transformer-based language models encode texts as high-dimensional vectors that capture semantic relationships across multiple levels of abstraction (Devlin et al., 2019; Jawahar et al., 2019; Vaswani et al., 2017). These models have been used to map the structure of conceptual knowledge, social representations and individual differences in language use, revealing coherent geometric organization in high-dimensional semantic spaces (Demszky et al., 2023; Lake & Murphy, 2021).

Applying this framework to dream reports makes it possible to ask whether the individual organization previously observed in specific dimensions of dream content is also reflected in the distributed semantic representation of the entire narrative and whether this organization varies across levels of semantic representation.

A continuous semantic space describes relationships among dream reports, but it does not by itself explain how those relationships evolve over time. Addressing this question requires a computational account of how earlier representations may contribute to later content. A basis for such an account is provided by models of memory retrieval, such as the multiple-trace model MINERVA2. In this model, a given cue activates stored experiences according to their similarity, and the activated traces combine into an echo whose content depends on both the cue and the available history (e.g., Hintzman, 1984 Hintzman, , 1986)). This architecture specifies how a representation can draw on several earlier experiences while remaining dependent on the current context. Applying this model to dream representations would allow us to test whether mixtures of earlier reports reconstruct current content, predict the next report, and benefit from a broader history. This account would thus connect the descriptive geometry of dreams to a computational account of representational continuity over time.

Here, we adopt this approach to investigate organization of dream content across representational levels. Using a large-scale corpus of over 21,000 dream reports (Bertolini, 2024; Domhoff & Schneider, 2008b), we construct a high-dimensional semantic space in which each dream is represented as a vector. We then test whether this space exhibits systematic organization along multiple dimensions, including individual identity, gender, and historical time. Our primary focus is on individual identity: if individual differences in dreaming are reflected in the distributed semantic organization of dream narratives, dreams produced by the same individual should be more similar to one another than dreams produced by different individuals. We further examine whether this organization varies across the representational hierarchy of the transformer model(s), also considering gender and temporal information as complementary dimensions of organization. Finally, we apply a MINERVA2-inspired retrieval procedure within individuals to test if earlier dream reports can help explain and predict the content of later reports.

By combining large-scale dream data with representation learning and a classical model of memory retrieval, this work provides a quantitative framework for investigating both the structure and temporal evolution of dream narratives, thus connecting an observable geometry of dream reports to explicit computations that can be evaluated against subsequent content. More broadly, this framework provides testable quantitative account on the organization of internally generated mental representations.

Method

Material

The analyzed data come from the public DreamBank archive (https://dreambank.net/), which contains more than 21,000 English dream reports, transcribed from a wide variety of individuals and compiled by researchers specializing in the study of sleep and dream activity. Within the archive, there are both personal collections, attributed to individual dreamers, and multi-subject dream groups, organized by demographic or thematic criteria.

The version selected for this experiment is the one compatible with the dataset distributed by Bertolini (2024). The main reason behind this choice was its solid structure and the presence of many informative metadata fields. This variant consists of 21,607 unique dreams from 57 different dreamers (31 females, 26 males). The metadata associated with each dream that were retrieved for our purposes included: i) the dreamer's gender (available for all dreams); ii) the date in which the dream was experienced or transcribed (when possible, defined unambiguously, available for 19,519 dreams); iii) the dreamer's identity, whenever it was possible to attribute the dream to a specific person or group of people; and iv) a short description (e.g., "Melissa: a young girl", "Elizabeth: a woman in her 40s", "Pegasus: a factory worker") associated to each dreamer.

Sentence embeddings: the construction of the dream space

Sentence vectors for all the dreams included were retrieved from https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2 via the Hugging face website (https://huggingface.co/). The pretrained sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2 is a Sentence-Transformers encoder built on the semantic representation.

At the architectural level, the model comprises an input embedding layer followed by 12 stacked transformer encoder layers with 768 hidden dimensions and 12 self-attention heads, and a final sentence-pooling layer that aggregates token-level outputs into a 768-dimensional dense embedding suitable for cosine-similarity-based comparison. In this stack, the input layer indexes subword token identities and positional information through learned embeddings, whereas the subsequent transformer blocks iteratively transform those token representations by integrating information from the full sequence through self-attention. This model is particularly suited for semantic search, clustering, and similarity estimation, with a practical maximum sequence length of 384 tokens; it was trained on more than one billion sentence pairs, and it is among the highest-quality general-purpose models in its series.

Importantly, the depth of the encoder can be interpreted in representational terms: evidence from layer-probing studies on BERT-like contextual encoders shows that earlier layers tend to retain more local and surface-oriented information -such as lexical identity, word form, and short-range syntactic regularities -whereas later layers encode increasingly context-sensitive, relational, and abstract information, including sentence-level semantics and task-relevant meaning. This indicates that the different layers encode a rich hierarchy of linguistic information, with surface features at the bottom, syntactic features in the middle and semantic ones at the top (e.g., Jawahar et al., 2019; Tenney et al., 2019).

With this model, we implemented a custom layer-wise extraction procedure to derive document embeddings from all hidden layers of the MPNet encoder. Each dream was tokenized once, segmented into overlapping chunks of up to 384 tokens (stride = 128), and encoded in batches with hidden-state output enabled. For each transformer layer (1-12), token-level hidden states were meanpooled over the valid tokens in each chunk using the attention mask, yielding one chunk embedding per layer; chunk embeddings were L2-normalized, averaged across all chunks belonging to the same text, and normalized again to obtain one final text embedding for that layer. This produced, for every input text, 12 distinct 768-dimensional representations corresponding to successive processing depths of the encoder. The resulting layer-specific matrices were stored separately, allowing direct comparison of embeddings derived from shallower versus deeper layers. This design is theoretically motivated by the view that lower layers in transformer encoders capture more lexical and structurally local information, whereas higher layers encode progressively more abstract and contextually integrated semantic content. A representation of these steps is included in Figure 1A.

Results

Six analyses were carried out (Figure 1B); complete outputs are available in the Supplementary Material and the OSF repository here: https://osf.io/v2spw/. The first four analyses were repeated separately for each of the 12 transformer layers to examine where different sources of information were most strongly expressed, with earlier layers expected to preserve more lexical information and later layers more integrated semantic structure.

These analyses used pairwise cosine similarities as the dependent variable across 20 Monte Carlo resampling iterations. For identity and gender, each target dream was paired with matched and mismatched reports; for decade, similarity was modeled as a function of temporal distance. In each iteration and layer, two nested linear mixed-effects models were fitted: a null model with a random intercept for the target dream and a comparison model adding the fixed effect of interest. Performance was summarized by ΔAIC relative to the null model (Akaike, 1973) and marginal R². Negative ΔAIC indicated improved fit, while larger marginal R² indicated stronger representation of the targeted property. This procedure provides k dimensions (2 in our case) coordinates of a set of items given their distance matrix.

For dreamer identity, each target dream was paired with one other dream from the same individual and one from a different individual. Adding pair type improved model fit at every layer, with increasingly negative ΔAIC and larger marginal R² in deeper layers, particularly layer 12 (Figure 3A).

These results indicate that person-specific regularities were represented throughout the network but most strongly in its deepest layers. The identity analysis was repeated after removing manually identified candidate personal names and re-encoding the cleaned reports using the same chunking and pooling procedure. The same layer-wise pattern was observed, indicating that person-specific structure extended beyond explicit name references (see Supplementary Material).

For gender, matched reports came from a different dreamer of the same gender, whereas mismatched reports came from a different-gender dreamer. Both conditions therefore excluded within-dreamer comparisons. Gender effects were much smaller than identity effects: improvements in model fit were less consistent, and marginal R² remained very small overall. Nevertheless, both measures showed the clearest effects at layer 12 (Figure 3B), suggesting that gender-related regularities were most strongly expressed in the deepest representations.

For decade, analyses included 19,519 reports from nine decades spanning the 1930s-2010s, retaining decades with more than 100 reports. Each target dream was paired with one report from the same decade and additional reports from other available decades, all from different dreamers. Absolute decade difference was entered as a continuous predictor to test whether semantic similarity decreased with historical separation. Temporal distance showed a negative association with similarity, strongest in earlier layers and progressively weaker with depth (Figure 3C). In contrast to identity and gender, historical proximity was therefore most clearly represented in layers 1-3, consistent with a contribution of variation in linguistic expression across periods. Fourth, complementing these effects on temporal proximity, we performed a representational similarity analysis (RSA) comparing temporal distance, expressed as the absolute distance in days between dream reports (Figure 4A), with dream cosine similarities extracted from the best embedding layer in the decade analysis, that is, the second layer (Figure 4B). We considered only pairs of dreams belonging to the same dreamer, as this allowed us to assess the structural alignment between temporal distance and embedding-derived dream representations within individualsfoot_0 . We further restricted the analysis to dreamers with at least 100 dream reports, resulting in 25 dreamers.

For each dreamer, we extracted the upper triangle of the within-dreamer pairwise matrices and computed a Spearman rank correlation between temporal distance and dream cosine similarity.

Participant-specific Spearman correlations were then Fisher-z transformed and combined using a random-effects meta-analysis, allowing the strength of the temporal-distance association to vary across participants while avoiding disproportionate influence from dreamers contributing very large numbers of pairwise observations. Results showed a significant average negative association between temporal distance and semantic similarity, pooled ρ = -.067, 95% cluster-robust CI [-.100, -.033], t(20.59) = -4.08, Holm-adjusted p = .0016. Thus, dreams farther apart in time were, on average, less similar in their semantic contents (Figure 4C). Then, we performed an additional RSA comparing similarity structures at two levels of abstraction, using the best embedding layer from the dreamer identity analysis. First, cosine similarities were computed among embeddings representing dreamer descriptions (Figure 5A). Second, dream embeddings were averaged within each dreamer, and cosine similarities were computed among these dreamer-level centroids (Figure 5B). We then extracted the upper triangles of the two similarity matrices and assessed their association using a classical RSA approach based on Spearman rank correlation. Statistical significance was evaluated with a permutation test in which the correspondence between the two similarity vectors was randomized across 10,000 permutations.

Results showed that similarity among dreamers' descriptions was positively associated with similarity among the corresponding dream contents, Spearman's ρ = .19, permutation p < .001. This indicates that pairs of dreamers described as more similar also tended to produce more similar dream representations. The same pattern remained significant after removing data points in which dreamer similarity was above 3 SD from the sample mean, Spearman's ρ = .17, permutation p < .001 (Figure 5C). Taken together, these results suggest that the semantic organization of dream content mirrors the semantic organization of dreamer-level descriptions, consistent with a meaningful alignment between person-level descriptive profiles and the aggregate structure of their dream representations. As a final set of analyses, we examined whether the temporal development of dream content could be characterized using distributed representations of each dreamer's earlier reports. We used a MINERVA2-inspired procedure in which earlier dream representations were weighted (by the cube of their positive cosine similarity to the current representation) and combined into a historical mixture. Cubing similarity emphasizes earlier content most closely related to the current observation while allowing several reports to contribute. This approach tested whether the temporal organization of dream representations in semantic space could be captured by retrieving and combining content from the dreamer's own history. Analyses included 28 individual dreamers with at least 50 distinct recording dates; collections pooling multiple individuals were excluded, and embeddings from dream reports recorded on the same date were averaged to form a single observation. Predictions for the next recording date were evaluated on the final 40% of each series, with the model layer and memory settings selected using only the training portions of the other dreamers' series. For each prediction, the current observation served as the retrieval cue, and accuracy was measured as the cosine similarity between the historical mixture and the next observation, which was excluded from the mixture.

The individual-observation comparisons tested the benefit of combining historical content, while the comparison with the strongest training-selected linearly weighted mixture assessed the added predictive value of the cubic version. Across 4,862 predictions, distributed mixtures outperformed both the most recent observation (mean gain in cosine similarity = .094, 95% CI [.084, .105], Holmadjusted p = .003) and the most similar individual historical observation (mean gain = .056, 95% CI [.048, .065], Holm-adjusted p = .003), while the gain over the strongest training-selected linearly weighted historical mixture was negligible and not significant (mean gain = .00024, 95% CI [-.00116, Next, we assessed whether the current dream representation could be reconstructed from contentdependent mixtures of earlier observations. Here, the historical mixture was compared with the current observation itself, rather than the next observation. The unweighted historical centroid, defined as the mean of earlier representations, provided a reference for assessing the contribution of content-dependent weighting; this analysis therefore measures recurrence of earlier content. At layer 12, the unrestricted historical mixture achieved a mean reconstruction cosine similarity of .696, 95% CI [.677, .717], and exceeded the unweighted historical centroid by .051, 95% CI [.042, .059], Holmadjusted p = .040, thus indicating that current dream content aligned with a similarity-weighted combination of earlier content (Figure 6B). This supports a content-dependent account of recurrence in dream geometry, linking current representations to combinations of related earlier representations.

Finally, we tested whether prediction depended primarily on recent contents or on a broader history (Figure 6C). Exponential decay progressively down-weighted older observations rather than excluding them, whereas fixed windows retained only the specified number of most recent recording dates; the unrestricted history included all earlier observations without age-based down-weighting.

On the same held-out dates and at the training-selected layers, exponential decay with 7-day and 30day time constants reduced accuracy relative to the unrestricted history (mean differences in cosine similarity = -.026, 95% CI [-.035, -.019], Holm-adjusted p = .007, and -.010, 95% CI [-.017, -.004], Holm-adjusted p = .012, respectively), while restricting the mixture to the last 100 recording dates produced a significant improvement (mean difference = .0010, 95% CI [.0004, .0017], Holmadjusted p = .028). These results indicate that prediction benefited from a broad history, while strongly emphasizing the most recent reports reduced performance.

Overall, this last set of analyses reveal a distributed temporal structure in the semantic geometry of dreams: current reports align with combinations of earlier content, and broad temporal histories predict subsequent reports better than single observations or strongly recent histories. Together, these findings support MINERVA2-inspired retrieval as a representational account of the temporal component of dream geometry, capturing both the recurrence of earlier content and the continuity of subsequent reports within a broad personal history. This approach connects the temporal dynamics of dream content to established computational accounts of memory retrieval and provides a basis for investigating how memory processes may contribute to the continuity of dream representations over time.

Robustness analyses

As a robustness check, we repeated all analyses with two additional frozen encoders: MiniLM-L12 (all-MiniLM-L12-v2; Wang et al., 2020), a compact distilled model with 12 layers and 384dimensional vectors, and Nomic Embed v1.5 (Nussbaum et al., 2024), a bidirectional model with 12 layers, 768-dimensional vectors and an 8,192-token context. Both models replicated the main patterns for identity (including the control analysis without personal names), gender, decade (Figure 7A) and both temporal as well as dreamer description RSAs (Figure 7B). They also replicated improved prediction from distributed histories over single earlier observations, reconstruction from content-dependent mixtures, and reduced prediction under strong recency weighting (Figure 7C). These replications across three different encoders strengthen the interpretation that the principal findings reflect reproducible structure in the reports. That is, while these encoders differ in their architecture, dimensionality, training, and text-length handling, the same broad organization and historical-mixture findings of dream content are observed.

Discussion

The present findings provide converging evidence that dream content exhibits systematic organization within a continuous semantic space. By mapping large-scale dream narratives into a high-dimensional representational space, we show that dreams are systematically organized along multiple dimensions, reflecting stable regularities across individuals, gender and historical context. This organization is most evident at more abstract levels of representation for identity and gender, suggesting that it reflects coherent semantic regularities, while historical-distance associations are stronger in shallower representations. Together, these results demonstrate that personal, demographic and historical dimensions can be represented within the same semantic space and across multiple representational levels. The MINERVA2-inspired analyses extend this geometry to an explicit account of within-person temporal continuity, where content-dependent mixtures connect current reports to earlier representations and predict subsequent content better than individual observations. Notably, these findings were replicated across three different encoders, supporting their robustness.

A central implication of this work is that dream content preserves stable structure across multiple scales simultaneously. Dreams cluster according to person-specific semantic signatures, indicating that they are shaped by enduring aspects of individual experience, memory and cognitive organization. At the same time, broader regularities are embedded within the same representational space, suggesting that dream content reflects both individual and shared dimensions of experience. This perspective is consistent with contemporary accounts of dreaming as a form of internally generated cognition grounded in the same representational systems that support waking thought (Windt, 2015). More generally, representing dream reports in a continuous semantic space provides a way to characterize graded relationships among dreams rather than restricting their comparison to predefined content categories. This allows individual and contextual variation to be examined as dimensions of a common representational space, while preserving the possibility that different levels of representation capture different aspects of dream content. Temporal structure provides complementary support for this interpretation. The finding that similarity between dream reports decreases gradually with increasing temporal distance suggests that dream content varies systematically across historical time. This result aligns with recent longitudinal and large-scale computational evidence showing that dream reports vary systematically with changes in waking-life context. For example, Elce and colleagues (2026) showed that dream features changed longitudinally over the years following the COVID-19 pandemic, progressively normalizing as the broader epidemiological context changed (for similar results, see Das et al., 2025). However, the effect observed here was most pronounced in lower-order representations, which is consistent with a contribution from language use and reporting style. This distinction is important because semantic similarity in written reports may reflect both the content being described and characteristics of language use and reporting style. As individuals navigate evolving cultural, technological and social environments, the contents of their waking experiences are continuously reshaped, with dreams appearing to preserve and recombine these changes. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that dreaming reflects memory consolidation and recombination processes (Hudachek & Wamsley, 2023; Wamsley et al., 2010; Wamsley & Stickgold, 2010) as well as with accounts emphasizing the role of mind-wandering and spontaneous thought in structuring dream experience (Fox et al., 2013).

More directly, these results are closely aligned with the continuity hypothesis of dreaming (Domhoff, 2011), according to which dreams are continuous with the thoughts, emotions, concerns, and social worlds that organize waking life. From this perspective, the gradual temporal drift observed in dreamreport similarity may reflect the changing structure of the experiences, language and contexts in which dreamers live and report their dreams.

The historical-mixture analyses provide a computational account of the temporal component of dream geometry. In MINERVA2, separate experiences jointly contribute to retrieval, with their relative contribution determined by the current cue (Hintzman, 1984 (Hintzman, , 1986)). Applied to dream sequences, this architecture captures how current representations align with combinations of earlier content: historical mixtures predicted subsequent representations more accurately than individual observations, and content-dependent mixtures reconstructed current representations more accurately than an unweighted historical centroid. The former result establishes predictive value on later recording dates, while the latter describes cue-dependent recurrence, because the current report itself determines the retrieval weights. Together, these findings support distributed retrieval as a useful representational model of dream continuity and they are consistent with the incorporation and recombination of memory elements in dreaming (Fosse et al., 2003; Wamsley & Stickgold, 2010).

Memory breadth further clarifies this account as strongly down-weighting older observations impaired prediction, indicating that useful historical information extends beyond the most recent reports. Retaining the last 100 recording dates yielded a small improvement over the unrestricted history, indicating that a useful trace can be broadly distributed while remaining (temporally) bounded. On a theoretical level, these results favor temporally extended integration within a personal history, rather than a model dominated by very recent content, and the absence of a reliable cubic advantage leaves the precise selectivity of the activation rule open.

More generally, these findings indicate that variability in dream content is organized along multiple dimensions that integrate personal experience, demographic variation and historical context within a common representational space. Differences across individuals and groups do not arise as isolated effects but as variations within a common representational geometry shaped by lived experience. This aligns with broader work in cognitive science and computational linguistics showing that semantic spaces derived from language encode systematic information about social categories, individual differences and cultural change (Demszky et al., 2023; Kozlowski et al., 2019). The present results extend this perspective to dream reports, showing that multiple sources of variation can be examined simultaneously within a continuous semantic representation of dream content. The same framework characterizes person-specific organization and within-person continuity, providing a basis for testing how retrieval settings alter the temporal relationships among dream representations.

A potential limitation of the present work is that it relies on dream narratives rather than direct measures of dream experience. Dream reports are reconstructed after awakening and are therefore influenced by metacognitive and linguistic processes, including memory reconstruction, narrative organization and communicative constraints (Montangero, 2012). As a result, part of the observed structure may reflect how dreams are reported rather than how they are experienced. This limitation is particularly relevant for a semantic analysis, because similarities between reports may arise from shared content as well as from shared ways of describing that content (Koppel et al., 2002; Newman et al., 2008). However, narrative reports remain the primary method for accessing dream content in both experimental research and applied contexts such as psychotherapy (Domhoff, 2018; Mallett et al., 2024; Pesant & Zadra, 2004). Although recent neuroimaging work has begun to identify neural markers of dreaming and, in some cases, to decode aspects of dream-related visual imagery, these approaches remain closer to proof-of-concept demonstrations than to scalable alternatives to dream reports. For example, Horikawa and colleagues (2013) showed that machine-learning models could predict aspects of visual imagery from fMRI activity during the sleep-onset period, but this work focused on early sleep and on broad visual contents rather than on the full semantic structure of dreams during stable sleep. Similarly, Siclari et al. (2017) identified neural correlates of dreaming across REM and NREM sleep, including activity in posterior cortical regions associated with reports of experience upon awakening, but this approach primarily addressed the presence and neural correlates of conscious experience rather than decoding the detailed content of dreams. Thus, while neural approaches offer a promising direction for future research, they do not yet provide a scalable substitute for narrative dream reports in large-scale analyses of dream content.

Taken together, these findings characterize dream reports as a structured semantic geometry and provide a MINERVA2-inspired model of its temporal continuity. Personal, demographic and historical dimensions are expressed across representational levels, while distributed histories capture recurrence and predict subsequent content and replication across frozen encoders makes this account less dependent on any single representation model. The resulting framework gives dream research explicit, testable computations for relating each report to its personal history. Future work combining this approach with independent measures of waking experience, dream content and memory will be important for determining which aspects of this geometry reflect the experience of dreaming itself and which reflect the processes involved in remembering and reporting dreams.
