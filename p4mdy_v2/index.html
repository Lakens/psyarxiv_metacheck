Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Most individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) exhibit sensory challenges in daily life. Findings regarding temporal information processing in autism have been inconsistent.

The association between subjective or caregiver-reported sensory abnormality and impaired time sensitivity in autism is still unclear.

Objective: The aim of this systematic review is to synthesize the evidence of the relationship between sensory features and time perception in autistic individuals.

We searched the literature databases of PubMed, Scopus, and PsycINFO by using terms relevant to autism, time perception, and sensory feature. We excluded reports that used no comparison groups, that set no experimental condition to examine the change of response to a time, and that did not assess self-or parent/caregiver-reported sensory features. We checked the quality of the included studies by using checklists relevant to the individual article types. We summarized the results of group differences of sensory abnormality and time sensitivity and/or correlations between these indices and synthesized the findings based on the study characteristics.

Results: The database searches detected 773 titles, and we finally included 20 articles after screening. Seventeen quantitative research articles that met the inclusion criteria included a total of 1,136 participants (503 autistic individuals and 633 controls). Most reported heightened sensory abnormalities in autism, but a majority of the studies showed comparable time sensitivity examined by behavioral, neural, and physiological measures relative to controls. The included narrative reviews discussed the potential association between the sensory abnormality and poor temporal processing in autism. However, synthesized findings from the experimental studies indicated that no study reported an association between sensory abnormality and reduced sensitivity to time. Reported correlations between sensory abnormality and time sensitivity were inconsistent.

In contrast to the prevailing narrative of poor temporal processing in individuals with autism, its linkage with sensory features is not evident. Some studies displayed inappropriate use of sensory assessment tools that may also be a limitation of the present findings.

Introduction

The brain organizes sensory information to create our perception of time. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder, and 90% of autistic individuals may have altered sensory experiences, including sensory hyper-or hyporesponsiveness and seeking behavior [1]. It has been suggested that neural disinhibition by altered functioning of brain metabolites related to neurotransmission may be involved in autistic symptoms [2]. The reduction of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) has been broadly reported [3], and a growing number of empirical studies have suggested an association between neural excitatory/inhibitory imbalance and sensory abnormality in autism [4] [5] [6] [7]. Such neurophysiological alternation is expected to affect a variety of information processing, including time perception.

The existence of a tight relationship between the dynamics of brain metabolites and temporal information processing has been broadly accepted, and thus temporal distortion in disorders and diseases relevant to neurotransmitters is frequently suggested [8]. However, previous systematic reviews have reported mixed findings regarding temporal processing differences between autistic and non-autistic individuals [9, 10].

A systematic review examined the alternation of temporal information processing in autism based on three types of experimental tasks: time sensitivity, interval timing, and higherorder time perception [9]. According to this article, there are inconsistent findings regarding group-level differences in performance of these task types among the 46 included studies. The discrepancy is especially evident in studies that used simple demand; for example, a discrimination task for durations between two beeps. In contrast, task performance demanding the cognitive function of prospective memory such as "we will lunch in five minutes" were consistently worse in autistic cohorts. This suggests that group differences in tasks may be enhanced by the cognitive demands or complexities of information, rather than by temporal information processing itself.

Another meta-analysis examined the performance of timing tasks in individuals with autism or dyslexia [10]. This review focused on studies using temporal order judgment (TOJ) and simultaneity (synchrony) judgment (SJ) tasks and analyzed the findings according to unisensory and multisensory domains. For these tasks, subjects received two unisensory or multisensory stimuli with synchronous or asynchronous intervals ranging from milliseconds to supraseconds (stimulus onset asynchrony [SOA]). In the TOJ tasks, subjects were required to answer which stimulus occurred earlier or later, whereas, in the SJ tasks, subjects had to report whether the stimuli were presented synchronously or asynchronously. The temporal thresholds of these tasks were calculated based on a sufficient time interval to correctly answer. In the review, 18 studies on autistic individuals and 13 studies on dyslexic individuals were included in the meta-analysis.

Overall, the authors found a moderate effect of poorer sensitivity to time interval in autistic individuals compared with controls. The effect was very small in unisensory studies but moderate in multisensory studies, although this difference in effect was not significant. Summarizing the two evidence syntheses, group-level difference seemed to be derived from information complexity, such as the involvement of other cognitive function or the number of sensory modalities, rather than temporal processing itself.

Considering the sophisticated heterogeneity of sensory features in autism, it is not surprising that temporal processing of sensory stimuli may not differ from that in non-autistic individuals at a group level. Rather, it is important to elucidate how temporal processing characteristics relate to daily sensory challenges in autism. In this review, we synthesized findings examining the associations between timing tasks and sensory symptoms that are clinically or subjectively assessed from the perspective of autistic individuals and their caregivers. The aim of this systematic review was to elucidate the relationship between sensory features and time perception in individuals with autism. Since time perception should assume the specific biological response to temporal differences, any studies that examined behavioral, neural, and physiological measures were deemed eligible for the review.

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

Search Strategy

We designed a search strategy based on the PICO framework to ensure the validity, applicability, and completeness of the systematic review. We defined "Patient" as ASD, "Intervention" as sensory symptom, "Comparison" as normal sensation, and "Outcome" as performance of the time perception task. Search terms were related to autism, time perception, and sensory hyperresponsiveness. Table 1 shows the full queries used for the search on each database. Based on the definitions of 125 behavioral, physiological, and neural responses to a temporal dimension of stimulus. Quality Assessments

The quality of each study included in our review was assessed using checklists; the Kmet checklist (QUALSYST, [13]) was used for research reports, including theses, and the Scale for the Quality Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) [14] was used for narrative reviews. Items considered as not applicable to the quality assessment were excluded from the calculation of total score. The SANRA checklist includes six items, ranked on a scale from 0 to 2, where a higher number indicates higher quality. Using those tools, the quality of the included articles was assessed independently by two reviewers (T.A. and M.I.).

Risk of bias Assessments

We performed the risk of bias assessment for the included experimental studies by using JBI Assessment of Risk of Bias for Analytical Cross-sectional Studies [15]. This assessment comprises eight items addressing participant eligibility and characteristics, measurement of exposures and outcomes, identification and management of confounding factors, and statistical analysis. Each item is rated as "Yes," "No," "Unclear," or "Not applicable," based on the information reported in the study. Rather than applying a universal numerical cutoff, item-level judgments are used to identify potential sources of bias. The risk of bias assessment was performed by two authors (T.A. and M.I.) independently.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

Quantitative studies were coded into four categories: experiments measuring behavioral indices for time sensitivity; brain responses using, for example, electroencephalogram (EEG) and magnetoencephalogram (MEG); physiological indices such as pupillary response; and both behavioral and brain response using, for example, event-related potential (ERP) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). To examine associations between performance on timing tasks and sensory features in autism, we prioritized findings based on correlational analyses in clinical groups. If correlational analyses were not performed in the study, we checked the differences of indices between groups and estimated the group-level association between metrics of interest. If there was a statistical value in the study, we reported the effect size of group differences and correlations, based on Cohen's d and correlation coefficients, respectively. If no effect size value was given but mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported in the text, we calculated Cohen's d. If an article reported the effect size, mean, and SD but not the 95% confidence interval (CI), we calculated the CI. If the experimental study did not report group differences or values of the outcome of interest but reported individual data graphically, we estimated the values shown in the graph using WebPlotDigitizer (https://automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer/). WebPlotDigitizer was used to extract individual data points from scatterplots (e.g., [16]). The reliability and validity of this method have been confirmed [17]. Estimated individual data were used for the statistical analyses; the Mann-Whitney U test and permutation-based correlational analysis with 2,000 simulations were used to examine group differences and correlation within a group, respectively. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2. The narrative reviews included in this study were summarized to identify the theoretical arguments linking temporal processing to sensory features in autism and to compare these arguments with the empirical findings. These narrative reviews were considered separately from the experimental studies and were not used to draw conclusions about correlations between temporal processing and sensory features. They were not assigned statistical weights.

In research in children, one study examined tactile TOJ among a relatively large sample of autistic children with and without attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and compared their task performance with that of neurotypical (typically developing, TD) children [25]. However, the children showed a comparable Sensitivity Index in SP-2 between groups.

There were no correlations between the tactile or sensitivity index and task performance.

Correlation analyses were not reported between the auditory raw score and the task, nor between the task performance at the shorter SOA and scores. Feldman et al. [22] examined the temporal binding window (TBW) of stimulus simultaneity by using the McGurk illusion. They used composite scores of SEQ and SP and assessed hyper-and hyporesponsiveness and seeking characteristics, and reported greater scores of those metrics in autistic children (ds ≤ 1.33; means and SDs were not reported). They reported positive correlations between TBW and hyporesponsiveness/seeking scores across all children (rs = 0.44). No reports of group differences of TBW and the correlation in the independent autistic group were described.

In research in adults, 5 studies examined TOJ in autistic adults [18, 27, [31] [32] [33]. Poole et al. [31] used multisensory TOJ tasks combining visual, auditory, and tactile, which delivered two stimuli bilaterally. Another study by the same group performed unisensory visual and tactile GD and multisensory TOJ tasks and examined the distractor effect of crossmodal congruencies [32].

They reported that GSQ scores were higher in autistic individuals than in controls (GSQ total: 33 [1.31, 2.95]; hypersensitivity: 1.77 [1.0, 2.54]; hyposensitivity: 2.12 [1.3, 2.93]; effect sizes and CIs were calculated by the authors of this review) although mean thresholds were comparable.

The articles [31, 32] did not report correlational analyses in the independent autistic group. Poole and colleagues also performed a multiple audiovisual timing assessment, including TOJ, temporal generalization, verbal estimation, and temporal discrimination tasks in a larger sample of 58 autistic and 91 non-autistic individuals [33]. They revealed reduced sensitivity of temporal generalization in autistic individuals by showing the group effect on the task (η² = 0.043). There were no group differences or effects on other tasks. The autistic group showed higher scores of GSQ than did controls (d = 2.19, CI = [1.77, 2.61]). The authors also conducted a hierarchical clustering on principal components using the task metrics and questionnaires including GSQ and across all subjects and found three clusters. The cluster that was predominantly autistic individuals (36/40) showed increased timing performance and more GSQ scores. There was no report of correlational analysis with linear regression model in an independent clinical cohort. Ide et al. [27] examined a TOJ delivering vibrotactile stimuli to bilateral index fingers. They found comparable TOJ threshold and sensory hyperreactivity scores of AASP between groups. However, there were negative correlations between the threshold and scores (rs ≤ -0.63), indicating higher precision of the task and sensory hyperresponsiveness. Kaneko et al. [30] introduced a vibrotactile GD task and AASP. They found a higher score of Sensation avoiding in the autistic group compared with controls (d = 0.76, CI = [0.13, 1.38]) but comparable scores in the other three domains; they also reported a negative correlation between the GD threshold and Sensation avoiding score (r = -0.63), but not for the other domains.

In summary, most behavioral studies in autistic children found a comparable or increased threshold in timing tasks. One study reported a positive correlation between the multisensory temporal threshold and hyporesponsiveness or seeking metrics [22]. Studies in adults used a variety of timing tasks but increased thresholds in autistic groups were found only for the temporal generalization task [33]. Interestingly, some correlation-based analyses revealed negative associations between reduced threshold and heightened sensory scores in autistic or predominantly autistic cohorts [27, 30, 33].

One research study in children [24] examined the somatosensory cortex (S1) response to difference of tactile time intervals that measured by MEG. The authors delivered pneumatic tactile pulses to two fingertips of the unilateral hand. The researchers used the touch score of the One study reported an interventional experiment in adults [26] that examined the dose effect of arbaclofen, a GABAB receptor agonist, on brain responses measured by EEG using the oddball paradigm for the response to auditory duration differences (i.e., ERP). They found that the P1 EEG component amplitude in response to duration deviants under the placebo condition was greater in autistic adults (N = 20) than in control adults (N = 22). The GABAB sensitivity index was smaller in the autistic group. The authors assessed sensory traits in each of 19 autistic and control participants by using Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ), extracting item #5 regarding auditory ("I often notice small sounds when others do not"). The question item #5 score was greater in the autistic group than in the control group (d = 1.82, CI = [1.06, 2.58], calculated by the authors). There was a negative correlation between the AQ-Q5 and GABAB sensitivity index across all participants (r = -0.45) but no correlation in the independent autistic group (r = -0.45, p = 0.11). We estimated that statistical power (1 -β) of the latter within-group correlation was 0.51, indicating that if there was statistical significance, it would be detected at 51% of chance.

In summary, two studies in children reported comparable or reduced brain response to temporal change of stimuli, and no studies reported correlational analysis between sensory traits and time sensitivity indices. One study in adults demonstrated an enhanced response to duration change in autism. The change of time sensitivity by dosage may be involved in auditory sensitivity assessed by AQ, but we could not estimate that this was also evident within autistic individuals.

Physiological measures

Our search revealed one study on pupillary responses to stimulus asynchrony in children [34]. The authors manipulated the synchrony of audiovisual stimulus (simultaneity) and its dimensions of socialness, language, and emotion. The subject's task was to simply view a short clip, but they were explicitly told about the audiovisual asynchrony in the clip beforehand. To assess the sensory features, the authors used the SP-2, but did not report group differences in the scores. There was no interaction effect between group and synchrony, but time-course analyses revealed group effects on pupil dilation under social conditions, both synchronous and asynchronous. We could not determine whether the autistic children were more or less sensitive to the stimulus synchrony from the results. Correlation analyses showed a negative correlation between the pupillary response to social stimuli and SP-2, but there was no report of correlation analysis of the response to synchrony.

Behavioral and brain response measures

Foss-Feig et al. [23] performed an ERP study on children, examining the auditory GD threshold and EEG components in response to stimuli. They used the auditory section and poor registration scores in SP, but did not report group differences in the scores. Thus, we extracted the individual data from the graph in the article (see Methods). In the visually inspected scatter plot of the P2 amplitudes and auditory scores, data from 9 autistic (mean ± SD = 23.78 ± 5.93) and 12 non-autistic (35.92 ± 2.35) children were available. A less abnormal auditory processing score in autism was evident (Mann-Whitney U test: W = 2.5, p < 0.001, effect sizes r = 0.8 and d = -2.86, CI = [-4.08, -1.63]). The article reported that autistic children had a higher GD threshold than non-autistic children. There were correlations between the central P2 amplitude to detected gaps and auditory processing abnormalities (r = 0.45) and poorer registration of sensory input (r = 0.63) across the children. There was no report of the correlations in the independent autistic group.

One study in adults performed an fMRI experiment [18] and examined the effect of presenting an emotional face on a visual TOJ task. The autistic and control groups showed comparable TOJ thresholds in emotion-free situations.

The authors used the AASP and found higher scores for the Low registration (d = 1.48, CI = [0.85, 2.1]), Sensory sensitivity (d = 1.33, CI = [0.72, 1.94]), and Sensation avoiding domains (d = 1.16, CI = [0.56, 1.76]), but a lower score for Sensation seeking (d = 0.83, CI = [0.26, 1. 41]). The control group showed higher TOJ performance under the emotional condition than the autistic group. Correlations between the TOJ performances and AASP scores were not reported. However, multiple regression analysis of the blood-oxygen level-dependent signal changes revealed an association between the right superior temporal gyrus and the emotion effect on TOJ performance in the autistic group but not in the control group. Isenstein et al. [28] examined duration-based somatosensory sensitivity by using ERP with a mismatch negativity (MMN) paradigm in each of 30 autistic and control participants.

In the EEG recording, participants were asked to ignore vibrotactile stimuli delivered to their right index fingertip while they were watching a silent video. Isenstein et al. used the score of touch processing from AASP and found a higher score in the autistic group than in the control group (d = 0.94, CI = [0.39, 1.49], calculated by the authors). They separately examined tactile duration sensitivity based on a behavioral discrimination task. They found no main effect of group or interaction effect of group and deviant durations on the behavioral task. There was a group effect on MMN latency but no interaction between duration-related factors or group. They also reported no interaction between the duration-dependent effect and group on MMN amplitude. The group effect was evident regardless of the standard or deviant stimuli on amplitude. The authors did not report the results of correlation analysis between touch processing scores and behavioral task performance. The authors performed exploratory analysis of the P220 amplitude in response to standard stimuli, i.e., a single duration that was repeatedly presented. The P220 amplitude was not correlated with touch processing score across all participants. However, there was no report in an independent autistic group.

A meta-analytic result from the child study [23] revealed poorer time sensitivity and fewer auditory abnormalities in the autism group. Although two adult studies reported increased sensory abnormalities in autism, group differences of behavioral tasks and brain responses free from timing-irrelevant manipulation were not evident. Only limited portions of brain response such as the P220 and the superior temporal gyrus appear to be involved in the sensory features in autism, but this remains unclear.

Narrative reviews

One of three included narrative reviews [37] focused on multisensory TBW in autism, dyslexia, and schizophrenia because of the increasing recognition that multisensory temporal acuity is altered in a number of neurodevelopmental disabilities. They noted that most children with autism have some form of sensory alteration and suggested that sensory dysfunction may be a core component of the condition. The authors noted that the finding of poorer multisensory temporal acuity in autism is robust, as is the prevalence of enlargements of the multisensory TBW among clinical cohorts. Martínez-Sanchis [35] reviewed the findings in the role of medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) in sensory processing problems in autistic people, especially multisensory integration.

Because the ability to combine information from multiple sensory modalities into a unified perception depends on the temporal synchrony of the stimuli, the author noted that the deficit in multisensory integration may lie in the temporal synchronization of neural networks. She noted that the mPFC may play a role in top-down information integration, and that insufficient connectivity between the mPFC and other areas would enhance low-level perception processes, such as overly focused attention to sensory details.

The third review, by Rotschafer [36], discussed possible causes of autism-related irregularities in processing sound. The author pointed out that altered sensory processing, especially auditory sensitivities, is common in people with autism. She also described how the aberrant cortical response and temporal processing in autism may contribute to errors in sound discrimination, such as difficulty listening to speech. The author cited experimental studies using auditory GD.

In summary, the three narrative reviews discussed the potential association between sensory alternation and worsened temporal processing at both the behavioral and neural levels in autism. Evidence cited in those studies was weighted toward poorer performance on the timing task. None of the reviews cited empirical evidence of an association between sensory challenges and time sensitivity in autism.

Synthesizing the correlational analyses

Despite the existing narrative reviews, it remains unclear whether the deficits in temporal information processing are associated with increased abnormality of daily sensory experiences in autism. We determined that six of 17 included studies performed correlational analysis (for more details, see Table 4). Five studies performed a single correlation analysis in the independent autistic group, and one performed a principal component analysis across two groups.

The latter study [33] found three clusters in which the majority of the participants were autistic (90% ASD and 10% TD), neurotypical (2.63% ASD and 97.37% TD), and mixed (57.69% ASD and 42.31% TD). In the six studies reporting correlations in independent autistic or autisticmajority groups, no study demonstrated a correlation between high sensory abnormality and lower time sensitivity.

Discussion

In the present systematic review, we examined the association between sensory features in daily experiences of autistic people and temporal information processing measured by timing tasks. Table 5 summarizes group differences in time sensitivity. Four studies using unisensory stimuli reported poorer time sensitivity indices in children with autism free from timing-irrelevant manipulations without other clinical statuses of interest, and 3 studies showed comparable indices.

Five unisensory studies demonstrated comparable sensitivity and 1 reported heightened sensitivity to time. In multisensory studies, 1 study in children reported comparable performance and 2 studies in adults demonstrated pooper performances in autism. Studies rarely reported better time sensitivity in autism, but the majority of studies demonstrated the comparable degree of time sensitivity indices between experimental groups (n = 8) in comparison with poorer sensitivity (n = 6). The weak group effect on time sensitivity reported above is not surprising, as previous have reviews demonstrated [9, 10]. One study reported similar thresholds in the visuotactile TOJ task, so multisensory integration may not matter. One study measuring the P1 component revealed a greater amplitude in response to the auditory duration deviant in autistic adults under placebo dose condition [26], suggesting that the brain response to temporal difference is more sensitive than non-autistic adults. The present results suggested the inconsistent evidence regarding narrative acknowledgment of temporal processing dysfunction in autism.

As sensory abnormalities have been identified as an important clinical feature of autism, narratives have referred to the alteration between sensory and temporal processing [35] [36] [37].

However, our analysis revealed that most of the relevant literature did not suggest an association between heightened sensory challenges and worse time sensitivity in autism, even though most studies (n = 12) reported an increased score in sensory feature assessments. Rather, some studies reported an association between heightened sensory abnormality and enhanced time sensitivity [18, 27, 30, 33]. These findings are not inconsistent, because many studies have reported enhanced task performance in specific situations [38] [39] [40] [41].

Of our identified studies, 2 reported lower sensory assessment scores [19, 23]. These studies used the Sensory Profile and selected items related to auditory processing. Bharadwaj et al. [19] assessed sensory traits using the SPQ-Auditory Profile Subscale (SPQ-APS), in which they excluded the response to question 8 regarding sensory seeking. In other cases, Huang et al. [26] employed a part of the AQ as the sensory assessment. They used the responses to AQ question 5: "I often notice small sounds when others do not." Item 5 is involved in the factor of attention to details, and thus it should be identified as an assessment for auditory trait with caution. To improve the robustness of future studies, researchers are encouraged to use sensory assessment instruments with established factor structure, reliability, and construct validity in the target population. Validated scales and subscales should be scored according to their established structure; ad hoc item subsets require separate psychometric validation. Within the assessment tools included for our review, Gunderson et al. [11] indicated that the SEQ has been developed and validated in autistic children. It has also been suggested that the SEQ could be useful for characterizing sensory features in children with autism and ADHD [42].

If population-level time sensitivity in autism is intact, the aspect of the task that induced heterogeneity across the studies should be examined. Three studies in children demonstrated poorer performance on the GD threshold in autism [20, 21, 23], whereas two studies in adults reported comparable performance between groups [30, 32]. One of the major hypotheses of autism etiology, the neural excitatory/inhibitory imbalance [2], describes reduced inhibitory neural function in the brain. Several studies have reported that reductions in GABA concentrations are associated with higher sensory hyperresponsiveness scores in autistic individuals [6, 7].

Neural inhibition plays an important role in discriminating the presence or absence of sensory inputs in a receptive field [25]. In an earlier developmental stage of autism, it is thought that reduced inhibition may disrupt the detection of the stimulus onset and offset during a GD task with stimulation in the same receptive field. If this idea is correct, the heightened threshold may originate from the low-level sensory cortices rather than timing-related circuits [43]. Further research using multiple timing tasks to target the different stages of information processing [33] is needed in autistic children.

Future studies should also examine the effect of the underlying mechanism for each timing task. Recent articles note that inconsistencies of findings across timing tasks may be caused by differences in the underlying mechanisms of temporal processing and/or the contributions of attention, working memory, and motor demand [44, 45]. Due to task demands, sensory measurement noise may contaminate time estimation, and computational approaches may distinguish between the two [45]. Using different timing tasks may reveal that inconsistent findings are induced by multiple clocks. In other words, timing may be mediated by multiple distinct procedures [46]. Because our systematic review does not include enough studies with tasks that demand different mechanisms, it is not possible to conclude whether a single or multiple clock theory can explain the inconsistencies in the studies.

Limitations

In the present study, the included studies were highly heterogeneous. We intended to perform a comprehensive review by minimizing the likelihood of arbitrarily excluding relevant studies. However, this strategy can result in large variability of study characteristics. Despite this strategy, only a small number of studies was included, suggesting that empirical research connecting the academic interest in temporal processing to the everyday sensory difficulties in autism is still in its infancy. We found five studies in which single correlational analyses were performed between sensory features and time sensitivity indices in an autistic cohort. Two of the studies involved children [19, 29], and three involved adults [18, 27, 30]. These studies used single or multiple sensory domains and a variety of experimental tasks and sensory assessment instruments. Including these study characteristics, the confounds of age, IQ, ADHD comorbidity, and medication were not examined in our analyses. The risk of bias analysis also revealed that the majority of studies did not address confounding factors; therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that these factors biased the synthesized results. The small number of studies for each confound prevents us from establishing a generalizable relationship between sensory features and temporal processing in autism.

Another limitation of our study was that the protocol of this review was not prospectively registered due to time constraints. The lack of prospective registration makes it difficult to verify that the review methods were prespecified and to assess whether the eligibility criteria, outcomes, or synthesis methods changed in response to the findings. While this does not prove selective reporting, it reduces methodological transparency.

It should be acknowledged that our systematic review might have missed relevant literature. We selected the PubMed, Scopus, and PsycINFO databases because they are advantageous for identifying literature in medical, psychological, and interdisciplinary research areas and are accessible through our institutional network. Furthermore, we defined eligible articles as those written in English, so other possible articles of interest might have been missed.

The search terms were based on MeSH terms from a standard thesaurus to ensure the use of robust umbrella terms [47]. Previous systematic reviews used more specific terms related to psychological tasks [9, 10], a procedure that may be efficient if the study intends to focus on specific tasks. However, because our study did not focus on specific tasks, our more liberal procedure is better suited to a systematic review. Rather, the fact that only one reviewer (T.A.) extracted data might have biased the selection of literature.

We should also note that some of our result syntheses are based on ambiguous information or estimated data. One study provided insufficient information about the sensory assessment tool [24]. Moreover, we estimated individual data points using WebPlotDigitizer to examine group differences in scores [23]. While these studies were not included in the synthesized result of the correlational analysis across the experimental studies, they may still contribute to the overall uncertainty of our findings.

Conclusion

In our systematic review, we examined associations between sensory features in autism and sensitivity in timing tasks. Despite the prevailing narrative of poor temporal processing in autism, we found that most studies reported comparable time sensitivity between autistic and nonautistic individuals. No study reported a correlation between enhancement of sensory abnormalities and poor time sensitivity. However, the absence of supporting evidence should not be interpreted as evidence that no association exists between sensory features and time sensitivity.

Some studies, moreover, made inappropriate use of assessment tools [19, 26]. To conduct a more robust and reliable study, researchers are encouraged to use psychologically valid tools for sensory assessment. Inclusion of a variety of timing tasks is necessary to characterize the multiple stages of temporal processing in autism.
