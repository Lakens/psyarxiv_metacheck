Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Who Is Visible in Autism Research? Demographic Reporting and Representation of Autistic Participants: A Systematic Mapping Review of Australian Research

Autism research has expanded substantially over the past decade, with growing recognition that autistic people have diverse experiences, communication profiles, support needs, identities, and life contexts (Mahajan et al., 2025). To understand whose experiences inform this growing evidence base, researchers need to clearly describe who participates in their studies. How participant characteristics are reported therefore has important implications for interpreting and applying research findings and determining who is represented in the research. This is particularly important because sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are not simply background information. They have been shown to shape access to diagnosis and services (Nowell et al., 2015), educational and occupational outcomes (West et al., 2016), and participation in research itself (Keane et al., 2023).

Demographic Reporting in Autism Research

Demographic reporting in autism research serves a critical methodological and ethical function. Autism is highly heterogeneous, with variation across communication needs, intellectual ability, gender identity, co-occurring conditions, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status (Masi et al., 2017). Transparent demographic reporting is therefore needed to determine whether study conclusions reflect the broader autistic population or only a narrow subgroup of participants typically recruited in research settings (Martin et al., 2025). This lack of transparency limits whose experiences are rendered visible and "knowable" within autism research, thereby shaping which needs are recognised and addressed (Cascio et al., 2021).

The impact of limited reporting of demographics has been previously highlighted in areas of autism research. Girolamo et al. (2023) reviewed 59 studies examining language abilities in autistic young people. The review was restricted to articles published in English and studies using English-language assessments with US normative data. They found inconsistent reporting of core demographic characteristics, including race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. For instance, only 28.9% of studies reported race and ethnicity data and, where reported, samples were predominantly White (77.0%). Socioeconomic indicators were reported infrequently (16.9%) and often in an incomplete manner (Girolamo et al., 2023). The impact of these issues is particularly significant in the context of evidence-based practice, where autism research directly informs supports, programs, education, diagnostic frameworks, and policy development. A large systematic review of 1,013 autism studies evaluating supports and programs for those aged 0-22 years found that only 25% of studies reported race and ethnicity, with samples disproportionately White (64.8%) and underrepresenting Black (7.7%), Hispanic/Latino (9.4%), and Asian (6.4%) participants (Steinbrenner et al., 2022). This suggests that many supports and programs are developed and evaluated using non-representative samples, raising concerns about equity and applicability of evidence-based practice across diverse autistic populations. As these reviews have focused on specific age groups, areas of research, or focussed on research using country-specific norms, it is unclear whether similar patterns of demographic reporting and representation are evident across the broader autism research literature and in other national contexts.

Demographic underreporting intersects with broader concerns about sampling bias in autism research, where participants are disproportionately speaking, without an intellectual disability, and already engaged with diagnostic or support systems. This can result in the exclusion or underrepresentation of autistic people with higher support needs, intellectual disability, and communication differences. In their meta-analysis, Russell et al. (2019) examined 301 studies involving 100,245 autistic participants and found that participants were more likely to use speech to communicate (with only 2.0% non-speaking or minimally speaking) and to have average or above-average intellectual ability. Furthermore, among studies reporting intellectual disability, 94.0% of autistic participants did not have an intellectual disability. Eight out of 10 studies demonstrated selection bias against participants with an intellectual disability, defined as studies in which over three quarters of the sample did not have an intellectual disability. This skew in representation toward autistic people with lower support needs further highlights research inequities and leads to questions about how generalisable research findings are for autistic people with higher support needs (Russell et al., 2019). However, Russell et al.'s review only reported on papers published within a single calendar year (2016) and only in autism-specific journals with an impact factor higher than 3, so further work is needed to establish whether these patterns of underrepresentation are also evident across a broader range of autism research, including studies published in non-autismspecific journals and more recent research.

Equity Frameworks and PROGRESS-Plus

One way to address challenges in representativeness is through structured and consistent approaches to demographic reporting. Frameworks such as PROGRESS-Plus provide a systematic approach to reporting demographics in health research to ensure clear representation of the participants who participated (Cochrane Equity Methods Group, 2026; O'Neill et al., 2014). The PROGRESS-Plus framework identifies social determinants of health outcomes and service accessibility, including Place of residence, Race/ethnicity/ culture/language, Occupation, Gender/sex, Religion, Education, Socioeconomic status, and Social capital. The framework aims to shift demographic reporting toward a more systematic account of the contexts in which people live and the conditions under which they are included in research. Use of the PROGRESS-Plus framework in health and clinical trial research has highlighted ongoing gaps in the inclusion and documentation of socially diverse populations (e.g., Emmert et al., 2025; Hall et al., 2025).

Although PROGRESS provides a comprehensive framework for equity-relevant demographic characteristics, it does not specify several characteristics that are particularly relevant to inclusion and representation in autism research. The "Plus" component of PROGRESS-Plus allows consideration of additional context-specific characteristics, including personal characteristics associated with discrimination and relationship contexts (Cochrane Equity Methods Group, 2026; O'Neill et al., 2014). For autism research, this may include intellectual disability, communication support needs, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) use, non-speaking status, diagnostic status, LGBTQIA+ identity, and higher support needs, all of which can intersect with autism to shape or influence people's experiences (e.g., Adams et al., 2025). Within the Australian context, the National Autism Strategy (NAS) identified specific priority cohorts and intersectional identities that require greater attention in research and policy (Australian Government Department of Social Services, 2024). These include groups such as First Nations people, culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) and culturally and racially marginalised (CARM) people, gender diverse people, people with higher support needs, and autistic people with cooccurring neurodevelopmental and health needs, amongst others. Using PROGRESS-Plus alongside the NAS provides an autism-specific lens through which demographic reporting and participant representation can be examined. Combined, these two frameworks allow for an assessment of whether equity-relevant demographic characteristics are reported and whether typically underrepresented groups are included in autism research.

Current Review

Previous reviews (e.g. Russell et al., 2019) have identified gaps in demographic reporting and participant inclusion in autism research, particularly in relation to race and ethnicity and intellectual disability in studies evaluating supports and programs. However, these reviews have typically focused on specific populations or intervention studies, and it remains unclear how consistently demographic characteristics are reported across the broader autism literature or the extent to which priority groups can be identified within published samples. This systematic mapping review addresses this gap by examining Australian autism research published between 2023 and 2025. Although this review focuses on Australian autism research, the problem it addresses is not specific to Australia. Across countries, autism research is used to inform policy, practice, diagnostic pathways, supports, and evidence-based recommendations, yet demographic reporting is often too limited to determine whose experiences underpin that evidence. Australia provides a useful case study because the NAS identifies priority or intersectional groups for policy and service reform, allowing reporting practices to be examined against an explicit national equity framework. The findings therefore have relevance beyond Australia by showing how a national research landscape can be assessed for demographic representativeness, equity, and policy relevance. Recent policy changes by large Australian funders requiring applicants to consider sex, gender, variations of sex characteristics and sexual orientation in their proposed research, make this issue particularly timely.

A staged mapping approach was used so the demographic reporting could be interpreted within the context of the broader evidence base. First, the review characterised the Australian autism research landscape by describing the volume and study design or methodological features (primary/secondary data, descriptive/intervention focus, community involvement, sample size) of all identified articles published within the specified time period (2023) (2024) (2025). The period 2023-2025 was selected to review a substantial but manageable body of contemporary Australian autism research. This period also spans the development and launch of Australia's first National Autism Strategy; public consultations commenced in 2023 and the strategy was launched in 2025. The findings therefore provide a baseline against which future changes in demographic reporting and participant representation can be examined. The second stage focused on studies involving human participants. Here, the focus was on demographic reporting using the PROGRESS-Plus framework, explored in relation to study characteristics, including study approach, informant type, and sample size. Finally, the review assessed the inclusion and exclusion of NAS priority or intersectional groups and their representation within study samples, to evaluate the extent to which current Australian autism research reflects national equity priorities. This staged approach allowed the review to distinguish between the volume of Australian autism research, the proportion involving human participants, and the extent to which autistic participants were demographically characterised within that research. By using Australia as an example, this review demonstrates that useful mapping of autism research can extend beyond topics or volume.

Systematically mapping the demographic reporting of the autistic people who participated helps us to understand whose data are shaping the evidence base.

Eligibility Criteria

The broader mapping review used broad criteria to capture autism-related research across human, animal, cellular, genetic, and secondary data studies. Eligible articles: (a) focused on autism; (b) reported separable autism-related data for mixed samples or conditions; (c) had a lead author affiliated with an Australian or New Zealand institution and/or stated that data were collected in Australia or New Zealand; (d) were published between 2023 and 2025; and (e) were peer-reviewed journal articles reporting original, completed empirical research. Articles were excluded if data were collected outside Australia or study location could not be determined (n = 132). Studies were classified as human participant research when people were actively recruited for the specific study, including interviews, surveys, trials, observational studies, and secondary analyses of participant data. Studies were excluded if they used non-human data sources or humanrelated data without active recruitment for the specific research, including administrative or systems data, human-derived biological material, human-generated digital or public data, or computational analyses of human data (n = 49).

Data Extraction and Coding

Data were extracted using a structured form developed for this review. Three reviewers extracted study and participant characteristics, and a fourth independently checked 23.9% of articles (n = 63) for consistency. Complete agreement across all demographic characteristics was observed for 62 articles (98.4%). Agreement was 100% for all demographic characteristics except one where a transcription error was made for age data for one article. This discrepancy was resolved through discussion and rechecking extraction. Data extraction addressed two elements. First, all articles were coded for whether they reported each PROGRESS-Plus domain (yes/no). This provided an overview of demographic reporting across Australian autism research. Second, participant numbers or percentages were extracted for a priori specified NAS priority or intersectional groups where categories were sufficiently comparable across articles. These analyses examined the representation of autism-specific priority or intersectional groups identified in the NAS (i.e., First Nations people, CALD and CARM people, gender diverse people, people with higher support needs, and autistic people with co-occurring disabilities).

Not all reported demographic characteristics were suitable for participant-level synthesis because definitions and reporting methods varied substantially between studies. For these characteristics, only article-level reporting was coded. Table 1 shows how each reporting domain was coded, specifies whether corresponding representation data were extracted and, where data were not extracted, provides the reason. Eligibility criteria were examined to identify explicit restrictions affecting priority or intersectional groups, including diagnostic status, intellectual disability, English proficiency, English literacy, and communication requirements. Restrictions were coded when articles stated that participants were excluded on that basis. Where no relevant criterion was reported, this was coded as "not reported" rather than interpreted as inclusion.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarised study characteristics, demographic reporting, priority or intersectional group representation, and eligibility restrictions. Where participant n/% was extracted for a characteristic, sample compositions were summarised for articles reporting it. Demographic reporting was summarised at the article level. The number and proportion of articles reporting each demographic was calculated and reported by study approach, informant type, and sample size. Each article contributed once, regardless of its participant sample size. Study approach was classified as primary data collection or secondary analysis, consistent with prior autism evidence-mapping approaches (Steinbrenner et al., 2022). Informant type was classified as autistic self-report, proxy report, or mixed report. Because sample size was highly skewed, articles were grouped as smaller or larger using the median sample size (n = 58).

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was not assessed in the present review, in line with methodological guidance for mapping reviews which indicates that critical appraisal is not required when the purpose of the review is to describe the scope of the evidence rather than assess the effects of supports or outcomes (Khalil et al., 2025).

Results

Characteristics of the Australian Autism Research Landscape

To address the first research question, stage 1 explored the study design characteristics of the Australian autism research landscape. Of the 513 articles that met the inclusion criteria and reported on Australian autism research published between 2023 and 2025, 464 involved human participants. The majority of the remaining 49 articles (9.6%) used existing human data sources, including human administrative or systems data (n = 26; 5.1%), human-derived biological material (n = 11; 2.1%), human-generated digital or public data (n = 6; 1.2%), and computational analyses using human data (n = 3; 0.6%). A smaller number of articles did not involve human data, including animal studies (n = 1; 0.2%), research funding analyses (n = 1; 0.2%), and environmental assessments (n = 1; 0.2%).

The 464 articles involving human participants were then included in Stage 2 (see Figure 1), where study design characteristics were further described. Of these, 409 (88.1%) reported primary human data collection and 55 (11.9%) reported secondary analyses of existing human participant data.

Most articles were descriptive (n = 395; 85.1%), while 69 (14.9%) evaluated supports or programs designed to promote change. Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from a single participant in an ethnographic study to 7,240 participants in a secondary analysis of existing data. Fourteen (3.0%) had a sample size of 1,000 or more.

Most articles (61.6%) did not report whether community involvement had occurred. Of the 178 (38.4%) articles where information on community involvement was reported, 162 (89.9%) stated they had some level of community involvement, whereas 16 (9.0%) stated they had no community involvement.

Visibility of Participant Demographic Characteristics Through Reporting Practices

Stage 3 demographic analyses focussed on the 264 articles that reported demographic characteristics specifically for autistic participants (see Figure 1). Reporting of demographic characteristics varied (see Table 2). Reporting was especially limited for First Nations identity, CALD/CARM background, socioeconomic disadvantage, intellectual disability, AAC use, LGBTQIA+ status, and very high support needs. In this section, unless otherwise specified, demographic reporting analyses and percentages are based on the 264 articles with demographic data that could be extracted for autistic participants.

As per Table 2, gender/sex was reported in most articles (n = 241; 91.3%), although only 31.2% included options beyond binary categories, and 14.4% did not distinguish between gender/sex. Of the articles reporting gender/sex, 39.7% of participants were women and girls and 13.7% were gender diverse. Age was also commonly reported (75.4%); within the articles reporting age, 45.2% included participants aged 17 years or younger and 17.1% included those aged 65 years or older.

In contrast, other PROGRESS demographics were reported far less consistently. Race and ethnicity were reported in 29.5% of articles, First Nations participants 17.8%, CALD/CARM background 13.3%, AAC users 11.7%, Family or household composition 17.4%, and socioeconomic disadvantage in 9.1%. Where applicable for the participants age, level of education completed was reported in 27.9% of articles and occupation 18.0%.

Religion (1.1%) was the least frequently reported. Within studies reporting these demographics, First Nations participants comprised 5.7% of the sample, CALD/CARM participants 25.9%, AAC users 30.1%, and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups 14.9%.

The "Plus characteristics" (i.e., those related to potential discrimination and marginalisation) were rarely reported. Therefore, some reported sample percentages in the final column of Table 2 appear high because they are calculated only among the small number of articles that reported the characteristic, and in some cases may include studies intentionally recruiting a specific priority or intersectional group.

Eligibility Restrictions Affecting Priority or Intersectional Group Representation

Of the 264 articles with demographic data reported for autistic participants, 197 (74.6%) reported eligibility criteria for autistic participants.

The most common restriction was diagnostic status: 142 articles (72.1%) had eligibility criteria that either required a formal autism diagnosis or excluded self-diagnosis. Language and communication criteria were also common. Forty-four (22.3%) articles excluded participants based on English proficiency and a further 22 (11.2%) excluded participants based on English literacy. Additionally, 38 (19.3%) studies excluded participants with an intellectual disability, and 15 (7.6%) excluded participants based on AAC use or non-speaking status. Thirteen articles (6.6%) reported gender/sex-based eligibility criteria. Most focused on specific gendered groups, such as women, girls, gender-diverse participants, or transgender participants.

Only one study exclusively recruited autistic males (Sarmukadam et al., 2023).

Discussion

Systematically mapping three years of Australian autism research addresses each of the four research questions and revealed three key findings. First, autism research activity in Australia is high. Most articles reported human participant data (90.4%), although only a minority (14.9%) evaluated supports or programs and approximately one third included community involvement in their design. Second, participant demographic visibility is low.

Using the PROGRESS-Plus framework alongside NAS priority and intersectional groups revealed that while demographic reporting of age and gender/sex is relatively consistent, reporting across most other important demographics was variable and limited. This makes it difficult to determine whether many groups recognised as priorities for research and policy, including First Nations participants, CALD/CARM participants, AAC users, LGBTQIA+ participants, people with an intellectual disability, and people with high/complex support needs, were represented in the evidence base. Third, inclusion or exclusion criteria further limit participation for some autistic people before a study has even begun.

Autism is an active research area in Australia. Across three years, there were 513 empirical articles relating to Australian autism published, equating to almost one empirical article every other day (in addition to other article types, including reviews). A recent global bibliometric analysis identified Australia as publishing the sixth-highest number of articles on prevalence of autism per year (González et al., 2026). Among the six highest publishing countries, only the United Kingdom produced more autism research per capita than Australia (González et al., 2026). This high level of research activity is supported by an estimated AUD$10 million per year in autism research funding (Adams et al., 2026).

The largest proportion of this funding has consistently been directed towards biological research (den Houting & Pellicano, 2019), receiving almost twice the funding allocated to lifespan research and 2.5 times that allocated to the Intervention and Applied Autism Research Category (IAAC) "treatments and interventions." This pattern can also be seen in the publication output, where only a minority (14.9%) focus on supports or programs for autistic people.

Given that Australia makes a substantial contribution to the international autism evidence base, mapping the demographic characteristics, reporting representativeness, and focus of this evidence is particularly important. Applying the PROGRESS-Plus framework alongside NAS-identified priority populations and intersectional groups revealed that reporting of demographic characteristics beyond age and gender was inconsistent and often absent. High (although not universal) reporting of gender, alongside lower reporting of other demographic characteristics (e.g., education, socioeconomic status, race, and ethnicity), is common in psychology journals (e.g., 94.8% gender, 36.7% race, 26.5% socioeconomic status; Petrutiu et al., 2026), even when some journals specifically encourage the reporting of diversity data. However, high rates of gender/sex reporting should not be read as high-quality reporting: reporting was often binary, ambiguous, or did not distinguish between sex assigned at birth and gender identity. This is surprising, given that all research mapped was published within the last 3 years, during which the overrepresentation of gender diversity among autistic populations and autism among gender-diverse populations has been well recognised (see review by Rea et al., 2026).

In contrast to the relatively consistent reporting of gender/sex and age, there was substantial underreporting of First Nations identity (17.8% of articles), CALD/CARM status (13.3%), socioeconomic status (9.1%), intellectual disability (28.0%), AAC use (11.7%),

LGBTQIA+ status (3.0%), and very high support needs (7.2%). Limited reporting makes it difficult to judge the extent of underrepresentation. Despite publications such as Reuben et al. (2025) suggesting that certain marginalised or intersectional subgroups of autistic people are underrepresented, it is possible that they participate in more research than is estimated but are invisible due to demographic underreporting. Without consistent reporting, it becomes difficult to identify articles that have successfully included priority groups and to use those studies to inform evidence synthesis, policy, and future research with inclusive designs.

Eligibility criteria provide a second mechanism through which participant representation can be narrowed, before data collection even begins. For example, requiring a formal autism diagnosis may exclude people who are undiagnosed, self-identified, or awaiting assessment, despite their relevant lived experience. It is recognised that many adults can face substantial barriers to obtaining a diagnosis, including cost, difficulty accessing knowledgeable professionals, anxiety and communication demands, stigma, and complex health-system pathways (Lewis, 2017). In Australia, barriers to getting an adult diagnosis have also been reflected in adults' accounts of missed diagnostic opportunities, variable clinician expertise, difficulty locating adult autism specialists, living in remote areas, and assessment-related costs (de Broize et al., 2022).

Similarly, criteria requiring English proficiency, literacy, or the ability to complete written questionnaires may systematically exclude autistic people from CALD/CARM communities, recent migrants, and those with lower literacy, intellectual disability, or people who use AAC. These groups may also be indirectly excluded when study methods do not explicitly preclude their participation but are designed or delivered in ways that make participation inaccessible (Thurm et al., 2022). Such restrictions may reflect practical considerations, such as consent procedures, data collection costs, or the use of nonstandardised tools. However, they risk producing samples that disproportionately represent autistic people who are formally diagnosed, English-speaking, verbally fluent, literate, and have fewer support needs within research contexts. Representation within research samples is influenced not only by who researchers recruit but also by how studies are designed.

Researcher decisions around eligibility criteria and methodological decisions do more than determine who can participate: they also shape whose experiences become part of the evidence used to inform research, policy, and practice.

Implications, Future Directions, and Limitations

Previous reviews have largely asked whether particular autistic groups are underrepresented in research. The findings suggest that an equally important problem is that, for many characteristics, representation cannot be judged because demographic reporting is too limited. The findings from this mapping review have led to two key implications: first, the need for researchers to be more transparent about the samples reported on and what that means for the knowledge generated; and second, a call for more inclusive research methods that enable a broader proportion of autistic people to participate. Implications for researchers, journals, and funding bodies are identified as a way to drive change in research practices.

Inconsistent demographic reporting is not unique to autism research; similar concerns have been raised in other fields, including psychology (Petrutiu et al., 2026) and radiology (Parsons et al., 2024). Previous reviews of the autism research in specific areas have also called for more transparency in sample descriptions (e.g., Steinbrenner et al., 2022). This review extends previous work by demonstrating the consequences of inconsistent demographic reporting. Without sufficient demographic information, it becomes difficult to assess representation, to compare studies, and to judge the extent to which findings can be generalised to the broader autistic population. The development of minimum reporting demographic standards for autism research, co-designed by autistic people, researchers, and journal editors, would help to improve transparency and support stronger evidence synthesis.

Some journals already have minimum demographic reporting standards (e.g., Trials) or guidelines for reporting data on race or ethnicity (e.g., The Lancet group), demonstrating that such expectations are feasible. Autism's submission instructions also encourage authors to report characteristics such as age, gender, race or ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, even when these are not included in the analysis. Improving demographic reporting is critical to allow readers to assess the applicability of findings, to identify gaps in the evidence base, and to recognise studies that have successfully included autistic people who are often absent from research. Research funders are also beginning to recognise the importance of this. Multiple

Australian funders now require grant applications to state how sex, gender, variations of sex characteristics, and sexual orientation data will be considered in data collection and analysis (National Health and Medical Research Council, 2025). Establishing a baseline of demographic reporting in Australian autism research can help evaluate change as this policy context develops. This could then also inform whether expectations could be extend to a broader range of demographic characteristics would improve transparency and strengthen future evidence synthesis.

The implications of this work highlight the need for inclusive research processes during recruitment, provision of access supports and accommodations, and adapting research materials and processes into individualised accessible formats for data collection. Research methods that allow for expression in mediums other than spoken or written language, such as imagery (anonymised for review), photovoice (Simpson et al., 2022), and card sorting (Ambrose et al., 2024), may broaden opportunities for people to participate. There also needs to be careful consideration of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Although exclusions may be justified for some study aims, they should not be default design choices; no decision is neutral. Where such restrictions are used, authors should explain why they are necessary and how they affect the interpretation and reach of the findings.

Broadening opportunities to participate can be informed through lived experience input and co-design from the early stages of the study design. Community involvement statements were included in 38.4% of articles identified in this mapping review, with 34.9% of articles involving human participants reporting some community involvement. This statistic adds to the review by showing who participated in the research as well as who shaped it. Whilst this proportion is encouraging, if reflective of a growing enthusiasm for participatory research approaches (Tan et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2026)

Conclusion

Autism research has an important role in shaping policy, practice, and the supports available to autistic people. Understanding who that evidence represents is therefore fundamental to understanding who it serves. This review demonstrates that determining who is represented first requires knowing what is reported. It calls on autism researchers to proactively adopt more consistent demographic reporting practices, inclusive research methods, and greater transparency in eligibility criteria. These changes are needed to strengthen confidence in autism research, ensuring that it reflects the diversity of autistic people and the experiences of the communities it is intended to benefit.
