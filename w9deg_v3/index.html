Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Irresistibly Logical: Automatic Logical Inferences in Visual Scene Processing

The capacity for logical thought is responsible for outstanding intellectual achievements -from everyday reasoning to formal mathematics, complex financial systems, and the advent of computing machines. Yet the mental processes underlying such reasoning remain elusive (Fodor, 2000; Mercier & Sperber, 2017; Pinker, 2021). What is the nature and origin of the cognitive mechanisms underlying the human potential for reasoning?

An influential account of human logical reasoning emphasizes its intentionality, effort, and slowness (Evans & Stanovich, 2013; Kahneman, 2011), perhaps mirroring the gradual and incremental nature of our intellectual achievements themselves. According to this view, logical cognition relies on our deliberative reasoning, drawing heavily on working memory resources (De Neys, 2006), employing explicit strategies for maintaining consistency across multiple propositions (Johnson-Laird & Byrne, 2002), and demanding cognitive control to override our mind's intuitive, heuristic responses (De Neys et al., 2008). Indeed, evidence has shown that reasoning performance deteriorates under cognitive load (De Neys et al., 2005), improves with explicit instructions emphasizing accuracy over speed (Pennycook et al., 2015), and frequently requires conscious inhibition of intuitive, but incorrect, responses (Frederick, 2005). In light of this work, there is no doubt that deliberation is required to engage with complex problems via effortful and protracted calculations.

At the same time, our minds are also guided by logical intuitions that are neither the result of intentional inferences nor the result of conscious effort, and yet logically follow from the available evidence. Hence, while an influential tradition treats logical reasoning and intuition as contrary categories (Evans, 2010), others have made a case for intuitions that are themselves logical. For example, when presented with explicit linguistic input (e.g., reading sentences, IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL following instructions, and so on), our minds engage in abstract logical computations, in a way that is rapid, automatic, and possibly independent of deliberate reflection (Braine & O'Brien, 1998; Johnson-Laird, 2010; Khemlani et al., 2018; Quilty-Dunn et al., 2023; Rips, 1994). Parsing natural language sentences requires quick logical computations (Chierchia, 2013 (Chierchia, , 2017;; Icard & Moss, 2014), and readers spontaneously draw deductive inferences upon encountering sufficient premises (e.g., Disjunctive Syllogism: "p OR q", "NOT p", therefore "q", Lea, 1995; Aristotelian Syllogism: "ALL x are y", therefore "SOME x are y" Dabkowski et al., n.d. ; Modus Ponens: "IF p, THEN q", "p", therefore "q"), even when one of the premises is subliminally presented (Reverberi et al., 2012). And even when people are induced to give logically incorrect responses via heuristics and biases, conflict-detection paradigms reveal implicit sensitivity to logical correctness (De Neys, 2012), manifested in longer reaction times and reduced confidence when their answers conflict with logic (De Neys & Pennycook, 2019). More recently, new evidence suggests that even implicit attitudes may follow deductive rules, with responses in evaluative tasks shaped by propositional logic rather than mere association (Kurdi & Dunham, 2021; Quilty-Dunn et al., 2023). This suggests the existence of logical operations that require neither intentional control nor awareness.

Where, then, do such logical intuitions come from? One view traces their emergence to repeated exposure and extensive practice with logical problems (Piaget, 1952), which gradually transforms deliberate deductive inferences into intuitive, automatic processes (De Neys & Pennycook, 2019; Schneider & Shiffrin, 1977). In contrast, a second influential view argues that automatic logical operations originate from our linguistic competencies and are the product of the computational resources responsible for the compositional and generative properties of language (Berwick & Chomsky, 2015; Davidson, 1982; Descartes, 1637).

IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL Though there may be truth in both these accounts -which are not mutually exclusivehere, we articulate and test a third, contrasting hypothesis for the source of logical intuitions. On this hypothesis, spontaneous and automatic deductive inferences stem from a core logical system that traffics in mental logical operations (Braine & O'Brien, 1998; Fodor, 1975; Quilty-Dunn et al., 2023). Crucially, such a system may also function independently of linguistic structures and may predate extensive practice with logical problems and deliberate reasoning. In line with this proposal, it has been argued that deductive inferences realized through mental logical operations should exhibit the hallmarks of fast and intuitive processing: they should be triggered automatically, afford limited intentional control, and, once triggered, resist suppression (Braine & O'Brien, 1998; Fodor, 1975; Quilty-Dunn et al., 2023).

The hypothesis of a core logical system has gained support from the recent discovery that preverbal human infants spontaneously generate a disjunctive inference to resolve the identity of visually ambiguous objects. In one study, 12-and 19-month-olds watched as two distinct objects disappeared behind an occluder, after which a container retrieved one object-partially hidden and therefore ambiguous in identity. Upon revealing the other object, infants looked longer when the unknown item was inconsistent with a deductive inference about its identity, suggesting that they had framed a disjunctive hypothesis ("A OR B") and resolved it through elimination ("A is excluded, therefore B") (Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2018). Anticipatory gaze shifts and pupillometry markers of inference-making indicated that infants made disjunctive inference as soon as they saw all the evidence required for deducing the hidden object's identity. Similar studies have also revealed that infants can deploy disjunctive inference to identify the goal of an agent reaching for a partially hidden object (Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2020). Moreover, from at least the age of 19 months, toddlers use disjunctive inference to learn new words (Bohus et al., 2023), and in their IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL third year of life they can deploy these inferences to process explicit verbal negation and reason about others' preferences (Cesana-Arlotti & Halberda, 2024). Importantly, these successes predate mastery of the logical vocabulary of natural language, which is slowly acquired between the second birthday and school age (for production of "not," see Bloom, 1977; Pea, 1982; for comprehension of "not," see Austin et al., 2014; De Carvalho et al., 2021; Feiman et al., 2017; Szabó & Kovács, 2025; for production of "or," see Morris, 2008; for comprehension of "or," see Chierchia et al., 2001; Singh et al., 2016; Skordos et al., 2020). Thus, basic logical operations seem to manifest quite early in development, in apparent tension with the first two accounts of the origin of logical intuition: Young infants succeed at generating deductive inferences before they have extensive practice with such problems, and these logical competencies emerge before infants have acquired logical vocabulary.

Inspired by these discoveries, we hypothesized that these core logical operations persist beyond infancy and may underwrite some of adults' logical intuitions. Here, we test a key prediction of this hypothesis: under the right experimental conditions, the logical inferences found in infancy will emerge automatically in adults from visual events alone, without the presentation of linguistic input containing logical words, and even when they conflict with their task. In other words, when presented with non-linguistic visual stimuli (and with the addition of constraints such as time pressure to respond), adults may reason like logical babies: generating deductive inferences automatically while passively watching visual scenes.

If confirmed, this would suggest the presence of a core logical system that is operative throughout the lifespan, generating fast and irresistible deductions alongside slower and more effortful forms of thinking. Further, this proposal may open a previously unexplored opportunity to study logical representations by recruiting the experimental tools of vision science (Morales & IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL Firestone, 2023; Võ et al., 2019) together with the statistical power of large-scale, online behavioral experiments with human adults (Peer et al., 2017).

Across five preregistered experiments (N = 1,153), we show that disjunctive inference emerges in adults' processing of visual scenes and displays key properties of logical computations. To foreshadow our findings, we show first that disjunctive inference is generated automatically, as it emerges without instructions and persistently affects participant responses, even when it provides no advantage to participants' tasks (Experiment 1). Second, disjunctive inference captures logical relations, since it is entirely suppressed when the logical dependency among scene elements is eliminated (Experiment 2). Third, disjunctive inference is predictive, as it anticipates the unfolding of a visual scene instead of reacting to its outcome (Experiment 3).

We also show that this core logical inference is impervious to counterevidence as it is irresistibly generated whenever logical preconditions are met, even if its conclusion is contradicted by overwhelming statistical evidence (Experiment 4). Finally, we demonstrate that disjunctive inference is functionally distinct from non-logical forms of object classification by showing that the latter is not as impervious to statistical counterevidence (Experiment 5). These findings highlight the logical and intuitive character of the representations involved in disjunctive inference.

Stimuli and Procedure.

Each movie displayed animated events in which a pair of objects (e.g., a snake and a ball) were hidden behind a wall (see Figure 1A). The top parts of each object were identical, such that-when only the top of the item was visible-its identity was ambiguous. An empty cup then dipped behind the wall and "scooped" one of the objects, reemerging with an object inside. Only the top part of the object was visible above the rim of the cup, so its identity was visually concealed but disjunctively related to the initially seen pair of objects (i.e., "the object in the cup is the snake OR the ball"). To prompt the inference by exclusion, one of the two original items was briefly revealed from behind the occluder, logically implying that the object in the cup must have been the other one (i.e., "it cannot be the ball; therefore, it must be the snake"). After a brief delay, the cup revealed which item was inside, and participants were asked to classify the object (as either a snake or a ball) with a keypress. Importantly, half of the trials presented an outcome predicted by the logical inference (Different-object trials), while the other half were inconsistent with the inference (Same-object trials; see Figure 1). Hence, by design, participants were instructed to focus only on the outcome, and the logical inference was not statistically predictive of the final object's type.

Participants viewed eight kinds of animated scenes, which fully counterbalanced the initial configuration of the objects (snake on the left vs. ball on the left), the object appearing from behind the occluder (snake vs. ball), and the object revealed by the cup (snake vs. ball). The order of presentation of the eight movies was randomly determined at the start of the experiment.

Before beginning the experiment, participants received an introduction to the task via text and graphical illustrations, instructing them to classify the final object revealed by the cup by pressing '1' or '2' on their keyboard. The key mapping (i.e., whether "1" corresponded to a snake IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL or ball) was randomized between subjects. Participants could watch example movies until they chose to start the task (for details of the instructions used in each experiment, see experiment demos).

Participants completed one block of 40 trials. Each trial began when the movie started playing and ended when a response had been given or when 2 s had elapsed after the final object had been revealed. If participants failed to answer within the 2 s window, the trial ended, and participants were informed they were "too slow". If participants answered incorrectly, they were informed of their error. Either way, they were required to press a "continue" button, which immediately began the next trial. In contrast, if participants answered correctly, the experiment automatically proceeded to the next trial, which started after 1.5 s.

Results and Discussion

We started by testing whether rapid disjunctive inference spontaneously emerges as subjects watch our movies. All analyses filtered out the first four trials for each subject (preregistered as practice trials) and any trials with response times faster than 200 ms (0.08% of trials). For each participant, we computed the average response time by experimental condition (same-object and different-object). To assess whether response time differences met assumptions of normality, we computed a difference score for each participant by subtracting their average RT on differentobject trials from their average RT on same-object trials. A Shapiro-Wilk test on these difference scores confirmed normality (W = .995, p = .822). We therefore conducted a paired t-test, as our main analysis, which found that participants were faster in classifying the logically predicted kind relative to the logically incongruent one (M different-object = 761.65 ms, SD = 173.41 ms; M same- object = 776.85 ms, SD = 177.94 ms; t(167) = 2.64, p = .009), revealing a rapid logical inference spontaneously triggered by merely perceiving the event (Figure 2A).

IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL Next, we tested whether participants persisted in making the disjunctive inference, even after learning that it didn't help to classify the object. To assess whether the effect of the inference persisted across time, we computed a running difference between reaction times on same-object and different-object trials. At each trial, we subtracted the most recent differentobject response time from the most recent same-object response time, then added that difference to a cumulative total reflecting the aggregate advantage (or disadvantage) conferred by logical consistency. The shape of the curve across the experiment reveals whether the inference's effect persists, diminishes, or reverses as participants accumulate experience with trials: a rising cumulative difference indicates that the inference continues to benefit object classification on each trial, a flat curve indicates that the per-trial benefit has vanished, and a decline indicates that the direction of the effect has reversed, with same-object trials now faster for participants than different-object trials.

We found that the cumulative total rose steadily throughout the experiment (β = 19.47, SE = 1.36, t = 14.36, p < .001), indicating a sustained visual processing advantage on differentobject trials that persisted to the end of the session (Figure 2F; see SI for additional details). This is a striking finding; participants continued to make logical inferences while watching visual scenes, even after seeing dozens of events that violated their predictions. Thus, Experiment 1 revealed that logical inferences emerged spontaneously and persisted even when counterproductive to the participants' task -consistent with the hypothesis that disjunctive inference operates not entirely under participants' intentional control.

Experiment 2

IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL An alternative account of the results of Experiment 1 is that the participants learned an association between the two frequently co-occurring objects in the scene. In other words, the repeated pairing of the snake and ball may have caused a mutual priming relationship, where seeing one object prepared participants to classify the other one. To rule out this and other lowlevel explanations, including inhibitory priming (Tipper, 1985) and a generic processing advantage for the less recently seen object in each scene (Posner & Cohen, 1984), we ran Experiment 2.

Stimuli and Procedure.

The stimuli and procedure for Experiment 2 were nearly identical to Experiment 1, except that we adjusted the scene to eliminate the logical connection between the two visible objects and the one revealed in the cup (Figure 1B). In this version, a third object was placed inside the cup from the beginning of the scene. Instead of scooping an object from behind the occluder, the cup moved toward the wall but stopped short, never going behind it, and then returned to its original position with the third object still inside. Thus, the third item remained in the cup for the whole scene until the cup disappeared, removing the disjunctive relation between the object in the cup and the two objects covered by the wall (i.e., the object in the cup didn't have to be the snake OR the ball that were initially seen). Identical to Experiment 1, participants were asked to classify the object in the cup as soon as it was revealed, pressing "1" if they saw a snake and "2" if they saw a ball (with the key mapping randomized between subjects).

Results and Discussion

The trial exclusion criteria were identical to Experiment 1. We hypothesized that canceling the major disjunctive premise of the inference (i.e., the object in the cup is the snake OR the ball) should suppress spontaneous logical inferences, meaning that different-object trials should not be any faster than same-object trials. Participant-level difference scores between same-object and different-object trials deviated from normality (Shapiro-Wilk: W = .947, p < .001), so we conducted a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which found no significant difference in response times between conditions (M different-object = 790.63 ms, SD = 171.86 ms; M same-object = 791.23 ms, SD = 174.50 ms; V = 8,851.5, p = .733; Figure 2B). To confirm that this null result reflected genuine evidence for the absence of an effect, we compared mean response times between the same-and different-object conditions, by fitting two linear models: one containing only an intercept and one containing the same/different condition as a fixed effect (Wagenmakers, 2007). The added term failed to improve model fit (BIC₁ = 86,048.95; BIC₂ = 86,057.68), and a BIC-derived Bayes factor strongly favored the null model (BF₁₀ = 0.013). These results suggest that object condition had no discernible effect on response times when the logical relation between scene elements was removed (Figure 2B). Consistent with this, the accumulation of response time differences between same-object and different-object trials in Experiment 2 was effectively absent (β = 2.38, SE = 1.33, t = 1.78, p = .075) and significantly flatter than the robust upward trajectory observed in Experiment 1 (Interaction: β = -17.09, SE = 1.91, t = -8.97, p < .001; Figure 2F). Altogether, these results show that when the disjunctive relationship in the scene is cancelled, participants no longer showed an object classification advantage on different-object trials, undermining associative learning or other low-level explanations for Experiment 1.

Experiment 3

Another alternative explanation for the results of Experiment 1 is that participants didn't make a disjunctive inference to predict the type of object hidden inside the cup but instead reacted to disruptions of object continuity (e.g., the magical appearance of a second snake from the cup).

That is, in the same-object trials, the object seen reemerging from behind the gray occluder (e.g., a snake) was the same type as the object revealed from the cup (e.g., a snake; see Figure 1A). But our visual system expects objects to move across spatiotemporally continuous paths (Spelke, 1990; Spelke et al., 1995; Yi et al., 2008). Thus, the same-object outcome implies the appearance of a new, third object into the scene (e.g., a second snake). As a result, subjects' reaction time in the same-object trials might slow down because an object has magically appeared. To test these accounts, we ran Experiment 3.

Method

Participants.

We recruited 507 Prolific participants, with a preregistered target of N = 400 postexclusions. One hundred seven participants were excluded for failing to meet the preregistered 80% accuracy criterion.

Stimuli and Procedure.

We modified the scene setup to include two cups: one initially empty and the other containing a third object of a different color from the others in the scene (red or blue; see Figure 1C). As in Experiment 1, the empty cup moved behind the occluder, scooping one of the two red objects and then returning to its original position with a partially occluded object inside. Thus, there was a disjunctive relation between the red object in a cup and the initial two red objects (i.e., the object in the cup is the snake OR the ball). The other cup (e.g., the cup with the blue item) merely moved straight up and back down, staying fully visible without dipping behind the occluder. Thus, there was no disjunctive relationship between the blue object IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL and the two red objects covered by the wall. Each cup's movement lasted 1.5 s. Then, one of the two original items in the center moved vertically upwards, briefly emerging (500 ms) from the occluder before re-hiding behind the wall. Finally, one of the cups disappeared, revealing which item was inside. Each movie was 15.5 s. At the end of each trial, one of the cups revealed its contents. In half of the trials, it was the cup that had scooped an object (e.g., the cup with the red item; scooping-cup trials), while in the other half, it was the cup with the extra item from the very beginning (e.g., the cup with the blue item; no-scooping-cup trials).

There were 64 types of movies varying along six dimensions. In addition to the three dimensions from Experiments 1 and 2, we manipulated between subjects the initial position of the filled cup (left or right of the occluder) and the color of its object's top (red or blue). Within subjects, we also manipulated whether the disappearing cup was the one that contained a scooped object (scooping-cup trials) or the one that contained an object from the beginning of the scene (no-scooping-cup trials). These within-subject manipulations were counterbalanced such that participants saw each combination of conditions an equal number of times.

In the instructions phase, subjects were introduced to two cups, both of which contained partially hidden objects with unknown identities. Participants completed one block of 48 trials, divided into six bins of eight. Each bin included an equal number of trials for each combination of the two main manipulations: scooping / no-scooping and same-object / different-object. The order within each bin was random, except that each condition was prohibited from repeating more than twice consecutively. On each trial, participants had 1.5 seconds to classify the final object before the trial ended. All other aspects of the procedure were identical to Experiments 1 and 2.

The setup with two cups allowed us to test whether participants perform disjunctive inference or merely detect a violation of continuity. If present, a logical inference should facilitate object classification by creating an anticipatory representation of the hidden object's identity (e.g., "the item in the cup IS a ball") on scooping-cup, different-object trials. The trial-exclusion criteria matched Experiment 1, aside from withholding eight practice trials to accommodate the larger condition set. IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL Experiments 1 to 3 offer clear evidence that our mind generates automatic disjunctive inference to resolve visually ambiguous events and facilitate visual processing. But what is the nature of the operations underlying these inferences in visual scene processing? Logical rules like disjunctive inference are deductively valid regardless of the statistical likelihood of their conclusions, and some have proposed that logical operations should arise irresistibly whenever their logical premises are encoded-even when statistical data oppose their conclusions (Braine & O'Brien, 1998; Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988; Quilty-Dunn et al., 2023). In Experiment 4 (N = 200), we tested the prediction that disjunctive inference is impervious to statistical counterevidence by measuring error rates in an attentionally engaging object classification task designed to pit logical inferences against conflicting outcome frequencies.

Method

Participants.

Two hundred Prolific participants were recruited with the same eligibility criteria as previous experiments. At the end of the task, participants rated their focus during the experiment on a scale from 1 to 100, and per our preregistration, participants with a focus rating below 80 were excluded from analysis. Thirty-three additional individuals were tested but excluded for this reason.

Stimuli and Procedure.

The stimuli for Experiment 4 were similar to Experiment 1, with two key differences designed to reduce the spatiotemporal attentional demands of the scene. First, the initial position of the cup was adjusted to be spatially closer to the object pair. We also accelerated the timing of the events: Soon after the snake and ball entered each scene, they were fully occluded by the wall; at the same time, the empty cup moved behind the occluder and reappeared 783 ms later with a partially visible object inside. After another 1.2 s, one of the IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL original objects emerged from behind the wall and remained visible for 1.3 s. Finally, the object inside the cup was fully revealed for 1 s. Each movie lasted a total of 5.53 s, considerably shorter than the 15.5 s movies used in previous experiments. These changes helped participants sustain attention to the unfolding events and shortened the interval over which the premises of the disjunctive inference had to be maintained.

During the instructions phase, participants were trained to use the "S" and "B" keys to classify the objects (replacing the "1" or "2" keys used in previous experiments). After completing one demo trial, participants proceeded directly to the main experiment. Every other aspect of the instructions was identical to Experiment 1.

The notable difference to the procedure was that we manipulated the outcomes' frequencies so that one of the objects was revealed far less often than the other (Figure 1D).

Regardless of the preceding events in each movie, one of the objects was revealed in 66.6% of trials (e.g., a snake; frequent condition), while the other was revealed in only 33.3% (e.g., a ball; infrequent condition). Within each frequency condition, logical consistency was balanced, yielding four trial types: frequent-same (33%), frequent-different (33%), infrequent-same (16.5%), and infrequent-different (16.5%). This structure allowed us to test whether the effect of the logical prediction persisted even when it was statistically unlikely.

Finally, in accordance with our preregistration, we changed the primary dependent measure from response time to error rate, reasoning that errors more strongly index automaticity since they show participants not only generating but acting on their logical predictions.

If disjunctive inference is difficult to suppress despite contradicting the underlying frequencies of the event, logical inferences should continue to facilitate object classification-IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL even when the predicted outcome is statistically unlikely. Thus, participants should commit fewer errors classifying an unlikely outcome when it is predicted by the disjunctive inference than when it is not. On the contrary, if statistical counterevidence suppresses logical predictions, error rates should be comparable across infrequent-same and infrequent-different.

As in Experiments 1 and 2, the first four trials for each subject were excluded as practice trials. For each participant, we computed the average error rate-the percentage of trials in which participants provided an incorrect response-for each experimental condition (frequent-same, frequent-different, infrequent-same, and infrequent-different). We then computed participantlevel difference scores between infrequent-same and infrequent-different trials and conducted a Shapiro-Wilk test, which revealed a significant departure from normality (W = .793, p < .001).

General Discussion

What is the source of logical intuitions in the human mind? Here we found evidence that a fast and automatic logical inference spontaneously and irresistibly arises during the processing of dynamic visual scenes. This disjunctive inference implicates the process of inferring the identity of a hidden object, in the absence of positive evidence, by excluding other physically plausible alternatives. Our finding supports the proposal that our logical intuitions may reflect, at least in part, core logical operations which emerge even in the absence of perceivable, external linguistic structures.

Across five large, preregistered online experiments, we presented adults with physical events involving sufficient ambiguity to prompt a logical inference: the identity of an object was concealed but could be logically inferred by contextual elements of the scene. Participants had to quickly detect the conclusion of our movies: an object's appearance which violated the laws of logic in half of the trials. Our approach uncovered striking properties of disjunctive inference as it arises during visual scene processing, beyond the presentation of explicit linguistic input.

The disjunctive inference exhibited analogous properties to the logical inferences implicated in the processing of natural language (Chierchia, 2013; Montague, 1973). The disjunctive inference was triggered when the logical relations among the elements of the scene IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL while others have argued that these judgments reflect sensitivity to superficial syntactic cues in written arguments that merely coincide with logical validity (Ghasemi et al., 2022). Thus, by eliciting deductive inference in the absence of any explicit syntactic processing, our findings offer a convergent source of evidence for rapid and automatic logical intuitions in the adult mind.

Future research can deploy our non-linguistic approach to the study of intuitive logical inferences to further disentangle the contribution of genuine logical processing and syntax-based heuristics.

Strikingly, the logical inference reported here displays a form of irresistibility: its effect was impervious to statistical patterns in the visual input and affected participants' responses even when predicting an outcome that was statistically unlikely, and thus facilitating the classification of statistically likely and unlikely outcomes alike. That is, in Experiment 4, we manipulated the frequencies of the objects' appearances, to the extent that one was twice as likely to appear as the other. Participants' false-alarm rates increased in the unlikely outcome trials, revealing sensitivity to the outcomes' frequencies. However, the magnitude and persistence of the logical inference facilitation effect was not different for likely and unlikely outcomes. This is especially remarkable considering that disjunctive inference's imperviousness to counterevidence was higher than that of a non-logical form of object classification; in Experiment 5, our frequency manipulation did affect a closely matched non-deductive object-mapping inference that participants used to classify objects, and which was suppressed when conflicting with statistical counterevidence.

Importantly, disjunctive inference's higher imperviousness to counterevidence highlights the logical nature of this deductive operation, and suggests that it is distinct from non-logical processes of object mapping. From a logical point of view, logical inferences like the disjunctive IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL inference are deductively valid regardless of whether their conclusion turns out to be empirically corroborated. As a result, theories and models of logical reasoning have proposed that these inferences are realized by mental operations irresistibly generated whenever sufficient conditions (evidence) are met, regardless of the statistical support of their predictions (Braine & O'Brien, 1998; Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988; Quilty-Dunn et al., 2023; Rips, 1994). Thus, the imperviousness of disjunctive inference aligns with this logical framework and suggests a new behavioral footprint to track the language-of-thought format beyond the domain of natural language (Dehaene et al., 2022; Fodor, 1975; Quilty-Dunn et al., 2023; Sablé-Meyer et al., 2021).

From a developmental perspective, our results reveal that the same disjunctive inference documented in human infants (Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2018) persists in adults and arises automatically from visual events alone. This developmental continuity aligns with the hypothesis that adult logical intuitions may originate from core logical capacities that are independent of linguistic structures or extensive practice with logical tasks. Whether these logical operations shared by infants and adults also extend beyond inferences about visual scenes -playing a role in language comprehension (Chierchia, 2013), written text processing (Lea, 1995), and the acquisition of natural language logical vocabulary (Saponaro et al., 2026) -is an avenue for future research.

The proposal of a core logical inference raises additional questions about the relationship between this logical operation and infants' core understanding of physical objects (Spelke, 1990; Spelke & Kinzler, 2007). Our results begin to bear on this question: in Experiment 3, adults anticipated the identity of the object inside the cup rather than merely reacting to violations of object permanence, consistent with the presence of a logical prediction that is not reducible to the mere violation of object permanence. Additionally, in Experiments 4 and 5, we compared this IRRESISTIBLY LOGICAL disjunctive inference to object mapping, a prototypical computation of the core object system.

The inference proved more irresistible, a result that distinguishes disjunctive inference from previously uncovered core object computations. Therefore, our results raise two possibilities for future investigation: infants' object representations may be richer than previously assumed, encompassing domain-specific logical computations that represent and exclude the possible identities of an object. Alternatively, these computations may belong to an independent early logical system operating across domains. Adjudicating between these accounts is an important task for future research, and ongoing work has started to address this question by documenting that infants and young children make disjunctive inference in multiple domains (Bohus et al., 2023; Cesana-Arlotti & Halberda, 2024; Wang & Cesana-Arlotti, 2025).

The persistence of core logical operations across the lifespan -and the seamless interface of these operations with adult vision -also opens new opportunities to investigate the cognitive foundation of logical thought with the methods and tools of vision science. For example, future research should investigate the relationship between deductive inferences in the processing of visual scenes and inner speech. Our procedure reliably elicits automatic logical inferences in the absence of external linguistic symbols. By combining our methods with verbal shadowing, it is now possible to test whether inner speech is dispensable for these rapid mental deductions, as is the case in preverbal infancy.

In conclusion, we found that human adults, when presented with visual events compatible with multiple possibilities, generate logical deductions that are automatic and irresistible. These inferences emerge in the absence of external linguistic symbols and bear the key signatures of computations that are logical and intuitive. Constraints on Generality.

Participants were U.S.-based, English-speaking adults recruited through the online platform Prolific. Previous findings suggest that the results of our experiments may generalize broadly across adult populations. The same logical operation has been documented in preverbal infants (Cesana-Arlotti et al., 2018 , 2020), suggesting that it does not depend on formal schooling, literacy, or a particular linguistic environment. Our paradigm also presupposes typical adult visual processing and would require adaptation for populations with severe visual impairment. We have no reason to believe our findings depend on other characteristics of the participants, materials, or context.
