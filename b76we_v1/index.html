What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

LEGO® Serious Play® (LSP) is a methodology designed to help individuals and groups engage in meaningful problem-solving and communication as they address a variety of individual and organizational challenges. An LSP intervention was designed to help undergraduate students gain experience in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics. Results revealed that the intervention significantly increased students' self-reported experience of expressing a Christian perspective on ethics (Mdifference = .54, SDdifference = .68, d = .80, p < .001). The results suggest that LSP can effectively facilitate student experience in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics and can potentially contribute to other learning outcomes.

[div-01]

Christian higher education has historically focused on providing instruction based on biblical principles and values (Ringenberg, 2006). While Christian institutions also provide market-relevant instruction that prepares graduates for a variety of vocational pursuits (Perrin, 2023), integrating faith into the classroom content remains a priority and thus an ongoing area of study (Alleman et al., 2016). A Christian worldview holds that the Bible is the primary source of spiritual authority through which all subjects are taught (Young, 2023). In a Christian college, a faith-based curriculum may be part of a holistic experience that includes extracurricular experiences such as chapel and mission trips (Sriram & McLevain, 2016). However, the primary way a Christian worldview is integrated into a student's studies occurs in classes. One of the desired student learning outcomes in these classes may be that students can express a Christian perspective on the topic of the class.

This study focuses on helping undergraduate students express a Christian perspective on business ethics. In many business programs, business ethics is a commonly taught subject and is emphasized by leading accrediting bodies (Darnell et al., 2021). This ongoing call for business ethics courses stems from the fact that business decisions require not only objective analysis but also a framework for ethical thinking (Thiel et al., 2012). While core ethical philosophies and sources of authority will differ between Christian and non-Christian institutions, business ethics is taught in both types of schools. Because the effectiveness of such courses is not guaranteed, approaches to teaching business ethics continue to be studied (Rutherford et al., 2012).

The ethical frameworks of business professionals can range from utilitarianism to individualism (Fraedrich & Ferrell, 1992). Broadly speaking, professionals in the marketplace may consider the general good of others (an approach often associated with ethical behavior) or focus on what singularly benefits them as individuals (an approach often associated with unethical behavior). As in philosophical ethics, there can be a wide range of interpretations, perspectives, and ambiguities in determining what is ethical in business (Pheifer, 2021). Hence, learning to express a Christian perspective on business ethics is deemed appropriate by many faith-based institutions so that students can consider how their beliefs may impact both their professional practices and general approach to vocation (Locke et al., 2021).

Christian colleges are likely to emphasize Christ-centered values, moral behavior, and the pursuit of the common good when teaching ethics (Collins & Clanton, 2018). Functionally, an institution of higher education may impart cognitive knowledge and also provide pragmatic mechanisms for application. Therefore, Christian (and most other) colleges focus on outcomebased education (Spady, 1978 (Spady, , 1982 (Spady, , 1994)), which assumes that clearly defined learning outcomes with learning activities and assessments focused on these outcomes will lead to greater learning than without clearly defined learning outcomes. In this study, investigators carefully defined the outcome of articulating a Christian perspective on business ethics, designed an intervention to achieve it, and used the desired outcome as the basis of the assessment.

A Christian Perspective on Business Ethics

A Christian perspective on business ethics is rooted in principles and values that may differ from those found in the general marketplace (Hill, 2018). These differences may include the definitions and measures of success, both quantitative and qualitative (Teichmann et al., 2025). Pragmatically, a Christian perspective on business may lead to different priorities regarding profit, the needs of individuals, and the environment (Crick & Crick, 2024).

In addition to priorities, a Christian perspective on business may especially focus on the moral behavior of organizations, teams, and individuals (Lenka & Kar, 2021). What the secular world may generally refer to as virtue, Christian colleges may frame as a faith perspective on professional practices (Kocur, 2023). The goal of Christian institutions is for their graduates to not only be aware of Christian values related to ethics, but to also act on them regularly (Van Duzer et al., 2006).

The behavior of Christians in the marketplace is one indicator of their ethical values. In addition, there are situations in which Christians may need to verbalize their ethical beliefs, such as when they face morally ambiguous situations that need to be addressed in group contexts (Martin, 2021). To facilitate this, Christians need to understand biblically based ethical principles, engage in self-reflection, and participate in exercises or experiences that provide an opportunity to articulate what they believe (Seng Teck et al., 2025). This is why LEGO® Serious Play® (LSP) may be an appropriate methodology for individuals to examine and articulate a Christian perspective on business ethics.

LSP as a Methodology for Expressing Ethical Perspectives

LSP is a methodology designed to facilitate thinking around intangible concepts, enhance group communication, and promote problem-solving in a variety of organizational settings (Kristiansen & Rasmussen, 2014). In an educational environment, LSP could be described as a pedagogy because its content can be customized to support specific learning outcomes. Functionally, LSP begins with a carefully crafted prompt aligned with the learning outcomes, followed by building a model using LEGO® bricks in response to the prompt and sharing an explanation of the model in a group setting. LSP has been used in business, education, nonprofit, government, church, and military settings, both in small and large group environments (Dykes, 2018). A core principle of LSP is enabling 100% participation (Rasmussen Consulting, n.d.), which means that every individual in every group must contribute to the group activities of constructing individual models with LEGO® bricks and then discussing their meaning. This way, all participants have an equal opportunity to share their models and perspectives associated with the prompt. The required participation of everyone can be particularly important in settings where organizational superiors are present, or some participants are dominant or excessively talkative. LSP has been shown to create a "level-playing-field" through this required sharing (McCusker, 2014). McCusker (2020) found that LSP helped achieve authentic, non-hierarchical communication leading to new insights, a description of participatory learning. Cerezo-Narvaez et al. (2019) also noted that it generated communication resulting in opportunities from an individual point-of-view, through a relaxed, fun approach that generated involvement.

LSP builds on the principles of constructivism (Srivastava, 2020) and constructionism (Papert & Harel, 1991). Constructivism suggests that any interpretive meaning can be assigned to a model by its creator. Pragmatically, this is expressed in LSP by allowing participants to assign any meaning they choose to their LEGO® models, regardless of whether others can see it or not (Henderson et al., 2025). Constructionism suggests that learning occurs especially when individuals are engaged in constructing something external to themselves. While a model may physically resemble a certain value, belief, or idea, participants are encouraged to think beyond the constraints of the visual elements and to allow for abstract expression. This aligns well with the concept of business ethics, which can encompass both pragmatic mechanisms of law and policy and more ambiguous elements of morality and subjectivity (Pheifer, 2021). LSP participants are encouraged to not limit themselves to imitating the branded LEGO® products they can purchase from retailers. While expressing abstract or intangible ideas could be accomplished with a variety of hands-on products, the LEGO® brick provides a convenient, versatile tool for doing so, one that is often familiar to and much-loved by students.

In the context of ethical formation and the development of a Christian perspective on business ethics, LSP offers an intriguing methodology for exploration, discovery, introspection, and, in many cases, for gaining confidence in self-expression and experiencing confirmation from others. Dziubaniuk and Nyholm (2021) suggest that a constructivist approach is an appropriate method for teaching ethics in a classroom setting. Similarly, Sexton and Garner (2020) studied various pedagogies. They found that examining case studies, which are often used in LSP activities, is one of the more effective means for teaching business ethics. Bodell (2022) uses the expression "faith and learning in action" to describe a Christian pedagogy that links knowledge with practical application. This relates to the idea that ethical development is reinforced through case studies and realistic marketplace scenarios. Roos and Victor (2018) stated the original formation of LSP helped address identity, particularly organizational. Ethical climate is a key part of this organizational identity awareness, which suggests the methodology might be a good fit for helping students express ethical values.

The exploration of ethical frameworks may be more productive when an environment of creativity, shared experiences, and teamwork (Martin-Cruz et al., 2022) is created, as well as when self-awareness is fostered. Quinn et al. (2022) suggest that LSP is "an enabler for creating new awareness and insights within the individual" (p.102). In addition, Dykes McGehee (2018) suggests that the LSP method builds the mental muscle of creative confidence and "affirms the need for the 'other' in the creative process and exposes increased levels of perspective-taking among participants" (p.279). Lear et al. (2020) successfully used LSP to develop confidence in collaboration towards problem solving. Moreover, Kolb's (1984) theory of experiential learning posits that true learning occurs when the individual gains knowledge through experience and then internalizes that new insight through reflection and abstract conceptualization. McCusker (2014) affirmed the value of reflection through the iterative model-building process, which added richness and meaning. The use of symbolism and metaphor with LEGO bricks allows for easier entry or access for participants as they tell stories about their models (McCusker, 2014). The experience of using LSP to build a model, accompanied by self-reflection and the application of that reflection to case studies, is a practical application of Kolb's (1984) experiential learning theory. Grientiz and Schmidt (2012) noted the development of intrinsic motivation and participant satisfaction with the use of LSP, resulting in common understanding among participants. Additional benefits of LSP include active engagement and collaboration. The use of story-telling resulted in productive debate and articulated thinking, using metaphor for inclusivity (Wijayratna & Rocha, 2025). Wheeler et al. (2020) has noted that LSP promotes the development of psychological safety, where team members feel willing to take risks and express vulnerability without fear of shame or embarrassment (Edmondson, 1999). A sense of psychological safety is needed for people of all levels to engage or participate in group settings (Sanders & Stappers, 2008), thus its importance is relevant in LSP experiences. Components of psychological safety such as group awareness (i.e., belonging), participation, and expression of personal perspective are encouraged in the LSP process (Cerezo-Narvaez et al., 2014).

Higher education has traditionally used a variety of pedagogical activities for encouraging both introspection and expression. These methods have included writing papers, taking tests, listening to lectures, responding to case studies, and taking notes. While these methods are often effective in appropriate situations, there is an increased desire to diversify teaching methods that adapt to the shifting demographics of today's college students (Gilbert et al., 2017). LSP provides educators and students with an environment that fosters contemplation, collaboration, creativity, and divergent thinking (Dykes McGehee, 2018; McGehee, 2022; Ferreira et al., 2024). Although students may learn through traditional pedagogical activities, Kahane (2021) argues that LSP offers a novel opportunity for personal and group development that can be used to address a broad range of issues and content, while enhancing the classroom environment. Lopez-Fernandez (2021) obtained a measurable outcome of students successfully completing a content-related exam after an LSP intervention, with more than 90% of students passing, demonstrating the ability for LSP to effectively engage students. Even students who were initially reluctant to participate eventually succeeded in the evaluated course due to the engaging nature of LSP (McCusker, 2014).

One of the strengths of the LSP methodology is its use of scaffolding, grounded in Vygotsky's (1978) Zone of Proximal Development Theory. The theory predicts that students can only learn material that is closely associated with what they already know. LSP provides a literal framework (of plastic bricks) for students to apply their theoretical knowledge. This approach is especially appropriate for exploring ethical perspectives and building upon them. The design of this study, which includes instruction followed by hands-on and verbal expression, provides both a literal framework and a symbolic scaffold to help participants build within their particular "zone."

The effectiveness of LSP in educational settings, particularly in higher education, is an area of emerging study. Several studies have explored the efficacy of LSP for facilitating the growth of college students. James (2015) found that LSP in university courses improved students' understanding and articulation of the material, leading to increased classroom engagement. Other studies point to similar findings regarding engagement, which led to increases in positive educational outcomes. These studies include Nolan (2010), Peabody and Noyes (2017), Zenk et al. (2018), Wheeler (2023), and Peabody and Turesky (2018). However, in an experiment conducted by several of the authors of this study (Dykes McGehee et al., 2024), a LSP intervention had no significant effect on learning outcomes, most likely due to a mismatch between the intervention's content and the desired outcomes. This study seeks to rectify such a mismatch by using an intervention specifically designed to align with the desired student learning outcomes.

When linking broader ethical formation and the LSP methodology, it is important to differentiate between experience expressing a viewpoint, and the actual formation of a worldview. It is also worth noting that self-reported confidence does not guarantee competency, growth, or development when it comes to ethical or spiritual formation. That said, Mansi (2026) suggests that post-pandemic students who have been heavily influenced by digital interfaces may be increasingly hesitant to share viewpoints in public spaces. Similar declines in classroom engagement have been found by Chirikov et al. (2025). This suggests that while self-reported confidence may be less robust than measured growth in competency, the current landscape of higher education will benefit from opportunities for students to gain experience and confidence in sharing their beliefs in classroom settings.

This Study's Hypothesis

A properly designed LSP intervention may help undergraduate students' to express a Christian perspective on business ethics. The active, experiential processes of LSP (Gauntlett, 2007) encourage individuals to first reflect and then collaborate with others through the building and sharing of LEGO® models. This methodology creates a safe environment to share ideas about the topic under discussion. We hypothesize that students will have self-report having greater experience expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics after a properly designed LSP intervention than before the intervention. Such a goal aligns with the learning outcomes of many undergraduate business courses at Christian universities.

Methods

A quantitative pre-experimental study utilizing a survey-based pretest-posttest design was conducted in an upper-division business ethics class at a Christian university in Southern California that does not require a profession of Christian faith from the students. The purpose of this study was to understand whether participation in a LSP intervention can help students gain measurable experience in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics.

Experimental Design

One section of a required ethics course for business majors, consisting of upper-division undergraduate students, provided data before and after the LSP intervention. The quantitative data came from a pretest and a posttest. The pretest (a survey measuring the participants' selfreported experience with expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics) was conducted at the end of Week 4 of a 16-week semester. The posttest (an identical survey) was conducted at the beginning of Week 8 of the same semester. The intervention was administered between the pretest and posttest, during Weeks 5, 6, and 7. The intervention consisted of six 85-minute class sessions during this period. After the post-test, students participated in small group interviews to debrief. These interviews provided additional insight into their experiences during the intervention.

Participants

Participants were upper-division (typically in their final year) undergraduate students with a business-related major. All participants were 18 years or older. Although the LSP intervention was part of the required coursework assigned in the syllabus, participation in the pretest and posttest was optional. The right of the students to opt out of the pre-and/or posttest was communicated multiple times. The research project received Institutional Review Board review and students provided their informed consent before participating. The consent provided clear instructions on the purpose of the study, the rights of participants, steps that had been taken to ensure confidentiality, and actions that could be taken by participants if they had questions or concerns about anything associated with the study. This included contact information for the institution's Research Integrity Officer.

It is reasonable to suggest that the academically based power relationship between the instructor and students, along with the culture of the Christian university setting, might solicit outcomes that did not reflect autonomy. To mitigate this risk, participants were told multiple times (both verbally and in writing) that while the intervention itself was a required class activity, their responses would not be graded and would not have any impact on the outcome of the course.

All 25 students in the class participated in the intervention and the anonymous, online survey-based pretest/posttest after providing their informed consent. However, only 21 participants provided codes (the last 4 digits of their phone numbers) in the pretest that could be matched to the codes that they provided in the posttest. The only demographic information collected was sex. Of the 21 participants with usable data, 12 were female, and 9 were male.

The Intervention

The LSP intervention consisted of 6 class sessions, each led by 2-3 certified and experienced LSP facilitators. The participants were divided into four groups of approximately six students, ensuring that all participants would have adequate time to share their ideas. In LSP, a build consists of a verbal or written prompt provided by the facilitators, a time for building during which each participant uses LEGO® bricks to respond to the prompt, and a time for sharing the meaning of what one has built with other participants in one's group. Builds are sometimes followed by a time where a spokesperson from each group shares with the other groups what his or her group experienced.

Throughout the six sessions, the facilitators followed the prescribed LSP protocol. This protocol includes initial builds to familiarize participants with the LSP method, while also building trust among group members and with the facilitators. The facilitators encouraged communication, valued contributions, and ensured every individual and group had adequate opportunities to share. The facilitators also issued appropriate challenges to ideas, which provided further opportunities for participants to reflect on and share their respective models. For each session, participants changed groups to experience a greater variety of perspectives.

The first session was not related to the intervention's main content. As prescribed in LSP, the goal of this session was to familiarize participants with the LSP method and process (Kristiansen & Rasmussen, 2014). The following sessions included case studies and ethical dilemmas that participants might encounter in a workplace setting. Topics included how participants would handle the responsibility of remote work positions when unsupervised, and how they would react to co-workers who violated company rules. Each build included several minutes of constructing a model, followed by brief opportunities for all participants to individually share the meaning of their models.

During the final session, the facilitators encouraged participants to express their beliefs concerning a Christian perspective on business ethics. Participants were asked to participate in builds that were introduced by these prompts: 1.

Build a model of your personal Christian business ethics worldview. 2. Add some ethical challenges faced by Christians in business ethics. Do not propose solutions at this point. 3. Adapt your model to include your worldview, challenges, and how you will guard against those challenges in the future. As with prior builds, each participant was instructed to verbally describe their model to their small group. The full details of the six sessions, including all the build prompts, are found in Appendix A.

Measures

For both the pretest and posttest, the Experience Discussing a Christian Perspective on Ethics Scale was used to measure the degree to which the participants saw themselves as having experience discussing expressing a Christian perspective on ethics in workplace settings. This ad hoc scale, created for this study, consists of 8 Likert items such as "I have experience explaining to others what it means to have a Christian perspective on business ethics" and "I have experience explaining how I would act ethically from a Christian perspective when new situations arise" (1 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree). The items were chosen because of their face validity and were reviewed by subject matter experts who had experience teaching undergraduate and graduate business ethics courses. The focus was on measuring the students' experience (not confidence, self-efficacy, or perceived competence) in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics, which mapped into the learning outcome of "express a Christian perspective on business ethics." See Appendix B for the complete scale. In addition to having high face validity, Cronbach's coefficient of reliability for the scale was high (𝛼 = .89).

Small Group Debriefing Interviews

Participants verbally shared their experience during small group interviews with six to eight participants each, which were conducted after the intervention and the posttest. The participants were randomly divided into three groups, and each group was led by one of the facilitators who conducted the intervention. This increased rapport and leveraged existing relationships of trust that had been built over the previous weeks. Students were not required to answer questions, but facilitators encouraged participation and utilized a discussion format in presenting the questions. Six questions were presented to the participants, who could respond to the degree that they desired: 1. Tell me what it means to have a Christian perspective on business ethics. 2. How would you explain to people how you personally apply a Christian perspective to business ethics? 3. Do you feel prepared to make ethical decisions in the workplace? Explain. 4. Are there ethical challenges in the workplace that you do not feel prepared to face? If so, please describe them. 5. Looking back at the activities and the discussions, what is your biggest takeaway about business ethics? 6. How do you see your faith influencing the way you approach ethical decisions in business?

Responses to the six questions were recorded by facilitators and then consolidated into a shared spreadsheet. The three facilitators worked together to informally conduct a thematic analysis (Ahmed, 2025) using Braun and Clarke's (2006) 6 phase approach: 1) They familiarized themselves with the data, 2) they proposed initial codes, 3) they found initial themes, 4) they reviewed the texts together for additional themes, 5) they chose final categories for themes, and 6) they wrote a summary and analysis. One of the purposes of the posttest interviews was to evaluate whether there were key deviations between the quantitative results and verbalized themes from participants.

Results

Quantitative Results

To test the hypothesis that participants would have self-report having increased more experience expressing a Christian perspective on ethics after the intervention than before, both a pretest test score (M = 3.43, SD = .54) and a posttest score (M = 3.97, SD = .67) was calculated for each participant by averaging their responses to the 8 items on the Experience Discussing a Christian Perspective on Ethics Scale (Appendix B). A paired two-sample t-test comparing the means indicated that participants, as hypothesized, perceived they had significantly increased experience expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics after the intervention than before, t(20) = 3.66, p < .001, 1-tail. A one-tailed test was used because our hypothesis was directional.

The effect size of the intervention (Mdifference = .54, SDdifference = .68, d = .80, 95% CIdifference [0.25 -0.83], 95% CIeffect size [0.37 -1.22]) was large according to Cohen's (1988) criteria. This suggests that the intervention may have greatly increased students' perception of having experience of discussing a Christian perspective on ethics. However, the preexperimental design used does not allow for claims of causation.

There was no significant evidence that the intervention affected males and females differently. The correlation between the change in score and sex was not significant, rpb(19) = -.21, p = .36, 2-tails,0 = male,1 = female.

Small Group Debriefing Observations

A consistent theme from the small group discussions included an increased sense of confidence in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics. Moreover, perhaps because of increased confidence, participants indicated that they believed common ground on ethical issues is often possible and that individuals can agree on the right thing to do, even when approaching situations from different perspectives.

Participants acknowledged their lack of professional experience and that true tests of their conviction would occur as they encountered ethical quandaries in professional life. In addition, participants noted that ethical decisions are complicated and that listening to other perspectives may be required. Participants also noted the need to connect theory with practice. An example response was, "I think the biggest takeaway I took was when we're talking about ethics, we kind of tend to think these things are just theoretical and won't actually happen in everyday life, but it's important to prepare yourself for these everyday situations and to continue to practice these seemingly theoretical problems." Overall, the content of the group interviews was consistent with the quantitative results. Students expressed the perception that they gained experience in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics.

Discussion

This study examined whether a carefully designed LSP intervention could increase would be associated with an increase in students' self-reported experience in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics. A pretest-posttest survey showed that the six-session LSP intervention was perceived by students to greatly increase their experience in this domain. Information shared during the small group debriefing interviews was congruent with this outcome, as participants verbally indicated that the intervention gave them notably greater confidence and experience in expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics.

Relationship with Previous Study

This project was a standalone study, but it followed a previous study that also measured the effectiveness of LSP (Dykes McGehee et al., 2024). In the previous study, the intervention involved first-year students who were supposed to develop their sense of vocational identity (Gupta et al., 2015). Post-intervention interviews from the first study indicated that the students had considered ideas relevant to vocational identity, but the pretest-posttest data showed no significant change in the learning outcome, namely, a more defined sense of vocational identity. The reasons for the null results are speculative. However, inadequate preparation of the participant group, flaws in the intervention design, and a lack of life experience among the participants may have contributed.

For this study, the authors revised the design process and incorporated lessons learned from the first study. These revisions appear to have contributed to the success of the current LSP intervention. In addition to the quantitative results, the information shared during the debriefing discussions indicated that the intervention was successful. This was not the case in the first study.

Pre-LSP Content

In the previous study (Dykes McGehee et al., 2024), which aimed to develop students' vocational identity through an LSP intervention, participants were not provided with preintervention content on the subject matter. The decision not to include preparation material was intentional, as it was hypothesized that presenting such content might bias participants' perspectives. While this decision was made with good intentions, the lack of life experience and understanding of the concept may have made the LSP intervention difficult for students to understand and apply to their vocational identity.

In contrast, participants in the current study received four weeks of traditional classroom instruction before the workshop, providing a context where scaffolding of concepts was possible. This included introductions to ethical theories, law, and policy, along with a variety of marketrelevant case studies, which provided a framework for understanding the content of the LSP intervention. The introductory lessons also included small group discussions of case studies, which may have made it easier for participants to engage with and benefit from the LSP intervention.

Relevance of the Intervention for the Learning Outcome

The concept of scaffolding draws on Vygotsky's (1978) Zone of Proximal Development, which suggests that students learn most effectively when new concepts are intentionally connected to what they already know. It is reasonable to suggest that the ethical decision-making models grounded in Christian values and organizational principles presented prior to the workshop provided a shared foundation. LSP was then used to help students work through realistic workplace situations, allowing them to translate theory into practice. It appears that the main reason for the significant results in this study (in contrast to the previous study, Dykes McGehee et al., 2024) is that the LSP intervention and the learning outcome measured (experience expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics) were closely aligned.

Design of the LSP Intervention

Another factor that appears to have contributed to the success of this study was the careful design of the LSP interventions. Most of the six sessions of the intervention began with a skills-building exercise designed to engage students with the LSP methodology and expose them to new idea concepts that could be expressed with LSP (See the warm-up build prompts in Appendix A). While participants displayed increasing comfort and familiarity with the process throughout the three-week intervention, these first builds in each session may have helped students recall material from prior sessions and re-engage quickly with the LSP process. Moving from the case study builds to the more personal builds may have helped participants become more comfortable sharing their own perspectives. Finally, as students moved from creating individual models to collaborating on shared builds by merging and adapting their personal models, they developed a better understanding of how to express a Christian perspective of ethics through these verbal and hands-on interactions.

In addition to experience expressing a Christian perspective of business ethics, students may have strengthened their ethical judgment, organizational awareness, and sense of responsibility, all of which are competencies required for effective management and ethical behavior in organizational settings.

LSP in Christian Higher Education

This study has demonstrated that LSP may be used to achieve the desired learning outcomes through a well-designed series of prompts that closely align with those outcomes. Similar interventions could be implemented with other materials, but because LEGO® has a brand reputation (Wang et al., 2025), LSP interventions are able to build upon students' prior experience with the product. So, student engagement is likely to be high, and the activity structure ensures that everyone participates. Because of the versatility of LEGO® bricks and figures, LSP activities are easily adaptable to Christian contexts. Christianity often addresses abstract concepts (e.g., the non-material world, faith, love, and service), and LSP enables students to think more concretely about them. Such activities are not limited to business classes but can be used in any class that deals with abstract ideas.

Limitations

A pre-experimental design, such as that used in this study, cannot demonstrate that the intervention caused the change in the outcome. Because Christian institutions often provide multiple concurrent programs that contribute to an individual's personal growth (Dunaetz, 2025), we cannot be sure that the increase in experience expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics is due to the LSP intervention. More specifically, lectures, discussion, reflection, and case studies in other classes taken at the same time limit the ability to specifically attribute outcomes to the LSP intervention. Nevertheless, the results suggest that the intervention had a statistically significant impact and is therefore an intriguing pedagogical activity for future study and application.

The participants in the study were full-time students at a private, Christian university that has intentionally designed a holistic development experience for all students. These experiences include faith-integrated curriculum, dedicated Bible classes, required chapel attendance, and a myriad of opportunities to participate in faith-integrated extracurricular activities, all of which may provide students with the opportunity to discuss business ethics from a Christian perspective.

The study was conducted with one group of participants and did not include a control or comparison group. A future study that includes a control group may allow researchers to more effectively isolate the impact of LSP intervention. However, the intervention effect size was large, and there were only 4 weeks between the pretest and posttest, providing evidence that at least part of the outcome was due to the intervention.

A follow-up study could examine whether increased experience expressing a Christian perspective on business ethics leads to more consistent ethical behavior in professional settings. In addition, researchers could study not only perceptions of expression, but also competency development.

Conclusion

LSP is increasingly used in higher education. Its constructivist and constructionist pedagogy is adaptable to any course that deals with abstract ideas that should apply to real-life situations. This study has demonstrated that a well-designed LSP activity may contribute to accomplishing the learning outcomes in a business ethics class in a Christian university.
