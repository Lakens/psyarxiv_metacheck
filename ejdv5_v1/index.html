Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Gambling-related harms are an important public health issue, with suicide representing one of their most serious potential consequences. However, although previous literature has identified elevated prevalence of suicidality among people who gamble, less is known about how gambling-related harms develop and subsequently relate to suicidal crisis.

Aims: To characterise the events and circumstances preceding gambling-related suicide and serious suicide attempts, and to examine the interacting socioecological, environmental and psychological processes through which gambling-related harms may have been linked to suicidal crisis.

Design: Exploratory multi-source psychological autopsy and qualitative inquiry, co-produced with people with lived experience. Narrative interviews were integrated, where available, with banking, gambling operator, health and coronial records. Interviews underwent iterative thematic analysis; records were analysed descriptively at case level using time series, case timelines and cross-case matrices.

Setting: United Kingdom. Study period: Jan 2023 to Dec 2025.

Participants/Cases: Twenty core cases were examined: 10 people who had died by gamblingrelated suicide, represented by close relatives or other affected people, and 10 people who had survived a serious suicide attempt associated with gambling-related harms. People who died were aged 19-46 years (9 male, 1 female); attempt survivors were aged 34-62 years (7 male, 3 female).

Measurements: Narrative interviews lasted approximately 3-4 hours and covered broad life history, key aspects of gambling history, industry interactions, suicidality, health, money, perceptions of gambling, and help-seeking and treatment. Record data were available for 14 of 20 cases. Sources included banking and operator activity, communications, health records and, in a small number, coronial or inquest material; coverage varied substantially between cases.

Results: Ten interconnected themes were identified: normalisation of gambling; the 'responsible gambling' narrative and individualisation of responsibility; industry practices and products; regulatory and policy weaknesses; lack of knowledge, information and understanding; shame, selfblame and stigma; money, debt and financial desperation; lack of recognition and ineffective treatment; lapse and recurrence; and entrapment and hitting the lowest point. Documentary records were consistent with interview findings, including intense or renewed gambling close to serious suicide attempts in several cases, substantial financial losses and borrowing, inducements and, in some cases, account restrictions followed by switching between operators.

In these cases, gambling-related suicidal crises involved interacting commercial, financial, psychological, relational and service-level processes. The findings identify potential opportunities for earlier recognition, stronger safeguards and sustained support.

INTRODUCTION

Gambling-related harms are increasingly recognised as a public health issue, with causes and consequences extending beyond the person who gambles, to families and wider communities and other organisations. These harms can include (but are not limited to) financial difficulties, relationship disruption, emotional and psychological distress, poorer health and disruption to work and other areas of everyday life [1]. Additionally, suicide represents one of the most serious potential consequences of gambling-related harms. Clinical, population and register-based studies have reported substantial levels of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts among people experiencing gambling-related harms or gambling disorder [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]. Longitudinal and register-based evidence also supports an association between gambling harms and subsequent suicidal behaviour [3, 6]. These studies are important in establishing the association between gambling harms and suicidality but provide less insight into how gambling harms develop and become embedded within the circumstances preceding a suicidal crisis.

Gambling-related suicidality is unlikely to be explained adequately by any single factor, such as financial harm, mental health difficulties or individual vulnerability. A systematic review of qualitative evidence identified financial harm, shame, relationship difficulties and gambling as a means of escape among the factors associated with gambling-related suicidality [7]. A previous scoping review similarly highlighted the need for more detailed research into the mechanisms linking gambling and suicide, including the use of psychological autopsy methods [8].

Psychological autopsy reconstructs the circumstances preceding a death through interviews with people who knew the deceased and, where available, documentary material. It is an established approach in suicide research because it allows psychological, social and environmental information to be considered together in ways that are rarely possible using routine datasets [9]. Wong et al. identified pathological gambling in 17 suicide cases in a Hong Kong case-control psychological autopsy [10], while Şahin et al. described 24 suicide or suspected-suicide cases with documented gambling history or gambling-related debt in a retrospective forensic autopsy series from Türkiye [11]. Together, these studies provide important clinical and forensic evidence but there is scope for more detailed investigation of how commercial, financial, psychological, relational and service-level processes develop and interact before suicidal crisis.

How gambling harms are conceptualised and communicated is important, because it can shape how warning signs are recognised and where responsibility for prevention is placed. 'Responsible gambling' frameworks have traditionally emphasised individual self-regulation [12], whereas public health approaches direct attention to commercial and structural determinants, including product characteristics, and availability and marketing [13]. Gambling-related stigma can further intensify shame and inhibit disclosure or help-seeking [14].

The policy context around gambling harms in England also changed during the study. The 2023 Suicide Prevention Strategy identifies harmful gambling as a population-level suicide risk factor and includes actions intended to improve awareness and support, and to improve data and evidence bases [15]. NICE guidance published in 2025 recognises suicide as a possible outcome of gambling-related harms and recommends asking about gambling when people present with mental health concerns, including self-harm or suicidal thoughts [16]. These developments strengthen the case for examining how gambling harms escalate and where opportunities for recognition and intervention may arise. The rationale for this programme of work has been outlined previously [17].

The present study addressed this evidence gap through an in-depth psychological autopsy and qualitative inquiry co-produced with people with lived experience of gambling-related harms. Two research questions were addressed: (1) what events and circumstances precede gambling-related suicide or serious suicide attempts; and (2) what patterns illuminate the socioecological, environmental and psychological pathways underlying these events?

METHOD

Design and setting

An exploratory psychological autopsy study, supplemented by an in-depth qualitative inquiry, was conducted in the United Kingdom between January 2023 and December 2025. The design intentionally brought together two forms of knowledge: retrospective accounts from people close to someone who had died by gambling-related suicide, and first-person accounts from people who had survived a serious suicide attempt associated with gambling harms. Documentary evidence was incorporated where available to strengthen chronological reconstruction and to allow verifiable corroboration of gambling behaviour. All participants provided fully informed consent prior to participation, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Lincoln Human Research Ethics Committee (Ref. 16661).

Participants and case definition

The core sample comprised 20 cases. Ten people who had died by gambling-related suicide were represented through interviews with a close relative or other affected person. Additionally, 10 people who had experienced gambling-related harms and survived a serious suicide attempt were interviewed directly. For all core cases, the suicide death or serious suicide attempt had occurred between two and seven years before the research interview.

A suicide or attempt was classified as gambling-related via explicit attribution by the individual or informant that gambling harms were a key driver. Additional supporting evidence, where available, included: a suicide note referring to gambling or gambling-related harms; documentary evidence of significant gambling activity temporally proximal to the event, such as operator records or bank statements; or coroner or clinician documentation identifying gambling as a contributory factor. This classification was used for case inclusion and did not constitute a causal determination that gambling caused the suicide or suicide attempt. A serious suicide attempt was self-defined by participants. Accounts described suicidal intent and, where applicable, medical treatment or hospital attendance. The survivor group was not intended as a control group. Its inclusion allowed first-person accounts of processes that proxy informants could not directly observe, including loss of control, lack of understanding, concealment and suicidal thinking. The design therefore integrated proxy psychological autopsy accounts with survivor accounts within the same analytic framework. The original protocol sought multiple proxy accounts per case. However, given the sensitivity of bereavement following suicide and the need for recruitment to remain participant-led and consistent with the study safeguarding procedures, additional informants were approached only where this was considered appropriate and feasible. This resulted in two additional interviews and two written survey responses.

Recruitment and eligibility

Participants were recruited through three lived-experience partner organisations -Gambling with Lives, GamLEARN and GamFam -who support people affected by gambling-related harms, including bereaved families. These organisations have established relationships with people affected by gambling-related suicide and serious suicide attempts and could provide appropriate safeguarding and mental health and wellbeing support, essential when participants were asked to revisit an exceptionally traumatic event. The necessary depth and details of the investigation could only be achieved through a trust-based approach between the research team and those with lived experience; people bereaved by gambling-related suicide were identified through Gambling with Lives, while people who had survived a serious gambling-related suicide attempt were identified through GamLEARN and GamFam. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older, able and willing to provide informed consent, and considered sufficiently prepared to participate under the study safeguarding protocol. All participants provided written informed consent before participation. Separate consent was obtained for access to documentary records where applicable.

Co-production and lived-experience involvement

The study was co-produced with people with lived experience through Gambling with Lives, GamLEARN and GamFam. Two lived-experience co-investigators were members of the core team and three additional representatives joined the Research Advisory Group. Before the core interviews, 42 people took part in eight lived-experience focus groups, divided equally between people bereaved by suicide and people with experience of gambling and suicidality. These wider consultation groups informed the topic guide, study language and interview procedures. The livedexperience co-investigators undertook ongoing roles in recruitment, interviewing, analysis and interpretation. Wider focus-group participants were subsequently invited to comment on the emerging thematic framework.

Interview topic areas

The topic guides covered basic life story, gambling history and patterns, financial and health circumstances, industry interactions, understanding of gambling and gambling harms, suicidality, personal and relational context, attempts to control gambling, stigma and help-seeking, including treatment. Survivor interviews additionally explored early-life experiences, protective factors and recovery. Full topic areas are provided in Appendix A.

Narrative interviews lasted approximately 3 to 4 hours and were conducted by two researchers, with at least one interviewer with lived experience present at each interview. Given the sensitivity and depth of the material, the interviews were designed to be flexible and participant-led rather than tightly time-limited. They took place either in person or by secure video according to participant preference, with planned breaks and the option to continue across more than one day. Safeguarding procedures operated throughout recruitment, interviewing and follow-up. Participants had access to pre-and post-interview support through Gambling with Lives or GamLEARN, with routes for immediate referral to mental health professionals where required. A distress protocol specified graded responses and stopping rules, and researchers undertook risk assessment and debriefing. Primary interview participants received a £100 voucher, while additional affected-other informants received £20 in recognition of their time.

Documentary case material and analysis

Records relating to gambling and its consequences were requested where available. Some form of record data was obtained for 14 of the 20 core cases (8/10 suicide-death cases and 6/10 attemptsurvivor cases). Sources included banking activity records, gambling operator activity records, account flags, communications with gambling companies and health records; coronial or inquest material was also available in a small number of cases. Most banking and gambling records were obtained by participants or family members through data subject access requests. The period covered varied substantially, from several months around the suicide (~6 months) or suicide attempt to many years of historical activity (>10 years). The majority of the records provided were banking records and data access requests from gambling operators (i.e., gambling activity histories, correspondence with operators).

Record data were provided to the research team in a variety of formats (Excel spreadsheets, PDFs, Word documents, paper records). Data processing was undertaken to encode these records as machine readable, time-stamped data frames. Paper records were first scanned. Scans without text metadata then underwent optical character recognition (OCR) to extract the text and embed it in the scanned PDF files. Then, the PDF files were converted into data tables using a script written in Python. The script used the Camelot package to read PDF images, parse the structure of the table in each image, extract tables and text, and convert them into data frames. The outputs from this were then exported as Excel spreadsheets and imported into R. Data cleaning was then undertaken (e.g., merging of multi-line transactions, gambles with multiple outcomes). Dates and times were converted into a standard format using the lubridate package. Monetary amounts were standardised by removing currency symbols and ensuring that credit-card and current-account transactions used a consistent sign convention. Other administrative information, including balances and statement figures, was also cleaned. For some gambling activity records, further processing was necessitated because the DSAR did not contain necessary information (e.g., about the event being bet upon), or it represented information about a single gamble across multiple rows (e.g., for wager and win, or if there were bonus wins).

Banking data were then searched using a mixture of string searches (in the stringr package) and visual inspection to identify gambling and suspected gambling transactions. Keywords associated with operators were used as terms for the string searches. Visual searches focused on ambiguous cases where a gambling transaction might have occurred but could not be conclusively determined (e.g., withdrawal of winnings where the transaction line did not include the operator, unlicensed gambling, land-based gambling via ATM transactions). Several indicators were used to identify these in the first instance. Transactions with round figures (i.e., £50, £100) were examined, as these were indicative of possible gambling deposits, or ATM withdrawals. ATM withdrawals were considered as potentially indicating gambling where large, multiple transactions occurred over a short period of time (i.e., the same day) and in proximity to locations with known gambling premises, or where these were confirmed in the interviews or by the participant directly. These were coded separately from transactions that could be confirmed as originating from a gambling operator. For both gambling operator and bank transaction records, the data were coded to signify features such as the operator or the type of gambling undertaken.

Once the data were imported into R and pre-processed, each participant's record data were converted into a time series using the fable [18] and feasts [19] packages, supported by fabletools, tsibble [20] and tsibbledata. The data frames were flattened to summarise gambling activity (e.g., number of gambles, total expenditure, net expenditure, average stake) at the daily, weekly, and monthly levels. Then, these were converted into time series objects for analysis. Time series were plotted against the number of days, weeks or months relative to the suicide or suicide attempt, as applicable.

Record analysis was descriptive and case-based because coverage and source formats were too heterogeneous for pooled analysis. Where possible, gambling expenditure or activity was plotted over time and aligned with financial events, operator actions and the suicidal event. Cross-case matrices were used to compare recurring patterns. Documentary records provided information about timing, activity, loss, borrowing and operator actions, while interviews provided contextual interpretation.

Qualitative analysis

Interviews were anonymised and transcribed. Several members of the research team, including people with lived experience, undertook iterative thematic analysis informed by Braun and Clarke [21]. The analysis involved repeated reading, collaborative development and refinement of candidate themes, and consideration of similarities and differences across the two participant groups. Participants from the interviews and the earlier focus groups were subsequently invited to comment on the emerging framework. Their feedback contributed to the final themes and subthemes.

Bereaved participants provided accounts of the experiences and circumstances of someone who had died by gambling-related suicide, while attempt survivors described their own experiences of gambling-related harms and a serious suicide attempt. Independent assessment and review compared the questions asked and topics explored in interviews with bereaved participants and attempt survivors. The review considered whether differences in questioning led to particular topics being explored more fully in one group than the other. No systematic differences were identified in the coverage of the core topic areas, although questions were adapted to reflect whether participants were describing another person's experiences or their own. Despite this similarity in core topic coverage, the two groups' accounts were treated as complementary rather than equivalent: bereaved participants offered an external perspective on events and circumstances, whereas survivors could provide first-person accounts of internal experiences, including compulsion, shame, loss of control and suicidal thinking.

The thematic analysis was collaborative and interpretive; to assess the operational clarity of the final thematic framework, a subsequent codebook-based coding exercise was undertaken independently. Independent coders applied a codebook based on the themes to a sample of transcripts, and agreement was examined using percentage agreement and Gwet's AC1. The exercise followed theme development and was not used to generate themes, validate the thematic interpretation or rank theme importance. Two coders independently coded eight transcripts to assess inter-rater agreement. Theme-level agreement was very strong for six themes, moderate to good for two themes, and weak for the remaining two themes. Intra-rater agreement was assessed separately, with each coder re-coding 20 transcripts after approximately six weeks. Intra-rater agreement was very strong (theme-level AC1 0.96-0.98; agreement 97-98%). The analysis plan was not preregistered and the analyses were exploratory.

Reflexivity

The interviews were treated as co-produced narratives. The team considered how interviewers' backgrounds and experiences, including lived experience, might have influenced questioning, participant disclosure and interpretation. This reflection was supported by collaborative development and refinement of themes involving researchers with different backgrounds and experiences. Developing interpretations were also independently reviewed against the underlying interview and documentary material to examine how closely they reflected participants' accounts and the available evidence.

RESULTS

Sample and gambling context

The 10 people who died were aged 19-46 years; nine were male and one female. The 10 attempt survivors were aged 34-62 years; seven were male and three were female. The 20 cases covered varied occupational and social backgrounds, and gambling histories were heterogeneous. Most had gambled for several years before the suicide or attempt, although one person had gambled at a substantial level for approximately six months. Most had started gambling before age 25, including some underage gambling, while others began later in life. Further demographic detail is not reported because of the small sample and risk of deductive disclosure. Core sample characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Gambling forms also varied, but most cases had involved substantial periods of high-speed electronic gambling, including online slots, casino products, betting exchange or in-play betting.

There was some evidence from the operators named in banking transactions of a transition towards higher risk forms of gambling (e.g. slots) in about half of cases. Substantial wins, sometimes early in the gambling trajectory, were described in many cases. By the time of the suicide or attempt, most had experienced substantial financial losses, although the scale varied considerably and not everyone incurred debt. This distinction became important analytically because the severity of gambling harm was not captured adequately by debt alone.

Some attempt-survivor accounts included trauma, bereavement, difficult relationships, financial hardship, mental or physical health difficulties and other adverse experiences. These experiences were not present in every case and did not indicate a single explanatory pathway but were understood as co-existing life stressors and inequalities which are present in many people's lives. Further, it was recognised that these experiences might not only shape the development of gambling harms and vulnerability to suicidal crisis but might also be caused by gambling. Across the dataset, gambling harms themselves also became major life stressors in their own right, including financial loss, secrecy, relationship strain, deteriorating health, loss of identity and feeling entrapped.

1. Normalisation of gambling

Gambling was described as deeply embedded in ordinary social and digital life through sport, television, advertising, celebrity endorsement, sponsorship and smartphones. Participants felt this continual visibility presented gambling as normal entertainment rather than an activity capable of serious harm, helping to blur the boundary between leisure and addiction and reducing perceptions of risk. Celebrity endorsement and sponsorship were seen as particularly important in creating legitimacy around gambling. As one bereaved participant explained: "They get all these celebrities to do the adverts and things like that.

Almost kind of portraying, -Oh, if they're advertising it... they're really famous... must be OK... a lot of the companies also sponsor... programmes... again portraying that -Well, they must be OK."

(GwL-02)

Concerns extended to children and younger people. Participants described gambling and gambling-like activities as becoming familiar from an early age, particularly through advertising, sport and digital environments: "They're targeting the younger generation, force feeding the younger generation into -This is normality, this is the normal way of things... everything is being subtly fed and geared up -This is alright, this is the way to do it." (GamLEARN-03)

Within the wider analysis, this normalisation was important because it could reduce perceptions of gambling-related risk and make the transition from ordinary gambling to harmful gambling more difficult to recognise. Participants' accounts elsewhere showed how gambling could subsequently become experienced as overwhelming and inescapable, with some describing suicide as the only perceived means of stopping the cycle of gambling and its consequences.

Normalisation also shaped how harms were recognised by families who described missing or misinterpreting warning signs, including assuming that safeguards such as self-exclusion meant gambling could no longer continue:

I was very naive to it... when I saw that October section on his bank statements... such a high volume, but then because he'd signed up to the GamStop, I just thought that was it. I thought it meant he couldn't gamble anymore." (GwL-02) Participants also described gambling-related cues as pervasive and difficult to escape: "...you don't get a minute's peace from them; whether that be e-mail, text messages, advertising, push notifications, you don't get a minutes break from it. It is everywhere." (GamLEARN-01)

2. The 'responsible gambling' narrative and individualisation of responsibility

Participants described safer-gambling messages and voluntary tools, such as deposit limits and self-exclusion, as placing responsibility on individuals to manage their own gambling. They contrasted this with features of the wider gambling environment, including continuous availability, inducements and product characteristics that could encourage continued play. Once gambling had become compulsive, participants questioned whether relying on individual self-management was realistic, particularly when their sense of control and ability to disengage had already diminished.

One bereaved participant described how this framing could reinforce the belief that the individual should be able to control the gambling themselves: "The effect of this was to entrench him even more into thinking it was within himself to control it and drive him even further away from seeking help... unable to gamble responsibly despite the industry's polite little messages to do so."

(GwL-06)

The perceived limitations of voluntary tools were also described directly by people who had experienced compulsive gambling. One survivor reflected on the extent to which these measures still depended on the individual being able to interrupt their own gambling:

The self exclusion tools are a waste of time. The deposit limits, you can change them after 24 hours anyway. It's all again on the individual, so none of that stuff worked for me. Again, if you're gambling all day and you get a safer gambling message come up, you are just like -Get off my screen." (GamLEARN-01)

Participants described a tension between the appearance of individual choice and the experience of losing control. This was articulated particularly clearly by one participant with lived experience: "You are the one pressing the button who should be taking this responsibility which is why the guilt and the shame and all of that is all cross-wired.

You are physically the one in control of all of this, but you are not. It completely controls you." (GamLEARN-02)

Expectations of personal control could therefore intensify guilt and secrecy when attempts to stop failed. Across these accounts, responsibility was not experienced simply as a neutral message about safer behaviour. When self-management tools failed to interrupt gambling, participants could interpret that failure as evidence that they themselves had failed to exercise sufficient control. This linked the individualisation of responsibility closely with shame, concealment and delayed helpseeking.

3. Industry practices and products

Across almost all cases, participants described a gambling environment characterised by constant availability, high-speed products, intensive and targeted marketing and repeated exposure.

Bereaved accounts also described VIP-style relationships in which personalised contact could facilitate continued high-stakes gambling: "They were emailing him all the time.

They gave him like a VIP account manager who really befriended him. They sort of tried to become your friend... If X wanted to put a bet on that was higher than what the limit was, he could just text him and he would do it, he would arrange it". (GwL-01)

In some accounts, this personalised engagement continued during periods of acute vulnerability.

One participant described being repeatedly contacted on the day of their father's funeral and receiving a substantial amount of promotional credit: "The day of my dad's funeral, actually phoned me, they kept on ringing, and I ended up picking up the phone and they asked me if it was a good time and I said no.

And they said that they didn't think it was... Just to let you know we have put some money in your account. I had £5,000 in my account put in, that was how much I was gambling."

Participants described offers, bonuses and personalised contact continuing during periods of substantial loss, distress or attempts to disengage.

The documentary records were consistent with important elements of these accounts. In some cases, operator data showed substantial inducements, including more than £100,000 in one case during a period of sustained losses. Safer-gambling "interventions" were also evident, including self-exclusion, deposit limits, safer-gambling messages and operator contact.

However, the available records showed little evidence that these "interventions" coincided with a sustained interruption of escalating recorded gambling activity. Where "interventions" were recorded, they were often generic and focused on time or money spent, while more decisive action usually involved account restriction or suspension. In some cases, restriction by one operator was followed by movement to another indicating the limited effect of operator-specific measures when access remained available elsewhere.

4. Regulatory and policy weaknesses

Participants expressed low confidence in the regulatory and policy framework intended to protect people from gambling harms. Regulation was described as fragmented, slow and reactive, with concerns about weak enforcement, inadequate protection for children and younger people, delayed intervention, limited transparency and unclear responsibility across operators and wider systems. A recurring concern was the gap between the existence of consumer-protection measures and their effectiveness in practice. This was evident in accounts of under-age gambling and early exposure. One bereaved participant described how her relative first gambled while underage: "He had such a long lunch break, and then one day he went into the betting shop with some friends, and he was underage, but they let him gamble, and that is what started him off because he won."

(GwL-01)

Participants also described gaps between safeguards operating in different parts of the gambling environment. One bereaved participant questioned why online self-exclusion did not prevent continued access to land-based gambling: "He'd signed up to GamStop. So why did that not then notify the shops?... He's signed up to GamStop because there's clearly an issue, but yet he can still freely go into the shops and do it... Why do they not talk?" (GwL-02)

The documentary records were consistent with aspects of these concerns. In some cases, restriction or closure of an account was followed by gambling with other operators, suggesting that safeguards operating at the level of a single company could only have limited effect when gambling remained accessible elsewhere. Operator records also contained relatively little evidence that safer gambling interventions had coincided with a sustained interruption of escalating recorded gambling activity. Where interventions were visible, they were often generic and focused on time or money spent, with more decisive action generally involving account restriction or suspension.

The study cannot establish that any specific regulatory intervention would have prevented a suicide or attempt. Participants nevertheless identified cumulative gaps across multiple safeguards, including failures to prevent under-age access, respond to escalating or changing patterns of gambling and expenditure, ensure effective self-exclusion, and prevent continued gambling following account restrictions.

5. Lack of knowledge, information and understanding

Knowledge gaps existed across individuals, families, professionals and wider systems. Participants described limited understanding of how addictive gambling could become, how it may not be recognised, how easily it could be hidden, how rapidly harms could escalate, and its relationship with mental health and suicidality. Gambling was often understood primarily as a financial or behavioural issue, with assumptions that severe harms would be visible or accompanied by substantial debt. For some bereaved participants, the scale and intensity of gambling became apparent only retrospectively through financial and operator records:

With his [Gambling] and [online payment] account, that is significant, it is all day every day; so he's purposely not mentioning it because it's like 14 hours of gambling... And that was one day... I didn't know nothing about gambling, anything about gambling. Nothing. So ten bets a day to me was significant... when I'm counting it up, and it's 100 bets -What the hell?" (GwL-04)

This illustrates the difference between information being available and understanding its significance. Gambling could remain hidden while individuals continued to work, socialise and appear to cope, with the frequency and intensity of activity becoming clear only when records were examined after a crisis or death.

Misunderstanding of the potential severity of gambling harms was also evident in bereaved accounts. One bereaved participant reflected on how gambling had initially seemed less concerning than substance use:

I remember X [daughter] saying to me at the time... that she was worried about X; addiction and gambling. And I said, 'Well at least he's not putting a substance into his body.' And that's actually what sums up what I thought, 'At least it's not drugs.'" (GwL-05) Documentary records made visible patterns of prolonged gambling, repeated transactions and financial change that had not necessarily been apparent at the time. Participants also described gaps in professional recognition, particularly when gambling was not explored alongside financial difficulty, deteriorating mental health or suicidality.

6. Shame, self-blame and stigma

Shame was one of the most pervasive emotional processes across the accounts. Participants described concealment, lying, living a "double life", self-disgust and a profound loss of identity.

Internal shame often centred on the belief that the person had failed to control their gambling, while external stigma involved anticipated or experienced judgement about gambling-related harms and suicide. Both could discourage disclosure and help-seeking, leaving people outwardly connected to others while experiencing intense internal isolation. Secrecy could become bound up with a loss of identity, as maintaining an outward appearance of coping required sustained concealment of their gambling: "I was spinning that many plates covering my tracks, all the time, I lost any identity, I didn't know who I was, my whole life is pretty much based on lies...

I was covering up that much stuff.

I was giving a false life about everything: everything is alright, everything is good."

(GamLEARN-01)

Bereaved accounts similarly described self-blame becoming bound up with repeated attempts to stop gambling and the perceived failure to do so: "He would have seen this as utter and complete failure, yet another promise he had made to himself and others that he could not keep... he believed he had a self-destruct button, and he utterly loathed himself for it. He told me he couldn't envisage his life living with this addiction, that he was a failure." (GwL-06) Shame could therefore become incorporated into identity rather than remaining confined to regret about gambling or money lost. Shame could also extend to affected family members, particularly around disclosure following a death: "For three days, I just couldn't get out of bed, I couldn't function, I couldn't eat properly. I just felt so ashamed, not of X's actions, because of my actions. It is out there now for all the families to see, her friends to see, the reason why she took her own life." (GwL-03)

Social judgement could further reinforce secrecy, self-loathing and reluctance to disclose harms.

7. Money, debt and financial desperation

Financial harm was central to nearly all cases but did not follow a single pattern. Losses and debt could drive further gambling through chasing, while access to wages, savings, credit or financial help could also enable gambling to continue. Participants described borrowing across credit cards, loans, overdrafts, payday lending and other payment routes, with financial harm often becoming intertwined with secrecy, shame and perceived failure.

One bereaved participant recalled the person who later died describing gambling as an attempt to recover losses and repay debt:

The only reason that I'm doing it now is to try to get some money back so I can pay off these bloody loans, I've got myself in such a mess, But I hate it, I hate it!" (GwL-04) A person who survived a suicide attempt described how a missed potential win could be experienced as a loss, rapidly translating into chasing, credit use and immediate financial deprivation: "I had just lost £500 sterling that I would have won from putting that bet on, so how would I claw back £500 that was never even mine in the first place... I went round the corner to the bookies, maxed out my credit card... trying to chase this £500 that wasn't even mine... I kept quiet for the rest of the weekend, starving hungry... because my pride wouldn't let me ask for £20." (GamLEARN-03)

Financial harm could therefore become self-reinforcing as losses increased pressure to recover money and borrowing provided further funds with which to gamble.

Available records documented losses ranging from comparatively small amounts to tens or hundreds of thousands of pounds, alongside overdrafts, loans, credit-card cash withdrawals, referrals to debt collection agencies, support from family and friends and failed or cancelled withdrawals. The findings did not support a single debt threshold for severe harm. Banking records indicated that few participants incurred substantial debt, but there was consistent evidence of month-to-month financial distress e.g., use of payday lending rather than larger loans. Of greater relevance was the wider trajectory of losses, borrowing, chasing, secrecy and exhaustion of available resources.

8. Lack of recognition and ineffective treatment

Several accounts described contact with health services in which distress, depression or suicidality was addressed without sufficient exploration of gambling. Participants reported that gambling was not routinely asked about, even where there were signs of financial difficulty or deteriorating mental health. In some cases, gambling was disclosed but did not lead to gambling-specific referral, further assessment or sustained support. In one bereaved account, review of the medical record indicated that gambling had been disclosed and specialist referral considered, but that gamblingspecific treatment did not follow before discharge: "I could see from his medical record that he told the Crisis team about his problems with gambling... there appears to be a consideration by the crisis team to refer X for specialist treatment for gambling addiction.

At the time of his discharge from the crisis team, this appears to be an issue that he was left to deal with himself." (GwL-07)

Difficulty identifying appropriate gambling-specific support was also described by bereaved participants who had actively sought professional advice:

I can't tell you how many very senior health professionals I spoke to about it... it wasn't like it [his gambling] was a secret, I was asking advice from people. And nobody knew what to do. Even then, there was no treatment in XXXX." (GwL-05) Importantly, disclosure of gambling did not always result in a clear route to appropriate support. One participant described repeatedly seeking help and eventually explicitly telling a health professional about his gambling: "I had been back and forth a lot of times, and nobody challenged me, it was just to try these pills... I actually went to him and told him that I was a gambler... [he said] your brain will heal... thank you very much for telling me... I was like where can I go?" (GamLEARN-02)

Support was also described as unavailable, short-lived or difficult to re-enter following recurrence, while in some survivor accounts suicidal thoughts and behaviours continued after gambling had reduced or stopped.

9. Lapse and recurrence

Recovery was rarely described as linear. Participants moved between periods of abstinence or reduced gambling and renewed episodes of harmful gambling. Recurrence could follow marketing and inducements, emotional distress, loneliness, financial pressure, boredom or the continuing ease of access to gambling through phones and online accounts. Even a relatively short lapse could quickly reactivate chasing and the belief that losses needed to be recovered:

I've had one lapse. It lasted a week and a half. I lost money, and now I feel maybe I should try and win! My brain is telling me to go and get that little bit back that I've lost." (GamLEARN-04) Recurrence could also follow longer periods without gambling and was sometimes preceded by disengagement from support. One survivor described how accumulating pressures coincided with withdrawal from Gamblers Anonymous and a return to gambling after 15 months: "I did stay gamble free for 15 months. But I wasn't processing, I wasn't actually dealing with all of the stuff that was going on at the time... the pressures of life started to mount up again... I slipped back into gambling... I stopped attending Gamblers Anonymous, probably a month or two before I relapsed... all I was doing was because of the compulsion. I continued to do it, I can see myself looking from above looking down seeing it destroy me, just being completely out of reach." (GamLEARN-03) The consequences of recurrence were also visible in people who were bereaved. In one case, a bereaved participant read from a letter written by the person who later died, describing repeated returns to gambling and the associated deterioration: "I started gambling again and lost everything and more. I can't help returning to this disgusting habit time and time again. I hate myself. I have thrown everything away again. I am ashamed. I am embarrassed... I've done nothing but gamble and think about gambling. I don't sleep, eat or wash... inside I am dying." (letter read by GwL-07)

The same case also illustrated recurrence after a much longer period of apparent stability: bank records indicated that gambling resumed after approximately two years without gambling. Across the wider accounts, recurrence could therefore follow both relatively brief and sustained periods without gambling.

Recurrence could reactivate chasing losses, shame, secrecy and loss of control, while making earlier progress feel as though it had been lost. The accounts also showed that recurrence could coincide with withdrawal from support, renewed financial pressure and accumulating emotional distress, reinforcing the need to understand recovery as dynamic rather than as a simple transition from gambling to sustained abstinence.

10. Entrapment and hitting the lowest point

Many narratives converged on a powerful sense of feeling entrapped, in which people felt there was no way out of their situation. This was usually described not as a single event but as the culmination of gambling-related financial, relational and psychological harms, repeated attempts to stop, and a progressive loss of hope and perceived options. Acute moments such as a major loss, an empty bank account or exposure of hidden gambling could intensify this state, although in other cases distress developed over a longer period. One participant described gambling becoming so dominant that suicide was perceived as the only means of escape:

It was the gambling, it had ruined my life... I never thought that I could stop gambling. I thought the only way for me to stop was by killing myself... if I had no money left, I would be thinking how do I get the money to gamble again tomorrow... it's scary how much control the gambling had over me." (GamLEARN-01) For others, entrapment was expressed through hopelessness and perceived burdensomeness following repeated unsuccessful attempts both to stop gambling and to resolve its consequences: "I felt I had no hope left. There was nothing left for me to live for. I have tried this, tried that, tried giving up gambling and that didn't work, tried going back to gambling and that didn't work. So I came to the conclusion that I would be better off, my family and everyone around me would be better off, if I wasn't around." (GamLEARN-03) A different survivor described acute hopelessness and a narrowing of perceived alternatives after repeatedly moving through the same cycle: "I didn't see any hope, I didn't have it in me to go through the same cycle again that I have been going through every week for the last five years... that is my best option, that is my only option." (GamLEARN-05)

Gambling could initially provide escape from distress while subsequently generating further loss, secrecy and shame. Across accounts, these processes were associated with a progressive narrowing of perceived options and hope.

Industry practices and products, normalisation and limited knowledge formed the wider context in which gambling harms developed. Within this environment, safeguards and self-management tools did not always interrupt continued or renewed gambling. When attempts to stop failed, responsibility could be internalised as personal failure, reinforcing shame, secrecy and reduced disclosure, while gaps in recognition and treatment could allow harms to continue. Losses and access to further funds could fuel chasing and recurrence, with financial, relational and psychological harms accumulating over time. Interviews helped explain how these events became bound up with perceived failure, loss of identity, hopelessness and burdensomeness. Documentary record analysis Record data were available for 14 of the 20 core cases. Five cases had banking records only, four had both banking and operator records, and five had operator records only. Medical records were also available for one of these 14 cases. The records represented people from varied backgrounds and differed markedly in type, duration and completeness. They were therefore used to examine case trajectories and recurring patterns rather than to estimate prevalence or compare demographic groups.

Temporal patterns and financial trajectories

For serious suicide attempts, available records showed that attempts occurred during or shortly after periods of intense or renewed gambling in several cases, frequently alongside exhaustion of available funds. Individual time series showed marked fluctuation rather than uniform escalation. Recorded net expenditure varied considerably. Records for one case indicated that the person had won more than they had lost during the period covered. Nonetheless, most of the available records showed losses in the tens to hundreds of thousands of pounds. Financial harm extended beyond gambling expenditure itself: records showed repeated use of overdrafts, payday loans or bank loans; withdrawal of savings and investments; financial assistance from family and friends; creditcard cash withdrawals; and failed or cancelled gambling withdrawals. Levels of debt related to gambling tended to be relatively small. In some cases, high-expenditure periods recurred over a long record history rather than appearing as a single isolated episode.

Gambling activity, operator restrictions and inducements

The banking records showed use of multiple operators over the periods covered, although use varied over time. Recorded activity was generally concentrated with one licensed operator at a given time. In some cases, restriction or closure of a main account was followed by switching to other operators. A number of cases indicated that safer gambling thresholds had been triggered, as emails were sent highlighting the amount of time or money spent on the operator's website.

However, there was little evidence in the available correspondence and activity records that safergambling contacts coincided with a sustained interruption of escalating recorded gambling activity.

The majority of correspondence focused on promotional materials and offers of free bets. Where available, data subject access requests provided evidence that promotional offers were taken up and were followed by renewed gambling in the run-up to a suicide attempt.

Intensity of gambling activity

Daily records also showed episodic periods of highly intensive gambling separated by much lower activity, further illustrating that trajectories could change rapidly.

DISCUSSION

This study identified interacting commercial, financial, psychological, relational and service-level processes associated with gambling-related suicidal crisis. Gambling trajectories were often episodic, and the significance of financial harm lay not only in the amount lost but in trajectories of chasing, borrowing, resource exhaustion, non-recognition, secrecy and perceived failure. Survivor accounts provided access to internal experiences that proxy informants could not necessarily observe, while documentary records helped establish when gambling and financial circumstances changed.

The findings are compatible with suicide models that emphasise dynamic risk and feeling trapped.

Fluid Vulnerability Theory conceptualises acute suicide risk as fluctuating against a more stable background vulnerability [22], while the integrated motivational-volitional model identifies defeat and entrapment as important in the emergence of suicidal ideation [23]. The Interpersonal Theory provides a complementary perspective on accounts of perceived burdensomeness and disconnection [24]. These constructs were not formally assessed and the study did not test these models. Rather, they provide useful theoretical context for the gambling-specific processes identified here, including recurrence, financial exhaustion, shame, fear of disclosure and narrowing perceived options.

The prominence of financial harm and shame is consistent with previous qualitative evidence [7].

Wong et al.'s Hong Kong psychological autopsy study found unmanageable debt in all 17 suicide cases meeting criteria for pathological gambling [10]. Financial harm was similarly prominent here, but the records were more heterogeneous, suggesting that absolute debt or loss alone is an insufficient indicator of crisis severity. The accounts instead highlighted exhaustion of available funds, inability to recover losses, fear of disclosure and perceived financial failure, suggesting that the trajectory and meaning of financial harm may be more useful for understanding these crises than a single monetary threshold. This is consistent with previous UK treatment research linking suicide attempts with broader relational and material harms rather than gambling severity alone [4].

The design also differs from previous autopsy studies. Wong et al. combined proxy interviews and death files within a case-control psychological autopsy [10], whereas Şahin et al. retrospectively analysed 24 forensic autopsy cases from Türkiye [11]. The present study combined bereaved proxy accounts with first-person survivor accounts and, where available, longitudinal banking, operator, health and coronial records, with a primary focus on how gambling-related, financial, psychological, relational and service-level processes developed and interacted over time.

The findings also locate suicidal crisis within the wider commercial and cultural environment surrounding gambling. Participants described high-speed products, continuous availability, inducements, personalised contact and repeated marketing exposure, consistent with public-health and commercial-determinants approaches to gambling harms [13, 25, 26] and with evidence on normalisation and advertising [27, 28]. The study cannot establish that any specific advertisement, inducement or operator action caused a suicide or attempt. However, participants described the cumulative effect of continued exposure to these features while attempting to reduce or stop gambling. This is important when considering the limitations of 'responsible gambling' approaches that place primary emphasis on individual self-regulation. In participants' accounts, repeated attempts to stop occurred within environments where gambling remained readily available and where marketing, inducements and other cues could continue during periods of vulnerability or recurrence.

Expectations of individual self-control could also shape how repeated unsuccessful attempts to stop were understood, linking perceived personal failure with shame, concealment and reduced help-seeking. A public-health approach therefore places greater emphasis on the wider conditions that shape opportunities to gamble and the effectiveness of safeguards, rather than relying predominantly on individuals to manage risk once harms have developed. This is consistent with evidence that gambling-related stigma can inhibit disclosure and help-seeking [14, 29]. Taken together, these findings suggest that a sense of entrapment could develop through the interaction of repeated losses, unsuccessful attempts to stop, recurrence, shame, fear of disclosure, relationship strain, diminishing access to money and gaps in recognition or support, rather than from any single factor.

Healthcare contacts represented another potential point for recognition. Participants described contacts with primary care, mental health and crisis services in which gambling was sometimes not explored or did not lead to gambling-specific assessment or sustained support. General mental health and crisis services have a different remit from specialist NHS gambling services, which specialise in the assessment and treatment of gambling disorder. The difficulties described in locating appropriate support highlight the importance of greater awareness of specialist NHS gambling services, where available, and clearer routes into them.

Primary care has previously been identified as an important setting for recognising gamblingrelated harms [30], and NICE recommends asking about gambling and assessing suicidal ideation where relevant [16]. Earlier recognition should be a shared responsibility across health, social care and financial services, including mental health and drug and alcohol services, housing and homelessness services, banks and debt-advice charities. Clear referral pathways from these settings could help people access gambling-specific assessment and support before a crisis develops. These implications also complement our NIHR-funded feasibility and pilot study examining routine screening and referral for gambling-related harm within mental health and drug and alcohol services [31].

The present findings also suggest that recognition alone may be insufficient: recurrence, financial crisis and suicidal thoughts and behaviours sometimes persisted beyond the immediate episode, supporting evaluation of sustained follow-up and rapid routes back into support when gambling recurs.

The findings identify potential points for prevention across gambling operators, financial institutions, healthcare and suicide-prevention services. Relevant information is distributed across these systems, meaning that no single organisation necessarily had sight of the overall trajectory.

Public-health approaches similarly emphasise action across availability, marketing, environmental design, information and treatment [13, 32]. The findings also suggest limitations to prevention approaches that rely predominantly on 'responsible gambling' and individual self-management once substantial harms have developed. A broader public-health response would place greater responsibility on systems and environments to identify and respond to escalating harms, rather than relying primarily on individuals to recognise risk and regulate their own gambling. Better recognition of rapid changes in gambling and financial behaviour, more effective responses to recurrence, stronger protections around high-intensity gambling and inducements, and improved coordination between systems warrant further investigation. More consistent investigation and recording of gambling-related factors following suicide deaths could also improve surveillance and learning from deaths [17]. These findings do not establish that any specific intervention would have prevented an individual suicide or attempt.

Although the study was conducted in the United Kingdom, the interacting processes identified here may also be relevant to jurisdictions characterised by high online gambling availability, commercial marketing, consumer credit access and regulatory systems that rely substantially on individualised harm-reduction measures. Their transferability to other regulatory and cultural settings requires further investigation.

Strengths, limitations and mitigations

A key strength was the integration of complementary evidence sources. Bereaved accounts described observable circumstances surrounding deaths, survivors provided first-person accounts of internal experiences, and documentary records provided chronological information on gambling, financial change and operator contact in 14 cases. The value of the design lies in the complementarity of these sources rather than in treating any single source as definitive. Coproduction was also a strength. Lived-experience organisations supported recruitment, trust, engagement and safeguarding, while preliminary focus groups shaped the topic guide, language and interview procedures. Lived-experience contributors provided insights in analysis and interpretation.

Several limitations should be considered. Recruitment through lived-experience partner organisations may have shaped the sample towards people already connected with support or advocacy networks, and experiences among people outside these networks may differ. Given the sensitivity of the topic and the importance placed on participant wellbeing during and after participation, close collaboration with lived-experience partner organisations was necessary for recruitment, real engagement, safeguarding and study delivery. The involvement of livedexperience organisations and researchers may also have influenced the questions emphasised and the interpretation of accounts, while contributing additional perspectives and depth to the analysis. Reflexive attention was paid to interviewer positioning, and the developing analysis was subject to independent review against the underlying interview and documentary material to ensure that interpretations remained grounded in the data. Documentary records also provided an additional source of evidence where available.

The sample was small and purposive and was not intended to be statistically representative of all gambling-related suicides or suicide attempts. The study instead prioritised in-depth examination of cases with detailed narrative accounts, supplemented where available by multiple forms of documentary evidence, allowing processes and interactions to be examined in greater depth than would be possible in larger routine datasets.

Psychological autopsy methods are retrospective, and this study was designed to identify common themes and factors across cases rather than to establish causal effects or definitive predictors. The interval between the suicidal event and interview may have affected recall, particularly for events occurring several years earlier. Where available, documentary records helped reconstruct the timing and sequence of events. The classification of a suicide attempt as 'serious' was based on participants' own accounts rather than a standardised severity measure. However, accounts included suicidal intent and, where applicable, medical treatment or hospital attendance, and the classification was used to define the study sample rather than to compare levels of attempt severity between participants.

Documentary evidence was available for 14 of 20 cases, but source type and coverage differed substantially, from months to many years, and not every case contained banking, operator, health and coronial information. To avoid drawing comparisons unsupported by these heterogeneous data, records were analysed descriptively at case level and used to examine trajectories and recurring patterns, and provide confirmation of interview data, rather than to generate pooled estimates. The figures are therefore individual examples rather than representative trajectories, and temporal proximity alone cannot establish causation. Operator data were also supplied in inconsistent formats, limiting direct comparison. Records were processed and standardised into common formats where possible before analysis; greater standardisation of operator data at source would nevertheless strengthen future research.

Multiple proxy informants could not be obtained for every case. Additional informants were sought where feasible, and proxy accounts were complemented, where available, by documentary records rather than being treated as the sole source of evidence. The inclusion of attempt survivors also provided first-person accounts of internal experiences, including loss of control, shame and suicidal thinking, which proxy informants could describe only indirectly. Bereaved and survivor accounts therefore represent different but complementary forms of knowledge and were not treated as directly interchangeable.

Conclusion

Gambling-related suicidal crises in this study involved more than debt, mental health difficulties or individual factors alone. Across interviews and documentary records, gambling harms developed within a wider environment of highly accessible and high-intensity gambling, commercial inducements, access to funds and credit, limited safeguards, normalisation, shame, recurrence and gaps in recognition and treatment.

The findings indicate how these processes could accumulate and reinforce one another and become associated with a profound sense of entrapment in which alternatives appeared increasingly unavailable. The study does not identify a single causal pathway or establish predictors of suicide, but it identifies points at which escalating harms might be recognised before crisis.

The findings suggest several potential priorities for prevention and practice. Healthcare and suicide-prevention services should strengthen recognition of gambling-related harms and ensure that disclosure leads to appropriate assessment, referral and sustained support, including rapid routes back into care following recurrence. Prevention should also extend beyond exhortations for individual self-management to stronger restrictions and protections around high-intensity gambling, inducements and continued access across operators, alongside improved coordination between gambling operators, financial institutions and healthcare services where escalating harms may otherwise remain fragmented across systems.

The findings also suggest that there needs to be a much greater public and professional understanding of the causes and awareness of the potential severity of gambling-related harms, including the suicide risk, through general education, public health messaging and dedicated training for health and other professionals. More consistent identification, investigation and recording of gambling-related factors following suicide deaths may provide further opportunities for earlier recognition and prevention, as well as providing clearer estimates of the scale of harms.

These findings can inform the development of more specific proposals for action across gambling regulation, gambling operators, financial institutions, healthcare and suicide-prevention services.

This could include stronger protections, earlier recognition of escalating harm and more sustained gambling-informed support. Any changes introduced should be developed with relevant organisations and people with lived experience and properly evaluated to assess how well they work in practice.
