Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 29 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Philosophers and laypeople often have two seemingly conflicting intuitions: that love must be caused by who the beloved is and that it should persist despite personal change. Little attention has been paid to which changes are, and which are not, compatible with the requirement that real love should persist -and associated obligations. Across three online studies with UK-based participants (total N = 2482), we used vignettes in which a person's moral character, memory, or physical appearance changed. We found that judgments on whether love that ended after the change was real and whether people should remain bound by their marital obligations depend on the same properties as judgments about the continuity of personal identity. For both the continuity of personal identity and love, together with the obligations that love brings, moral properties were the most important, followed by memory, while physical properties were considered considerably less important. Negative changes in moral character or autobiographical memory, compared to positive changes, were seen as more disruptive both to identity and marital obligations, and more compatible with past love being real.

INTRODUCTION

There is a puzzle in the philosophy of love. On the one hand, many agree that love, to be desirable, or perhaps even real, must be caused by who the beloved is as a particular person. If someone loved us simply because Cupid shot an arrow into a stranger who happened to be passing by, many of us would not find being loved in such a way desirable. The stranger would not know us sufficiently well, and so the love would not be caused by who we are, but by something else [1]. On the other hand, love is also expected to persist over time, even if the beloved changes in various ways -the lover's disposition to care for the beloved should not depend on merely contingent features of this person [2, 3, 4, 5]. How can these two conditions be compatible? One possible explanation, and the one we aim to explore in this paper, is to say that real love depends on the beloved's continued personal identity. The persistence of love depends on those properties that are seen as constitutive of who one is. As long as the beloved remains the same person, no matter how many of their superficial properties change over time, real love ought to persist. However, if the beloved changes so much that they are not seen as the same person anymore, which can happen if they lose their essential properties, the lover can stop loving them without threatening the reality of past love.

One example is that real love ought to persist through hair colour change. If our hair greys, our beloved should continue to love us. However, one who loves Dr Jekyll would not be expected to love Mr Hyde. That is a different person.

Love often carries with it a set of commitments toward the beloved. This raises the question of whether such commitments (such as marital obligations) should survive radical personal change.

In Derek Parfit's Russian nobleman case, a young man with socialist ideals anticipates becoming cynical later in life and asks his wife to promise never to act against his present ideals. Parfit argues that his psychologically transformed future self cannot release his wife from her promise because her commitment is directed toward the young man she once married: "She loves, not her middle-aged husband, but the young man she married. This is why it is to this young man that she believes she ought to be loyal. We can love, and believe we are committed to, someone who is dead. And the object of such love and commitment may be [...] some living person's earlier self." [6], p. 328. This suggests that love and commitments depend on the continued existence of the person to whom those commitments were made in the first place.

One well-known finding in psychology is that people make a distinction between a person's "superficial self" and their "true self", or the properties that reflect who one really is deep down.

The "true self" is considered to be the central and most essential part of the self, which is, moreover, widely believed to consist of moral traits specifically [7]. Previous research suggests that while moral traits are considered to be the most central to personal identity, memory is also important, though slightly less so, followed by lower-level cognition and perception, and purely physical traits are seen as the most peripheral. When moral properties change, research participants are most likely to judge that the person who has undergone such a change is now a different person 1 , while changes in perceptual properties or physical traits -the more peripheral ones -are seen as more compatible with the continuity of identity [11]. This is consistent with Parfit's Russian Nobleman example. More broadly, psychological changes -and especially moral ones -disrupt the continuity of personal identity more than physical changes.

The true self is seen as not just moral, but fundamentally morally good. Positive moral traits are more likely to be considered part of "true self" than negative ones [12, 13], and "true self" is considered to be better reflected in good behaviours than in bad ones [14]. To know what one's "true self" is, research participants need to know about the moral character, and the more highly moral individuals are, the more participants tend to feel like they know their "true selves" [15].

Positive personal changes, including but not limited to moral ones, are more likely to be described as "self-discovery" than negative changes [16]. This helps to explain why people often perceive individuals who undergo moral deterioration, such as, for instance, in personality changes associated with drug addiction [17], as becoming an entirely different person [18, 13].

Judgments about whether one remains bound by personal obligations after personal change, however, do not necessarily follow psychological continuity. Many people believe that individuals remain responsible for their previous obligations even after radical psychological change [19].

Although previous work discussed above has identified which traits are perceived to be the most important for the continuity of personal identity, it remains unknown whether judgments about love show a similar pattern. If love and related obligations depend on the beloved's perceived identity, then the hierarchy of traits and directions of change known in the true self literature should also predict judgments about love. If people understand love this way, the kinds of changes that disrupt personal identity should similarly disrupt the continuity of love and associated personal obligations.

As Shakespeare wrote in Sonnet 116, "Love is not love which alters when it alteration finds."

But if one loses their moral goodness, memory, or physical attractiveness, are they still the same person? Some authors argue that saying someone is no longer the same person may convey dissimilarity rather than discontinuity of identity [9, 10]. We do not take a stand on whether the same over time implies qualitative or quantitative identity. When we say "the same over time", we are just referring to judgments of a certain sort.

undergone each type of change? And if love fades after the change, does this mean love was never real in the first place [2]?

Conceptions of personal identity can be divided into psychological, biological, and non-reductive accounts. In what follows, we expect intuitions to be consistent with a psychological account of personal identity as psychological continuity and connectedness [6]. The biological accounts see personal identity as dependent on the continuity of the biological organism, while non-reductive accounts see personal identity as a further fact that cannot be reduced to psychological or biological continuity.

In Study 1, we explore how three different types of personal change -deterioration of moral character, memory, or physical appearance -influence judgments about personal identity, marital obligations, and love. Our aim is to examine whether the same hierarchy of traits that is known to predict personal identity judgments also predicts judgments about love and marital obligations. Love may be expected to survive only those changes that leave the beloved's perceived identity intact. Changes to the properties regarded as central to who one is, on the other hand, would allow love to disappear without meaning that the past love was not real. We also examine judgments about promises, and whether they follow the same pattern as judgments about love, to find out whether marital obligations are expected to be kept despite different kinds of change.

In Study 2, we explore the role of the direction of change, comparing the effects of positive and negative changes in all three domains. This allows us to distinguish between two explanations: whether judgments of identity, love, and marital obligations depend only on the domain of change or also on the direction of change, which would show that judgments on whether love is real are related to the true-self account. Changes described in Studies 1 and 2 happen suddenly and may be relatively uncommon in real life. In Study 3, we describe changes in memory and morality associated with common neurodegenerative and psychiatric conditions. This allows us to test whether the effect holds in more common real-life scenarios in which, moreover, moral traits and memory change occur together and gradually rather than suddenly.

After the patient wakes up, they are:0 = Completely the same person as before the accident,100 = A completely different person than before the accident." Participants were then told that after the accident, observing the moral, memory, or physical changes, Morgan stops loving Robin and decides to seek a divorce. (0 = Definitely not,100 = Definitely yes) and "Love. Results

Extent of change.

One-sample t-tests confirmed that all three ratings (Moral condition: M = 93.46, SD = 11.77; Memory: M = 96.17, SD = 9.35; Physical: M = 85.03, SD = 14.27) were significantly above the midpoint (50), all ps < .001, suggesting that all three descriptions were perceived as reflecting a substantial degree of change in the respective domain. However, the ratings were not identical; therefore, we included those ratings as covariates in subsequent analyses.

Identity.

Discussion

In this study, like in the previous one, we found that the same features of a person that matter for judgments of personal identity also matter for judgments about love and personal commitments. Moral changes were seen as more disruptive than memory changes, while physical changes were the least disruptive. Moreover, negative changes in moral character and memory were seen as more disruptive than positive changes, whereas the direction of physical change made little difference. These findings suggest that judgments about whether love was real and whether marital commitments should persist follow the same pattern as judgments about the beloved's identity: changes to properties perceived as more central to personal identity have greater consequences for judgments about love and commitment, and negative changes -greater than positive.

Discussion

Study 3 provided some support for the general pattern from Study 2, where a negative change is perceived as more disruptive to personal identity, more likely to release from marital obligations, and more compatible with past love being real than a positive change. This suggests that the asymmetry between negative and positive moral change appears also in the context of more gradual neurodegenerative or psychiatric change. However, most pairwise comparisons within each diagnosis were not statistically significant. There are several possible explanations. First, the study may have had insufficient power to detect smaller effects within each diagnosis. Second, there was a substantial memory decline in all conditions. Since memory change by itself affects judgments of interest, the common memory deterioration may have reduced the effect of moral direction. Third, the moral changes in this study were described as gradual. They may have been perceived as less sharply defining the change between the former and the future self.

GENERAL DISCUSSION

We started with a puzzle about love: on the one hand, for love to be real, it should be caused by who the beloved is. On the other hand, love is also expected to persist despite the beloved changing [2, 3]. But which changes is love expected to survive? If it disappears because the beloved's appearance changes, was it really love in the first place? And should we think the same if the beloved undergoes a substantial change in their moral character or loses access to their autobiographical memories? While previous work has explored which properties are the most important to the continuity of personal identity, no work has been done to explore the hierarchy of traits relevant to judgments about the persistence of love and relevant personal obligations.

Our findings suggest how ordinary intuitions reconcile two conflicting intuitions: that love must be caused by who the beloved is, and that it must persist despite change. Love is not expected to survive every possible change. Love is seen as robust to only some types of personal change: it seems to be understood as depending on the more central aspects of who one is, primarily one's moral character, followed by memory. These are the same properties that are seen as responsible for making people the same over time. Negative changes in moral character and memory are more disruptive to identity, and more likely to make the end of love seem compatible with that love having once been real. They are also more likely to be seen as releasing from past promises.

The main finding of Study 1 was that the participants judged that what disappears with physical change was never really love, but what ends with moral change may once have been. We also found that moral change is more likely to release the lover from marital promises compared to memory change, and memory change compared to physical change. This latter result partially contradicts recent work suggesting that obligations are often viewed as attached to bodily continuity and less often as attached to psychological continuity [19]. In this way, our participants may have held a psychological account of personal identity rather than biological accounts. Participants in our study did not believe that obligations must be kept even after a radical psychological change. However, there is an important difference between these two approaches: our study focused on obligations directed toward the person who had undergone a significant change, rather than obligations the person undergoing change might have. Judgments of personal identity in our study were consistent with previous research: moral change led participants to judge that Robin is more of a different person after the change (rating above the midpoint of the scale), physical change led them to judge that Robin is more of the same person, and memory change fell in between these two conditions. The effect on both promise and love judgments was partially mediated by Robin's perceived identity, which suggests that people may partially rely on intuitions about whether someone is the same person when deciding whether love and marital obligations should persist. However, correlational mediation analysis can not determine a causal relationship between perceived identity and these judgments, so these results should be interpreted with caution.

We also found that these judgments depend, in the case of psychological changes, on the direction of change as well. Negative changes in moral character and memory were seen as more disruptive to the continuity of identity than positive changes, but there was no such asymmetry for changes in physical appearance. The asymmetry between positive and negative moral and memory changes in identity judgments we found, as well as the lack of such asymmetry in the physical domain, is consistent with previous research [12, 13, 18, 21]. More importantly, we found a similar pattern in judgments of love and personal obligations, which have not been examined before. Negative psychological changes made the end of love more compatible with that love having once been real and weakened expectations that marital obligations should persist. The asymmetry between negative and positive change previously observed only in judgments of personal identity also extends to judgments about love and associated commitments.

When moral and memory changes happen more gradually, and memory decline is present in all conditions, negative moral changes give rise to stronger judgments that personal identity was disrupted, weaker expectations that marital commitments should be kept, and stronger judgments that the end of love is compatible with that love having been real. Although most pairwise comparisons within diagnoses were not statistically significant, exploratory analyses collapsing across diagnoses showed a consistent effect of direction on all three dependent variables. One possible explanation is that the effects in this study were smaller because it was not possible to clearly separate changes in memory and morality -all vignettes contained a memory decline. Another possible explanation is that the vignettes contained considerably more detail than those in Studies 1 and 2, which may have had an effect on the results. However, the overall pattern remained stable and consistent with that observed in the previous two studies.

There is a reason why love and personal commitments might depend on the same hierarchy of traits as personal identity. In the literature on personal identity, it has been hypothesized that the most central part of the self is considered to be moral because moral traits are the most important when judging whether someone can be a reliable partner for social cooperation [11].

This hypothesis has received some empirical support. In one study, the extent to which participants thought a change would have an influence on their relationship with others in their community was positively correlated with judging the character as more of a different person after the change [23]. Perceived identity also mediates the extent to which family members of patients with neurodegenerative conditions find that their relationship with the patient has deteriorated [22]. Intuitions on identity and love may both place morality in a privileged place because they share the same underlying motivation -sustaining cooperative social relationships.

CONCLUSION

Our main findings suggest that radical personal change, such as a change in moral character, can bring love to an end without undermining the reality of the love that preceded it. Returning to Parfit's thought experiment, it can be used to illustrate our main findings. What makes a young idealistic man and his later cynical self not the same person is his moral change. That is also what is most likely to release romantic partners from their past commitments and past love, even if the love was real before the change. Our findings suggest that ordinary people's intuitions are consistent with a psychological account of personal identity, and that obligations follow core psychological features.

Our findings also suggest that we can separate the reality of past love and the persistence of the obligations associated with it. The moral changes that made participants more willing to regard past love as real also weakened expectations that marital promises should persist. Parfit claims that love and commitment can be directed toward an earlier version of the self, in which case a later change does not necessarily weaken commitments made to the self before the transformation. While there may be no way to be released from our promises to an earlier self of the beloved, our findings further suggest that a commitment made to an earlier self does not necessarily give rise to expectations to keep obligations to a transformed later person.
