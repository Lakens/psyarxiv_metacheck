To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 10 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Abstract

Photographs of empty corridors and commercial facilities, known online as "liminal spaces," are judged uncanny by many online viewers. Because most theories of uncanniness locate its cause in the features of humans depicted in the photograph, the appreciation of images that contain no human or animal figures can be used to investigate whether this impression requires a depicted human or whether the expectation that people should be present is sufficient. In a previous empirical study of liminal images, images of rooms from which furniture, doors, and windows had been removed were rated as more than twice as uncanny as the corresponding typical rooms. The effect of human absence, however, has been examined only by replacing human figures with furniture of the same size. Each of four existing accounts, including the structural deviation account, which attributes the impression to deviation from a familiar configuration, explains, on its own, only part of the findings of that study. Here we propose a four-stage model in which uncanniness arises when an observer who judges a place to be in use cannot find a reason for the absence of people or objects that should be there. On the basis of the model, we specify five issues to be tested empirically on which the model and existing accounts make different predictions.

1. Introduction

>Introduction

1.1 Liminal Spaces

Since around 2019, photographs of empty corridors, shopping malls whose lights and displays remain after closing, unoccupied indoor swimming pools, or empty offices lit by fluorescent lamps have been disseminated online under the name "liminal spaces." These photographs contain no blood or dirt, no ghosts or monsters, and no abnormal human figures, yet the description on a user-edited wiki states that they are eerie and unsettling and also somewhat nostalgic (Aesthetics Wiki, 2021). In May 2019, a photograph of a yellow interior was posted on the anonymous imageboard 4chan, and a ghost story (the Backrooms) about an unoccupied space in which people who have slipped out of reality are trapped was attached to it (ALT236, 2023; Wiggins, 2025). Separately, a category named "Liminal spaces" was created on Wikimedia Commons, a collaboratively edited repository of images, and an entry named "Liminal Space" was created on the Aesthetics Wiki, a wiki on which users create entries that describe the moods of images found on the internet; these pages also collected photographs that are not accompanied by a story such as the Backrooms (hereafter, liminal images). The phenomenon has also spread beyond the internet. The video game The Exit 8, which its publisher describes as inspired by Japanese underground passages and by liminal spaces, has sold more than 3 million copies in total (PLAYISM, 2026), and a live-action film based on it, Exit 8, was released in Japan in 2025 (Kawamura, 2025). In 2026, a feature film based on the Backrooms was released in the United States (Parsons, 2026). ALT236 (2023) wrote a French book on liminal spaces for general readers, and the book has been translated into Japanese.

Most liminal images contain no figures of humans or animals, that is, of agents, at all.

No one has yet adequately explained why photographs that depict no agents are rated as uncanny. The purpose of this article is to review existing findings on the psychological mechanisms that produce this evaluation and to propose a testable explanatory model. The research that can be consulted first to explain this evaluation concerns the uncanny valley, the phenomenon that psychological research on uncanniness has mainly addressed: objects are evaluated more favorably the more they resemble humans, but some objects that are very close to humans are instead evaluated as uncanny (Mori, 1970 (Mori, /2012)). Most theories of this phenomenon assume mechanisms that respond to features of the agents depicted in an image.

For example, one account attributes the uncanniness of humanlike objects with a flawed appearance to disgust, which serves to avoid infection, or to deviation from the standards of bodily beauty by which mates are chosen (MacDorman & Ishiguro, 2006), and another attributes it to the perception of parts that lack humanness in an object that the observer has begun to treat as human (Wang et al., 2015). These accounts cannot explain the uncanniness of liminal images, which lack agents altogether (Diel & Lewis, 2022a).

Because many liminal images show places that are usually occupied by people, such as stations and commercial facilities, observers can expect the presence of people who are not depicted in the image. Cultural criticism has proposed that the absence of people from a place where people should be present produces uncanniness (Fisher, 2016); if this idea is correct, eliciting uncanniness does not require an agent depicted in the image, and the absence of expected people is sufficient. In this respect, liminal images differ from the unpleasant images used in emotion research. Standardized affective image sets include, as unpleasant images, photographs depicting negative content such as accidents and injuries, snakes and spiders, and norm violations (Marchewka et al., 2014). Liminal images consist only of neutral elements such as corridors, lighting, and floors, and the people who should be present are also, in themselves, neutral elements. If the uncanniness of liminal images arises from the absence of expected people, then removing a neutral element from an image that consists only of neutral elements produces an unpleasant evaluation.

Because online viewers also report nostalgia together with uncanniness, an explanation of this evaluation also needs to address its relation to nostalgia. Mixed emotions, in which pleasantness and unpleasantness occur simultaneously, have been studied in situations in which the causes of the pleasant and the unpleasant feelings can be identified separately (Larsen et al., 2001), whereas for liminal images both feelings are reported for a single still image that contains neither people nor a story. However, these reports are viewers' self-descriptions and have not been measured with rating scales. It therefore remains unknown whether nostalgia arises from the same image features as uncanniness.

1.2 Empirical Research on the Uncanniness of Places

A relation similar to the uncanny valley has also been reported for images of places that contain no agents. At present, Diel and Lewis (2022a) is the only peer-reviewed empirical study that has used liminal images as its main stimuli. The authors presented adults in the United Kingdom with 50 liminal images collected from the Wikimedia Commons category and the Aesthetics Wiki entry, 25 architectural sketches and drawings as lowrealism images, and 25 photographs of real places drawn at random from a scene image database, and asked them to rate uncanniness, abnormality, threat, and realism. When the realism of the place, rather than human-likeness as in typical uncanny valley studies, was plotted on the horizontal axis, the ratings showed a valley, and uncanniness ratings were best described by a cubic function (an N-shaped curve). Most stimuli in the range of the valley were liminal images, and the sketches and drawings lay to the left of the valley, in the range of lower realism. Furthermore, images with more structural anomalies (distortions in the size of elements, missing elements, unexpected placement, and repetition of elements) received higher uncanniness ratings. The authors summarized these results with the explanation that structural deviation from familiar configurations produces uncanniness (hereafter, the structural deviation account).

Subsequent studies have also relied on the structural deviation account, and none of them has measured the nostalgia that viewers report. The studies published after Diel and Lewis (2022a) added the same kinds of distortion to immersive virtual environments (Musolino et al., 2025), examined the outputs of a classifier of liminal images (Sonoda & Ueki, 2024), or examined individual differences that predict uncanniness (Diel & Lewis, 2024); each of them either applied the structural deviation account to other conditions or described properties of the image set. In addition, the definition of uncanny that Diel and Lewis (2022a) gave to participants included the phrase "strangely familiar," so uncanniness and familiarity cannot be separated in those ratings.

1.3 Structure of This Article

We first point out that, because images collected on the internet as liminal spaces were selected with uncanniness as one of the criteria, ratings of these images do not provide evidence about the causes of uncanniness. Next, from the results of Diel and Lewis (2022a) and from a reanalysis of the data that the authors made public for Experiment 3, which manipulated the presence or absence of people, we present four facts that are difficult to explain with the structural deviation account alone. We then specify which of these observations each of four existing accounts of uncanniness explains (the structural deviation account, the categorization difficulty account, the threat ambiguity account, and the violatedexpectation-of-social-presence account based on the interpretation of Diel & Lewis, 2022a), and propose a four-stage model that integrates these accounts as descriptions of different stages of a single process. In this model, uncanniness arises when the observer judges a place to be in use (that is, still operating or still being used) and cannot find a reason why people or objects are missing from it; the absence of people is one case of this. Finally, we present five issues to be tested empirically on which the model and the existing accounts make different predictions, and we discuss the implications for research on the uncanny valley and for emotion research, as well as the limitations of this article.

2. Liminal Images Are Collected Using Uncanniness as a Criterion

Using liminal images in research requires establishing which images are classified as liminal images and on what criteria they were collected. The word liminal has three uses. In anthropology, it refers to the threshold phase (limen) of a rite of passage, which lies between separation and incorporation, and to the state of a person in that phase (Turner, 1969; van Gennep, 1909). In tourism research, it refers to transitional spaces, such as airports, in which travelers are between one place and another (Huang et al., 2018), and in architectural and urban studies, it refers to intermediate settings in which the distinction between public and private is ambiguous (Nagata, 2026). On the internet, since around 2019, it has mainly referred to photographs of unoccupied places that viewers find uncanny and nostalgic. The first two uses describe the state of a person or the function of a place, and they do not imply uncanniness. A brightly lit airport lobby with people passing through is a transitional space, but photographs of it are not among those collected on the internet as liminal spaces. The two collections list uncanniness as a criterion or a characteristic of the images they collect. The description of the Wikimedia Commons category lists ambiguity, disorientation, and a state of transition as criteria for including an image, and states that an eerie placelessness is often regarded as a characteristic of liminal spaces (Wikimedia Commons, 2021). The Aesthetics Wiki states that, among photographs of empty rooms and corridors, only those with an eerie and unsettling atmosphere belong to this aesthetic, and it lists nostalgia along with uncanniness among the emotions associated with the aesthetic (Aesthetics Wiki, 2021). Diel and Lewis (2022a) restricted the object of their study to this third use on the grounds that the definition as transitional space cannot explain uncanniness, and we follow the same restriction.

If images collected on the internet as liminal spaces are used as stimuli without modification, the criterion by which the images were selected overlaps with the variable being measured. Because the two collections list uncanniness as a criterion or a characteristic of the images they collect, the finding that liminal images were rated as uncanny can quantify how uncanny the collected images are, but it cannot show why they are uncanny. Used as evidence about why, it only measures the selection criterion a second time, and the inference is circular. The methodological warning that samples should not be selected on the dependent variable (Elwert & Winship, 2014) concerns this problem. Because the Aesthetics Wiki also lists nostalgia, the same problem arises for ratings of nostalgia. One could also have participants rate the liminality of images. However, liminality in this sense refers to how closely an image resembles the images collected on the internet under this name. Ratings of it therefore depend on how many such images participants have seen and do not show which features of a place produce uncanniness. Furthermore, when associations among image features are examined within a set of images selected for uncanniness, the selection itself produces spurious associations. For example, suppose that darkness and the absence of people each independently increase uncanniness and that only sufficiently uncanny images are collected. Even if darkness and the absence of people are unrelated in the population, a dark image can enter the collected set even when people are present, whereas a bright image enters the set only when no people are present. As a result, darkness and the absence of people are negatively associated within the collected images. This phenomenon, in which selecting a sample on an outcome common to two causes produces a spurious association between the causes, is called endogenous selection bias or collider bias, and in general the direction and size of the association cannot be predicted without additional assumptions (Elwert & Winship, 2014). Therefore, uncanniness ratings of liminal images show whether the evaluation shared by those who posted the images can be reproduced in a different group of participants, but they do not provide evidence about the causes of uncanniness. For evidence about the causes of uncanniness, we rely mainly on the results of experiments in which researchers manipulated elements of images.

Because the set of liminal images can be used in future research as a list from which stimuli are drawn, that is, as a sampling frame, building such an image set is desirable as a first step. A sampling frame is the list of population units from which a sample is drawn in a survey. In this method, researchers randomly draw images from the list and either edit the drawn images to add or remove people or objects, or photograph places of the same kind with and without people or objects. The influence of selection for uncanniness is then present equally in the images of every condition; thus, the effect of the manipulated element can be estimated separately from the influence of selection. In addition, because randomly drawn images represent the population of liminal images better than a small number of images that researchers selected or created, the results can be generalized more readily.

3. Previous Findings and a Reanalysis

>Previous Findings and a Reanalysis

3.1 Evidence for the Structural Deviation Account

The conclusion that uncanniness increases when the structure of a place departs from a familiar configuration is supported both by studies that examined the association between image features and ratings and by experiments in which researchers manipulated the structure of places. Among images of places, including liminal images, images that contained more structural anomalies were rated as more uncanny (Diel & Lewis, 2022a, Experiment 1). In Experiment 2, they added anomalies to rooms created with interior design software, such as removing furniture, doors, and windows, repeating objects, placing objects in unexpected positions, or distorting their size, and the rooms with anomalies were rated as more uncanny than the corresponding typical rooms. The effect of the anomalies did not change when lighting and escape routes were controlled, and because the researchers created the images, the selection of images could not confound the results.

Whether adding a person increased or decreased uncanniness depended on the type of place. In Experiment 3, the authors had participants rate images that combined the presence or absence of a person, the presence or absence of distortion, and whether the place was a private space or a public space. Adding a person to an image of a private space increased uncanniness, adding a person to a distorted public space decreased it, and in undistorted public spaces adding a person produced no significant change (Diel & Lewis, 2022a). In the images without a person, the authors replaced the person with a piece of furniture of the same size so that the unoccupied area of the image was equated. They compared several hypotheses about the effect of a person. The hypotheses that a person diverts attention from anomalies, makes anomalies appear normal, or reduces threat all predicted that a person would reduce the perception of abnormality or uncanniness regardless of the type of place, and therefore did not explain the finding that a person increased uncanniness in private spaces.

The hypothesis that the absence of people from a place where people are expected constitutes a deviation from the configuration predicted that a person would decrease uncanniness in public spaces and increase it in private spaces, and the authors explained the results with this hypothesis.

3.2 Four Facts Difficult to Explain with the Structural Deviation Account

The results of Diel and Lewis (2022a) include four facts that are difficult to derive from the structural deviation account alone. Two of them also constrain the idea that uncanniness arises because an expected person is absent (hereafter, the human-absence account). Because all of them are observations from a single article, we call them facts in the sense of observations whose reproducibility has not yet been established.

The first fact is that the ratings that changed together with uncanniness differed between removing and adding people. Adding a person to undistorted private spaces raised uncanniness substantially and also raised ratings of threat and abnormality. Removing the person from undistorted public spaces changed uncanniness only slightly, a change that was not significant in the authors' post hoc tests, and left ratings of threat and abnormality unchanged (Diel & Lewis, 2022a). The distinction that people are expected in public spaces, however, is the authors' classification, and participants' expectations were not measured. If a person is counted as one element of the configuration, both absence and appearance are deviations, and the structural deviation account predicts that images from which people have been removed also depart from the typical configuration and should therefore receive higher abnormality ratings. Removing the person from public spaces, however, did not raise abnormality ratings. The authors explained the results of the two manipulations with separate processes: in public spaces, a person helps observers recognize the kind of place, and in private spaces, a person increases deviation and threat.

The second fact is the effect of the absence of objects. Rooms from which furniture, doors, and windows had been removed were rated as more than twice as uncanny as the corresponding typical rooms (Diel & Lewis, 2022a). This result is consistent with the structural deviation account, but because neither of the two rooms compared contained a person, the human-absence account must explain the effect of the absence of objects separately. Because we regard these elements as neutral in themselves, this result shows that removing neutral elements increases uncanniness ratings. However, whether participants could identify the type of room was not measured, and the absence of objects is confounded with the difficulty of identifying the type of room.

The third fact is the uncanniness of corridors. Corridors were frequent among the images rated as most uncanny, and in post hoc comparisons, corridor images were rated as more uncanny than images of other places (Diel & Lewis, 2022a). We assume that a corridor has a high probability of being unoccupied at any given moment. If so, the human-absence account cannot explain why corridors are the most uncanny places. The most uncanny corridor images, however, had extremely narrow and long proportions, repeated walls, and dim lighting, and the structural deviation account can explain part of their uncanniness.

However, this comparison was made among images collected on the internet with uncanniness as a criterion, and how uncanny the collected images of each kind of place were depended on how those who posted them chose them. The difference between kinds of places is therefore likely to contain a large selection bias, and we treat this fact as supplementary.

The fourth fact is the distribution of the reasons that participants gave in open-ended responses for the 10 images rated as most uncanny. The most frequent reasons were emptiness/lack of features and lighting, few responses were classified as absence of people, and some responses stated that it was unclear what the place was for (Diel & Lewis, 2022a).

However, in the authors' coding, emptiness was placed in the same category as lack of features, and whether the missing elements were objects or people was not distinguished.

Because the reasons that participants report do not necessarily indicate the causes of their evaluations correctly (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977), we also treat this fact as supplementary.

3.3 Reanalysis: The Effects of the Absence and Appearance of People

The post hoc tests of Diel and Lewis (2022a) used participants as the unit of analysis and did not include in the error term the variability among stimuli, of which there were only five per condition. With many different images, differences in the effect among images largely average out, but with five images per condition, the estimated effect can change depending on which images were chosen. A test that leaves this variability out of the error term therefore underestimates the standard error (Judd et al., 2012). In addition, the definition of the uncanniness index differs between the analysis script that the authors made public and the text of the article. We therefore used the raw data from Experiment 3 (37 participants) to estimate the change in uncanniness when the person was removed from undistorted public spaces (the absence effect) and the change when a person was added to undistorted private spaces (the appearance effect), and we examined whether each change was accompanied by changes in ratings of threat and abnormality. The estimates of the effects on uncanniness were obtained from linear mixed models with crossed random effects for participants and stimuli. We computed the uncanniness index using both the definition in the original script (the mean of four items: eerie, creepy, uncanny, and strange) and the definition in the text (the mean of three items: eerie, creepy, and uncanny). The procedures and detailed results are reported in the Supplementary Material.

These results are limited by the stimulus pairs and by the rendering of the rooms and the people. The absence effect depended on a single pair of images; when this pair was excluded, the participant-level estimate with the four-item index fell from 3.7 to 1.3 points. In addition, the rooms in Experiment 3 of Diel and Lewis (2022a) were virtual rooms that the authors built with interior design software, whereas the images in their Experiment 1 were photographs collected as liminal images. The results therefore do not necessarily apply directly to liminal images. Because the people were also human figure models rendered with the same software, the presence or absence of a person is confounded with the rendering of a human figure model. If a human figure model raises uncanniness merely by appearing in an image, the rise when a person was added to private spaces is overestimated, and the rise when the person was removed from public spaces is underestimated. The finding that adding a person to private spaces raised abnormality ratings is also consistent with the possibility that participants judged the human figure model itself to be abnormal. Researchers can judge to some extent whether the human figure models themselves raise uncanniness by presenting the models from the stimuli on their own, having participants rate their uncanniness and abnormality, and comparing these ratings with ratings of photographs of real people.

However, evaluations of a human figure model rendered within a place may differ from evaluations of the same model presented alone.

3.4 Unmeasured Variables

Previous studies have measured uncanniness as a negative evaluation alongside threat and abnormality. The variables that are relevant to our argument but have not been measured are the difficulty of categorizing each stimulus; observers' judgments of whether the depicted place is in use, together with the time of day and the era in which the photograph was taken; positive evaluations such as nostalgia, along with valence and arousal; and properties of the photographic medium (resolution and color tone). Some participants mentioned the image quality of old cameras as a reason for uncanniness (Diel & Lewis, 2022a). Because participants were limited to residents of the United Kingdom, cultural differences in which types of places are considered uncanny have also not been clarified.

Among the unmeasured variables, nostalgia has been studied as a sentimental longing for the past in social psychology (Wildschut et al., 2006) and in cognitive psychology (Kusumi et al., 2010). Nostalgic narratives mainly describe scenes in which the self interacts with close others and important events, and they contain more expressions of positive affect than of negative affect (Wildschut et al., 2006). Nostalgia is more likely to occur under negative mood and loneliness (Wildschut et al., 2006; Zhou et al., 2008), and it is also more likely to occur when people recall the past while bored (van Tilburg et al., 2013). Nostalgia during loneliness increases perceived social support (Zhou et al., 2008). Among studies related to the term liminal, there are design guidelines for virtual environments that evoke nostalgia (Sarcinella et al., 2025) and an analysis of reviews of games said to evoke a liminal feeling (Guzsvinecz, 2025). Neither examined static images, and to our knowledge, no study has had the same participants rate both uncanniness and nostalgia for static images. The finding that nostalgia is more likely to occur under negative mood is consistent with viewers' reports of nostalgia for images that they evaluate as uncanny, but no study has examined the direct relation between the two evaluations.

4. Four Accounts

The idea underlying the human-absence account was stated in psychoanalysis and cultural criticism before psychology examined it empirically. Freud (1919 Freud ( /1955) ) explained the uncanny as something once familiar that reappears after repression, but listed silence, solitude, and darkness as possible counterexamples to this explanation. Fisher (2016) stated that one of the two forms of the eerie, the feeling that arises when something that should be present is absent, arises more readily in landscapes from which humans have partially disappeared, involves speculation about what happened, and disappears once that speculation is answered. Tokuyama and Nakamura (2025) also argued that in liminal spaces the absence of people causes places to lose their meaning and that observers who speculate about the cause obtain no answer. These essays are not based on empirical evidence, and they do not state in a measurable form the conditions under which uncanniness increases or decreases.

4.1 Comparisons

To compare the four accounts on the same set of phenomena, we operationally define uncanniness as an unpleasant state that differs from fear, disgust, and low liking and in which the direction of behavior, whether to approach or move away from the object, remains undetermined. The distinction from low liking is based on research on rating scales for the uncanny valley. That research held that feeling a person to be cold and unlikable differs from feeling the person to be eerie, and it developed an eeriness index that is not correlated with indices of likability and warmth (Ho & MacDorman, 2010). The distinction from fear and disgust, and the feature that the direction of behavior remains undetermined, are based on what McAndrew and Koehnke (2016) stated about creepiness toward persons. Using the example that an armed robber pointing a gun, whose threat is clear, is frightening but is not called creepy, they argued that creepiness is anxiety that arises when it is ambiguous whether there is anything to fear and, if so, what the threat is, and that it differs qualitatively from fear and disgust, whose conclusions are clear. They further described it as an unpleasant state in which a person cannot move because of ambivalence: running away from someone who has done nothing is rude, but staying may be dangerous. We extend this distinction and the feature that the direction of behavior remains undetermined from persons to images of places.

We treat ratings of eerie, creepy, and uncanny as measures of the same construct, but no study has demonstrated their convergent validity for images of places, and the definition of eerie that Diel and Lewis (2022a) presented to participants included fear. Therefore, distinguishing uncanniness from fear requires obtaining threat ratings separately.

We take up four accounts from which testable predictions can be derived for manipulations of place images: the structural deviation account (Diel & Lewis, 2022a), the categorization difficulty account (Yamada et al., 2013), the threat ambiguity account (McAndrew & Koehnke, 2016), and the violated-expectation-of-social-presence account. The last account is our restatement, as an independent account, of the prediction that Diel and Lewis (2022a) made as an a priori hypothesis for their Experiment 3. 4.2 Structural Deviation

According to the structural deviation account, places have predictable configurations, and observers judge places that depart from these configurations as uncanny (Diel & Lewis, 2022a). The authors applied to places the explanation proposed for faces and objects, namely deviation from familiar configurations (Diel & Lewis, 2022b), and distinguished four types of deviation: changes in size, missing features, unexpected placement, and excessive repetition.

In this account, uncanniness increases with the amount of deviation, and the shape of this relationship is not specified (Figure 2A). Diel and Lewis (2022a) obtained the valley shape with realism on the horizontal axis; with the number of structural anomalies on the horizontal axis, images with more anomalies had higher uncanniness ratings. Because the authors' coding of anomalies counted only features visible in the image, we treat this account as the claim that observers form expectations only from the configuration visible in the image.

Under this treatment, adding a sentence that states the time the photograph was taken or whether the place was open for business, without changing the image, does not lower uncanniness. This account explains the effect of the absence of objects, and it also explains the rise in uncanniness and abnormality when people were added. Because images from which people have been removed also depart from the typical configuration, however, this account predicts that abnormality ratings should also rise when people are removed, and in Experiment 3 they did not. Explaining this result requires the auxiliary assumption that public spaces from which people have been removed are also within the range of typical configurations. In their General Discussion, the authors also included the expectation of human presence among the targets of deviation, but because that version makes the same predictions as the violated-expectation-of-social-presence account regarding the presence or absence of people, we treat the two as separate accounts. 4.3 Categorization Difficulty

According to the categorization difficulty account, observers evaluate objects negatively when the objects are difficult to classify into either of two categories (Yamada et al., 2013). For morphs that blended human faces with the faces of animated characters, liking was lowest at the point where categorization latency was longest (Yamada et al., 2013), and for morphs between tomatoes and strawberries as well, the lower the classification confidence for an image, the less participants wanted to eat the depicted item (Yamada et al., 2012). This tendency is stronger in observers who score high on the behavioral inhibition system (Sasaki et al., 2017). According to a later formulation of this account, the negative evaluation arises because an object with an unlikely appearance does not fit any known category and is classified into the stranger category, a category of things to be avoided (Kawabe et al., 2017; Yamada & Sasaki, 2025), and this category can extend to unfamiliar or ambiguous objects in general (Yamada & Sasaki, 2025).

This account concerns the categorization of objects. Applied to images of places, the account predicts that an image is uncanny when the situation of the depicted place (whether it is open for business or closed for the day) cannot be assigned to a known category. Diel and Lewis (2022a) showed that observers' tolerance of ambiguity did not predict uncanniness, but this result concerns a trait of observers, and the difficulty of categorizing each stimulus was not measured. For written text, words and sentences whose ambiguity had been verified did not increase uncanniness (Diel & Lewis, 2022c), but this account has not yet been tested with images of places.

4.4 Threat Ambiguity

According to the threat ambiguity account, uncanniness is a response that maintains vigilance when the observer cannot determine whether a threat is present, and the response becomes fear once the threat is confirmed (McAndrew & Koehnke, 2016). This account has also been applied to places, with the argument that places containing cues to the presence of harmful agents are uncanny (McAndrew, 2020). For images of places, including liminal images, uncanniness increased with threat ratings (Diel & Lewis, 2022a), but that scale asked about the amount of threat and did not ask about the difficulty of determining whether a threat was present. This account explains the appearance effect if one assumes that a person appearing in a private space is an agent whose threat is uncertain, and it explains the uncanniness of corridors if one assumes that corridors contain hiding places. However, under this account, the more people a place contains, the lower the estimated likelihood of being threatened by an unseen agent should be, yet in Experiment 3, adding people to images of public spaces did not lower threat ratings (Diel & Lewis, 2022a).

4.5 Violated-Expectation-of-Social-Presence

According to the violated-expectation-of-social-presence account, the uncanniness of a place is proportional to the difference between the number of people expected in that place and the number of people visible. Diel and Lewis (2022a) explained the interaction in Experiment 3 by whether people are expected in the place; this account is the simplest version of that interpretation, in which the number of people expected is determined only by the type of place. A book on liminal images written for general readers also repeatedly states that when people disappear from a place built for people, the place loses its purpose and becomes uncanny (ALT236, 2023). This account explains the interaction, but because adding or removing the same number of people changes the amount of violation by the same amount, it cannot derive the finding that ratings of threat and abnormality rose only when people were added, and it does not explain the effect of the absence of objects, which occurred in comparisons between rooms that contained no people. In studies that manipulate only the absence of people, this account and the structural deviation account make the same predictions. Because public passageways are places where people are expected, this account explains the uncanniness of corridors only for public passageways.

4.6 Summary of the Existing Accounts

As Table 1 shows, none of the four accounts explains two or more of the four facts from its central claims alone. No account explains, without auxiliary assumptions, why the ratings that changed together with uncanniness differed between adding and removing people.

The structural deviation account explains the effect of the absence of objects, and the threat ambiguity account explains the uncanniness of corridors with auxiliary assumptions, but neither of these two facts can be derived from the other of these two accounts without auxiliary assumptions. The categorization difficulty account has not yet been tested with images of places, and the violated-expectation-of-social-presence account has not been tested separately from the structural deviation account. We therefore consider the possibility that the four accounts each describe a different part of the process by which uncanniness arises, in which case they are not mutually exclusive alternatives.

5. Proposal: Unexplained Absence in Places in Use

>Proposal: Unexplained Absence in Places in Use

5.1 Overview

We explain the uncanniness of liminal images as a process consisting of four stages (Figure 2B). The observer first infers what the place in the image is for (a station, a store, a school, etc.) (categorization). Next, from that category and from a hypothesis about the state of use (in use or closed), the observer anticipates the elements that should be present in the place and compares them with the image. When elements that should be present are missing and the observer cannot find a hypothesis that explains the absence (that it is after closing time, that it is early morning, etc.), the observer appraises the depicted state as an unlikely state in reality (comparison). The observer repeats comparison for each new hypothesis that explains the absence, and each comparison consists of comparing the elements that the hypothesis predicts with the image. When the observer considers, as the reason for the absence, agents who are not visible in the image but may be present at the location, hiding places and darkness intensify this appraisal (amplification). Finally, the observer applies one or more concepts, such as uncanny, nostalgic, boring, and quiet, to the unpleasant bodily state that accompanies this appraisal (labeling). This model assigns each of the four existing accounts to a particular stage. The categorization stage corresponds to the initial categorization in the view that the brain predicts sensory signals using categories constructed from past experience (Barrett & Miller, 2026). The categorization difficulty account describes the comparison stage, the violatedexpectation-of-social-presence account describes one of the expectations used in comparison, and the threat ambiguity account describes amplification. The labeling stage incorporates constructionist theories of emotion (Barrett, 2017; Russell, 2003), according to which an emotion is constituted when sensations from inside the body and from the external world, together with information about the situation, are categorized with concepts based on past experience. The model divides the anomalies that the structural deviation account counted as a single factor into anomalies that interfere with categorization and violations of expectation in comparison.

We call these processes stages because we claim a dependency in which each process uses the output of the preceding process. We do not claim a temporal order in which a process begins only after the preceding process has finished. This dependency can be tested by examining whether the effect of a later stage is absent in images for which the preceding stage fails. Because the observer cannot determine what should be there in an image of an unidentifiable kind of place, the appraisal of plausibility should not change even if a caption gives the reason for the absence. Because the appraisal of an unlikely state does not arise for an image in which nothing is missing, adding hiding places or darkness should not increase uncanniness. If results contrary to these predictions are obtained, the two processes in question need to be redescribed as processes that change uncanniness independently of each other.

5.2 Stage 1: Categorization

Observers can identify the category of a natural scene even from extremely brief presentations (Greene & Oliva, 2009), and objects and object positions that are inconsistent with a scene elicit distinct EEG responses (Võ & Wolfe, 2013). These findings suggest that observers anticipate elements on the basis of their categorization of a place, and the anticipation that people should be present also presupposes that the observer has categorized the place as one where people should be present. We regard the categorization at this stage as the initial categorization performed by the process that predicts sensory signals using categories constructed from past experience (Barrett & Miller, 2026). This stage determines the type of place and provides the expectations that the comparison stage uses, and it does not by itself produce an appraisal of uncanniness. Because the elements that should be present are not determined for an image that cannot be categorized, giving the reason for the absence should not change the appraisal of plausibility. The outcome of categorization can be measured by the response time of judgments of place type and by the rate of agreement among participants (hereafter, categorization agreement).

5.3 Stage 2: Comparison

From the category of the place and from a hypothesis about the state of use, the observer anticipates the elements that should be present in the place. In a sales floor in use, the observer expects merchandise, lighting, staff, and customers. When the image lacks these elements, the observer searches for a hypothesis that explains the absence, and if the observer can form a hypothesis such as after closing time, early morning, or a derelict place, the appraisal of an unlikely state weakens according to the probability of that hypothesis.

According to the meaning maintenance model (Heine et al., 2006), when people perceive an anomaly that does not fit an expected framework of relations, they reinterpret the event so that the anomaly is no longer anomalous, revise the framework, or reaffirm a framework in another domain. The comparison stage is close to the reinterpretation route; it restricts the target of the search to the reason for the absence and treats the probability of that reason as a measurable variable. Fisher's (2016) observation that the feeling of uncanniness is weaker in places where the reason for their dereliction is explained contains the same idea. The state at issue can occur in reality, but the observer finds no reason for it; it therefore differs from the uncanniness produced by apparent impossibility (Windsor, 2019).

The probability of a hypothesis that explains the absence depends on the cues of use that remain in the image. We use the term cues of use for elements from which the observer judges that someone did something a short time ago (lights that are on, escalators that are running, merchandise on display, doors that are open). The more cues of use there are, the lower the probability of hypotheses that do not include agents (after closing time, a derelict place). In a place that appears to have been abandoned with the lights left on, abandonment explains the absence of people but does not explain the lighting. Thus, the absence remains unexplained. In this case, even after the observer repeats comparison under the hypothesis of abandonment, the lights that are on, which that hypothesis does not predict, remain unexplained. The same applies to the mall at 4 a.m. that the Aesthetics Wiki lists as a typical example of this aesthetic (Aesthetics Wiki, 2021): the time of day explains the absence of people but does not explain the lights that are on or the neatly arranged displays. This claim predicts that images of derelict places with a full set of cues of abandonment are rated as less uncanny than images of empty places with the lights on. Derelict places for which the reason people left is known have also been cited as examples of uncanny places (McAndrew, 2020), but we assume that many of the reactions reported for derelict places are fear of a place where a threat has been established or disgust at decay. The output of comparison is an appraisal of the degree to which the depicted state could occur in reality (hereafter, plausibility). Plausibility is similar to the plausibility illusion in research on virtual environments (Slater, 2009), but plausibility in our sense is an appraisal of the state depicted in an image, whereas the plausibility illusion concerns the environment being experienced. The realism ratings in Diel and Lewis (2022a) asked how close the depicted place looked to a real place and may therefore overlap with plausibility, but they did not ask whether the depicted state could occur in reality. We assume that the appraisal of plausibility arises when the observer interprets the image as a depiction of a real place. In research on faces, the uncanniness of photographs showing rows of identical faces covaried with low ratings of perceived reality, whereas anime-style drawings showing rows of indistinguishable characters were rated low in uncanniness even though they were also rated low in perceived reality (Yonemitsu et al., 2021a). The idea that an unlikely appearance produces a negative evaluation already exists in the stranger avoidance account (Kawabe et al., 2017), the later formulation of the categorization difficulty account; the comparison stage extends this idea from the appearance of objects to the state of places and adds the claim that the appraisal of plausibility for the same image changes when a hypothesis that explains the absence is provided.

The comparison stage can be described as categorization into the stranger category of the categorization difficulty account. Once the categorization stage has determined the type of place, each category of situation that a place of that type can be in (a sales floor during business hours, a sales floor being prepared before opening, etc., which correspond to hypotheses about the state of use) predicts what should appear in the image. Signals that the prediction cannot explain, and the absence of predicted signals, remain as prediction errors (Barrett & Miller, 2026). In audition, only when the next sound can be predicted with confidence does the auditory cortex produce, at the moment when a predicted sound fails to occur, a response similar to the response to a sound that actually occurs (SanMiguel et al., 2013). At the behavioral level as well, a silence in which ambient sound stopped was represented as an event in the same way as a sound (Goh et al., 2023). These findings suggest that the absence of an expected stimulus can be represented without inference. We therefore assume that in places where the expectation is confident, the fact that no one is present is also represented at the comparison stage. Because the category of a sales floor during business hours predicts a signal that people are present, a prediction error remains for an image without people. If another category, such as a sales floor being prepared before opening, can predict both the absence of people and the other cues in the image, no prediction error remains, and this is the case in which a hypothesis that explains the absence is found. In the framework of predictive processing, this corresponds to a higher-level hypothesis explaining away lower-level prediction errors (Clark, 2013). When a prediction error remains under every category, the observer cannot assign the situation in the image to any known category and assigns it to the stranger category (Kawabe et al., 2017; Yamada & Sasaki, 2025). In morphed images of faces and foods as well, objects that could not be categorized into a known category were evaluated negatively (Yamada et al., 2012 (Yamada et al., , 2013)). We regard the judgment that assigns a situation to the stranger category as the appraisal of an unlikely state. Jentsch (1906) had already proposed that a lack of orientation, in which a person does not know how to deal with things (Mangel an Orientirung), produces uncanniness.

We call the number of instances of an element that the observer anticipates should be present in the place the expected quantity, and the narrowness of the range of that anticipation the precision of expectation. The precision of expectation can be measured at the group level by the smallness of the between-participant variability in responses about the expected quantity, and at the individual level by confidence in the expected quantity. We assume that the precision of expectation for people is highest in public spaces in use. If the decrease in appraisal that the absence of an element produces is proportional to the precision of expectation, then in a station or sales floor during business hours, the absence of people has a larger effect than the absence of furniture or merchandise. By contrast, in images without cues indicating the time of day or whether the place is open for business, the hypothesis that people are temporarily absent explains the absence, and the probability of this hypothesis decreases as the expected quantity of people increases and as the angle of view widens.

In Experiment 3 of Diel and Lewis (2022a), the change in uncanniness when the person was removed from public spaces was small and was not accompanied by changes in threat and abnormality, whereas when a person was added to private spaces, uncanniness rose together with ratings of threat and abnormality. In the present model, the two results arise from different processes. If the images of public spaces contained few cues indicating business or activity, the absence of people would have been explained by the hypothesis that people are temporarily absent, the appraisal of an unlikely state would not have arisen at the comparison stage, and uncanniness would have changed little. The finding that abnormality ratings did not change is also consistent with this explanation. An unfamiliar person appearing in a private space may have been interpreted as an intruder, and because threat ratings increased, the appearance effect may arise through the appraisal of threat and may include fear, which we have distinguished from uncanniness. Because the two effects arise from different processes, the present model does not predict which of the two is larger.

Because the appearance of a person adds a salient element, part of the appearance effect could also be explained by salience, by analogy with cognitive explanations of search asymmetry (Treisman & Souther, 1985); however, an explanation based on salience does not predict an effect of a caption, which leaves the image unchanged.

5.4 Stage 3: Amplification

We assume that when the observer rejects hypotheses such as after closing time or early morning because they contradict the cues in the image, the remaining candidates are that people had a reason to leave, that something is keeping people away, or that someone unseen is still present, and that each of these produces a state in which the observer cannot determine whether a threat is present. Because these candidates posit events or agents that do not appear in the image, the observer cannot verify them against the cues in the image, and the mismatch with the prediction remains. We call cues in the image that make it easier for the observer to think that an unseen agent is present amplifiers. The number of hiding places and limited visibility have been treated as variables related to fear of crime (Fisher & Nasar, 1992), and darkening the laboratory increased the startle response (Grillon et al., 1997). In Experiment 2 of Diel and Lewis (2022a), distortion increased uncanniness even when lighting and escape routes were controlled, and in Experiment 3, which manipulated the presence or absence of people, adding people to distorted public spaces decreased uncanniness while threat ratings remained unchanged. Because these results suggest that threat alone cannot explain uncanniness, we assume that amplifiers are moderators that increase uncanniness only in images for which the appraisal of an unlikely state has occurred.

Accordingly, adding hiding places to an image with neither structural anomalies nor absence should increase threat ratings but should not increase uncanniness. Amplification may also involve a process in which the observer pictures unseen agents in mental imagery. However, picturing people who belong in the place should not intensify uncanniness. In Experiment 3 of Diel and Lewis (2022a), adding a person to images of public spaces, where people are expected, did not raise uncanniness. Therefore, if any imagery intensifies uncanniness, it is the image of a person who should not be there, pictured as a candidate explanation for the absence after the absence has remained unexplained at the comparison stage. For this process to operate, two conditions are necessary: the pictured person must be treated as a representation close to perception, and that representation must produce a fear response. Regarding the first, people with stronger imagery are more likely to judge that a stimulus was present when it was not (Dijkstra & Fleming, 2023) and are more likely to believe stories in which they filled in, through their own imagery, objects that were not shown (Sparks et al., 1995). Regarding the second, people with little or no mental imagery (aphantasia) showed no bodily fear response when reading frightening scenarios but did show one when viewing frightening photographs (Wicken et al., 2021). This result suggests that for fear to arise from an object that is not shown, the object must be pictured in imagery. From these findings, we assume that the more vivid an observer's imagery, the higher the observer's threat ratings and the more intense the observer's uncanniness for images with an unexplained absence. However, vivid imagery does not necessarily intensify fear. In a previous study in which people with a fear of heights experienced heights in a virtual environment, those with higher habitual imagery vividness measured by questionnaire reported less fear, whereas those with higher vividness experienced during exposure reported more fear (Huang et al., 2000).

5.5 Stage 4: Labeling

In constructionist theories of emotion, the bodily state is described as core affect consisting of valence and arousal, and the observer applies different emotion concepts to the same core affect depending on the context and on the concepts the observer already has (Russell, 2003). We predict that, compared with a version of the same place with people present, an image that contains only an unexplained absence will receive lower valence ratings and unchanged arousal ratings, and that adding amplifiers will also raise arousal ratings. The observer applies one or more of the concepts uncanny, nostalgic, boring, and quiet to this bodily state. The labeling stage is supported if adding the words "liminal space" to an image changes the ratings of uncanniness and nostalgia more than adding a descriptive phrase that conveys only the meaning of those words, without changing valence or arousal.

Viewers' simultaneous reports of uncanniness and nostalgia could result from any of four causal structures (Figure 3). First, the two appraisals may arise from separate cues and merely be reported together (Figure 3A). Uncanniness arises from unexplained absence, nostalgia arises from cues to a type of place where the observer spent their childhood or to the period in which the photograph was taken, and for images that contain both kinds of cues, the two are reported together. Second, a single cause may act on both (Figure 3B). One candidate is familiarity with the type of place. Images of types of places that a person has encountered repeatedly in the past increase both nostalgia and the familiarity described as "as if I had been to the place before" (Kusumi, 2021), and in our model, the same familiarity increases the precision of expectation about the state in which people were present and thereby strengthens the effect of absence. Separately from this, if observers apply the concept uncanny on some occasions and the concept nostalgic on other occasions to a single bodily state produced by viewing the image, that bodily state becomes a common cause of the two ratings. In this case, among observers whose bodily states are equal, the more strongly an observer applies the concept uncanny to that state, the more weakly the observer applies the concept nostalgic. Thus, the partial correlation between the two ratings controlling for valence and arousal will be negative. In the structure in which the two appraisals arise from separate cues (Figure 3A), this partial correlation will be close to 0. Third, uncanniness may cause nostalgia (Figure 3C). Because nostalgia tends to arise under negative mood (Wildschut et al., 2006; Zhou et al., 2008), the unpleasant state produced by unexplained absence may prompt recollection of the past. Fourth, nostalgia may increase or decrease uncanniness (Figure 3D). Because experimentally induced nostalgia is mainly accompanied by pleasantness (Leunissen et al., 2021), it may decrease uncanniness. On the other hand, the more intense pleasantness and unpleasantness are at the same time, the lower people's confidence about what emotion they are feeling (Vaccaro et al., 2025). In images in which the pleasantness of nostalgia and the unpleasantness of absence occur to a similar degree, this decrease in confidence may increase reports of uncanniness. These structures can be distinguished by manipulating cues of absence and cues of nostalgia independently and measuring the two ratings separately. 5.6 Relation to Structural Deviation

The four-stage model is consistent with the results of Diel and Lewis (2022a). In the model, the sum of anomalies that interfere with categorization and violations of expectation in comparison predicts uncanniness; the model thus accounts for the result that the number of structural anomalies predicted uncanniness. In addition, the model predicts that uncanniness arises even in images without anomalies of configuration when cues of use and absence are present together, that uncanniness decreases for the same absence when a reason for it is found, and that the two types of anomaly can be measured separately by categorization agreement and by plausibility ratings. With respect to repetition, the difference in uncanniness between images showing rows of identical faces and images showing rows of different faces disappeared when the local features of the faces were rearranged (Yonemitsu et al., 2021b). The model predicts that repetition in images of places also increases uncanniness when the repetition represents an unlikely state.

6. Issues to Address Empirically

The four-stage model provides a framework for the process by which uncanniness arises, and it incorporates existing accounts into each stage. We incorporated the categorization difficulty account and the violated-expectation-of-social-presence account into the comparison stage, the threat ambiguity account into the amplification stage, and constructionist theories of emotion into the labeling stage. None of these accounts has yet been tested for whether it applies to images of places. The following five issues therefore test both whether the stage framework holds and whether the account incorporated into each stage applies to that stage. The four-stage model and each of the four existing accounts make different predictions on these issues. Table 2 summarizes the direction of each account's prediction for each issue. Each issue can be tested either by manipulations that change the observer's expectation or the reason for the absence without changing the configuration of the image, or by manipulations that change the elements of the image one at a time, and each test can be conducted with ratings of existing images or of photographs taken by researchers.

6.1 Issue 1: Is the Effect of an Absence Proportional to the Precision of Expectation?

In the four-stage model, the absence of people is one instance of absence in general, and the uncanniness produced by the absence of an element depends on how confidently that element is expected in that place. Therefore, the difference between the effect of removing people and the effect of removing objects should be explained by the difference between the precision of expectation for people and that for objects. The violated-expectation-of-socialpresence account and the threat ambiguity account predict an effect specific to the absence of people. The categorization difficulty account predicts that uncanniness increases with the absence of either people or objects if the situation becomes harder to categorize, but it makes no prediction about the relation to the precision of expectation. The structural deviation account could also predict that the more precise the norm, the larger the effect of a deviation (Diel & Lewis, 2022b), but because the expectation in that account is formed from the visible configuration, changing only the judgment of the state of use without changing the configuration does not change the effect of the absence. In this model, the state of use changes the precision of expectation, and the effect of the absence changes with it. If the effect of removing people exceeds the effect of removing objects, and the difference exceeds the magnitude predicted from the difference in the precision of expectation for people and for objects, a mechanism specific to people must be assumed.

Because the precision of expectation is learned from experience of using places of a given kind, this issue can also be tested through development. If the four-stage model is correct, the uncanniness of liminal images should arise only after children have acquired expectations about the state of use for each kind of place. Both the objects that elicit negative emotions and the strength of those emotions change with experience during development.

Most of the objects of disgust are determined by social learning (Rottman, 2014), and discomfort toward clusters of circles already arises at 4 to 5 years of age, although more weakly than in adults (Imura et al., 2024; Suzuki et al., 2023). For uncanniness as well, children begin to rate a humanlike robot as more uncanny than a mechanical robot from around 9 years of age (Brink et al., 2019; Chien et al., 2025), and Brink et al. (2019) interpreted this as the result of children learning expectations about how machines look and behave. If children of the same age report uncanniness only for images of the kinds of places they use frequently, uncanniness can be said to depend, apart from the effect of age, on expectations formed through experience of places. The threat ambiguity account predicts that uncanniness arises for images with many threat cues regardless of the amount of experience with the kind of place.

6.2 Issue 2: Does Uncanniness Arise When No Hypothesis That Explains the Absence Is

Found?

In the four-stage model, uncanniness arises when the observer cannot find a reason for the absence. Therefore, images for which observers cannot state why no people are present should be more uncanny, independently of how difficult the place is to categorize, and images with more cues of use should be more uncanny unless a reason is provided. In addition, providing a reason for the absence without changing the image should lower uncanniness through a change in the appraisal of plausibility. As a precedent in which information not contained in an image changed evaluation without changing categorization, adding a fruit odor to tomato-strawberry morphs increased the liking of hard-to-categorize images while visual categorization remained unchanged (Yamada et al., 2014). Because the categorization difficulty account also holds that the inability to place a situation in a known category produces uncanniness, it predicts that providing a reason lowers uncanniness; on this issue, it makes the same prediction as this model. The violated-expectation-of-socialpresence account uses only whether a place is public or private as the cue for its prediction, and the structural deviation account predicts no difference unless the configuration changes.

The threat ambiguity account also predicts that providing a harmless reason lowers uncanniness. In this account, however, uncanniness decreases whenever uncertainty about whether there is danger decreases, even without a reason for the absence. Information that assures only safety can therefore distinguish the two accounts. If providing a reason does not change uncanniness, or if the change is not mediated by the appraisal of plausibility, the description of the comparison stage is wrong.

Depending on the content of the reason given, uncanniness may decrease and the evaluation may also shift toward the pleasant. The reason that a sales floor before opening has been reserved for one person explains the absence and also adds the pleasant meaning that one can use the place alone. The experience of sensing that someone unseen is nearby (felt presence) can also be frightening or comforting depending on the situation. One person, alone in an old mine, felt watched by someone not necessarily benevolent, whereas another reported that, when hypothermic and about to give up in an endurance race, something urged them to open their eyes and they were able to finish, and another felt a touch on the back of the head while working and described it as a pleasant experience (Alderson-Day et al., 2023).

Depending on how people interpret the situation, they may experience the same unseen someone as a watcher or as a helper. Therefore, by attaching a neutral reason, a pleasant reason, and a reason indicating threat to the same image and comparing the ratings, researchers can test whether the valence of the emotion is determined by the explanation of the absence or by the elements of the image. Because liminal images on the internet were collected with uncanniness as a criterion, they are biased toward high uncanniness and low pleasantness. To test whether a reason shifts the evaluation toward the pleasant, newly taken photographs of unoccupied places that retain cues of use are therefore suitable.

6.3 Issue 3: Do Threat Cues Intensify Uncanniness Only When There Is an Unexplained

Absence?

The threat ambiguity account predicts that threat cues such as hiding places and darkness increase uncanniness regardless of whether people are present. In the four-stage model, these cues are amplifiers that intensify uncanniness only when the appraisal of an unlikely state has been made, and in scenes with people present they increase threat ratings but do not increase uncanniness. The violated-expectation-of-social-presence account predicts that only the absence of people increases uncanniness, regardless of threat cues.

Therefore, threat ratings and uncanniness ratings should dissociate in scenes with people present, and this issue can be tested by manipulating the presence of people and threat cues while keeping cues of use. If threat cues increase uncanniness even in scenes with people present, threat ambiguity is an independent cause of uncanniness and does not act as an amplifier.

6.4 Issue 4: Does Conceptual Knowledge Change Reports of Uncanniness and Nostalgia

Without Changing the Bodily State?

In the four-stage model, observers apply emotion concepts to the bodily state produced during comparison and amplification, according to context and knowledge.

Therefore, observers who know a concept that links uncanniness and nostalgia, such as the term "liminal space," should report high levels of both uncanniness and nostalgia when that concept is presented, without changing their ratings of valence and arousal. The structural deviation account, the threat ambiguity account, and the violated-expectation-of-socialpresence account predict no difference, and the categorization difficulty account could predict that uncanniness decreases if the term helps categorize the situation. Unless the effect of the term is compared with the effect of a descriptive phrase that conveys only the meaning of the term, the effect can also be explained by demand characteristics (Orne, 1962). If the effect of the term is explained by changes in valence and arousal, the description of the labeling stage is wrong.

6.5 Issue 5: Does Imagery Vividness Intensify Threat Ratings Only for Images With an

Unexplained Absence?

In the four-stage model, the pictured agent is generated after the absence has remained unexplained at the comparison stage. Imagery vividness should therefore intensify threat ratings and reports of sensing that someone is present only for images with an unexplained absence, and it should not change whether uncanniness arises. The threat ambiguity account predicts that imagery intensifies threat in scenes with threat cues in general, without distinguishing whether there is an absence. The structural deviation account, the categorization difficulty account, and the violated-expectation-of-social-presence account make no predictions about individual differences in imagery. Imagery vividness varies across individuals, from people with little or no mental imagery (aphantasia) to people who report imagery close to actual seeing (hyperphantasia), and these groups can be identified by questionnaire (Zeman et al., 2020). This issue can be tested by showing both groups images with an unexplained absence, images in which the reason for the absence is given, and images with people, and comparing their reports of uncanniness, threat, and sensed presence.

If uncanniness arises equally in the aphantasia group and only reports of threat and sensed presence are intensified in the hyperphantasia group, imagery is a process that belongs to the amplification stage. Because the relationship between imagery vividness and fear is not uniform (Huang et al., 2000), however, participants should also report, after rating each image, whether they pictured a person and how vividly.

7. Implications and Limitations

For research on the uncanny valley, this article distinguishes agents depicted in the image from expected people. Because removing objects increased uncanniness even in comparisons between rooms that contained no people (Diel & Lewis, 2022a), agents depicted in the image are not a necessary condition for uncanniness. In our four-stage model, people who are not depicted in the image are also involved in the appraisal of uncanniness, as expected elements at the comparison stage and as candidate unseen agents at the amplification stage. If the increase in uncanniness when people are removed does not exceed the magnitude predicted from the difference in the precision of expectation for people and for objects, no mechanism specific to expected people is needed, and uncanniness can be described in general terms as an appraisal that arises when observers cannot explain an absence in a realistic image. If the explanation that objects whose appearance is unlikely are categorized into the stranger category (Kawabe et al., 2017; Yamada & Sasaki, 2025) also applies to faces and bodies, the valley observed with images of faces and robots and the uncanniness of images of places can be described by the same process, the appraisal of an unlikely state.

For emotion research, liminal images make it possible to measure mixed emotions of uncanniness and nostalgia with images that are drawn from a sampling frame and in which people and objects are manipulated. In liminal images, the unexplained absence, which is a candidate cause of unpleasantness, and cues to the type of place and the period in which the photograph was taken, which are candidate causes of nostalgia, can be manipulated independently by editing the image. Therefore, whether the two appraisals arise from separate causes or one causes the other (Figure 3) can be distinguished within the same set of images.

In addition, standardized affective image sets have collected photographs depicting negative content as unpleasant images (Marchewka et al., 2014), whereas the present model predicts that in liminal images, the absence of neutral elements relative to expectation produces the negative appraisal of uncanniness. If this prediction is supported, the valence of an image will need to be predicted not only from the elements the image contains but also from the difference between those elements and the elements the observer expects in that place.

Furthermore, because the term "liminal space" is a concept that internet users created outside the laboratory, manipulating the presentation of the term makes it possible to test whether a learned concept changes reports of emotion.

The claims and evidence of this article have the following limitations, each of which requires further research. Our claims concern static images presented on a screen. Because the probability of the hypothesis that people are temporarily absent and whether the observer is alone can be manipulated separately in immersive virtual environments, the predictions of the present model can be tested in immersive environments. Because being alone could be an amplifier, uncanniness is predicted to be stronger in immersive environments than with static images. In addition, the four facts that we argued are difficult to explain with the structural deviation account alone (the difference between the effects of adding and removing people, the effect of the absence of objects, the uncanniness of corridors, and the distribution of openended responses) come from a single article with participants in the United Kingdom, and whether they replicate in other populations needs to be tested. In particular, the effect of removing people was examined in an experiment with only five stimuli per condition, in which the people were rendered human figure models, and when variability among stimuli was taken into account, the effect could not be distinguished from zero with either index.

Experiments that manipulate the presence of people with many photographs of real people per condition can determine whether the effect of removing people exists and how large it is.

Furthermore, much of the evidence supporting each stage of the four-stage model has come from studies of targets other than places (faces, morphed images of food, and fear of crime).

Studies of Issues 1-5 will be the first to test these relations with images of places.

8. Conclusion

This article explained the uncanniness of liminal images as an appraisal that arises when, in a place that the observer judges to be in use, the observer cannot find a reason why people or objects that should be there are missing. This explanation incorporates the deviation from configurations, the difficulty of categorization, the ambiguity of threat, and the expectation of human presence, which the four existing accounts have addressed, as stages of a single process consisting of categorization, comparison, amplification, and labeling.

Research on liminal spaces has so far described which images are rated as uncanny in terms of the features depicted in the images. If our explanation is correct, research on liminal spaces becomes research on how observers represent people and objects that are not depicted in an image. Research on uncanniness has explained uncanniness by the features of depicted objects such as faces and robots. The manipulations that have been studied as producing uncanniness include mixing faces from two categories (Yamada et al., 2013), duplicating the same face side by side (Yonemitsu et al., 2021a), and excessively repeating elements of a place (Diel & Lewis, 2022a). Many liminal images show places from which people or objects that should be there are missing, and they are cases in which uncanniness arises because elements are missing. In audition, a silence in which ambient sound stopped was perceived in the same way as a sound, and Goh et al. (2023) proposed studying the perception of absence in other senses. Liminal images can be used to study how the absence of people is perceived and appraised in vision.

Emotion research has determined the valence of stimuli from the content they depict, but liminal images consist only of neutral elements, and their uncanniness arises from the difference between the depicted elements and the elements that the observer expects in the place. Liminal images therefore make it possible to study, with experimental manipulation, how removing elements from an image elicits emotion. In addition, "liminal space" is a concept that internet users created and shared, and the process by which viewers who have learned this term come to report their experiences with it is an instance of the process, described by constructionist theories of emotion, by which acquiring a concept changes reports of emotion. Research on liminal spaces should therefore be conducted as research on a question shared by research on uncanniness and emotion research: how negative appraisals arise from the absence of people and objects and from the expectations of observers.

Our reanalysis also has limitations. First, because there are only five stimuli per condition, a test that uses stimuli as the unit of analysis is a paired test on five pairs of images, with 4 degrees of freedom. When stimuli are used as the unit of analysis (the small points in Figure 1A and 1B), the power of tests of differences between conditions is low (in paired t tests over the five pairs, with the four-item index, t(4) = 0.82, p = .459 for undistorted public spaces and t(4) = 2.58, p = .061 for undistorted private spaces; with the three-item index, t(4) = 1.05, p = .352 and t(4) = 2.26, p = .087, respectively). In the per-stimulus means, one of the five stimuli without a person for undistorted public spaces (Stimulus c32) had an uncanniness of 51.5, which was higher than the other four (20.5-42.7). Its difference from the version with a person that has the corresponding number (Stimulus e32) was 11.9 points, and the differences for the remaining four pairs ranged from -10.2 to 6.1 points. The absence effect of 3.7 points obtained with participants as the unit depends on this one pair. When we excluded the pair of c32 and e32 and recomputed, the absence effect was 1.3 points (d = 0.12), and with the three-item index it was 1.9 points (d = 0.17). Second, we cannot conclude that there is no absence effect in undistorted public spaces. The absence effect was significant in the uncorrected test with the three-item index and was in the same direction with the four-item index. We claim only that the point estimate of the absence effect was small, that its confidence interval was wide (mixed model: [-8.1, 14.7]), and that the effect was not accompanied by changes in threat and abnormality ratings.

Third, the people in Experiment 3 were three-dimensional human figure models rendered with interior design software, dressed appropriately for the place and posed to show meaningful actions or interactions. Therefore, in the conditions with a person, the authors added to the images not only the rendering of a human body but also a depiction of action.

Stimuli in research on the uncanny valley have included computer-rendered people (Diel et al., 2022). If a human figure model itself adds a certain amount of uncanniness, the change when a person is removed from public spaces is underestimated by that amount, and the change when a person is added to private spaces is overestimated by the same amount. In Experiment 3, adding a person in undistorted private spaces raised both the ratings of perceived abnormality with 500-ms presentation (d = 1.1 as reported by the authors) and the abnormality ratings (d = 1.58 as reported by the authors). This result is consistent with the possibility that participants perceived the person itself as abnormal. Diel and Lewis (2022a) themselves listed as a limitation the possibility that participants perceived the virtual representations of people differently from real people, and they proposed replication with photographs that include real people as future research. The same problem exists in research on immersive environments. In a study that added humanoid avatars to public scenes, uncanniness was likewise not lower than in scenes without avatars (Musolino et al., 2025).

This result of Musolino et al. (2025) is consistent with the result of the reanalysis that the absence effect is small in public spaces. However, the avatars in that study were moving in the outdoor scenes, the scenes were uniformly designed in a dark, neon, futuristic style, and 5 of the 46 participants answered that the avatars instead increased their anxiety. Therefore, the results of Musolino et al. (2025) do not constitute independent evidence that the absence effect is small, and they are also consistent with the possibility that rendered people themselves add uncanniness. Part of the estimates of the two effects may derive from the rendering of the human figure models. For this reason as well, studies that manipulate the presence of people in photographs taken by researchers are needed.

The d and t values reported by the authors are negative because of the direction of comparison, and in this Supplementary Material we report them as absolute values. The authors also reported an interaction between the presence of a person and room type, F(1, 36) = 62.61, η²p = .63. The authors' post hoc tests used participants as the unit of analysis and did not include the variability among stimuli in the error term. Preprocessing followed the authors' R script, and of the 39 participant files made public, we used the 37 that this script reads. Experiment 3 had five stimuli per condition, and the pairs with and without a person were five pairs that the authors created by placing a person or furniture in images of the same room. Tests that use participants as the unit do not take into account the error that arises from sampling five sets of stimuli (Judd et al., 2012). We therefore fitted a linear mixed model with crossed random effects for participants and stimuli to the trial-level data from the undistorted conditions.
