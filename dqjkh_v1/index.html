Following Vize et al. (2025), we will examine three specific forms of negative affect: shame and guilt as a two-item composite, anger and frustration as a two-item composite, and nervous as a single item. The three positive affect items will be used to create a three-item composite of positive affect.

Stressful/Unpleasant and Pleasant Interpersonal Events. After participants answered items assessing current positive and negative affect and impulsivity, they were asked if anything stressful had occurred since the last assessment. Participants were instructed to consider whether they themselves felt stressed when the event occurred, not whether they or another person would typically feel stressed in the same situation. If a stressful event occurred, participants answered additional questions about the event, including what type of event they encountered. The options were “argument/disagreement/conflict”, “social disappointment/let down”, “work/school related event”, “home related event”, “health issue or accident”, “event that happened to others”, and “other stressor.”

Participants who indicated that they had not experienced a stressful event since the last assessment were asked if any of the following had occurred since the last assessment: “argument, disagreement, conflict”, “social disappointment/let down”, “difficulties involving work or school”, “difficulties at home”, “health issue or accident”, “negative event that happened to others”, or “other unpleasant event.” These selections were written to closely mirror the content of the stressful events but were presented in this manner to differentiate situation characteristics from the perception of stress. If participants indicated an unpleasant event occurred, they continued on to answer questions about the event.

If participants did not indicate either a stressful or unpleasant event occurring since the last semester, participants were asked anything pleasant or positive had occurred since the previous assessment and could similarly select from a range of categories of pleasant events.

Our analyses will be focused on stressful/unpleasant events that participants classified as being an “argument/disagreement/conflict” or “social disappointment/let down” given their explicit interpersonal nature (13 different types in total). Similarly, we will focus on positive events that are interpersonal in nature (10 different types in total). The specific types of interpersonal stressors/unpleasant/pleasant events are listed below in Table 1.

Inventory of Daily Expressions of Psychopathology (IDEP; https://osf.io/j9kyh). The IDEP was originally developed to assess personality disorder symptoms at the daily level (Wright & Simms, 2016). In the IDEP, items begin with the stem “Over the past 24 hours…” and are rated on a seven-point Likert scale from 0 (Not at All) to 7 (Very Much).

An 81-item version of the IDEP was developed for use in the present study. Item stems remained the same, but ratings were made on a 101-point slider from 0 (Not at All) to 100 (Very Much). The 81-item version of the IDEP assesses 27 facets of psychopathology. Items related to positive symptoms of thought disorder were excluded because the sample did not include individuals diagnosed with thought disorders.

For the present study, the following ten facets (each assessed with three items) will be used for analyses: Anger, Callousness, Domineering, Grandiosity/Entitlement, Hostile Aggression, Manipulativeness, Norm-violation, Irresponsibility, Impulsivity, and Risk Taking.

Details on Points of Overlap with Previous Publications Using the AAPECS dataset

We highlight important points of overlap between our proposed analyses and previous studies using the AAPECS dataset.

1. Vize, C. E., Kaurin, A., & Wright, A. G. C. (2023). Personality Pathology and Momentary Stress Processes. Clinical Psychological Science.

In Vize et al. (2023), broad CAT-PD domains were tested as moderators of specific within-person links between negative affect and momentary stress. The CAT-PD facet scales were combined to create domain scales of Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Psychoticism, Antagonism, and Disinhibition.

Momentary stress was operationalized as any report of a stressful or unpleasant event occurring since the last EMA prompt. Preregistered MSEM testes were used to examine 1) within-person links among negative affect (items: angry, ashamed, guilty, nervous, sad, frustrated) and stress and 2) whether these links were enhanced by CAT-PD domain scales. Secondary analyses also explored if effects differed based on stressors being interpersonal or non-interpersonal in nature (using the same classification approach described below in Table 1). At the within-person level, both stress and negative affect at time t were regressed on negative affect at t-1 to test a mediation model.

The important points of overlap include the known relations between a broader assessment of negative affect and subsequent stress, stress and subsequent negative affect, and negative affect and subsequent stress. In addition, CAT-PD domains and their relations with these processes are known. While our assessment of antagonism, disinhibition, and the psychopathy specifier are more specific, these facets contributed to the broader domain scores used for tests of cross-level moderation. Similarly, our proposed approach for assessing affect is more narrow in scope, but the negative affect items we will be focused on all contributed to the broader negative affect composite in Vize et al. (2023).

2. Wright, A. G. C., Ringwald, W. R., & Zimmermann, J. (2025). Measuring Psychopathology in Daily Life. Clinical Psychological Science, 13(3), 649–663.

Wright et al. (2025) sought to test the within- and between-person structure of the IDEP facet scales. To further validate the use of the IDEP scales, Wright and colleagues also correlated the IDEP facets and domains with corresponding CAT-PD facet and domain scales assessed at baseline in the AAPECS dataset.

The important points of overlap are the relations between the CAT-PD facets and the IDEP facets reported in Table 3, which represent the between-person correlations between the baseline facets of the CAT-PD and the average IDEP facet scores from the EMA surveys. Wright et al. also reported domain level between-person correlations (e.g., CAT-PD Antagonism correlated with IDEP Antagonism).

3. Ringwald, W. R., Edershile, E. A., Mostajabi, J., Nielsen, S. R., Woods, W. C., Simms, L. J., & Wright, A. G. C. (2025). Daily manifestations of psychopathology in response to stress. Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science, 134(2), 117–131.

Ringwald et al. (2025) focused on the relations between daily psychopathology and stress in two samples, one of which was the AAPECS sample. Specifically, the authors examined the relation between daily stress exposure (a count variable of stress events reported on a given day) and stress severity (the mean of momentary stress ratings on a given day), and daily psychopathology as assessed by the IDEP. No facet level relations were examined (IDEP facets were combined to create domains of anakastia, antagonism, detachment, disinhibition, and negative affectivity).

The important points of overlap are the tests the authors conducted to examine whether stress exposure and severity were tied to daily psychopathology at the within- and between-person levels. The authors also examined whether within-person correlations varied depending on the specific kind of stressful event reported (grouped into argument, social disappointment, stressful event happening to friend, health-related, home-related, and work-related events).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Abstract

The Alternative Model of Personality Disorders (AMPD) in DSM-5 represents psychopathy through the antisocial personality disorder domains of antagonism and disinhibition and a psychopathy specifier tied to boldness, but no research has examined how this model relates to functioning in daily life. In a sample enriched for interpersonal difficulties (N = 296), we examined whether AMPD psychopathic traits moderated the effects of interpersonal events on momentary affect, and the effects of daily interpersonal stress on daily expressions of psychopathology, during a 14-to 21-day ecological momentary assessment protocol. Multilevel structural equation models showed that stressful and unpleasant interpersonal events were robustly related to within-person increases in negative affect, particularly anger/frustration, and daily interpersonal stress was related to increased expression of most psychopathy-relevant traits (e.g., callousness, hostile aggression). However, AMPD psychopathic traits rarely moderated these processes. Exceptions included antagonism and disinhibition amplifying positive affect following positive interpersonal events, and the psychopathy specifier amplifying the association between daily stress and grandiosity/entitlement. The AMPD components were primarily related to average levels of daily functioning. Antagonism and disinhibition were related to higher negative affect and daily expressions of psychopathology, while the psychopathy specifier was related to higher positive affect and less daily expressions of psychopathology. Replicating and extending prior work, these findings suggest that differences in average daily functioning associated with psychopathic traits are not explained by heightened or blunted reactivity to interpersonal stress, and raise questions about the role of the psychopathy specifier in the AMPD.

An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life

Psychopathy is a multifaceted personality disorder characterized by traits related to disinhibition (e.g., lack of planning, sensation seeking), antagonism (e.g., callousness, exploitativeness), and boldness (e.g., social dominance, emotional resilience) and is of significant interest due to its robust relation to harmful antisocial behaviors (De Brito et al., 2021). Many conceptualizations of psychopathy have been developed, spawning debates about what traits should be considered essential to the construct (Patrick, 2022). These debates and corresponding research shaped how psychopathy was represented in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

Specifically, the Alternative Model of Personality Disorders (AMPD), introduced in Section III of the DSM-5, includes a psychopathy specifier that can be added to the diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder. Although earlier editions of the DSM (DSM-I; American Psychiatric Association, 1952) included closely related diagnoses (e.g., "sociopathic personality disturbance"; Horley, 2014), the AMPD represents the first formal representation of psychopathy informed by modern research on the disorder, marking a significant addition that reflected decades of effort documenting psychopathy's clinical importance (Drislane et al., 2019).

The AMPD has generated significant research attention (Al-dajani et al., 2016; Clark et al., 2022), including research comparing its assessment of antisocial personality disorder and psychopathy against categorical approaches and established psychopathy measures (Dunne, 2021; Wygant et al., 2016). However, as with psychopathy and antisocial personality disorder research more generally, little is known about how the AMPD conceptualization of psychopathy relates to interpersonal and affective outcomes in daily life. The present study aims to address this gap, and provide the first examination of the AMPD conceptualization of psychopathy in day-to-day life via ecological momentary assessment (EMA). We seek to replicate and extend An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life recent research on the socioaffective dynamics of psychopathy in daily life (Vize et al., 2026) using a larger sample and a wider array of momentary and daily outcomes. Specifically, we examine whether and when psychopathic traits enhance or dampen the effects of interpersonal stress on momentary negative affect, but also the effects of interpersonal stress on daily expressions of traits central to psychopathy (e.g., callousness, manipulativeness, impulsivity, hostile aggression).

Conceptualizations of Psychopathy

Modern research on psychopathy has been heavily influenced by Cleckley's (Cleckley, 1941 (Cleckley, /1988) ) seminal work, The Mask of Sanity, in which he developed sixteen criteria (e.g., lack of remorse, glibness, poor behavioral control) thought to characterize psychopathy, most of which map onto what modern researchers would label as antagonistic and disinhibitory traits.

The domains of antagonism and disinhibition are prominently featured across empirically based models of maladaptive personality (Krueger & Markon, 2014). Some of Cleckley's criteria (e.g., superficial charm, lack of nervousness) also tap interpersonal boldness and fearlessness and were thought to capture a "mask" of adaptive functioning that disguised underlying personality pathology (Patrick, 2022). Since Cleckley's initial work, empirical research on psychopathy has expanded significantly, and many empirically validated measures have been developed.

However, measures of psychopathy diverge in their coverage (or lack thereof) of traits tied to interpersonal boldness and fearlessness.

Some conceptualizations consider these traits essential to the construct, such as the influential Triarchic Model, which was developed to reconcile various competing notions of psychopathy (Patrick et al., 2009). This model conceptualizes psychopathy using three primary domains: disinhibition, meanness, and boldness and is commonly assessed using the Triarchic An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life Psychopathy Measure (TriPM; (Patrick, 2010) or one of its derivatives (e.g., (Ruchensky et al., 2018). Other popular measures of psychopathy that provide explicit coverage of boldness include the Psychopathic Personality Inventory (PPI; Lilienfeld & Andrews, 1996), which includes two higher-order factors, Fearless Dominance and Impulsive Antisociality, alongside a more specific Coldheartedness facet (Benning et al., 2003); the Fearless Dominance factor is largely synonymous with boldness (Sleep et al., 2019), whereas Impulsivity Antisociality is a blend of antagonistic and disinhibitory content. In contrast, the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R; Hare, 2003), and its derivatives like the Self-report Psychopathy Scale (SRP; (Paulhus et al., 2006), are much more limited in their coverage of boldness. The PCL-R, for example, assesses boldness-related content with only two of its twenty items (glibness/superficial charm and grandiose self-worth). Other measures exclude boldness-related content entirely, such as the Levenson Self-Report Psychopathy scale (LSRP; Levenson et al., 1995). In summary, while there is strong agreement across all measures of psychopathy that antagonistic and disinhibitory traits are central, there is less consensus regarding the importance of boldness, which in turn has led to diverging empirical findings on psychopathy depending on which instrument is used to assess the construct.

Although boldness has been linked to specific externalizing behaviors (Lilienfeld et al., 2012), and clinicians often view it as an important feature of psychopathy (Berg et al., 2017), most empirical evidence has shown that boldness is primarily related to adaptive functioning. A meta-analysis of the Triarchic Model (Sleep et al., 2019) found that boldness showed null to minor relations with maladaptive constructs such as Machiavellianism (r = .01) and reactive aggression (r = .05), was only weakly related to Disinhibition (r = .16) and Meanness (r = -.05), and was instead associated higher extraversion (r = .58), and lower neuroticism (r = -.60).

Psychopathy in the Alternative Model of Personality Disorders

Although the Section II conceptualization of personality disorders in the DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) remained unchanged relative to DSM-IV, the AMPD was introduced in Section III of DSM-5 due in part to the well-known, longstanding issues with the categorical diagnostic approach to personality disorders (Widiger & Trull, 2007). The AMPD takes a dimensional approach to assessing pathological traits, in contrast to the categorical approach of Section II. In the AMPD trait model (Criterion B), antisocial personality disorder is characterized by seven facets-three facets from the personality domain of Disinhibition (Risk taking, Impulsivity, Irresponsibility) and four facets from Antagonism (Manipulativeness, Callousness, Deceitfulness, Hostility 1 ). Importantly, psychopathy is treated as a variant of antisocial personality disorder in the AMPD and represented by a psychopathy specifier, defined by the facets of (low) Anxiousness, (low) Withdrawal, and Attention Seeking, that can be added to a diagnosis of antisocial personality disorder. The psychopathy specifier describes, "A distinct variant often termed psychopathy (or "primary" psychopathy) … marked by a lack of anxiety or fear and by a bold interpersonal style that may mask maladaptive behaviors (e.g., fraudulence)" (American Psychiatric Association, 2013, p. 765).

1 Hostility is also cross-listed in the Negative Affectivity domain in the AMPD, given its interstitial nature: it reflects both affective and interpersonal content.

Since its introduction, the AMPD conceptualization of psychopathy has been examined in numerous studies. This work has shown that the AMPD antisocial personality disorder trait profile converges with measures of psychopathy more strongly than the traditional Section II diagnosis (Few et al., 2015; McCabe et al., 2023; Wygant et al., 2016). Although other work has shown that the psychopathy specifier converges with boldness and fearless dominance (Crego & Widiger, 2014; Miller et al., 2018) it appears to add little incremental validity outside of accounting for variance in boldness-related external criteria (Few et al., 2015; Wygant et al., 2016). Given its close ties to boldness and fearless dominance, it is unsurprising that the psychopathy specifier is largely unrelated or negatively related to other components of psychopathy (e.g., antagonism, disinhibition) and has shown negative or null correlations with maladaptive criteria such as aggression and negative affect (Miller et al., 2018). Overall, consistent with prior work on boldness, cross-sectional research has shown that the specifier is primarily associated with adaptive functioning, whereas the AMPD antisocial personality disorder domains of antagonism and disinhibition are tied to maladaptive functioning and externalizing behavior. However, this research has relied entirely on cross-sectional data, and no study has examined how the AMPD model of psychopathy functions in daily life. Given the significance of psychopathy's formal inclusion in the DSM, this is an important gap. EMA designs are uniquely suited to capturing personality pathology as it unfolds in daily life and can clarify the specific contexts in which the AMPD traits of antisocial personality disorder and psychopathy lead to maladaptive, or adaptive, outcomes.

Studying AMPD Psychopathy and Antisocial Personality Disorder in Daily Life

Although the number of EMA studies focused on maladaptive personality has grown rapidly over the past decade (Kaurin et al., 2023), psychopathy researchers have only just begun An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life to leverage the strengths of these designs. This methodological gap in the research literature is important to address. The problems associated with psychopathy, like those of other personality disorders, occur most often in the social situations that unfold within the ebb and flow of daily life (Hopwood, 2018). Psychopathic traits are therefore likely to be most consequential in specific interpersonal contexts, particularly stressful or conflictual ones, and EMA designs can capture these events as they occur. Moreover, research has consistently demonstrated that maladaptive traits, including those related to psychopathy (e.g., antagonism), vary in their expression at the momentary and daily level (Wright & Simms, 2016; Zimmermann et al., 2019).

EMA designs thus allow psychopathic traits to be studied in two complementary ways: as stable dispositions that moderate how individuals respond to momentary events, and as dynamic states that fluctuate from day to day.

Regarding the AMPD model of psychopathy, EMA designs can strengthen our understanding of how each individual component (antagonism, disinhibition, and the psychopathy specifier) and their combination relate to momentary outcomes. Earlier work using momentary assessments has supported the idea that features of antagonism are related to higher average negative affect in daily life, as well as more consistent interpersonal coldness across interpersonal contexts (Vize et al., 2022). Similarly, across three EMA samples, trait disinhibition was consistently associated with higher negative affect in daily life, above and beyond trait antagonism (Mostajabi & Wright, 2025). Disinhibition was also associated with less warmth during social interactions, but this effect was largely accounted for by antagonism, and disinhibition showed no consistent associations with positive affect or dominance. Yet key questions that EMA designs are uniquely positioned to address are when and how the psychopathic traits influence maladaptive and adaptive functioning (e.g., within a stressful An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life interpersonal situation vs. in other contexts). To date, very few studies have addressed these questions.

A recent exception is Vize et al. (2026), who examined socioaffective dynamics associated with psychopathic traits (assessed with PPI-based self-report measures) in two adult samples. They examined both person-level outcomes (e.g., average hostile, sad, and positive affect; average ratings of negative and positive interactions) and within-person processes (e.g., changes in affect following negative interactions), including whether psychopathic traits moderated these processes. Evidence linking psychopathic traits to average outcomes was mixed.

Impulsive Antisociality was the only trait with a replicable association, relating to higher average hostile affect in both samples. Other effects emerged in only one sample: Impulsive Antisociality related to higher sad affect and more negative interactions, whereas Fearless Dominance related to lower hostile and sad affect. Notably, PPI total scores were unrelated to all average outcomes in both samples, likely because the opposing effects of Impulsive Antisociality and Fearless Dominance offset one another. Further, despite robust within-person effects (e.g., notable increases in hostile affect following negative interactions), psychopathic traits rarely moderated these processes. Although these initial results provided valuable data on the momentary relations of traits related to psychopathy, Vize and colleagues (2026) pointed to several important directions for future work.

First, the sample sizes (Ns of 142 and 159) provided adequate power only for moderate or large effects. These analyses may therefore have missed the smaller effects expected when relating traits derived from self-report questionnaires to EMA-based outcomes. Second, the negative interaction measures were broad in scope and varied across samples, and psychopathic traits may exert their strongest influence in more specific interpersonal contexts (e.g., An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life interpersonally stressful situations). Third, the divergent effects of Fearless Dominance and Impulsive Antisociality complicate interpretation of total scores and highlight the value of examining the total scores alongside narrower, more homogeneous trait dimensions.

The Current Study

The present study sought to address these limitations while being the first study to examine the AMPD model of psychopathy in daily life. Using a larger sample (N = 296) and AMPD-aligned measures of antagonism, disinhibition, and the psychopathy specifier, we focus specifically on interpersonally stressful and unpleasant experiences, contexts in which psychopathic traits are theorized to be particularly consequential. First, aiming to replicate findings from Vize and colleagues (2026), we tested whether AMPD psychopathic traits enhance or dampen within-person links between interpersonal stressful or unpleasant experiences and momentary negative affect, as well as the within-person links between positive interpersonal interactions and positive affect. Second, we extended past work by examining daily expressions of traits relevant to psychopathy (e.g., callousness, manipulativeness, hostile aggression) assessed with the Inventory of Daily Expressions of Psychopathology (IDEP; Wright et al., 2025). Building on work linking daily stress to daily psychopathology domains (Ringwald et al., 2025), we test whether baseline AMPD psychopathic traits amplify or dampen these daily expressions on days marked by greater interpersonal stress.

Participants completed a baseline in-person lab session and an ambulatory assessment protocol. Participants reported the degree to which they were experiencing negative affect during each random survey. Participants were asked "How [affective adjective] do you feel right now?" and used a 101-point sliding scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 100 (extremely) to report the intensity of feelings of shame, nervousness, sadness, anger, guilt, and frustration. Positive affect items were confident, content, and excited.

We examined three specific forms of negative affect: shame and guilt as a two-item composite, anger and frustration as a two-item composite, and nervous as a single item. The three positive affect items were used to create a three-item composite of positive affect.

Momentary Interpersonal Events

After participants answered items assessing current positive and negative affect and impulsivity, they were asked if anything stressful had occurred since the last assessment.

Participants were instructed to consider whether they themselves felt stressed when the event occurred, not whether they or another person would typically feel stressed in the same situation.

If a stressful event occurred, participants answered additional questions about the event, including what type of event they encountered. The options were "argument/disagreement/conflict", "social disappointment/let down", "work/school related event", "home related event", "health issue or accident", "event that happened to others", and An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life "other stressor." Participants were also asked to rate how stressful the situation was, using a 0 (not at all) to 100 (extremely) sliding scale.

Participants who indicated that they had not experienced a stressful event since the last assessment were asked if any of the following had occurred since the last assessment: "argument, disagreement, conflict", "social disappointment/let down", "difficulties involving work or school", "difficulties at home", "health issue or accident", "negative event that happened to others", or "other unpleasant event." These selections were written to closely mirror the content of the stressful events but were presented in this manner to differentiate situation characteristics from the perception of stress. If participants indicated an unpleasant event occurred, they continued to answer questions about the event, including how unpleasant the event was using a 0 (not at all) to 100 (extremely) sliding scale. If participants did not indicate either a stressful or unpleasant event occurring since the last assessment, participants were asked anything pleasant or positive had occurred since the previous assessment and could similarly select from a range of categories of pleasant events.

Our analyses focused on stressful/unpleasant events that participants classified as being an "argument/disagreement/conflict" or "social disappointment/let down" given their explicit interpersonal nature (13 different types in total). Similarly, we focused on positive events that were interpersonal in nature (10 different types in total). The specific types of interpersonal events participants could choose from are listed in the supplement (Table S1).

In total, participants endorsed 4,616 momentary stressful or unpleasant events over the course of the study. We specifically focused on stressful or unpleasant interpersonal events (13 different kinds in total) given the importance of interpersonal difficulties to personality pathology in general, and psychopathy in particular (e.g., Vize et al., 2026). A total of 514 stressful or unpleasant interpersonal events were reported across the duration of the study.

Similarly, we included positive interpersonal events in our analyses (10 different kinds in total). There were 2,509 positive interpersonal events endorsed across the duration of the study. Thus, even among a sample of adults enriched for interpersonal difficulties, stressful or unpleasant interpersonal events occurred much more infrequently than positive interpersonal events.

Analysis Plan

Our primary analyses used multilevel structural equation models (MSEMs) to estimate the relations between baseline predictors (the CAT-PD-derived AMPD psychopathy domains of antagonism, disinhibition, and the psychopathy specifier, as well as a psychopathy total score) and our selected outcome measures. Like typical multilevel modeling approaches, MSEM partitions the overall variability in individuals' intensive longitudinal data into between-and within-person components. MSEM offers additional advantages, including latent decomposition of between-and within-person variance in repeated assessment data (Sadikaj et al., 2021).

Between-person variance reflects individual differences in average responses and captures traitlike content. Within-person variance reflects deviations from an individual's own average level from one occasion to the next. Thus, within-person associations reflect how occasion-level deviations relate to one another. All within-person regression paths (e.g., the relation between stressful interpersonal events and momentary anger/frustration) were estimated as random slopes, which allow individuals to differ in the strength of their within-person associations.

Including random slopes allowed us test whether the strength of any within-person associations varied as a function of individual differences in psychopathic traits (i.e., a cross-level interaction). We tested this by regressing each random slope on the relevant between-person predictor. All MSEMs were estimated in Mplus Version 8.10 (Muthén & Muthén, 2025) using Bayesian estimation with default, diffuse priors for all model parameters. Each model was first estimated using the default convergence criterion (potential scale reduction values below 1.10).

Once convergence was established, each model was re-estimated with the number of MCMC iterations fixed at 10,000.

MSEMs were estimated separately for each psychopathy predictor (antagonism, disinhibition, psychopathy specifier, and total score) and each outcome. Momentary models examined the within-person relation between interpersonal stressful/unpleasant events (coded 0/1 at each prompt) and three forms of negative affect: anger/frustration, shame/guilt, and nervousness (4 predictors x 3 outcomes = 12 models). Within-person covariates included in all models were time of day, weekday/weekend (0/1), and time since the interpersonal event. Discussion

The present study was the first to examine the AMPD model of psychopathy in daily life, while seeking to replicate and extend recent findings on the socioaffective dynamics of psychopathy (Vize et al., 2026). Consistent with that work, stressful and unpleasant interpersonal situations were robustly linked to within-person increases in negative affect, particularly anger/frustration, and at the daily level, to increases in the expression of psychopathic traits (e.g., anger, callousness, hostile aggression). However, with some exceptions, psychopathic traits assessed at baseline rarely moderated these within-person processes. The three components of daily variables-antagonism and disinhibition were generally related to maladaptive functioning and the psychopathy specifier was generally related to adaptive functioning. These divergent effects meant that AMPD psychopathy total scores obscured meaningful differences among components. Together, these findings clarify how the components of AMPD psychopathy relate to everyday functioning and highlight important opportunities for future EMA work focused on psychopathic traits.

Psychopathic Traits and Reactions to Interpersonal Events

At the within-person level, there was consistent evidence that stressful and unpleasant interpersonal events led to subsequent increases in all forms of negative affect, while positive interpersonal events were linked to subsequent increases in positive affect. In these momentary analyses, there was no evidence that AMPD psychopathic traits enhanced or dampened within-person reactivity to stressful or unpleasant interpersonal events. This general scarcity of cross-level moderation effects for such events is consistent with Vize et al. (2026), despite using a larger sample, more homogenous scales to tap the antagonistic, disinhibitory, and boldness-related features of psychopathy, and assessments focused on specific stressful or unpleasant interpersonal events. In the general personality processes work, researchers have historically distinguished personality differences in exposure to stressful events and reactivity to those events once they occur (e.g. Bolger & Zuckerman, 1995). Theoretical accounts of psychopathy imply differential affective reactivity in both directions, such as dampened anxious or guilt-related responses to interpersonal stress among individuals high in boldness or antagonism (Blair, 2005), and heightened anger among individuals high in antagonism or disinhibition (e.g., Kosson et al., 2020). Yet there was scant evidence that psychopathic traits moderated affective reactivity to negative interpersonal events. Instead, there was some evidence that psychopathic traits were tied to exposure to such events. Disinhibition was associated with more frequent stressful and unpleasant interpersonal events, but the psychopathy specifier with fewer events.

In contrast, there was evidence of cross-level moderation involving positive interpersonal events, such that higher antagonism and disinhibition (and also the total AMPD psychopathy score) were related to significantly greater positive affect following positive interpersonal interactions. Although researchers have taken a similar approach in thinking about reactions to positive events, they have instead framed these reactions as responsivity to positive events (Zautra et al., 2005). In our results, antagonism and disinhibition were associated with greater responsiveness to positive interpersonal events. At the same time, disinhibition was associated with lower average positive affect across the EMA protocol, and antagonism showed a negative association of similar magnitude, although its credible interval included zero. This suggests that individuals higher in these traits may benefit more from positive interactions when they occur, despite experiencing lower positive affect on average.

This pattern parallels findings for extraversion, where less extraverted individuals showed steeper increases in daily enjoyment following positive events (Zautra et al., 2005). The finding is also somewhat surprising, because these domains, particularly antagonism, are defined in part by interpersonal styles that undermine the conditions in which positive interactions can occur (Wright et al., 2012). However, there was no evidence that any of the AMPD psychopathy domains were related to fewer positive interpersonal interactions, on average (see Supplementary Table S4).

Thus, whether this heightened responsiveness reflects genuine sensitivity to social reward or greater room for improvement from a lower baseline for positive affect is an important question for future work.

Overall, the present results suggest that psychopathy is not characterized by a general pattern of blunted or heightened affective reactivity to interpersonal stress in daily life. In turn, higher averages of negative affect associated with antagonism and disinhibition could not be explained by heightened reactivity to the interpersonal events assessed here. Nonetheless, these results cannot rule out more circumscribed deficits that arise in social contexts, or reactivity to events that may have not been assessed during the EMA protocol. For example, the assessment approach used here could not identify interpersonal events where participants harmed another individual and thus had an opportunity to express guilt for their own behavior. Similarly, the more notable emotional deficits linked to psychopathy involve responses to others' distress (Blair, 2005), whereas the stressors assessed here were largely self-referent (e.g., conflicts, criticism, being let down).

AMPD Psychopathy and Daily Expressions of Psychopathology

Importantly, the momentary findings concern affective responses to interpersonal events, even though psychopathic traits are theoretically tied to both affective and behavioral responses to interpersonal situations. Although some daily-level results also reflect affective responses to stress (e.g., IDEP-Anger), others capture behavioral expressions of psychopathy (e.g., IDEP-Hostile Aggression) and provide distinct insights into daily-level dynamics. The expression of psychopathy-related traits was consistently tied to days with more stressful or unpleasant interpersonal events than usual, with the exceptions of Manipulativeness, Norm Violation, and Risk-taking. Similar to results from the momentary data, evidence of cross-level moderation was limited at the daily level.

Thus, at both the momentary and daily level, the magnitude of increases in negative affect and daily expressions of psychopathology in the context of interpersonal stress was not reliably associated with antagonism or disinhibition.

Instead, antagonism and disinhibition were associated with higher average levels of daily expressions of psychopathology relevant to psychopathy, providing further validity for the AMPD conceptualization of psychopathy as it pertains to antagonism and disinhibition.

The exceptions for cross-level moderation effects in the daily data were found for the psychopathy specifier. Specifically, the psychopathy specifier amplified the within-person association between daily interpersonal stressors and Grandiosity/Entitlement, an effect that was consistent whether daily stress was indexed by the number of stressors or by daily mean stress ratings. 6 This moderation effect was modest in magnitude but offers some evidence that the generally adaptive profile of the psychopathy specifier may be context dependent. Individuals high in boldness-related traits may respond to interpersonal stress with greater selfaggrandizement, at least when assessing these events at the daily level. However, outside this exception, the psychopathy specifier demonstrated strong relations with adaptive outcomes (i.e., positive affect) and consistent negative relations with maladaptive outcomes. Most notably, higher scores on the psychopathy specifier were related to significantly lower person-means for most IDEP facets and did not demonstrate positive associations with specific IDEP facets thought to be relevant to the construct (e.g., Risk-taking). These divergent pattern of associations with outcomes derived from daily life raise important questions for the AMPD model of psychopathy, given that the psychopathy specifier is currently designated as an add-on element to the AMPD antisocial personality disorder diagnosis.

Implications for the AMPD Model of Psychopathy 6

In the secondary models focused on mean stress ratings, the psychopathy specifier also amplified the withinperson association between daily stress and Risk-taking. On the other hand, disinhibition dampened the same withinperson effect. Because these effects did not emerge in the primary models using stressor counts (Supplementary Table S5), and daily stress was not associated with Risk-taking on average, they should be interpreted with these caveats in mind.

Across momentary and daily analyses, the three components of the AMPD psychopathy model showed distinct patterns of associations. These results, focused on outcomes derived from repeated momentary and daily assessments, are consistent with findings from other study designs and assessment methods (Miller & Lynam, 2012; Blasco-Belled et al., 2024 , Sleep et al., 2019).

In turn, these findings have important consequences for how researchers and clinicians can effectively use a total AMPD score. Because boldness-related traits are represented by the psychopathy specifier, the AMPD total score primarily reflects antagonism and disinhibition, as these two domains contribute most of its content. Specifically, antagonism and disinhibition contribute seven facets compared to the three facets for the psychopathy specifier, although in the present study six facets from the CAT-PD were used to represent antagonism and disinhibition. In the AMPD model of psychopathy, the psychopathy specifier is applied only in the presence of antisocial personality disorder. Others have noted that this creates a conceptual problem (Miller et al., 2018). Because the specifier traits have thus far been shown to relate to adaptive functioning, including in the present study focused on outcomes from daily life, the specifier functions less as a marker of psychopathy and more as a marker of better psychological adjustment among individuals with antisocial personality disorder. This runs counter to the dominant view developed over the past decades that psychopathy is a more severe and impairing condition than antisocial personality disorder alone (De Brito et al., 2021; Hare, 1996).

Moreover, as others have noted, it is unusual for diagnostic criteria to include features that would reduce or offset impairment (Miller et al., 2018). The present results support these concerns surrounding the psychopathy specifier in the AMPD.

One way to reconcile these issues is to propose that the psychopathy specifier alters how antagonism and disinhibition are expressed, rather than their combination being additive in An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life nature. Researchers have proposed curvilinear relations (e.g., Blonigen, 2013) or trait-by-trait interactions (e.g., Lilienfeld et al., 2019), in which boldness-related traits become tied to maladaptive functioning in the presence of high scores on other components of psychopathy.

However, there is limited evidence for these types of effects (e.g., Benning & Smith, 2019; Kennealy et al., 2010; Sharpe et al., 2021; Weiss et al., 2019). When testing these proposals using the AMPD model of psychopathy, Miller and colleagues (2018) did not find evidence that the psychopathy specifier strengthened associations between antisocial personality disorder traits and dysfunction. In the one significant interaction, antisocial personality disorder traits were less strongly related to self-reported impairment at higher levels of the specifier.

Another way to reconcile the apparent adaptivity of the psychopathy specifier is that it may contribute to positive first impressions and temporarily obscure an individual's disinhibited and antagonistic interpersonal style. Work on narcissism provides some support for this hypothesis, as narcissistic individuals are well-liked at first (Back et al., 2010), but become less popular over repeated interactions as their antagonistic behaviors become more apparent (Leckelt et al., 2015). Examining whether the psychopathy specifier relates to favorable initial impressions that diminish over repeated interactions, and whether these effects depend on levels of antagonism or disinhibition, would be a valuable next step for future EMA and laboratory research. However, a capacity to make favorable first impressions still reflects better interpersonal functioning, at least in the short term, which remains at odds with the view of psychopathy as a more severe condition than antisocial personality disorder alone (Miller et al., 2018). Reconciling these issues would require evidence that this initial advantage translates into greater harm to others, for example by enabling more effective exploitation of new targets before An Examination of the DSM-5 Alternative Model of Psychopathy in Daily Life antagonistic behavior becomes apparent, and that this outcome is not achieved for individuals with elevations on antisocial personality disorder traits alone.

Limitations and Future Directions

The present study provided the first exploration of the AMPD model of psychopathy and its relations to interpersonal stress and its consequences in daily life. Although the results meaningfully extend past EMA work focused on psychopathy, there are important limitations of the present study that can be addressed in future research. First, although we focused specifically on interpersonal stressful or unpleasant events, our assessment approach still combined different kinds of interpersonal events (argument/conflict vs. a promise not being kept) into a single broad category. This may have obscured situations in which psychopathic traits did lead to greater reactivity in certain situations (e.g., provocation, reacting to others' distress). Relatedly, because daily stressors and IDEP facets covered the same period, the daily-level models cannot disentangle whether interpersonal stressors preceded or followed the expression of psychopathic traits. Event-contingent designs targeting specific interpersonal contexts, and momentary assessments of trait expression, would be an important improvement on the current design.

Another important limitation that has been noted in other research on the AMPD framework for psychopathy is that it is somewhat constrained in its assessment of the traits tied to psychopathy, as it does not include traits like grandiosity and (low) submissiveness, for example. Past work has shown that the AMPD model of psychopathy can be improved, including the psychopathy specifier, by expanding its coverage of psychopathy related traits. Thus, future EMA work can examine the utility of the current AMPD model of psychopathy compared to other approaches that expand the current AMPD model.

Last, we combined facet scales to create broader domain scores, consistent with how the AMPD operationalizes psychopathy (e.g., Wygant et al., 2020). However, more nuanced effects may be detectable only when examining specific facets individually. Divergent associations within antagonism and disinhibition facets have been found in previous EMA research (Vize et al., 2022; Mostajabi & Wright, 2025). Our daily results also point to heterogeneity across facets in the expression of psychopathy in response to stress. Specifically, increases in daily stress was related to greater expression of some IDEP facets but not others. These facet-level patterns are obscured when examining domain level relations, which has been the approach in prior work examining daily stress and the IDEP in the present sample (Ringwald et al., 2025). Future EMA research on psychopathy would benefit from testing whether facet-level predictors and outcomes provide more useful information than broad domains as it pertains to the representation of psychopathy in the AMPD.
