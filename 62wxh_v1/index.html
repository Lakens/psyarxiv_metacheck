Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Full text

Abstract

Eye-tracking has become central to process-tracing research in judgment and decision-making, yet access to this paradigm remains constrained by the cost and logistical demands of researchgrade infrared systems. We present CaliGaze, an open-source webcam eye-tracking framework that uses geometric iris landmark estimation, individually calibrated piecewise spatial mapping, and a recorded single-eye eyelid-opening validity flag to support ROI-level gaze classification in binary choice experiments. In a within-subject validation study (N = 51), CaliGaze was evaluated against a Tobii Pro Spark infrared eye tracker while participants completed a risky binary choice task with simultaneous dual-system recording. CaliGaze showed strong cross-device agreement in ROI-level dwell proportions and reproduced the core fixation-choice signatures of the task.

Both gaze streams consistently supported gaze-dependent over gaze-independent evidence accumulation, and attentional-weighting estimates showed strong cross-device correspondence in between-participant rank ordering. These results suggest that CaliGaze can support ROI-level process tracing and gaze-dependent computational modeling in binary choice tasks, offering a scalable and accessible alternative for decision researchers, with particular potential for largescale, remote, or resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Eye-tracking technology has emerged as a fundamental tool in the behavioral sciences, providing an empirical window into the latent cognitive processes that govern decision behavior and information acquisition (Orquin & Mueller Loose, 2013; Wedel & Pieters, 2008). In the fields of behavioral economics and decision neuroscience, the measurement of visual fixation dynamics has evolved from a descriptive mapping to a mechanistic explanatory variable, a transition driven primarily by the development of the attentional Drift Diffusion Model (aDDM; Krajbich et al., 2010). The aDDM formally specifies that during value-based binary choice, the momentary rate of evidence accumulation is systematically amplified by the fixated option, thereby providing a formal process-level account of the robust gaze-bias effect observed across diverse experimental contexts (Cavanagh et al., 2014; Krajbich & Rangel, 2011). This mechanism suggests that preferences are jointly determined by the relative subjective values of the alternatives and the high-frequency transitions of attentional allocation (Krajbich et al., 2012; Smith & Krajbich, 2018). Extensive empirical work has subsequently validated this framework across a wide spectrum of domains, including consumer choice, effort-based decision-making, intertemporal discounting, as well as social decision-making, establishing the aDDM as a standard methodological lens for decomposing the cognitive architecture of preference formation (Fisher, 2017; Kang & Fisher, 2026; Teoh et al., 2020; Westbrook et al., 2020).

Despite the inferential power of gaze-choice modeling, the transition of this paradigm from controlled laboratory settings to broader scientific applications remains hindered by barriers operating at two distinct levels. (1) At the infrastructural level, research-grade remote infrared eye trackers generally require substantial capital investment, controlled acquisition conditions, and trained personnel (Holmqvist et al., 2011). These requirements can constrain deployment in To address these requirements, the present study introduces CaliGaze, an algorithmic framework tailored for binary choice experiments. CaliGaze combines four implementation components: (1) a within-eye-width-normalized iris-position ratio (ρ) that reduces sensitivity to uniform changes in facial scale (Lu et al., 2014); (2) median-based piecewise mapping for participant-specific task-specific calibration (Guestrin & Eizenman, 2006); (3) a recorded singleeye eyelid-opening validity flag for identifying samples potentially affected by eyelid closure (Cech & Soukupova, 2016); and (4) sequential webcam acquisition and processing within the Pygame stimulus loop, with host-clock timestamps used for post hoc alignment with the asynchronous Tobii callback. Because CaliGaze uses standard RGB video and anatomical landmark geometry rather than infrared corneal reflections, it is not affected by cornealreflection-specific mechanisms in the same way as infrared systems. This difference may reduce some failure modes, but it does not eliminate participant-or condition-specific data-quality problems: spectacle glare, optical occlusion, illumination, camera placement, and landmark loss may still increase calibration error or missing data. Eligibility and data-quality decisions should therefore be based on observed calibration and tracking performance rather than categorical assumptions about participant groups. By integrating these features, CaliGaze provides an accessible framework, validated here against a Tobii Pro Spark in a value-based binary choice task, for ROI-level process tracing and gaze-informed computational modeling.

Open Science Practices

All study materials are available on GitHub (https://github.com/decisionneuroscience/gaze-engine-core). The study's hypotheses, design, and analysis plan were preregistered prior to data collection. Eye-Tracking System

Dual-System Configuration

To evaluate low-cost gaze tracking, we implemented a dual-system recording setup in which a Tobii Pro Spark served as the high-precision hardware reference and CaliGaze served as the webcam-based validation system. Both systems recorded during the same decision task, enabling trial-by-trial comparisons. They were not hardware-synchronized: CaliGaze was sampled sequentially within the Pygame loop, whereas Tobii supplied samples asynchronously through its SDK callback. Trial phase labels and host-clock timestamps supported post hoc alignment within the unified stimulus-presentation program.

Both acquisition streams and the behavioral log recorded host time using Python time.time(). For CaliGaze, the host timestamp was written after camera capture, Face Mesh inference, smoothing, screen mapping, and ROI assignment; the Tobii callback stored a host time.time() value together with the device and Tobii system timestamps. Behavioral variables such as show_start and show_end also used time.time(). Because show_start was recorded before the decision display was drawn and flipped, and record_mp_frame() was called before pygame.display.flip(), these values are software event or logging times rather than direct measurements of physical display onset or camera exposure. They support post hoc trial-and phase-level alignment but not sub-frame synchronization.

The archived code did not establish a strict upper bound on cross-stream temporal misalignment. Residual differences could include camera exposure and readout, Face Mesh processing, Pygame-loop and display-update latency, and Tobii callback latency. The observed median intersample intervals were 32.0 ms for CaliGaze and 16.7 ms for Tobii. The present analyses aggregated gaze at the dwell and trial levels and therefore did not require claims of subframe temporal precision.

Primary System: Tobii Pro Spark

Gaze data were recorded using a Tobii Pro Spark remote infrared eye tracker (Tobii Technology AB, Stockholm, Sweden) sampling at 60 Hz. The device was attached to the lower bezel of a 16.1-inch laptop screen (1920 × 1080 resolution). Participants were seated at a distance of approximately 65 cm from the screen. Under controlled laboratory lighting conditions, the system has a typical manufacturer-reported spatial accuracy of 0.4°-0.6°. Each 147-trial context began with a nine-point calibration, and calibration was repeated after each 49-trial segment. The archived task code called the Tobii SDK compute_and_apply() method and printed the returned status, but it did not calculate a mean angular error or enforce a 1.0° acceptance threshold. All stimulus presentation and real-time integration of Tobii data were controlled using Pygame within Python (https://www.pygame.org/).

The CaliGaze Framework

Concurrent with the primary eye-tracking system, an EMEET S600 external webcam (Sony 1/2.8-inch CMOS sensor, 4K UHD native resolution; phase-detection autofocus) supplied live frames to the CaliGaze estimation pipeline. The validation setup specified 1280 × 720 pixels and a nominal acquisition rate of 40 Hz. This was an experimental configuration rather than a fixed requirement of the CaliGaze method, which can be used with nominal webcam rates of approximately 30-60 Hz. The archived OpenCV task explicitly requested the frame size; consequently, the realized processed-sample interval depended on the camera/driver and the time required by the sequential capture and processing loop and should be characterized from the recorded timestamps. The task logged derived gaze rows rather than writing a raw video recording. CaliGaze is a modular Python 3.8+ framework built on MediaPipe Face Mesh v0.10.21 with refine_landmarks ¿ True, yielding 478 face and iris landmarks per detected face (Lugaresi et al., 2019). As illustrated in Fig. 2, the framework covers system initialization, calibration, experimental integration, real-time gaze logging, ROI tagging, and data export. For the experimental investigation, we used nine calibration targets. At each target, the participant fixated the displayed dot and pressed the space bar; the program then collected 40 frames for which the iris-position ratio was available and stored their median ρ value. The collection loop did not require Valid = 1, did not require 40 consecutive valid frames, and did not apply a dispersion criterion. The center-target median anchored the midpoint of the piecewise mapping, and the minimum and maximum target medians defined the expanded calibrated range.

The archived task code did not implement a separate CaliGaze validation grid, calculate a pixel or angular calibration error, impose an 80-pixel or 1° threshold, or automatically repeat either CaliGaze or Tobii calibration on the basis of such an error.

Experimental Procedure Apparatus and Task Design

The experimental procedure was programmed and administered using the Pygame library.

Participants performed a computerized binary choice task designed to assess risk-taking behavior across 294 trials, divided into two conditions based on reward magnitude (0, 40 vs. 20, 60). On each trial, participants were presented with two simultaneously displayed options: a risky option and a safe option. To minimize potential spatial-response biases, the left/right positions of the risky and safe options were independently randomized on each trial, so that fixations toward a given location reflected attention to that option's value rather than a fixed spatial or motorresponse tendency.

The risky option offered a predetermined probability ( p) of obtaining a higher monetary reward ( x 1 ) and a complementary probability (1 -p) of a lower reward ( x 2 ), such that its expected value was defined as EV risk = p ⋅ x 1 +(1 -p )⋅ x 2 . In contrast, the safe option provided a guaranteed monetary amount (c) with 100% certainty (EV safe = c). By systematically manipulating the parameters p , x 1 , x 2 and c, the task generated a broad distribution of expected-value differences ( Δ EV=EV risk -EV safe ). This parametric design was specifically intended to introduce a range of "easy" trials (large Δ EV) and "difficult" trials (Δ EV near zero), allowing for a robust estimation of individual-specific psychometric choice curves.

To ensure that decisions reflected genuine subjective preferences, participants were explicitly instructed that there were no objectively "correct" responses. The task was also incentive-compatible: at the conclusion of the experiment, one trial was randomly selected and paid out according to the participant's actual choice, with the resulting earnings added to a fixed participation payment. This procedure was intended to maintain task engagement and encourage truthful valuation throughout the experiment. The complete instruction materials are provided in Supplementary Appendix S8.

Experimental Manipulations and Controls

To examine whether absolute stake magnitude shapes both risk preference and visual attention, the experiment employed a within-subjects design featuring two distinct reward-magnitude conditions administered within a single session: Across both conditions, risky gambles were constructed using seven probability levels ( p ∈ {5 % , 10 % , 25 % , 50 % , 75 % , 90 % , 95 % }), while safe-option values varied in fixed 2 RMB increments across the corresponding payoff range. The two conditions were matched on both the distributions of expected value differences ( Δ EV ) and the proportion of trials in which the risky versus safe option had the objectively higher expected value. This matching procedure ensured that any observed differences in choice behavior, gaze allocation, or computational-model parameters could be attributed specifically to changes in absolute stake magnitude rather than to confounding differences in task difficulty or choice structure.

Several procedural controls were additionally implemented to ensure data quality and internal validity. First, block order was counterbalanced across participants (i.e., half completed the Low-Magnitude block first) to mitigate potential order, carryover, and fatigue effects.

Second, participants completed a mandatory rest break between blocks to reduce cognitive fatigue and maintain stable task engagement throughout the session.

Trial Sequence and Stimuli

The task was entirely self-paced, with no response time limit, ensuring that the full gaze sequence underlying each choice was captured without truncation by time pressure. As illustrated in Fig. 3, each trial followed a standardized temporal sequence consisting of fixation, decision, and response-confirmation phases.

At the beginning of each trial, a central fixation cross (" +¿ ") was displayed for a jittered duration of 800 -1000 ms to orient participant's gaze and establish a neutral attentional baseline.

Subsequently, the risky and safe options appeared simultaneously on the left and right sides of the screen (positions randomized).

Participants indicated their choice by pressing the " f " key (left-hand option) or the " j " key (right-hand option). Immediately following a response, a red border appeared around the selected option for a jittered interval of 600 -800 ms, providing explicit visual confirmation of the participant's choice before the onset of the subsequent trial. Monetary outcomes were not revealed during the task but deferred until the final payment phase, minimizing possible learning, wealth effects, or sequential dependencies across trials.

Incentive Structure

The experiment employed an incentive-compatible payment scheme to ensure that participants' choices reflected genuine risk preferences rather than satisficing or random responding. All participants received a fixed attendance fee of 50 RMB regardless of their choices. In addition, a performance-contingent bonus was determined using the Random Lottery Incentive System (RLIS; Holt & Laury, 2002).

Upon completion of the 294 experimental trials, the computer randomly selected one trial to be played out for real payment. If the participant had chosen the safe option on the sampled trial, they received the guaranteed amount c ; if the participant had chosen the risky option, the actual outcome was determined by a computerized random draw based on the stated probabilities ( x 1 with probability p, and x 2 with probability 1 -p). The bonus payment ranged from 0 to 60 RMB, making total compensation meaningfully contingent on participants' revealed choices.

Total payment (the fixed fee plus the realized bonus) was transferred to the participant via WeChat Pay or Alipay immediately after the session.

Data Analysis

Gaze Data Processing

To transform raw gaze coordinates into discrete gaze events for computational decision modeling, the gaze time series from each system were processed according to their respective hardware and sampling capabilities. For the Tobii Pro Spark, raw 60 Hz gaze coordinates were processed offline using a velocity-threshold identification (I-VT) algorithm (Salvucci & Goldberg, 2000) under default configurations. Gaze samples with a point-to-point velocity below 30°/s were categorized as fixations, temporally adjacent fixations separated by saccadic intervals shorter than 75 ms were merged, and fixations shorter than 60 ms were excluded. For CaliGaze, up to K = 5 of the most recently appended iris-position-ratio values were averaged once online before piecewise screen mapping and ROI assignment. During the first K -1 appended samples, the mean used all values currently in the buffer; thereafter it used the most recent K values.

Values with Valid = 0 were still appended, whereas camera or landmark-detection failures produced no appended value. At the observed median intersample interval of 32.0 ms, a full K = 5 window spanned approximately 128 ms from its first to last sample and had a nominal group delay of approximately 64 ms. No second moving-average stage was applied. Because the lower and variable sampling rate of standard webcams precludes reliable velocity-based parsing, consecutive temporally adjacent CaliGaze samples assigned to the same target ROI were aggregated into ROI dwells (fixation-like ROI dwell episodes), rather than oculomotor fixations.

Processed gaze events were mapped to the left and right target regions. The two option rectangles each covered 30% of screen width and 40% of screen height. Tobii used its measured two-dimensional gaze point for point-in-rectangle classification. CaliGaze mapped only the xcoordinate and fixed y = H/2 for collision testing against the same rectangles; because H/2 lay within both rectangles, CaliGaze classification was effectively determined by their left-right boundaries. LEFT and RIGHT labels were translated post hoc into risky and safe identities using the randomized trial-specific layout for subsequent validation, behavioral, and aDDM analyses.

Subjective Value Computation

To quantify subjective value differences between the risky and safe options at the trial level, we estimated the subjective value of the risky option using each participant's certainty equivalent (CE). The CE represents the guaranteed amount at which a participant is indifferent between a risky gamble and a safe outcome and thus serves as a behavioral estimate of subjective value.

Although choices in the experiment were collected through randomized binary decisions rather than explicit price lists, the payoff structure allowed participants' revealed preferences to be reconstructed as an implicit price list for each probability level and magnitude condition.

Specifically, for each participant, magnitude condition, and probability level, the risky option was paired across trials with a series of safe amounts spanning the full payoff range in fixed increments. These safe amounts were then sorted in ascending order to reconstruct an ordered preference sequence.

Participants' choices across the reconstructed sequence were used to infer the CE. When a participant switched from choosing the risky option to choosing the safe option between two adjacent safe amounts, the CE was estimated as the midpoint between these values, following standard multiple price-list procedures (Holt & Laury, 2002; Andersen et al., 2006). In cases involving non-monotonic choice patterns with multiple switching points, an implied switching position was inferred based on the total number of risky choices, yielding a unique CE estimate.

Similar approaches to handling inconsistent switching behavior in multiple price-list paradigms have been discussed in prior work (e.g., Jacobson & Petrie, 2009; Andreoni & Sprenger, 2012; Engel & Kirchkamp, 2019; Chew et al., 2022).

Model-Independent Behavioral and Gaze Analyses

To establish an empirical foundation prior to computational modeling, we conducted a series of model-independent analyses to characterize choice behavior under risk, validate gaze measurement across devices, and examine the relationship between attentional allocation and choice behavior.

We first examined behavioral regularities using standard descriptive indices, including overall risky-choice proportion, sensitivity to objective risk probability, subjective value difference (derived from certainty equivalents), and response time as a function of decision difficulty.

We next characterized attentional allocation during decision-making using deviceappropriate gaze episodes, operationalized as Tobii fixations and CaliGaze ROI dwells. The extracted metrics were: (1) risky dwell proportion, defined as the proportion of total optiondirected episode duration allocated to the risky option; (2) the identity and duration of the first option-directed episode; and (3) the identity and duration of the last option-directed episode before response. Risky dwell proportion served as the primary index of attentional allocation, the principal cross-device validation metric, and the attentional input for the subsequent aDDM analyses.

To validate the reliability of webcam-based gaze estimation, we assessed the consistency of risky dwell proportion between Tobii and CaliGaze at the group, individual, and trial levels. The cross-device correlation analyses were preregistered; the ICC and Bland-Altman analyses were exploratory assessments of absolute agreement and systematic bias. We then examined whether gaze allocation was associated with choice behavior before fitting the computational models.

These exploratory signatures included the duration of first, middle, and last gaze episodes, associations of the first and last episodes with subsequent choice, and shifts in psychometric choice functions conditional on episode identity (Krajbich & Rangel, 2011). Here and below, "fixation" refers to I-VT-defined Tobii events, whereas CaliGaze events are ROI dwells; "gaze episode" is used when referring jointly to both devices. At the individual level, we examined whether stable differences in risky dwell proportion and gaze allocation toward the risky option were associated with risk preference.

The model-independent fixation-choice analyses and individual-difference correlations reported below were not specified in the time-stamped preregistration and are therefore treated as exploratory. Their p values are interpreted descriptively without adjustment for the family of exploratory tests.

Statistical analyses were implemented in R 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024). The archived analysis environment used lme4 1.1-35.5 (Bates et al., 2015) and lmerTest 3.1-3 (Kuznetsova et al., 2017); versions of the remaining R packages are supplied with the analysis archive. Binary outcomes were coded 1 for the focal response and 0 otherwise: risky versus safe choice, selection versus nonselection of the first-fixated option, and selection versus nonselection of the lastfixated option. Objective probability was divided by 100; fixation duration was analyzed in seconds; subjective-value differences were analyzed in the original task-value units. Continuous predictors were not mean-centered or z-standardized.

Trial-level binary outcomes were estimated with binomial mixed-effects logistic regression using a logit link, Wald 95% confidence intervals, and two-sided Wald z tests.

Response time was estimated with a linear mixed-effects model using Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Each mixed model included a participant-specific random intercept only; no random slopes were fitted. The behavioral models pooled the two stake-magnitude conditions and included one applicable fixed predictor: objective probability, subjective-value difference, or absolute subjective-value difference. Device-specific fixation-choice models were fitted separately for Tobii and CaliGaze. The final fixation-choice model included additive fixed effects of subjective-value difference and last-fixation identity (safe fixation as the reference level), without an interaction. Exact model formulas, singular-fit diagnostics, and analytic sample sizes are reported in Supplementary Appendix S5.

Cross-Device Agreement Analyses

To quantify cross-device agreement in average risky dwell proportion at the participant level between Tobii and CaliGaze, we used paired-samples t-tests, Pearson correlation, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), and Bland-Altman analyses. Analyses were conducted separately for each magnitude condition. Paired-samples t-tests were used to assess mean differences between devices, Pearson correlations were used to assess whether individual differences were preserved across devices, whereas ICCs and Bland-Altman analyses were used to evaluate absolute agreement. Absolute agreement was quantified using two-way mixed-effects, single-measure intraclass correlation coefficients for absolute agreement, ICC(A,1). This specification is appropriate for assessing agreement between measurements obtained from individual devices rather than averages across devices. ICC estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals. Following commonly used interpretation guidelines, ICC values below 0.50 were considered poor, values between 0.50 and 0.75 moderate, values between 0.75 and 0.90 good, and values above 0.90 excellent (Koo & Li, 2016).

Bland-Altman analyses were conducted to characterize systematic bias and limits of agreement between devices. For each participant and condition, the difference score was calculated as CaliGaze minus Tobii, and the mean of the two device measurements was used as the reference magnitude. Mean bias was defined as the average device difference, and 95% limits of agreement were calculated as the mean bias ± 1.96 times the standard deviation of the difference scores. Proportional bias was assessed by regressing the device difference scores on the paired-measurement means; a significant slope indicated that cross-device disagreement varied as a function of measurement magnitude. These analyses followed standard recommendations for Bland-Altman method-comparison studies (Bland & Altman, 1986; Giavarina, 2015; Gerke, 2020).

Drift Diffusion Modeling Framework

To further characterize the cognitive mechanisms underlying value-based decision-making, we implemented a gaze-independent drift diffusion model (DDM) and three variants of a gazedependent attentional drift diffusion model (aDDM0, aDDM1, aDDM2). The gaze-independent DDM provides a baseline account in which choices are determined solely by value differences, whereas the aDDM extends this framework by incorporating gaze allocation as a dynamic modulator of evidence accumulation. All models used the same behavioral variables, whereas the aDDM variants were fitted separately using trial-level gaze measures derived from Tobii and CaliGaze. Behavioral data were merged with device-specific trial-level fixation summaries.

Trials without any fixation classified within either target ROI for a given device could not contribute to the dwell-proportion predictors and were therefore omitted from that devicespecific aDDM dataset. Of the 14,994 behavioral trials from 51 participants, 32 Tobii trials and 24 CaliGaze trials lacked usable left-or right-ROI fixation data. The response-time criterion of RT > 0.2s removed no additional trials after this merge. Consequently, 14,962 Tobii trials (0.21% excluded) and 14,970 CaliGaze trials (0.16% excluded) were retained for modeling.

Drift Diffusion Model (DDM)

Following prior applications of drift diffusion modeling in value-based and social decisionmaking (Chen & Krajbich, 2018; Chen et al., 2023), we implemented a biased DDM to characterize individual risky-choice behavior. With this framework, each decision is conceptualized as a process of noisy evidence accumulation that evolves over time until the accumulated evidence reaches one of two decision boundaries (Fig. 4).

In the present model, risky choices were coded as responses of 1 and mapped to the upper boundary, whereas safe choices were coded as responses of 0 and mapped to the lower boundary.

Attentional Drift Diffusion Model (aDDM)

To examine whether visual attention was associated with trial-level modulation of evidence accumulation during risky decision-making, we implemented a regression-based attentional driftdiffusion model (aDDM) that extends the standard DDM by incorporating gaze-conditioned value weighting into the mean drift rate. In this implementation, gaze entered through trial-level dwell proportions rather than a fixation-by-fixation generative process. The relative contribution of each option to evidence accumulation could therefore vary with the distribution of gaze within a trial, such that attended and unattended information contributed differently to decision formation. This formulation builds on prior work showing that visual attention can influence value-based choice by modulating the integration of attended versus unattended information (Cavanagh et al., 2014; Sheng et al., 2020 Sheng et al., , 2023)).

Following the coefficient-based implementation strategy of Sheng et al. (2020), attentional effects were implemented through regression-based drift-weight coefficients. Specifically, separate value-weighting coefficients were estimated depending on whether gaze was directed toward the risky or safe option. For a given trial i, the mean drift rate was defined as: v i ~Gaze risk ,i * ( β risk ,GazeRisk * U risk ,i -β s afe ,GazeRisk * U s afe ,i ) +Gaze s afe ,i * ( β risk ,GazeS afe * U risk ,i -β s afe ,GazeS afe * U s afe ,i ) where Gaze risk ,i and Gaze s afe ,i denote the proportions of fixation durations within the two options' ROIs allocated to the risky and safe options, respectively, and U risk and U s afe denote the expected values of the risky and safe options.

The coefficients β risk ,GazeRisk , β s afe ,GazeRisk quantify the contributions of the currently attended risky and safe values, whereas β risk ,GazeS afe , and β s afe ,GazeS afe characterize the corresponding unattended-value contributions. Because the safevalue terms enter the drift equation with a minus sign, a larger safe-value weight shifts evidence accumulation toward the lower safe-choice boundary.

To facilitate interpretation relative to the original aDDM framework (Krajbich et al., 2010), we additionally derived attentional-weighting indices that characterize the relative contribution of unattended information during evidence accumulation. Specifically, θ safe = β s afe ,GazeRisk β risk ,GazeRisk θ risk = β risk ,GazeS afe β s afe ,GazeS afe

Here, θ safe is the weight of the unattended safe value relative to the attended risky value when gaze is directed toward the risky option, and θ risk is the weight of the unattended risky To evaluate alternative assumptions regarding attentional modulation, we estimated three variants of the attentional drift diffusion model that differed in the constraints imposed on gazedependent value-weighting coefficients.

The first model (aDDM0) corresponded to the original attentional drift diffusion model proposed by Krajbich et al. (2010). Within the regression-based implementation, coefficients associated with attended information were constrained to be identical across option types ( β risk ,GazeRisk =β safe ,GazeSafe ), and coefficients associated with unattended information were likewise constrained to be identical ( β risk ,GazeSafe =β safe ,GazeRisk ). Under these constraints, attentional modulation was characterized by a single attentional-weighting parameter, corresponding to the relative weighting of unattended versus attended value information irrespective of whether the option was risky or safe.

The second model (aDDM1) followed the coefficient-based implementation proposed by Sheng et al. (2020) and allowed all four gaze-dependent value-weighting coefficients to be freely estimated. Consequently, attentional modulation could vary independently as a function of both gaze state and option type. The model also estimated starting point (z), boundary separation (a), and non-decision time (t) analogously to the baseline DDM.

The third model (aDDM2) imposed a symmetry constraint on the coefficients associated with attended value information while allowing unattended value coefficients to differ between risky and safe alternatives. Specifically, the model assumed that the value coefficient associated with the currently fixated option was identical regardless of whether the attended option was risky or safe: β risk ,GazeRisk =β safe ,GazeSafe . Under this formulation, attentional weighting of unattended information remained asymmetric across risky and safe alternatives, thereby reducing model complexity relative to the fully unconstrained aDDM1 while preserving the key asymmetry of theoretical interest. Together, these models span different degrees of flexibility in attentional value weighting: a single attentional-discounting parameter (aDDM0), a fully unconstrained formulation (aDDM1), and an intermediate constrained formulation (aDDM2).

Parameter Estimation via Hierarchical Bayesian Inference

All models were estimated using dockerHDDM (Pan et al., 2025), a containerized environment for implementation of the HDDM framework (Wiecki et al., 2013). The archived environment used Docker image hcp4715/hddm:1.0.1 (linux/arm64; image digest sha256:9ea3d957ee1abfdc971acf30394abef7e4e395dfb8555d62cca406c08d529c01), Python 3.9.13, and HDDM 1.0.1rc0; the complete Python package versions are provided in requirements.txt. HDDM performs hierarchical Bayesian estimation of drift-diffusion model parameters via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling using the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. Use of a fixed container ensured consistent software dependencies across model runs.

The hierarchical (multilevel) Bayesian framework jointly estimates group-and participant-level parameters through partial pooling, allowing individual estimates to be informed by the population-level distribution while preserving between-participant heterogeneity (Gelman & Hill, 2007; Lee & Wagenmakers, 2013). The resulting posterior distributions quantify population-level tendencies, individual differences, and parameter uncertainty.

No custom priors were specified; models therefore used the default HDDM priors (Wiecki et al., 2013). For each model, four MCMC chains were run for 12,000 iterations per chain, with the first 2,000 iterations discarded as burn-in, leaving 10,000 post-burn-in draws per chain.

Convergence was evaluated using the rank-normalized R statistic (Gelman & Rubin, 1992), and sampling efficiency was assessed using bulk and tail effective sample sizes (ESS; Vehtari et al., 2021). R ≤ 1.01 and ESS values greater than 400 were treated as satisfactory.

Model Comparison

Model comparison was evaluated using multiple complementary criteria rather than a single fit index. The primary criterion for model selection was the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC; Watanabe, 2010), which estimates out-of-sample predictive performance while accounting for effective model complexity through integration over the full posterior distribution. Following the implementation in dockerHDDM, WAIC values were reported on the deviance scale (-2 × ELPD), with lower values indicating superior predictive performance.

Relative model performance was evaluated using pairwise differences in WAIC ( Δ WAIC), with larger differences indicating stronger evidence favoring one model over another.

For comparability with prior HDDM/aDDM work, the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC; Spiegelhalter et al., 2002) was also reported as a descriptive fit index. Because DIC and WAIC rely on different approximations and produced non-identical rankings, model adequacy was judged jointly from these indices, posterior predictive checks, theoretical interpretability, and parsimony.

Posterior Predictive Checks

Posterior predictive checks (PPCs) evaluated whether the fitted models reproduced key features of the observed choice and response-time data. Trial-level predictions were generated conditional on posterior parameter draws and the observed trial covariates. Model predictions were compared with the observed overall response-time distribution, participant-level mean response times, overall risky-choice proportion, participant-level risky-choice proportions, and the binned relationship between risky dwell proportion and risky-choice probability.

For each model, 100 posterior draws were sampled without replacement using a fixed random seed (2026). For each draw, 20 signed response times were simulated per observed trial from the Wiener first-passage-time (WFPT) density implemented in HDDM; the sign identified the simulated boundary choice and the absolute value represented response time. Predictions were summarized across posterior draws and repetitions and visually compared with the corresponding empirical patterns. These checks were used to assess absolute model adequacy and complemented the relative model-comparison indices.

Computational Modeling Results

Model Comparison Across Gaze-Independent and Gaze-Dependent Specifications

All fitted models showed satisfactory convergence ( R <¿ 1.01) and adequate bulk and tail effective sample sizes (all ESS ¿ 400), supporting adequate MCMC convergence. Detailed model-comparison statistics, convergence diagnostics, and posterior predictive check results for all fitted models are reported in Supplementary Appendix S7.

We first compared the baseline DDM with the three attentional models (aDDM0, aDDM1, and aDDM2) to evaluate whether incorporating gaze allocation improved model performance.

The original single-parameter attentional model (aDDM0), corresponding to the classic formulation proposed by Krajbich et al. (2010), failed to improve upon the baseline DDM and yielded substantially poorer fit according to both WAIC and DIC in the Tobii and CaliGaze datasets. This finding suggests that a single attentional-weighting parameter was insufficient to capture the attentional dynamics underlying risky decision-making in the present task. Detailed results for aDDM0 are reported in Supplementary Appendix S7.

In contrast, both asymmetric attentional models (aDDM1 and aDDM2), which allowed attentional discounting to vary across option types, substantially outperformed the baseline DDM. Across both datasets, these models yielded markedly lower WAIC and DIC values, indicating improved predictive performance when gaze-dependent modulation of evidence accumulation was incorporated. These findings indicate that risky decision-making was better characterized by asymmetric attentional influences than by value-based accumulation alone.

We further compared two variants of the aDDM that differed in their parameterization of gaze-dependent effects. The information criteria did not converge on a single ranking: WAIC favored the constrained variant (aDDM2) across both datasets, whereas DIC favored the fully flexible variant (aDDM1). The two variants nonetheless produced broadly comparable posterior predictive patterns, and the more flexible aDDM1 showed lower sampling efficiency (smaller effective sample sizes and higher R ] values). Accordingly, subsequent parameter analyses in the main text focus on the more parsimonious constrained variant (aDDM2), based on comparable empirical adequacy, greater parsimony, and more stable estimation rather than unanimous support across information criteria; results from the fully flexible specification (aDDM1), together with the classic single-parameter aDDM specified in our preregistration, are reported in Supplementary Appendix S7.

Parameter Estimates: Attention-Dependent Value Weighting and Cross-Device Correspondence

We next examined parameter estimates from the selected constrained attentional model (aDDM2) to characterize how gaze allocation modulated evidence accumulation during risky decision-making. Posterior parameter estimates are summarized in Table 2.

Across both datasets, the posterior mean of the shared coefficient for currently fixated option values was larger than the posterior means of both unattended-value coefficients, consistent with the core assumptions of the attentional drift diffusion framework. For example, in the Tobii dataset, the estimated coefficient associated with attended option values was 0.061, whereas the unattended-value coefficients were smaller, with greater attenuation for the unattended risky value than for the unattended safe value ( β risk , gazesafe = 0.006; β safe , gazerisk = 0.043). A similar pattern was observed in the CaliGaze dataset, in which the attended-value coefficient (0.056) exceeded the unattended-value coefficients ( β risk , gazesafe = 0.016; β safe , gazerisk = 0.048). Under the fitted model, this coefficient pattern indicates gaze-dependent value weighting, with currently fixated values receiving larger estimated weights than unattended values.

To facilitate interpretation relative to the classic aDDM framework, we additionally derived attentional weighting indices reflecting the relative contribution of unattended information during evidence accumulation. Across both datasets, the group-level estimates of both θ risk and θ safe were below 1, indicating reduced weighting of unattended option values relative to attended information at the group level. However, θ risk was markedly smaller than θ safe in both datasets (Tobii: 0.107 vs. 0.713; CaliGaze: 0.280 vs. 0.854), indicating that unattended risky information was more strongly attenuated than unattended safe information during evidence accumulation.

This asymmetry indicates that safe-option value continued to shape evidence accumulation even when it was not fixated, whereas risky-option value exerted its influence primarily while attended. Thus, safe-option value was comparatively gaze-invariant, whereas risky-option value was more attention-dependent during risky decision-making.

We further examined cross-device correspondence and agreement in parameter estimates using correlation analyses, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), and Bland-Altman analyses (Fig. 7; Table 3). Attentional weighting indices derived from Tobii and CaliGaze showed strong positive correspondence across participants for both θ risk (r=0.9 47 , 95% CI [0.908, 0.970], p < .001) and θ safe (r=0.8 42 , 95% CI [0.737, 0.908], p < 0.001 ; Fig. 7). Similar or even stronger correspondence was observed for the remaining DDM parameters, including starting point (z; r = 0 .9 95 , 95% CI [0.991, 0.997], p < 0.001 ), boundary separation (a; r = 0 .9 96 , 95% CI [0.994, 0.998], p < 0.001 ), and non-decision time (t; r = 0 .9 99 , 95% CI [0.998, 0.999], p < 0.001 ). These results indicate that individual differences in estimated model parameters were highly preserved across devices.

Absolute-agreement ICC analyses further supported substantial cross-device consistency (Table 3). Agreement was excellent for the core decision-process parameters z, a, and t, good for θ risk , and moderate for θ safe , indicating closer absolute agreement for the core DDM parameters than for the attentional weighting indices. Bland-Altman analyses further showed minimal systematic bias for z (Bias ¿ -0.002), a (Bias ¿ -0.040), and t (Bias ¿ 0.004), accompanied by relatively narrow limits of agreement. In contrast, both attentional weighting parameters displayed systematic positive bias, with CaliGaze yielding higher estimates than Tobii for θ risk (Bias ¿ 0.169) and θ s afe (Bias ¿ 0.142). The proportional-bias tests were not significant for either attentional weighting parameter, suggesting an approximately constant device-related offset rather than disagreement that increased across the observed parameter range.

The two devices yielded closely aligned estimates for z, a, and t, whereas the attentional weighting indices showed stronger between-participant correspondence than absolute agreement.

Thus, CaliGaze reproduced the broad parameter structure obtained with Tobii, but the devicespecific scale of θ risk and θ s afe should not be treated as interchangeable. It is also worth noting that these parameters were estimated from a hierarchical Bayesian aDDM rather than directly observed measures, and therefore inherently reflect both measurement variability and parameterestimation uncertainty. Cross-device agreement should therefore be interpreted more cautiously for the derived attentional weighting indices than for the core DDM parameters.

Individual Differences in Attentional Weighting and Risky Choice

To further examine the behavioral relevance of attentional weighting parameters, we analyzed the relationship between individual parameter estimates and the proportion of risky choices across participants (Fig. 8). We focused primarily on the attentional weighting indices ( θ risk , θ safe ) and the starting point parameter (z), as these parameters are theoretically linked to attentional bias and pre-decisional response bias during risky choice.

Across both the Tobii-and CaliGaze-based fits, θ risk showed a strong positive association with risky-choice proportion (Tobii: r = 0.74 1 , p < 0.001; CaliGaze: r = 0.7 75 , p < 0.001).

Participants with larger θ risk values, reflecting relatively greater weight on risky information even when attention was directed toward the safe option, tended to choose the risky option more frequently. Thus, greater model-estimated influence of unattended risky value was associated with a higher within-task risky-choice tendency.

By contrast, θ safe showed a negative association with risky-choice proportion. Participants with larger θ safe values, reflecting greater weight on unattended safe information while fixating the risky option, tended to exhibit lower proportions of risky choice. This negative association reached statistical significance in both the Tobii-and CaliGaze-based model fits (Tobii: r = -0.360 , p = 0.010 ; CaliGaze : r = -0.441 , p = 0 .001 ). Together, these descriptive associations are consistent with asymmetric attentional weighting: greater residual influence of unattended risky information accompanied more risky choices, whereas greater residual influence of unattended safe information accompanied fewer risky choices.

In addition, the starting point parameter (z) was positively associated with risky choice proportion across both systems (Tobii: r = 0.6 64 , p < 0 .001 ; CaliGaze : r = 0.6 55 , p < 0 .001 ).

Given that larger z values indicate an initial bias toward the risky option, this association is consistent with participants who had a starting point closer to the risky-choice boundary also exhibiting higher overall rates of risky choice. Broadly similar parameter-choice associations were obtained from the Tobii-and CaliGaze-based fits, demonstrating qualitative reproducibility across gaze streams.

Discussion

The present study evaluated CaliGaze, a webcam computer vision framework, by benchmarking it against research-grade infrared eye tracking during a within-subject binary choice task.

CaliGaze successfully reproduced established signatures linking fixation to choice. Across both gaze streams, attentional DDMs (aDDMs) outperformed the standard DDM that is independent of gaze, and CaliGaze closely matched Tobii on core decision parameters while preserving differences between participants in attentional weighting. Together, these findings demonstrate that gaze tracking via webcams can support process tracing with the aDDM, providing a more accessible approach to study the role of attention in decision-making.

Consistency of Eye-Tracking Results Across Hierarchical Levels

The central finding of this evaluation is the substantial consistency observed between CaliGaze and the Tobii infrared reference system across multiple levels of analysis. As shown in Figures 5 and 6, CaliGaze ROI dwells reproduced the model-independent choice associations observed with Tobii fixations (Krajbich et al., 2010; Shimojo et al., 2003), including the temporal ordering of first, middle, and last gaze episodes, the association between first-episode duration and subsequent choice, the value dependence of the final episode and its associated choice bias, and the participant-level association between dwell-time allocation and risky-choice tendency. These findings suggest that CaliGaze's ROI classification and temporal sampling captured the attentional regularities examined in the present binary-choice task, while its dwell events should not be interpreted as measurements of fine-grained oculomotor fixations (Orquin & Mueller Loose, 2013).

The hierarchical Bayesian modeling results extended this convergence to latent decision processes. For both gaze streams, the dual-weighting attentional models provided a better account of choice than the gaze-independent baseline, and the constrained variant (aDDM2) was retained for the main analyses. CaliGaze closely matched Tobii on the core DDM parameters (z, a, and t) and preserved differences between participants in attentional weighting. Absolute agreement was lower for the attentional weighting parameters, particularly θ safe , indicating that their absolute values should be compared cautiously across devices. This pattern supports the use of CaliGaze for aDDM research while showing that its strongest measurement correspondence lies in the core decision parameters (Krajbich & Rangel, 2011; Thomas et al., 2019).

Algorithmic Innovations and Measurement Precision

The observed consistency across devices may partly reflect the task-specific measurement choices integrated into the pipeline. The iris-position ratio ρ averages two Euclidean iris-tocanthus distance ratios, each normalized by the corresponding within-eye canthus-to-canthus landmark distance; it does not use intercanthal distance and should not be interpreted as a threedimensional head-pose correction. The single-eye eyelid-opening ratio was recorded as a validity flag. Median target values anchored the piecewise spatial mapping, although the archived code did not apply a separate calibration-error acceptance threshold. Together, these procedures defined the gaze coordinates used to derive trial-level ROI dwells.

CaliGaze webcam capture, Face Mesh inference, smoothing, mapping, and logging were executed sequentially inside the Pygame stimulus loop rather than in a dual-thread producerconsumer architecture. Tobii gaze data arrived asynchronously through the SDK callback.

CaliGaze, Tobii, and behavioral logs used the same host clock for post hoc alignment, but camera, processing, display, and callback latencies remained possible. The observed median intersample intervals were 32.0 ms for CaliGaze and 16.7 ms for Tobii. This temporal granularity was used for dwell-and trial-level analyses rather than fine-grained oculomotor timing (Nyström et al., 2013; Orquin & Holmqvist, 2018).

Research Accessibility and Scalability

CaliGaze's accessibility contribution lies not in replacing infrared eye tracking across all applications, but in lowering the entry barrier for paradigms that require reliable ROI-level classification in spatially separated displays rather than fine-grained gaze localization in two dimensions. Its use of commodity RGB cameras and open-source software reduces dependence on specialized hardware, dedicated laboratory infrastructure, and proprietary acquisition systems.

This portable and transparent measurement pipeline may be particularly valuable for laboratories with limited resources, multisite projects, and replications of decision paradigms that incorporate gaze data on a larger scale. In this sense, CaliGaze shifts the primary implementation challenge from hardware acquisition to calibration specific to the task and data quality control.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations define the scope of the present findings. First, CaliGaze was evaluated in a spatially separated binary-choice paradigm, for which its within-eye-width-normalized iris-position ratio was specifically optimized. Extending the framework to vertically distributed or multi-alternative displays, including 2 × 2 choice arrays and reading tasks, will require improved two-dimensional gaze mapping and task-specific validation. Second, the present validation used a fixed laboratory setup with controlled lighting and an external webcam. Although CaliGaze's modular architecture supports browser-based deployment, its performance across heterogeneous cameras, lighting conditions, viewing distances, postures, and computing environments remains to be established. The present sample also consisted of university students from a single institution; validation in more demographically and clinically diverse populations would help establish the broader generalizability of the framework. Third, the two devices showed close agreement for the core decision parameters and preserved individual differences in attentional weighting, but the absolute scale of the attentional weighting estimates differed across devices, particularly for θ safe . These parameters are therefore better suited to within-device comparisons and rank-order analyses than to direct comparison of absolute values across devices. Finally, the associations between parameters and choices were estimated from the same choices used to fit the models and therefore should not be interpreted as independent predictive validation. Future work should examine out-of-sample prediction, test-retest reliability, and parameter recovery using simulation and independent data.

Choice and response time were jointly modeled using four core parameters: drift rate (v), starting point (z), boundary separation (a), and non-decision time (t). The drift rate reflects the direction and speed of evidence accumulation; positive drift favors movement toward the upper boundary, whereas negative drift favors movement toward the lower boundary. The starting point captures pre-decisional bias before evidence accumulation begins. Boundary separation reflects decision caution, and non-decision time accounts for perceptual and motor processes. The drift rate ( v ) was modeled as a linear function of the expected values of the risky and safe options: v = β risk * U risk + β s afe * U s afe where U risk and U s afe denote the expected values of the risky and safe options, respectively. The coefficients β risk and β s afe represent the weights assigned to the two options during evidence accumulation. Under the present response coding, a larger positive β risk increases drift toward the upper risky-choice boundary, whereas a larger magnitude of the negative coefficient | β safe | increases drift toward the lower safe-choice boundary. To characterize the relative influence of the risky option on evidence accumulation, we additionally computed a risk-option weighting index defined as the ratio β risk /| β safe | , with larger values indicating greater relative weighting of the risky option during the decision process.
