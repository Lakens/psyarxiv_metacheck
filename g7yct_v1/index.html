Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Accepting an excuse lowers blame, but whether it changes what observers think of the person is unsettled. An excuse admits an act was wrong but pleads reduced responsibility. Read as folk psychology, character accounts of excuse imply that an excuse making an act less revealing should soften judgement of the person. Moralised Essentialism predicts otherwise. Its central rule, identity closure, holds that once a wrong is taken to reveal who the actor is, excuses explain the act and reduce blame but leave the view of the person unchanged. The claim is that excuses stop reaching that judgement, not that they carry less weight. As excuses strengthen, observers should attribute the act more to the situation and blame less, while their judgements of who the actor is, and their forecasts of the actor's conduct elsewhere, barely move. Retraction and reinterpretation, unlike excuses, change what the act was and should lower those judgements, which strict belief perseverance denies. A proposed experiment fixes two tests beforehand. Meaningful revision of those judgements under excuses observers credit as mitigating would reject the account. Judgements that hold or rise after accepted retraction or reinterpretation would reject closure as the explanation of their stability under excuses.

1. Introduction

An accepted excuse is supposed to do two things. An observer who accepts that a wrong was done under threat should blame the wrongdoer less. On diagnosticity accounts, which judge persons by how much an act reveals, and on character-based accounts of excuse read as folk psychology, the observer should also think better of the wrongdoer. The first expectation is well supported (Woolfolk et al., 2006; Monroe & Malle, 2019). I have found no study that tests the second across levels of excuse strength, with a credited excuse arriving after the person has been judged. If an accepted excuse can lower blame while leaving the judgement of the person untouched, then explaining an act and revising a view of character come apart, and a judgement of who someone is may, once formed, stop responding to information that would otherwise revise it. I propose that judgements of persons can behave in this way once a wrong has been taken to reveal character, and I describe an experiment that could show the proposal to be false. Suppose Alex, an accountant, knowingly puts a false figure in a company report to defraud its investors. After judging the act, an observer learns that her manager had threatened to fire her if she refused, that the threat was real and that she had objected in writing before giving in. The new information explains why she acted. It does not change what she did, or that she did it knowing it was wrong. Nor does it show that the act was right. The observer can accept all of it, understand the act better and blame Alex less, yet still think of her as a dishonest person who is not to be trusted with the next set of accounts.

On diagnosticity accounts of moral character, this observer's response is anomalous. On the person-centred view, acts matter to moral judgement as signals of character, and their force depends on what they reveal about the actor (Uhlmann et al., 2015). An act can be judged less harmful yet more revealing of bad character, because judgements of the person track diagnosticity, how much an act reveals, rather than outcome (Tannenbaum et al., 2011). Probabilistic accounts of person perception make the point formal. When person and situation jointly cause a behaviour, evidence that the situation produced it should weaken the inference to the person (Walker et al., 2022), provided that yielding to that situation is not itself diagnostic of character. Character-based accounts of excuse, on which an excuse can work by showing that the act does not reflect bad character (Bayles, 1982; Brandt, 1985), point the same way when read as folk psychology. If the observer's view of Alex rested on this act and the excuse removes what the act revealed, the observer should think better of Alex in proportion to what the excuse shows.

The observer may instead follow a different update rule. Once a violation has been encoded, which is to say taken to reveal who the actor is, later mitigating information still shapes the explanation of the act and still lowers blame. It no longer reaches the representation of who the actor is. I call the account Moralised Essentialism and its update rule identity closure. The difference lies in reach. Any diagnosticity account can give an excuse less weight, but closure says something stronger. For excuses alone, and only for the judgement of who the actor is, an excuse that arrives after encoding has no effect.

Several established findings make the proposal plausible. Moral traits are central to judgements of personal identity (Strohminger & Nichols, 2014), and negative moral behaviour can carry disproportionate weight because it is diagnostic (Skowronski & Carlston, 1989). Judgements of acts and of persons can also come apart at a single moment. An act can be judged right while its agent is judged bad (Uhlmann et al., 2013), judgements of wrongness and of punishment draw on different features of the same act (Cushman, 2008), and blame is distinct from character judgements, which are dispositional inferences drawn from moral events rather than one of the core classes of moral judgement (Malle, 2021). On choice and capacity accounts an excuse can lower blame without improving the view of character (Moore, 1990), so a dissociation at one moment is unsurprising. The proposal adds a rule for change over time, and the rule applies only to excuses. An excuse that would revise the judgement of the person before encoding fails to reach it afterwards.

2. What existing accounts establish

Several literatures come close to identity closure. None of them says that an excuse loses its reach once the wrong has been read as revealing the person.

2.1 Psychological essentialism and the grammar of persons

Psychological essentialism is the tendency to explain observable properties by an underlying nature that supports further inferences, even when the observer cannot say what that nature is (Medin & Ortony, 1989; Gelman, 2003). Its components, such as discreteness, immutability, informativeness and biological basis, can come apart (Haslam et al., 2004), so no single item can show that an observer has inferred an essence. Moral essentialism applies the same reasoning to character. Children and adults treat moral characteristics as stable and inborn to varying degrees (Heiphetz, 2019), and essentialist descriptions of an offender increase punitiveness (Martin & Heiphetz, 2021).

Linguistics supplies the distinction the proposal needs. Individual-level predicates attribute an enduring property to an individual, as in "Alex is a liar". Stage-level predicates, including verbs that describe single events, attribute a property to one temporal stage of an individual, as in "Alex lied under pressure" (Carlson, 1977; Kratzer, 1995). Noun labels lead children to judge properties more stable across time and contexts than verbal predicates do (Gelman & Heyman, 1999), and nouns support more inferences about further properties than adjectives (Carnaghi et al., 2008). Because an adjective such as "dishonest" also allows an episodic reading, as in "Alex was being dishonest", the proposed identity items (Section 8) use nominal frames such as "is a dishonest person".

2.2 Moral character and identity

Moral character dominates impressions of persons (Goodwin, 2015), and changes to moral traits weigh especially heavily on perceived identity (Strohminger & Nichols, 2014). Character also enters blame through a true-self route separable from judgements of intentionality (Murray et al., 2025). None of this shows that a judgement of the person stops updating after a violation. Negative acts can be highly diagnostic without being read as revealing an essence. Reeder and Brewer's (1979) schematic model explains asymmetric inferences to dispositions by expectations about what different kinds of actor would do, and some traits are easier to confirm than to disconfirm (Rothbart & Park, 1986). Where these accounts predict a stable judgement after a bad act, closure must predict something they do not (Section 6).

2.3 Attribution, probabilistic inference and impression revision

Work on correspondence bias shows that inferences stay tied to behaviour despite situational constraint (Gilbert & Malone, 1995), but a failure to discount the situation need not reflect a fixed dispositional inference, since it can reflect how situational information is integrated (Trope & Gaunt, 2000). Apparent correspondence bias can follow from rational inference about persons and situations (Walker et al., 2022). New moral information also revises impressions more than new information about sociability or competence does (Brambilla et al., 2019), so a durable first impression is not by itself evidence of a closed representation.

Closest of all is contextualisation. When later information contradicts a first impression, automatic evaluations tend to reflect the later information only in the context where it was learned, while the first impression generalises to other contexts (Gawronski et al., 2010). Contextualisation depends on order and context and is indifferent to the kind of later information. Extending it to explicit identity judgements takes an added assumption. So extended, it predicts revision where the excuse was learned, and the same context-bound pattern after a retraction. Closure predicts no revision after an excuse, even there. If identity moves there, closure fails where contextualisation succeeds.

2.4 Relation to Motivated Violation Construction and Trait Judgmentalism

The present account is narrower than two neighbouring proposals. Motivated Violation Construction (Boullineau, 2027) asks whether conduct is represented as a violation at all. Identity closure takes the answer as its input. Information that undoes the classification, such as a retraction or a justification, returns the case to that earlier question, which is why such information should revise identity. Trait Judgmentalism (Boullineau, 2026) is a stable individual difference in which an evaluator understands credible mitigation but does not revise the evaluation of the person, a failure of weighting after comprehension. Closure concerns reach, so under closure there is no weight to get wrong. It is claimed for population-average judgements and leaves stable differences between observers open, whereas such differences define Trait Judgmentalism. If the closure pattern appeared only in evaluators high in Trait Judgmentalism, the individual-difference account would be favoured.

2.5 Excuses, character and the two faces of responsibility

Philosophers of excuse ask what we ought to think of an excused agent, whereas the present account concerns what ordinary observers in fact think of one. On character-based accounts, an excuse can work by blocking the inference from the act to bad character or to a defective motivational structure (Bayles, 1982; Brandt, 1985). Tadros (2005) is a pluralist, allowing defences grounded in an inability to do otherwise as well as in the act's failure to reflect character. On choice or capacity accounts an excuse lowers blame without implying better character (Moore, 1990). Gardner (1998) holds that an excused agent met the standards of character that could fairly be demanded in the situation, which need not change predictions about the agent's dispositions. None of these views says what should happen to a judgement of the person formed before the excuse arrived. Excuses often come late, after the observer has judged, so order is where the present account adds a descriptive claim that these normative views leave open. The prediction proposed here treats them as conditional claims about how ordinary observers judge. With a stranger and a single violation the act is the only evidence about the person, which makes it plausible that an accepted excuse would revise the judgement of character. Excuses can also cost those who offer them, who may be seen as flawed in character (Schlenker et al., 2001). The closest empirical study discussed here examined which features of excuses damage impressions of the excuse maker (Pontari et al., 2002). It is not reported as testing whether a credited excuse that arrives after the person has been judged revises that judgement. Closure predicts that observers credit the excuse and blame less but keep their judgement of the person. That challenges the folk reading. It leaves untouched any account of when excuses are warranted. Strawson (1962) distinguished two groups of plea. Pleas such as "he didn't mean to" or "they left him no alternative" present the injury as one for which the agent was not fully responsible, and as consistent with the agent's attitude being what we demanded. They do not invite the observer to see the agent as other than a fully responsible agent, and they leave the agent an appropriate target of the reactive attitudes. Pleas such as "he wasn't himself" or "he has been under very great strain recently" suspend the reactive attitudes towards the agent as he then was, and pleas such as "he's only a child" invite the objective attitude. The proposed Context Integration Task (Section 8) excludes pleas of strain and uses only pleas of the first group, namely external threat, coercion and lack of alternatives. Closure predicts that the folk judgement of who the agent is survives them. Watson (1996) distinguished two faces of responsibility. Attributability concerns whether conduct discloses what the agent is like, including what the agent is prepared to do. Accountability concerns whether the agent may fairly be held to demands and subjected to blame and sanction. Shoemaker (2015) adds answerability, whether the agent can be asked to justify the judgements behind the act, and argues that the three can come apart. In these terms, closure is the pattern in which judged accountability falls while predictive identity, the judgement of who the person is that supports forecasts, holds. Judged attributability is closer to the evaluative true self, what the person values deep down, and closure predicts that true-self ratings will rise. On an identification reading, which I adopt as an explicit assumption rather than draw from Watson's disclosure view, attributability falls where the agent does not endorse the act, as at Level 6, the strongest excuse level of the proposed task (Section 8). Its folk relevance rests on evidence that observers hold coerced agents less responsible when they do not identify with the act (Woolfolk et al., 2006). The mapping interprets Watson's terms and does not identify his constructs with these. Attributability is a normative relation between agent and conduct, whereas the identity representation is a psychological state of the observer.

3. The identity-closure proposal

>The identity-closure proposal

3.1 Identity representation and occasion record

An observer judging Alex keeps track of two things. The identity representation attributes an underlying moral character to a particular person and supports predictions about that person beyond the observed event. The occasion record holds the act in its circumstances, together with the observer's explanation of it. Both are functional descriptions and make no claim about separate neural systems. An observer can consistently hold both, believing that Alex is dishonest and that the threat explains why she acted when she did, because a disposition and a situation can jointly produce an act. The identity representation corresponds to an individual-level predication that assigns the person to a moral category, and the occasion record to stage-level predications about the episode (Section 2.1). The account's claim concerns how each is later revised.

Measures of the identity representation must also be distinguished from three nearby measures: a rating of a trait in the domain of the act (here dishonesty), a same-pressure prediction of whether the person would repeat the act in the circumstances already described, and a global evaluation. Each generalises, so the identity measures must capture more than all three, as must unrelated-domain projection, which forecasts bad conduct in another moral domain.

3.2 A formal statement

Let E denote the identity representation, O the occasion record, V the violation as first described and C k the kth excuse, an item of later information that leaves the act, its authorship, the agent's knowledge and the agent's operative goal intact (Section 3.3). Encoding produces E 0 and O 0 . Under closure, the occasion record keeps taking in new information while identity stays fixed.

The open alternative lets later information revise identity through an update function h.

Figure 1a shows the updates each model permits.

By reach I mean whether any path exists from information about the occasion, such as Alex's written objection, to the identity representation. Weight is the strength of a path that exists. A model in which an excuse merely lowers the diagnosticity of the act is an open model with a small weight. In such a model identity still moves when the situation is learned, and explaining away, discounting one cause once another is found (Kelley, 1973), survives in weakened form. Closure therefore differs in kind from a weakened form of explaining away. Under closure the written objection has no route to the identity representation.

Yet within one class of information an absent path and a zero weight produce the same observations, and I take this to be the account's main vulnerability. If nothing else separated them, the two would be empirically indistinguishable. The task narrows the problem with three comparisons, none of which rests on a flat line alone. First, where independently elicited likelihoods imply that the strongest excuse should move identity by more than the prespecified margin (Section 9.1), the open model predicts that revision, whereas closure forbids it. Second, retraction and reinterpretation must move the same representation within the same episode, showing that a flat line under excuses does not come from an insensitive measure. Third, the same excuse should have more influence before encoding than after it (P3, Table 2). Beyond ordinary order effects, this separates closure from an excuse weight fixed across the episode. A weight that encoding itself sets to zero predicts the same timing, so I treat it as closure at the behavioural level, with the mechanism, an absent path or a gated weight, left unresolved. Closure, as hypothesised here, holds within a declared episode, which runs from the violation to the final judgement in a single session, not over a relationship or a lifetime. Closure predicts that manipulating the excuse leaves identity unchanged. It does not predict that identity and causal recognition, the judgement that the situation caused the act, are uncorrelated, since prior trust or construal can correlate them.

3.3 The scope of the claim

Later information about the encoded act can excuse it, justify it or deny it. Austin's (1957) distinction between excuse and justification, with outright denial added as a third kind, fixes the boundaries in advance. An excuse admits that the act was wrong and that the agent did it, and pleads circumstances that reduce responsibility. A justification accepts responsibility and denies that the act was wrong. A denial disputes that the act occurred or that this agent performed it. Mitigation, in the present sense, is excuse, whether partial or full, the category to which character theories of excuse apply (Section 2.5). It cites external pressure or constraint, such as threat and costly alternatives, or else the agent's reluctance. It leaves four things intact: the type of act, its authorship, the agent's knowledge that the act was wrong and the agent's operative goal, meaning its immediate aim, as distinct from the motive for yielding to a threat. Reinterpretation covers justification and any information that changes what the agent is taken to have known. Retraction is denial. Austin counted some forms of ignorance among excuses. The encoded act, however, is a knowing wrong, so information about what the agent knew changes the encoded act. Counting such information as an excuse would let closure claim cases in which the act had changed, so assigning it to reinterpretation is conservative.

Closure is claimed only for mitigation. Later conduct by the same agent is a new act in its own right and falls outside this scope. Reinterpretation and retraction replace the original description of the violation with a revised one, from which identity is encoded afresh. Neither equation applies. So the account predicts that they revise identity. This prediction separates closure from strict belief perseverance and continued influence (Ross et al., 1975; Ecker et al., 2022), taken here in a strict form on which an impression survives the withdrawal of its evidential basis unchanged. A fall that leaves corrected judgements above the no-violation baseline fits closure and partial continued influence alike, so the proposed experiment also compares corrected judgements with that baseline, a contrast that cannot reject the account (Supplementary Section S1.9). A broader account would claim closure for all later information, including retraction. That claim is not defended here. The exploratory reanalysis in Supplementary Section S2 found revision after reframing and after later good conduct. Its materials were classified after the results were seen, without norming, as likely to fall below the elicitation criterion for encoding (Section 4), so it is not a clean test of either version, but it makes the broader claim implausible as a default property of character updating. The present version, prepared after that reanalysis had been seen, restricts the claim to excuses.

Each item of later information is classified by condition-level norming, a pretest of the materials, before any confirmatory data are collected (Supplementary Section S1). A condition normed as an excuse remains one in every confirmatory analysis. Participants' own recategorisation of the act is reported as a diagnostic and cannot move a condition out of the mitigation arm. In the proposed study, no item is classified by its effect on identity.

3.4 Identity closure and the need for cognitive closure

The need for cognitive closure is a motivated, domain-general individual difference in the desire for a firm answer. People high in it seize on early information and freeze on the resulting judgement (Kruglanski & Webster, 1996). The motivational implementation specified here assumes that high scorers process later information less, so their occasion record should also update less. Identity closure is instead a population-average restriction on one representation, under which the occasion record keeps updating while identity does not. If only high scorers showed the pattern, the motivational implementation would explain it better than closure does.

4. How encoding might occur

Closure is defined relative to an encoding event, so the account must describe that event without identifying it by the stability it is meant to explain. A provisional description has four operations. An observer appraises the act as a violation, judges its relevance to the actor's character, encodes it as revealing an underlying identity, and then adds later information to the occasion record. The operations are analytically distinct, but whether they are separable in time is unknown. Smooth updating, updating that differs across observers, or noisy all-or-none updating could produce similar average curves.

The elicitation criterion says when encoding should occur. It is defined by features of the input. Encoding is expected when an act is judged deliberate, diagnostic of character and intended to harm, and each property is normed separately, because a person can act deliberately under coercion while disliking the outcome. A condition enters the confirmatory test only if its mean norming ratings meet prespecified criteria on all three. Norming checks are made at the condition level, on the violation page and before any context is shown. They never exclude participants. Alex is only an illustration, and her case would itself need norming on all three.

The strongest process test concerns timing. An excuse should have more influence on identity when it arrives before the violation is evaluated than afterwards (P3). Structural framing (Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2020), which explains behaviour by social structures, should likewise prevent encoding when it precedes the violation but not reverse encoding afterwards. Prior positivity is treated as a covariate. It is not allowed to become a second condition for encoding that could explain away a failed prediction.

Differences between observers threaten the population-level prediction. Suppose some observers are closed and the rest, the open observers, revise in the same direction. The condition means then move by roughly the open observers' change scaled by their share of the sample, so a large enough open share pushes them past the prespecified margin. Opposite-signed changes can cancel in an average. A scenario-level criterion (Supplementary Section S1.8) blocks cancellation across scenarios but not across observers, so the primary test can support only a population-average dissociation, and closure within each observer needs same-target trajectories and a heterogeneity criterion (Supplementary Section S3.6). No mixture correction is applied, and a subgroup that appears stable cannot be labelled closed because its outcomes are flat. 5. Evidence that motivates and challenges closure

>Evidence that motivates and challenges closure

5.1 What existing regularities contribute

No finding in the literature demonstrates closure, though several make it worth looking for. Blame updates in an orderly way as evidence arrives (Monroe & Malle, 2019), and closure predicts that it stays responsive. It can also update, though modestly, when the agent's moral ignorance comes to light after the act (Theisen & Andow, 2026), which the present scheme classes as reinterpretation. Closure alone cannot explain why a negative act should trigger encoding more readily than a positive one. Schema and confirmability accounts largely explain that asymmetry without it (Reeder & Brewer, 1979; Rothbart & Park, 1986). Predictions about later good conduct extend the account beyond its core scope. When the elicitation criterion is met, such conduct should be recorded as a new act, and identity should improve less than an open model calibrated on that conduct predicts. Otherwise it predicts ordinary graded revision. Evidence against this untested extension would leave the core prediction (P1) standing.

5.2 Retraction and reinterpretation

Since closure covers only excuses, work on corrected information bears on the retraction and reinterpretation controls. Retracted misinformation often continues to influence reasoning (Ecker et al., 2022), and that influence appears to depend on whether the reader updates their model of the situation (Hey et al., 2026). For newly formed impressions of persons, however, retracted negative information was often fully discounted, with mixed evidence of residual influence (Mickelberg et al., 2024 (Mickelberg et al., , 2025)). That pattern fits the scope, because a denial changes the encoded act. The influence of corrected false accusations against fictional political candidates nonetheless persisted, regardless of political orientation (Cohen et al., 2024). This is the most direct challenge to the retraction prediction. One reply is that participants may not have accepted the correction, which is why the Context Integration Task measures acceptance directly.

Reinterpretation is on firmer ground. First impressions, including implicit evaluations, can be reversed when the meaning of the original behaviour is recast (Mann & Ferguson, 2015), and such reversals are reported to last (Kurdi et al., 2026). They fit the prediction that reinterpretation revises identity. Only if a pure excuse produced the same reversal would they tell against closure.

An exploratory, non-preregistered reanalysis of both arms of Kim et al. (2022, Study 3), reported in Supplementary Section S2, found that character judgements were revised substantially after a moral dilemma was reframed (+0.59 SD) and after unrelated later good conduct (+0.54 SD). Because those dilemmas already give the "immoral" option a plausible justification, they are classified, after the data were seen and without norming, as likely to fall below the elicitation criterion. The reframing arm, first analysed as a test of mitigation, is likewise reclassified as reinterpretation. The result neither tests nor supports closure, but it indicates that Kim et al.'s character measure is sensitive. Siegel et al. (2018) found that beliefs about bad agents are more uncertain and more responsive than beliefs about good agents, which challenges any general claim that negative moral representations are fixed. It bears less directly on closure, because their participants observed repeated choices, which count here as new acts. A direct comparison would need repeated evidence and a learning model.

5.3 Uncertainty and the true self

Work on the good true self sets a further constraint. People distinguish a person's deep, authentic self from their behaviour and tend to see that self as good (Newman et al., 2015). Essentialist reasoning underlies belief in a true self (Christy et al., 2019), and an essence can be understood as an ideal rather than a hidden cause (Newman & Knobe, 2019). So the theory cannot assume that predictive identity and the evaluative true self differ merely because it names them differently (Section 6).

6. What would distinguish closure from its rivals

Several established accounts produce a flat line without closure. Each curve below is one specified implementation of an account, with auxiliary assumptions. Its family could predict other curves, and a failed curve tells against that implementation alone. Figure 1b plots four alongside closure, and Table 1 summarises eight. The serious rival is graded diagnosticity updating, in its person-centred (Uhlmann et al., 2015) and Bayesian forms (Walker et al., 2022). A confident prior or weak likelihood ratios can make its predicted decline in identity shallow. That flexibility makes the comparison difficult, since a prior free to vary could fit almost any flat line. For this reason the likelihoods are elicited independently, and the open model may still estimate small or zero effects. Two comparators belong to the same family, one adapted from inference about hidden situations and capability (Davis et al., 2025), the other from inference about latent structure that allows abrupt classification and slow reversal (Li et al., 2026).

The restrictive schema (Reeder & Brewer, 1979) predicts little discounting until the situation would drive even a moral person to the act. Reeder and Brewer did not specify where that point falls, so the implementation specified here places it at Level 6. The confirmability asymmetry of Rothbart and Park (1986) makes the same prediction for the trait and none for projection to other domains. Capacity or willingness inference (Reeder, 2009) holds that a coerced act shows what the person is able and willing to do, so same-pressure prediction should stay high at Level 6 while unrelated-domain projection falls. In a motive-inference implementation, threat or reluctance changes the motive inferred for yielding even when the immediate aim is fixed (Reeder, 2009; Carlson et al., 2022), so identity can move with inferred motive, whereas closure predicts that it stays flat even when inferred motive improves. Two features address this possibility, a measure of self-presentation concern taken before the scenario and a format that separates causal explanation from endorsement. The planned comparison will also fit compounds of rivals, and none is assumed to fail.

Predictive identity must also be separated from the true self. Predictive identity supports forecasts across situations. An observer who learns that Alex objected in writing can think better of her true self while still expecting dishonesty from her in other settings. Under closure, true-self ratings should rise with excuse strength while predictive identity stays flat. If true-self ratings stay as flat as predictive identity at Level 6, where the agent is normed as not endorsing the act, the predicted divergence fails, which alone does not make the two constructs identical. If both rise, predictive identity has moved. That is evidence against P1.

8. The Context Integration Task

>The Context Integration Task

8.1 Design

The Context Integration Task presents a violation normed as deliberate, diagnostic and intended to harm, followed by one of six levels of excuse assigned between participants. Level 1 supplies no excuse, and Levels 2 to 5 supply progressively stronger pressure, threat or constraint. Level 6 supplies credible severe coercion with explicit reluctance and non-endorsement. Norming must show that observers do not see the agent as identifying with the act (Woolfolk et al., 2006). P2 Representation Identity measures add information beyond the trait, global evaluation, same-pressure prediction and the evaluative true self.

P3 Process

An excuse has greater influence on identity before encoding than after, beyond calibrated ordinary order effects.

P4 to P7 Extension Deliberation (P4), act valence (P5), domain (P6) and delay (P7) each yield a further prediction that rests on added assumptions (Supplementary Section S3).

Levels 2 to 6 are each normed as an excuse in the sense of Section 3.3. Each participant judges four strangers, one in each of four moral domains (honesty, and the fairness, harm and sanctity foundations of Graham et al., 2013). Two positive-control arms should move identity. In the reinterpretation arm, later information supplies a morally good purpose that norming confirms changes whether the act was wrong. In the retraction arm, it establishes that the allegation was false or wrongly attributed, and a check confirms that participants accepted it. The main further conditions are a no-violation baseline, a low-diagnosticity condition and, to test P3, a condition that gives the excuse before the violation.

Identity and unrelated-domain projection are co-primary outcomes and are measured first. Causal recognition, blame, the domain-specific trait, same-pressure prediction, global evaluation, the evaluative true self and inferred motive are measured separately. So are factual credibility, normative mitigation (whether the information excuses and reduces responsibility), recategorisation (whether it changes wrongness) and forgiveness, since an appeal to who the agent really is may seek forgiveness rather than excuse (Attie-Picker, 2026). The main analyses use no post-treatment selection. Prior positivity, the need for cognitive closure and Trait Judgmentalism are measured before the scenario as covariates and candidate moderators. The formal sequence of Section 3, successive excuses about one person, is tested directly only by a same-target sequence with a comparison group that gives only a final rating (Supplementary Section S3.6). Supplementary Section S1 gives the full specification.

8.2 Decision rule

The test examines the whole trajectory of identity and unrelated-domain projection across the six excuse levels, not an average slope. Across those levels, each may vary by no more than a small margin fixed before confirmatory data are collected (Section 9.1). Every result is classed as invariance, revision or inconclusive. The claim concerns average effects for the declared population of observers and scenarios. Population-general support also requires a scenariolevel criterion, so that opposite-signed changes across scenarios cannot cancel out and mimic invariance. No verdict in either direction is returned unless the norming preconditions hold: the violation produces an identity attribution, each excuse level raises normative mitigation above the level below, and observers do not see the Level 6 agent as identifying with the act. A verdict also requires the uptake check, a rise in causal recognition from Level 1 to Level 6, showing that the strongest excuse was taken up. Any verdict further requires that factual credibility ratings at every level and normative mitigation ratings at Levels 2 to 6 show observers believe the excuse and credit it as mitigating, so causal uptake alone cannot reject the account. A failed blame check, in which blame does not fall, only withholds support. Supplementary Section S1.8 gives the full rule.

8.3 What would reject the account

The critical prediction has two parts, and each part is tested only while the preconditions and the uptake, credibility and mitigation checks of Section 8.2 are met. First, identity and unrelateddomain projection must stay practically invariant across the six excuse levels. Meaningful revision of either, under excuses that observers credit as mitigating, rejects the account outright. Second, in the accepted-retraction arm and in the normed-reinterpretation arm, identity and unrelated-domain projection must each fall below their Level 1 means by more than the prespecified margin. Closure is rejected as the explanation of the dissociation if, in either arm, either construct stays practically invariant or rises by more than that margin. If both arms fail, the pattern favours strict belief perseverance or continued influence. If only the reinterpretation arm fails, the scope rule of Section 3.3 is rejected as well. If only the retraction arm fails, the measure has proved sensitive but the withdrawn accusation persists, so the pattern favours its continued influence (Cohen et al., 2024). An inconclusive result in either part withholds support for closure without rejecting it. A failed construct-separation test under P2, such as a true self that stays as flat as predictive identity when the agent does not endorse the act, does not reject the dissociation. It removes that evidence for a distinct identity construct, and a trait-level description is preferred only if the measurement and incremental prediction tests of Section 7 also favour it.

9. Limitations and implications

>Limitations and implications

9.1 Limitations

The proposal has five serious limitations. (1) No direct test exists, and the public data reanalysed here can neither test nor support the account (Section 5.2). (2) Because a flexible open model contains strict closure as a special case, cross-validated predictive comparison cannot return decisive support for closure even when closure is true. Evidence for closure must come from the trajectory test of Section 8.2. Where open and closed models are indistinguishable, the mechanism is reported as unresolved. (3) The prespecified margin is a fifth of a baseline standard deviation (0.20) for the full range across levels. It is proposed without derivation, and the claim is invariance at that resolution, not an exact zero. (4) The measures are candidate items that require validation for content, response processes, reliability and measurement invariance (Supplementary Section S1.7). (5) Generality across cultures is open. Causal attribution matters less to blame for interdependent observers (Park et al., 2026), and close relationships or group membership may alter both encoding and updating. So may prior trust. These factors should be investigated prospectively and should not be invoked to explain away a failed test.

9.2 Implications

What follows is conditional on the account surviving its test. A test of relabelling would need few new materials. Person-first labels for people who have committed sexual offences softened attitudes towards the category but did not change judgements of named individuals (Harper et al., 2025). Extended beyond excuses, closure predicts that relabelling after encoding changes expressed affect but not identity or unrelated-domain projection, whereas relabelling before encoding reaches identity and projection too (Supplementary Section S3). If closure held beyond one session, an employer given a fuller explanation of an applicant's offence might keep the same expectations of the applicant, and whether such expectations matter for reintegration needs direct tests of contact, hiring and support.

The account is descriptive. On a situationist view of character (Doris, 2002), conduct under strong situational pressure is weak evidence of robust traits, so an observer who accepts Alex's excuse and still judges her a dishonest person would, on that view, be making an error. Doris (2006 Doris ( /2021) ) uses a situationist strategy to argue that the moral psychology underlying the character theory of excuses in legal philosophy is empirically inadequate. Whether closure is such an error or a defensible stance is a separate normative question, which the account informs by specifying which information stops reaching the judgement of the person, and when.

A second normative view points the other way. On Watson's (1996) account, a knowing wrong can disclose what an agent is prepared to do, so an observer who still judges Alex a dishonest person may not be in error, and closure would describe a sound response to the evidence. This view joins two questions that the account keeps apart: whether the judgement of the person is warranted, and whether later mitigation can reach that judgement at all. Even a warranted judgement could respond to an excuse, and a closed judgement could persist without warrant. Alex faced a real but ordinary threat. At Level 6, the agent instead faces credible severe coercion and is normed as not identifying with the act. Watson's own view may still count such a knowing wrong as disclosing what the agent is prepared to do. On the identification reading assumed here, however, attributability should fall there, because an act the agent does not endorse does not express what the agent values, and an observer tracking attributability should think better of the agent. Closure predicts that the identity judgement holds at Level 6 as at the lower levels. If that pattern appears, observers would not be tracking attributability on the identification reading.

10. Conclusion

I have proposed that an accepted excuse can change how an observer explains what Alex did without changing who she is taken to be. On this proposal, the observer would blame her less and still would not trust her with the next set of accounts. The claim concerns reach, and the Context Integration Task could reject it. Section 8.3 states the two results that would count against it. If a model in which excuses still make a small update fits the data as well, the question stays open. An inconclusive result would reject nothing. If closure holds beyond a single session, a full explanation of what someone did may leave unchanged what others expect of them in settings such as hiring or parole.
