To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 10 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. Abstract

This study explores the effects of multilingualism on self-esteem and linguistic identity among individuals living in the UK. Although the cognitive advantages of multilingualism are extensively proven for children and adolescents, its impact on socio-emotional aspects, especially self-esteem and identity in adults, is inadequately investigated. The current study aims to fill this gap using a quantitative methodology, utilising data gathered from 50 participants representing diverse age, gender, ethnic, educational, and linguistic backgrounds, implementing the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) and a Linguistic Identity Questionnaire (LQ). The primary argument put forward was that, multilingualism would improve self-esteem and foster a richer distinctive language identity. Descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, ANOVA, and independent sample t-tests were employed to investigate the relation among multilingualism, self-esteem, and linguistic identity, including differences among different language groups and sub-constructs of the assessments. The findings suggested no significant direct correlation between multilingualism and self-esteem; however, a noteworthy inverse relationship was identified between the 'Language and Social Status' sub-domain of linguistic identity and self-esteem, inferring that, individuals who associated higher social status to a language other than their native language experienced reduced self-esteem. These results indicate that societal perceptions of a language's prestige can negatively impact self-esteem, especially for native speakers of languages deemed less socially valuable. The hypothesis that multilingualism improves self-esteem was not entirely proven; nonetheless, the study elucidates the impact of linguistic identification on self-esteem, especially for the subcategories that constitute Linguistic identity. The limitations encompass the restricted sample size and reliance 19283975 6 on self-reported data. Future study may investigate these processes longitudinally with bigger, more varied samples to further understand the socio-emotional implications of multilingualism.

Language is a complex element of communication that can mean different things depending on one's perspective. On the one hand, language serves as a social tool, meaning that it is what sociolinguists call a vehicle of culture, a group marker for ethnic and national identity, and an important trait passed down from one generation to another within a community. Additionally, language also serves as a natural mode of communication, facilitating the exchange of information and the formation and expression of speakers' ideas, thoughts, opinions, emotions, attitudes, values, beliefs, and relationships. Language is not only a part of a culture and/or an instrument of power and pride, but also a means of performing, playing roles, and confirming one's own identity. In that same context, when a person knows more than one language, it is termed multilingualism. It is not only an individual or social matter, but it is also concerned with speakers' desire and need to create, maintain, and use both native and nonnative (additional) languages in different circumstances. As the world becomes more connected, knowing and speaking more than one language has become an important part of many people's lives. Past study has shown that speaking more than one language can bring about cognitive benefits such as improved memory, problem-solving skills, and executive function (Mercer, 2011). But not as much research has been done on its social and emotional effects, especially on self-esteem and identity.

Multilingualism, broadly defined as the ability to communicate proficiently in more than one language, has grown significantly in today's societies due to factors such as migration, education, and increased cross-cultural interactions (Bialystok, 2011). Numerous studies conducted over the years have highlighted the various cognitive and social advantages this phenomenon brings. However, beyond its cognitive implications, multilingualism has a profound psychological influence, particularly in shaping an individual's self-esteem and identity (Grosjean, 2010). While some studies have discussed benefits of multilingualism, such as improved executive function and cognitive flexibility (Bialystok, 2011), the impact of this phenomenon on self-perception remains uninvestigated, especially in adult populations, which may be less prominent than in younger individuals, whose identities and self-concepts are still in the process of formation. Orth and Robins (2014) found that self-esteem tends to stabilise in adulthood, making it less susceptible to external factors like language proficiency. Languagebased social position and identity issues can still affect self-esteem in adults, especially in multicultural countries like the ones we live in now (Lee, 2008). For example, Amer (2016) found that people who were bilingual had different levels of self-esteem based on how well they were able to interact with people who spoke more than one language. So, it appears like the relation between being proficient in more than one language and self-esteem is influenced by things that have nothing to do with a person's cultural background or the specifics of their linguistic skills (Cummins, 2001). For example, individuals who feel pride in their multilingual capabilities and receive positive reinforcement for such skills are likely to experience enhanced self-esteem. Conversely, people who think their native language is less important or who are discriminated against because of their language may feel less worthy of themselves (Matika et al., 2021). Based on what we know from earlier research, it seems like most of these findings are associated with multilingual children and adolescents and there still remains a question of how being multilingual affects adults' daily lives, sense of self-worth, and identity formation (Reyes, 2006; Spernes, 2012; Orth and Robin, 2014).

This study focuses on understanding different aspects of multilingualism, such as linguistic proficiency and language use, as well as its effect on self-esteem and linguistic identity. Self-esteem, as conceptualised by Rosenberg (1965), reflects an individual's overall sense of personal worth and self-regard. Various factors, such as personal experiences, social interactions, and cultural context, can influence self-esteem. High self-esteem is often associated with positive psychological outcomes, such as increased resilience, greater life satisfaction, and better mental health. Conversely, low self-esteem can lead to negative outcomes, including anxiety, depression, and a diminished sense of belonging (Brown & Dutton, 1995; Orth & Robins, 2014). The relationship between the individual, the environment, and self-worth, along with their social interactions, is central to various psychological theories.

Each of those theories emphasises the significance of environmental influences and interpersonal relationships, as well as the role of an individual's cognitive and perceptive activities in their development (Leary & Baumeister, 2000). Numerous approaches have evolved over time, providing potential solutions to the issue of lower self-esteem in adults.

Many researchers have suggested that enhancing self-perception or a sense of identity is crucial for fostering higher self-esteem (Orth & Robins, 2014). Social interactions, an essential condition for human existence and growth, shape these individualistic perceptions, which in turn affect the development of self-esteem (Rosenberg, 1965). In addition, the degree of positive reinforcement, joy, and warmth experienced during social exchanges largely determines the quality of self-esteem, with linguistic capabilities playing a critical role in driving these interactions. Harter's (2012) research supports this idea by highlighting how language proficiency can enhance social competence, which in turn affects the quality of social interactions. Harter specifically studied how children and adolescents develop self-esteem and found that those with higher language and communication skills are more capable of forming meaningful social relationships, leading to higher self-esteem. By being better equipped to express themselves and understand others, individuals with stronger linguistic abilities can foster more positive and enriching social exchanges, which contributes to a more solid and positive sense of self (Harter, 2012). Furthermore, the nature of self-esteem may differ depending on the social contexts that forms one's identity. A key factor in shaping these selfconcepts is an individual's family, which plays a significant educational role, influencing both children and adults through models of self-evaluation and acceptance, often rooted in the behaviours of parents and other significant figures in their lives. In multilingual households, this influence extends to language use, where the family's support of multilingualism can positively impact both self-esteem and linguistic identity by reinforcing the value of diverse linguistic skills (Cummins, 2001).

Linguistic identity refers to how individuals perceive themselves in relation to their language use and cultural background, influencing their social interactions and personal sense of belonging (Grosjean, 2010). When considering identity formation, it is common to perceive it as a singular and indivisible concept that revolves around one's personality. However, an individual's consciousness involves multiple roles, allowing a person to identify as a human being across various contexts. Thus, it is important to conceptualise 'identity' in plural terms meaning there are various sub-types of identities that one can recognise with. Aspects such as ethnic identity, gender identity, age, and status consistently reinforce an individual's selfrecognition perceived by others (Edwards, 2009). When it comes to linguistic identity in particular, it probably stays more stable than other dimensions of identity, since language tends to stay the same within a homogeneous community and is difficult for speakers to change rapidly because of specific laws and social rules associated with that community (Joseph, 19283975 10 2004). Furthermore, acquiring a second language in adulthood for professional or educational reasons does not automatically disqualify an individual from their native language community, thereby influencing their linguistic identity in the long run (Grosjean, 2010).

As seen till now, it looks like social interactions and the environment plays a crucial role in shaping both self-esteem and identity. Research has shown that self-esteem is strongly influenced by family dynamics, peer relationships, and broader social experiences (Leary & Baumeister, 2000). Similarly, linguistic identity is also formed through social relations, especially in multilingual settings where language use shows one's role and feeling of belonging in various groups (Edwards, 2009; Grosjean, 2010). Like self-esteem, linguistic identity gives people a sense of belonging and is usually steady because of the rules that guide how people use language (Joseph, 2004). But for people who speak more than one language, identity and self-esteem may become unstable based on how they navigate within different ethnic and linguistic situations. For instance, Norton (2011) inferred that language learning significantly impacts identity formation by shaping how individuals view themselves and their role within a society. She introduces the concept of "investment" in language learning, which extends beyond motivation to include the idea that learners invest in a language with the hope that it will grant them access to the social networks and resources necessary to shape and affirm their identity.

This investment is directly linked to power dynamics, as learners frequently navigate unequal relationships that limit their access to language and social opportunities. Similarly, Reyes (2006) highlights that multilingual individuals often navigate complex linguistic and cultural environments, which can either enhance or challenge their self-esteem depending on how they manage these diverse situations. In her study, Reyes examined Southeast Asian American youth and found that their frequent code-switching (switching between languages depending on the social context) was not just a linguistic tool but also a way to navigate social power and express belonging in different communities. The study showed that while some youth used codeswitching effectively to maintain connections to both their heritage and mainstream culture, others faced challenges, such as linguistic discrimination, which negatively impacted their selfesteem. This evidence supported Reyes' assertion that identity is fluid and shaped by multiple social, political, and cultural factors, and that language plays a central role in this process.

However, despite these insights, there is still a noticeable gap in research focusing on the selfesteem and linguistic identity constructs of multilingual individuals and their monolingual counterparts, particularly when data is directly collected from a diverse population.

Being multilingual isn't just a language skill; it's also a key part of how people get along with others. Since self-esteem is closely connected to how people see their own skills and how others see them (Leary & Baumeister, 2000), being able to communicate in more than one language can have both positive and bad effects on self-worth. One the one hand, being able to function well and express in more than one language can give a person a sense of competence and social connections, which can boost their self-esteem. On the other hand, dealing with different languages and the social expectations that come with those linguistic capabilities can cause internal conflict that can adversely affect self-esteem, especially if someone feels like their native language is devalued (Khatib and Rezaei, 2013). The specific effects of multilingualism on an individual's self-esteem and identity rests on a lot of things, like how well they speak the languages, how people in their community feel about them, and their own personal experiences. Thus, while multilingualism has the potential to positively influence selfesteem by enhancing cognitive and social flexibility, it can also negatively affect individuals who feel marginalized due to their linguistic identity.

A lot of research that has been conducted predominantly focuses on specific ethnic or cultural groups or even bounds within an age range; for instance, Cummins (2001) focused on the role of bilingualism in children's development, arguing that maintaining a strong connection to one's mother tongue is crucial for fostering a positive self-concept in multilingual youth. Similarly, Amer (2016) showed that bilingual adults in Arabic-English contexts could maintain high self-esteem when their cultural identity was supported within their social environments. While, Lee (2008) found that adults with greater confidence in their bilingual skills tended to exhibit higher self-esteem in social interactions, Dewaele and van Oudenhoven (2009) explored the effects of multilingualism and multiculturalism on personality traits, finding that multilingual individuals often display higher levels of openness and emotional regulation. However, these studies have often focused on transitional life stages, such as students or migrants, neglecting those individuals whose self-esteem and linguistic identity may have already been shaped by earlier experiences. For example, Dewaele and Wei (2012) found that multilingual adults showed increased empathy but did not address how this empathy is related to long-term self-esteem outcomes.

While these earlier researches provide valuable insights, they don't really discuss much about how the adult community is affected. This study aims to fill some of these gaps by not only investigating effects of being proficient in more than one language but also specific subconstruct of identity and their relationship with self-esteem. By analysing data from a diverse group of participants, this study will try to provide a more detailed understanding of how multilingualism might influence personal development in terms of their self-worth and identity.

Given their ability to navigate multiple cultural and linguistic settings, we expect participants who are proficient in more than one language to demonstrate higher self-esteem 19283975 13 and well-defined linguistic identities. The analysis will also explore how factors such as language proficiency and/or attachment to one's language influence these relationships. Based on these assumptions, we can formulate the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1 • Null Hypothesis (H0): Multilingualism has no significant effect on the self-esteem of individuals.

• Alternative Hypothesis (H1): Multilingualism significantly enhances the self-esteem of individuals.

Hypothesis 2

• Null Hypothesis (H0): Multilingualism has no significant effect on the linguistic identity of individuals.

• Alternative Hypothesis (H1): Multilingualism significantly affects the linguistic identity of individuals.

Hypothesis 3

• Null Hypothesis (H0): Socio-cultural factors, such as language proficiency, do not moderate the relationship between multilingualism, self-esteem, and linguistic identity.

• Alternative Hypothesis (H1): Socio-cultural factors, particularly language proficiency, moderate the relationship between multilingualism, self-esteem, and linguistic identity, with higher proficiency leading to increased self-esteem and a more complex linguistic identity.

Methodology Participants

The research included 50 participants aged 18 and above, living in the UK. All of them were individuals proficient in English, either as their first language or as an additional language.

The sample consisted of 29 women, 19 men, and 2 people who identified as otherwise or chose not to disclose their gender. Of the recruited individuals, 15 identified as monolingual (English being the only language they are fluent in), 8 claimed English as their first language, while the remaining 27 reported a different first language. The ethnic makeup comprised 13 participants identifying as White, 4 as Black or African/Caribbean, 28 as Asian, and 2 identifying as mixed or opting not to disclose their ethnicity. Participants possessed educational qualifications ranging from secondary education to graduate and professional degrees.

Design

The study utilises a quantitative approach, including self-administered surveys for data collection. The independent variable is multilingualism, whereas the dependent variables are self-esteem and linguistic identity. The objective is to evaluate the influence of multilingualism on these constructs. This design corresponds with the research aim of investigating the socioemotional impacts of multilingualism.

It is important to note that while the study initially aimed to include both multilingual and monolingual participants, data for monolingual participants was exclusively gathered for the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (multilingual individuals were administered both the tests), as the Linguistic Identity Questionnaire was deemed irrelevant for this cohort. The enquiries in the Linguistic Identity Questionnaire focused predominantly on the utilisation, comprehension, exposure to, and identification with more than one language, which monolingual participants would not have been able to address meaningfully. Consequently, no linguistic identity data was gathered for monolingual people, and the comparison of self-esteem between multilinguals and monolinguals relied exclusively on self-esteem ratings, devoid of any language identity evaluation.

Data Collection

Data was gathered via Qualtrics, an online survey platform. The survey, requiring around 10 minutes for completion, was organised as follows: 1.

Demographic Information:

Participants provided basic demographic details, including age, gender, nationality, and native language(s). The questions also gathered information about language proficiency and frequency of language use forming a linguistic profile that was used later for understanding how these constructs also affect self-esteem and/or identity.

2. Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES):

All the participants completed the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (Rosenberg, 1965), a 10item scale designed to measure global self-worth. Each item was answered using a 4-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from ''strongly agree'' rated as '4' to ''strongly disagree'' rated as '1'. The total score was obtained by summing the responses for all ten items, with five items (Q2, Q5, Q6, Q8, Q9) reverse-scored to account for negatively worded statements. The final scores range from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating higher levels of self-esteem. A higher score reflects a greater sense of personal worth and positive self-regard, while lower scores suggest lower self-esteem. The scale is widely recognised for its reliability and validity in measuring self-esteem across diverse populations (Rosenberg, 1965).

3. Linguistic Identity Questionnaire:

A modified version of the linguistic identity questionnaire by Khatib and Rezaei (2013) was used to assess participants' language identity. It was only assessed on multilingual individuals. The adaptation process involved adjusting cultural references and modifying certain questions to suit a broader, non-Persian population, shifting focus from Persian and English language dynamics only to a more generalised view of native and additional languages.

The 20-item questionnaire used a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree,6 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 20 to 120. It had twelve items (Q2, Q3, Q5, Q6, Q7, Q8, Q12, Q15, Q16, Q17, Q19, and Q20) reverse-scored to account for negatively worded statements. Higher scores indicated a stronger attachment to the participant's native language and a greater role for language in shaping social identity, while lower scores suggested weaker identification with the native language (Khatib and Rezaei, 2013).

Procedure

Participants were encouraged to take the survey at their convenience. The informed consent procedure was carried out online, ensuring that participants comprehended the study's objectives, potential risks, and their choice to withdraw at any moment. The short duration of the survey (10 minutes) was anticipated to result in reduced dropout rates. All participants' data was obtained anonymously and securely maintained in accordance with university ethics rules.

The Psychology Research Ethics Committee at Oxford Brookes University authorised the project.

After the data was collected, firstly, reliability analyses were performed to check the internal consistency of both the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale and the Linguistic Identity Questionnaire. Subsequently, the data was evaluated utilising Descriptive Statistics to include participant demographics and essential characteristics, including self-esteem and linguistic identity scores. Correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationships among multilingualism, self-esteem, and linguistic identity. An ANOVA was conducted to examine variations in self-esteem among monolinguals, English first-language speakers, and speakers of other first languages. Additionally, independent sample t-tests were conducted to compare self-esteem and linguistic identity scores between language groups. Finally, a correlation analysis was conducted for each subconstruct of linguistic identity to evaluate its association with self-esteem, revealing that one subconstruct had a significant link.

Discussion

This study aimed at examining the relationship between multilingualism, self-esteem, and linguistic identity among a group of individuals residing in the UK. The primary hypothesis predicted that multilingualism would enhance self-esteem and contribute to a more complex and well-defined linguistic identity. Although the results did not entirely confirm this hypothesis, they provided substantial insights into the socio-emotional aspects of multilingualism, particularly for specific sub-constructs of linguistic identity and self-esteem.

The analysis revealed no significant direct relationship between multilingualism and selfesteem, as shown in the correlation analysis. However, an intriguing trend arose within the subconstruct of ''Language and Social Status,'' demonstrating a statistically significant inverse correlation with self-esteem. This finding suggests that individuals who correlate their native language with inferior social position are likely to report reduced self-esteem. Conversely, there were not much noticeable significant differences in self-esteem between monolinguals and multilinguals, nor between individuals who identified English as their first language and those with another first language. The ANOVA and independent samples t-tests revealed no significant differences in self-esteem or linguistic identity across the various language groups.

No significant association was seen between overall linguistic identity and self-esteem, except for the particular sub-domain of Linguistic Identity, 'Language and Social Status'.

The absence of a clear correlation between multilingualism and self-esteem contradicts earlier research suggesting that multilingualism enhances self-esteem through cognitive flexibility, improved problem-solving abilities, and greater social competence (Mercer, 2011).

This indicates that multilingualism, by itself, may not directly influence self-esteem in adult populations. A possible explanation for this may be the stability of adult self-esteem, wherein elements outside linguistic capabilities, such as personal and professional experiences, might exert a more significant influence. Given that adults' self-esteem is often shaped by a wider array of life experiences (Orth & Robins, 2014), language competency may hold less significance in their self-worth. These findings highlight the not so simple relationship between multilingualism and self-esteem and suggests that language does not inherently dictate selfesteem in adulthood.

The most notable finding in this study was the inverse correlation between the 'Language and Social Status' sub-construct of linguistic identity and self-esteem. This finding indicates that those who perceive other languages, especially English, as conferring more social status than their native language often experiences diminished self-esteem. This corresponds with Khatib and Rezaei's (2013) linguistic identity model, which explains that perceptions of language, particularly about social status, may profoundly influence an individual's identity and self-esteem. Individuals who internalise the notion that embracing a prominent language such as English can enhance their social capital may encounter a reduced feeling of self-esteem if their original language is regarded as inferior. This discovery also supports Norton's (2011) proposal that language acquisition and identity development are intricately linked to power relations. Multilingual individuals frequently encounter uneven social dynamics that might impact their self-esteem, especially when they perceive marginalisation due to their linguistic background. Moreover, the findings correspond with Reyes' (2006) research on Southeast Asian American adolescents, indicating that code-switching between languages served not just as a linguistic mechanism but also as a strategy for negotiating social power and articulating a sense of belonging. Consistent with her research, this study revealed that individuals who associated higher social status to other language proficiencies than their native language, had internal conflicts between their linguistic identity and self-esteem. The adverse correlation between 'Language and Social Status' and self-esteem indicates that social language hierarchies may diminish the self-worth of multilingual individuals who regard their first/native language as less valuable.

The findings of this study further contribute to the ongoing discussion of how multilingualism affects identity and self-esteem. While previous research has shown that multilingual individuals may exhibit greater adaptability and emotional regulation (Dewaele & Wei, 2012), this study highlights the complexity of these relationships. It posits that particular elements of linguistic identity, such as the perceived social status linked to language use, may exert a more significant influence on self-esteem than the overall concept of linguistic identity.

This subtlety enhances the theoretical understanding of the socio-emotional effects of multilingualism. The results emphasise the need of acknowledging the social environment in which language is utilised. Norton (2011) highlighted that language acquisition and identity development are influenced by power relations and access to social networks and resources.

This study found that participants who saw their native language as undervalued in society had diminished self-esteem, suggesting that cultural attitudes towards language significantly influence self-worth. This calls for a for a more detailed understanding of the formation of linguistic identity in heterogeneous communities, where language hierarchies can affect people's feeling of belonging and self-worth.

There are also some practical implications that can be drawn from this investigation.

Programs focused on education and community that foster linguistic parity and instil pride in one's native language may alleviate the negative impacts of language-related social status on self-esteem. For instance, programs that challenge cultural perceptions of language dominance and highlight the importance of multilingualism can promote good linguistic identity formation.

This approach is also endorsed by Reyes ' (2006) proposal to foster positive identity development via language pride, especially for those navigating diverse cultural and linguistic environments. Moreover, organisations and educational institutions might benefit from acknowledging the significance of linguistic identity in influencing individuals' socioemotional outcomes. By cultivating an inclusive atmosphere that appreciates linguistic variety, these institutions can enhance the development of positive self-esteem and linguistic identity, especially for those who may feel inferior because of their language background.

Despite the insights discovered in this study, there are certain limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, the restricted sample size may have constrained the study's statistical capacity to identify more detailed and intricate relationships between the variables. Future research should try to replicate these findings with bigger and more diverse samples to comprehensively include the spectrum of multilingual experiences. Furthermore, since the assessments used to evaluate self-esteem and linguistic identity were reported by the participants themselves, the reliance on such self-report measures can potentially introduce bias, including social desirability bias, wherein individuals may provide socially acceptable responses instead of their authentic thoughts and opinions. Moreover, this study emphasised a quantitative analysis; however, future research might need to integrate alternative methodologies that align with the design and results of the study to check if different results can be attained. Ultimately, the cross-sectional design of this study prevents the understanding of causal inferences between the variables. Longitudinal research may give more clarity on the evolution of multilingualism and language identity over time and its impact on self-esteem at various life stages.

In conclusion, this study illustrates how particular elements of linguistic identity, especially the perception of social status associated with language use, might adversely affect self-esteem. While no direct correlation between multilingualism and self-esteem was identified, the results indicate that social language hierarchies significantly influence the selfworth of multilingual individuals. Further study is required to investigate these relationships and to develop interventions that promote good linguistic identity formation in multilingual populations.
