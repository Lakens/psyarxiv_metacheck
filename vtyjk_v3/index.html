Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

While theoretical accounts of action imagery emphasize the role of the actor performing the movement, it remains unclear whether self-related kinesthetic imagery (selfimagery) compared to object-related visual imagery (object-imagery) supports mental transformations similarly for body-related and non-body stimuli. To clarify this relationship, 142 participants completed the mental paper folding task and the mental body rotation task under two imagery instructions: based on self-imagery or objectimagery. Instructions successfully shifted subjective imagery experiences, with selfimagery emphasizing bodily sensations and object-imagery emphasizing visualization.

Performance analyses revealed robust task-specific effects: mental paper folding performance decreased with the number of folds and direction changes, and mental body rotation performance decreased for larger (than smaller) rotations, for front-view (than back-view) stimuli and for foot (than hand) stimuli. Crucially, errors did not significantly differ between imagery instructions. For response times, however, the relative advantage of the instructions was task-specific. Response times were shorter under self-imagery in mental body rotations but shorter under object-imagery in mental paper folding. Yet, in mental paper folding, this effect varied with instruction order, which might reflect a carryover effect of self-referential processing from self-imagery to object-imagery. Cross-task response times resulted in medium correlations when imagery instructions matched, whereas error rate correlations showed no consistent pattern. Overall, the findings indicate that the advantage of self-imagery versus objectimagery depends on task demands. They are consistent with flexible recruitment of imagery processes, while leaving open whether this reflects different configurations of a shared system or partly distinct motor and visuospatial strategies.

Introduction

Mental imagery, the simulation of sensory experiences in the absence of direct perceptual input, plays a central role in a variety of cognitive and motor functions (Kosslyn et al., 1984; Thompson & Kosslyn, 2000). Within motor cognition, action imagery (also referred to as action simulation or motor imagery; Moreno-Verdú et al., 2024) is commonly conceptualized as the internal simulation of an action "as if" executed by oneself, relying on motor representations that overlap with those involved in actual movement (Hétu et al., 2013; Jeannerod, 2001). This raises a fundamental theoretical question of whether action imagery depends on representing oneself as the acting agent, or whether action-related transformations can also be performed from an object-centered perspective without an explicit self-actor (Callow et al., 2013; Dahm & Sachse, 2025). This distinction is particularly relevant because self-performed imagery is typically accompanied by kinesthetic sensations, whereas object-centered imagery can emphasize visual representations of an action or its effects without representing an actor (Chholak et al., 2019; Cumming & Eaves, 2018; Guillot et al., 2009; Naito et al., 2002; Steggemann et al., 2011).

Embodied cognition provides a framework for reconciling these perspectives.

Bodily experience can constrain and shape action representations without requiring the self to be explicitly represented as the actor (Barsalou, 2010; Wilson, 2002).

Accordingly, action imagery may range from strongly embodied, kinesthetic simulations centered on one's own body to more visually dominated, object-centered simulations that track an action or its effects. This distinction also corresponds to partially different sensory emphases: kinesthetic imagery is more consistently associated with motor and somatosensory processing, whereas visual imagery places greater demands on visuospatial transformation mechanisms (Guillot et al., 2009; Kilintari et al., 2016). Importantly, these differences need not imply categorically separate imagery systems. Instead, self-and object-imagery may represent different configurations within a partially shared imagery architecture, with the relative contribution of embodied and visuospatial processes depending on the actor represented and the task demands (Van Caenegem et al., 2024). Consistent with this flexible account, recent evidence indicates that even the temporal dynamics of action imagery vary with characteristics of the represented agent and action, with imagery unfolding differently for human versus robotic actions as a function of action duration (Viviani et al., 2026). The present study therefore contrasts two instructional sets that differ jointly in agency and sensory emphasis: kinesthetic self-imagery and visual object-imagery. The manipulation is intended to shift the dominant perspective and sensory experience rather than to create completely isolated imagery modalities.

Because agency and sensory emphasis are jointly manipulated, the present design does not isolate these dimensions experimentally. Rather, it tests the consequences of adopting a self-related, kinesthetic versus object-centered, visually oriented imagery set.

Mental paper folding and mental body rotation provide complementary tests of this distinction. Mental body rotation requires the transformation of a body representation and therefore provides a relatively strong context for self-related and motor-based processing. Mental paper folding, in contrast, requires sequential visuospatial transformations but does not inherently specify an acting agent; the paper can be mentally transformed either by imagining oneself performing the folds or by tracking the changing object configuration from an object-centered perspective (Dahm & Sachse, 2025). Thus, the two tasks allow us to ask whether the consequences of self-versus object-imagery depend on the representational demands of the task. If self-related embodied processing is generally advantageous for action imagery, selfimagery should facilitate performance in both tasks. Alternatively, if imagery representations are flexibly configured according to task demands, the effects of agency should differ between body-centered and object-centered transformations. A second primary question concerned the relationship between performance across the two tasks. If self-and object-imagery rely on partially distinct representational configurations, performance should be more strongly associated across tasks when the same imagery perspective is used than when different perspectives are used. Such agency-congruent associations would provide evidence that the distinction between self-and object-imagery extends beyond task-specific performance effects.

Mental paper folding (Dahm & Draxler, 2022) and mental body rotation (Dahm et al., 2022) provide complementary tests of this distinction. Alternatively, if imagery representations are flexibly configured according to task demands, the relative advantage of self-versus objectimagery should differ between the two tasks, with self-imagery expected to be more beneficial for mental body rotation than for mental paper folding.

As a secondary individual-differences question, we also examined whether selfreported imagery vividness (Dahm, 2022; Roberts et al., 2008) is related to objective performance. Previous research has reported weak or inconsistent associations between vividness ratings and behavioral measures of imagery (Czilczer, Martini, et al., 2026; Czilczer, Sachse, et al., 2026; Mizuguchi et al., 2019; Moreau et al., 2010; Suggate, 2024; Tabi et al., 2022). We therefore expected vividness to show, at most, weak associations with performance in mental paper folding and mental body rotation.

The instructional manipulation was additionally assessed through task-specific ratings of sensory experience. We expected self-imagery to increase reports of kinesthetic and self-related sensations, whereas object-imagery should increase reports of visually representing the object or action without an actor (Dahm et al., 2025; Dahm & Rieger, 2023; Guillot et al., 2009; MacIntyre et al., 2013). Because the manipulation targets the content and perspective of imagery rather than general imagery ability, overall vividness was not expected to differ substantially between conditions.

Because both imagery instructions were administered within participants, performance may also improve from the first to the second block because of task familiarity or carryover between instructional conditions (Dahm & Draxler, 2022; Dan & Reiner, 2018; Wright et al., 2008). We therefore treated block effects as contextual factors rather than as a primary hypothesis, using them to distinguish practice-related changes from theoretically central effects of imagery instruction.

Finally, we examined whether performance was related to broader cognitive abilities that are relevant to spatial transformation. Visual interference inhibition may support the maintenance of intermediate representations during sequential transformations, whereas inhibitory control may be particularly relevant when spatial information conflicts with the required response (Amorim et al., 2006; Harris et al., 2013). Spatial intelligence may likewise contribute to visuospatial transformation performance, although previous findings suggest that such associations vary across imagery tasks (Bühner et al., 2008; Gyselinck et al., 2007; Harris et al., 2013; Shipstead & Yonehiro, 2016). These measures were therefore included as secondary individual-differences variables to assess the extent to which performance in the two imagery tasks is associated with domain-general cognitive abilities versus task-specific imagery processes.

Methods

Participants

The required sample size for correlations of r = .25 (a "small-to-medium" effect per Cohen, 1992), α = .05 and a desired power = .8, is about N = 124, exceeding the sample size required for the mixed linear models. 196 adults participated in this study.

The data of 16 participants were excluded from the analysis due to inattentiveness (infrequency > 7 in the 18-item attentive responsiveness scale; Maniaci & Rogge, 2014). Further, 38 participants were excluded due to excessive errors in Mental Paper Folding (> 40 % in 3 folds) or in Mental Body Rotations (> 40%), or implausibly short response times in more than half of the trials (< 1000 ms), or a combination of them, illustrating they were not completing the tasks as indicated. The remaining 142 participants (39 males and 103 females) were between 18 and 42 years old (M±SD = 22.8±3.9 years). They rated their imagery ability with the German version (Dahm et al., 2019) of the Vividness of Movement Imagery Questionnaire (Dahm, 2022; Roberts et al., 2008) on a scale from 1 (no image at all) to 5 (perfectly clear and vivid) indicating clear and reasonably vivid external visual imagery (third-person perspective; M±SD = 3.6±0.7), internal visual imagery (first-person perspective; M±SD = 3.8±0.8), and kinesthetic imagery (M±SD = 3.9±0.7). All participants gave informed consent and received course credit for their participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Belmont Report (The Belmont Report, 1978) and the Board for Ethical Questions in Science of the University of Innsbruck (reference number 03/2021).

Material and Procedure

The experiment ran with the online version (Mathôt & March, 2022) of OpenSesame version 3.3.14 (Mathôt et al., 2012) using JATOS (Lange et al., 2015).

Data was collected remotely. The experimental file including all stimuli and instructions is placed at the study's Open Science Framework repository (https://osf.io/2wxcn/? view_only=f3927d82ef1749e08b2fdd2823b2d0ec). Participation lasted approximately 45 minutes.

In the within-subject design, all participants performed all tasks and conditions.

The order of instructions of kinesthetic self-imagery and visual object-imagery, hereafter referred to as self-imagery and object-imagery, was counterbalanced across participants (see Figure 1). Within each instruction condition, participants first completed the Mental Paper Folding Task, followed by the Mental Body Rotation Task.

The Mental Paper Folding Task

The Mental Paper Folding Task (Dahm & Sachse, 2025; Shepard & Feng, 1972) is a visuospatial reasoning task that assesses the ability to mentally simulate the physical transformation of a two-dimensional object into a three-dimensional object.

Participants are shown squares on paper (base grids) that can be folded along the marked lines. Participants shall imagine to move the paper and align the edges as the folds are made. At the end of the imagined folding action, they judge as fast and accurately as possible whether two specified edges of the folded paper would overlap.

Participants performed the task in two conditions (counterbalanced order). In both conditions, they were told that the grid represents a piece of paper that can be folded into a cube. Two lines in the grid were highlighted (yellow) and participants were asked to evaluate whether these lines would overlap in the cube. Each trial began with a visually presented countdown from 3 to 1 seconds. The responses were given by using the 'x' key (no overlap) or the 'm' key (overlap). This mapping was visually shown on the screen with a red 'X' key on the left and a green 'M' key on the right. In the kinesthetic self-imagery condition, participants were instructed to focus on the feeling while they imagined folding the paper themselves. In the visual object-imagery condition, participants were instructed to focus on visual aspects while they imagined that the paper is folded by itself (like an invisible magical hand), i.e., a no-person perspective from the outside (visual imagery without an actor). To familiarize participants with the task, four familiarization trials were performed in each condition.

The familiarization trials differed from the main block and included feedback about errors and response time. For the main block, two stimuli were chosen for each potential combination of folds (3 Folds, 4 Folds, 5 Folds), direction changes (Fewer, More), and overlaps (Overlap, No overlap), culminating in a total of 24 trials (as in Dahm & Sachse, 2025). The order of the trials was fixed to increase differences in subsequent trials in base grids, direction changes, and number of folds (Dahm & Sachse, 2025). To avoid memory-reliance across blocks, object-imagery stimuli were horizontally and vertically mirrored versions of the self-imagery stimuli. Performance was measured with response times and errors.

The Mental Body Rotation Task

The Mental Body Rotation Task (Dahm et al., 2022) engages motor-cognitive interactions by requiring participants map a body image onto their own bodily reference frame after one of them has been mentally rotated. This draws upon action imagery, spatial perspective-taking, and limb-specific processing (Dahm & Sachse, 2025).

Participants viewed abstract, gender-neutral schematic figures of human bodies in either front or back perspectives. Each figure was rotated at one of five angles (45°, 135°, 180°, -135°, -45°; positive indicating clockwise, negative counterclockwise), and one limb (left or right arm or leg) was depicted as raised (total of 40 trials). The participants' task was to judge as quickly and accurately as possible whether the raised limb was a left or right arm or leg-responding by pressing the 'x' and 'm' key. In the kinesthetic self-imagery condition, participants were instructed to focus on the feeling while they imagined turning themselves into the depicted position and raising one's own limb. In the visual object-imagery condition, participants were instructed to focus on visual aspects while they imagined the depicted figure to turn into one's own position and lowering the limb. To familiarize participants with the task, eight familiarization trials (angle 0°) were performed in each condition. The order of the items in the main block was fixed. Items were arranged in a quasi-random sequence to minimize obvious order patterns. To avoid memory-reliance across blocks, object-imagery stimuli were horizontally and vertically mirrored versions of the self-imagery stimuli. Performance was measured with response times and error rates.

Task-specific self-ratings

After each mental paper folding condition and mental body rotation condition, participants rated agency-specific sensory experiences and imagery vividness (see Figure 1). For this, participants rated on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 9 (very much) the extent to which they perceived kinesthetic actions of their hands / feeling their own body, visual actions of their hands / seeing their own body, a visual folding of the paper (without hands) / seeing pictured person acting, and acoustic aspects of the folding. Further, they rated the vividness of mental paper folding / body rotations from 1 (no imagery) to 9 (as vivid as in reality).

The Hagen Matrices Test for spatial intelligence

The Hagen Matrices Test (Heydasch, 2014) is a non-verbal intelligence test designed to measure fluid intelligence, specifically focusing on abstract spatial reasoning, pattern recognition, and problem-solving abilities. The Hagen Matrices Test presents participants with visual 3x3 matrices in which one element is missing. The task is to select the correct element from a set of six alternatives that logically completes the pattern, based on rules such as shape transformation, rotation, or progression. The test consists of 20 items of increasing difficulty. Participants have maximally 2 minutes to solve each item. As a score for spatial intelligence, we calculated the percentage of correctly solved items.

The Corsi Task for visuospatial span capacity

The Corsi Task (Corsi, 1973; Kessels et al., 2000) assesses visuospatial span capacity which is the maximum number of sequentially presented spatial locations that an individual can successfully retain, recall, and reproduce in the correct order within short-term. Ten squares (100x100 px) were arranged irregularly on the screen. Some squares flashed yellow (700 ms) in a certain sequence, and the participants were asked to reproduce the same sequence by clicking on the respective squares. In the first two trials, two squares flashed yellow. In the third and fourth trial, three squares flashed yellow. This procedure was continued, until in the thirteenth and fourteenth trial eight squares flashed yellow. As a score for visuospatial span capacity, we summed the number of yellow squares from all correctly answered sequences (highest possible number70 = 2*(2+3+4+5+6+7+8)).

The Simon Task for visual interference inhibition

The Simon Task was constructed in analogy to the selection of the left and right arrow keys in the Mental Body Rotation Task and the Mental Paper Folding Task. Each trial (total of 40 trials) started with a fixation dot for 1000 ms. Then an arrow (1 cm) appeared either at the left (-330 px) or right (+300 px) side of the screen. The position of the arrow was irrelevant to solve the task, but either congruent or incongruent with the response (Craft & Simon, 1970; Simon & Rudell, 1967). Participants were required to press the 'x' key if the arrow indicated towards the left side and to press the 'm' key if the arrow indicated towards the right side. As a score for visual interference inhibition, we calculated the negative Simon effect (difference in response times between congruent and incongruent trials).

Data analysis

As a manipulation check, comparisons between self-imagery and objectimagery were conducted using t-tests with Holm adjusted pairwise comparisons. We examined participants' response times and errors separately in mental paper folding and mental body rotations to assess their sensitivity to changes in imagery strategy.

Additionally, we exploratively examined potential practice or carryover effects resulting from the within-subjects design. To test whether the patterns observed in the data distributions reflected an interaction between Instruction and Block, indicating possible carryover effects, we fitted separate models for mental paper folding and mental body rotations. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used for log-transformed response times from correct trials, and generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a binomial distribution and a logit link function were used for trial-level errors. For mental paper folding, Folds and Direction Changes (both grand-mean centered) were included as covariates. For mental body rotations, Limb, Perspective (effect-coded), and Rotation (absolute values divided by 100 to ensure numerical stability) served as covariates. In all models, Participant was included as a random intercept, and Instruction (self-vs. object-imagery) and Block (first vs. second) were entered as effectcoded fixed effects. We compared models including and excluding the Instruction × Block interaction (see Table 1). Including the Instruction × Block interaction significantly improved model fit only for response times in mental paper folding (p = .02), but not for response times in mental body rotations (p = .17) or for errors in both tasks (p > .4).

This indicates that the instruction effect in mental paper folding varied depending on which instruction was performed first. Statistical significance was set at p < .05. To examine associations between imagery performance, visual interference inhibition, and spatial intelligence, we calculated Pearson correlations, interpreting effect sizes of r > .10 as small, r > .30 as medium, and r > .50 as large (Cohen, 1992).

Discussion

Our study compared effects of kinesthetic self-imagery and visual objectimagery instructions in mental paper folding and mental body rotations. Participants reported a stronger feeling of the hands with kinesthetic self-imagery instructions than visual object-imagery instructions, aligning with the manipulation. In mental paper folding, responses were faster in object-imagery than self-imagery, which was modified by an interaction with the order of imagery instructions. In mental body rotations in contrast, responses were slower in object-imagery than in self-imagery. However, in both, mental paper folding and mental body rotations, errors did not significantly differ between self-imagery and object-imagery. Our findings provide evidence that instructions can modulate processing efficiency, suggesting partially dissociable mechanisms. Correlations with self-reported vividness of movement imagery, inhibitory control, spatial intelligence, and visuospatial span capacity, were small or close to zero.

Self-ratings following mental paper folding confirmed that instructional manipulations successfully evoked distinct imagery experiences. Self-imagery instructions elicited higher ratings for feeling the own hands, seeing the own hands, and hearing the paper, whereas object-imagery instructions elicited higher ratings for seeing the paper without hands. Vividness ratings did not differ significantly between conditions, suggesting comparable subjective clarity of imagery across agency instructions. Similarly, in mental body rotations, self-imagery instructions elicited higher ratings for feeling the own body and seeing the own body, while object-imagery instructions elicited higher ratings for seeing an action in the picture. Again, vividness did not differ significantly between agency instructions. These findings align with previous work demonstrating that imagery instructions selectively modulate sensory-motor components of imagery experience without necessarily affecting overall vividness (Guillot et al., 2009; MacIntyre et al., 2013).

In mental paper folding, response times and errors increased significantly with the number of folds and direction changes, consistent with the notion that folding engages sequential spatial transformations and cumulative working memory demands (Harris et al., 2013). This pattern replicates earlier findings showing that response time in mental paper folding increases with the number of folds, not only in execution (Dahm & Sachse, 2025), but also in imagery (Dahm & Draxler, 2022; Dahm & Sachse, 2025).

Hence, more complex folding sequences require more cognitive resources. This reinforces the interpretation of mental paper folding as a sequential transformation task relying on cumulative representational updating. Alternatively, more folds may increase the probability of error occurrences which can either materialize in accuracy failure or in an increased response time (if participants repeat the step or start the mental folding from the beginning to correct the error).

In mental body rotations, responses were faster and more accurate for smaller rotation angles, for back-view compared to front-view perspectives, and for hand compared to foot stimuli. These findings replicate previous research (Dahm et al., 2022), showing that leg stimuli were more challenging than arm stimuli, particularly in rotated positions, suggesting that distinguishing between left and right legs is more difficult than for arms. Further, the front-view perspective requires a vertical rotation in the mind which slows down responses (Dahm et al., 2022; Habacha et al., 2017; Kaltner et al., 2014).

Across both tasks, performance improved from the first to the second block, indicating reliable practice-related gains. Such improvements are consistent with previous findings showing that repeated engagement with mental rotation and spatial transformation tasks leads to faster and more efficient processing, even over relatively short timescales (Dahm & Draxler, 2022; Shepard & Metzler, 1971; Wohlschläger & Wohlschläger, 1998). From a cognitive perspective, these gains likely reflect increasing familiarity with stimulus structures and task requirements, as well as the refinement of imagery strategies with practice (Onishi et al., 2025). Such rapid improvements suggest flexible adaptation of representational strategies rather than structural changes in imagery ability.

The significantly stronger practice effect after self-imagery than after objectimagery for response times in mental paper folding is consistent with the instructions influencing subsequent processing. Here, performance improvement was greater when switching from self-imagery to object-imagery than vice versa. Participants who used self-imagery in Block 1 may have continued to rely, at least partially, on selfreferential imagery processes during Block 2 when instructed to use object-imagery, thereby retaining the associated performance advantages. Participants who began with object-imagery, by contrast, likely did not spontaneously engage in self-imagery when instructed to use object-imagery. This asymmetry suggests that self-referential representations can persist even when task demands change. We did not observe such a carryover-effect in mental body rotations, likely indicating that body-related stimuli in the MBRT elicited motor-related processing during imagery rather automatically (e.g., through perspective taking; Moreno-Verdú, Waltzing, McAteer, et al., 2025), and thus largely independent of explicit instruction, whereas the non-body stimuli in mental paper folding did not. Mental paper folding stimuli do not inherently depict an actor and can be solved with an object-centered visuospatial imagery strategy (Dahm & Sachse, 2025). Motor involvement may therefore be less likely to arise spontaneously in mental paper folding unless it is explicitly induced by instruction.

This interpretation aligns with previous prior work showing that rotation of body-related stimuli, but not rotation of objects, activates motor areas and premotor areas (Kosslyn et al., 1998). Similarly, mental rotation of hand stimuli increased motor-cortical involvement during subsequent mental rotation with abstract, non-body stimuli, whereas prior rotation of abstract objects did not produce the same effect (Wraga et al., 2003).

Response times were more strongly correlated across tasks when imagery instructions matched than when they differed. This descriptive pattern is consistent with shared instruction-related contributions to processing speed, potentially reflecting individual differences in the use of self-related kinesthetic or object-based visual strategies. However, error rate correlations did not show a consistent corresponding pattern. Moreover, shared temporal context and order effects provide alternative explanations for the response time pattern. These findings therefore suggest possible instruction-specific commonalities across tasks but do not establish distinct representational systems.

Studies have demonstrated that imagery can span multiple sensory modalities (Floridou et al., 2022) supporting the idea of a multimodal representation system (Krüger et al., 2022). In the present study, imagery instruction did not influence the occurrence of errors. This supports the view that imagery is inherently multimodal, such that imagery engages a mixture of modalities rather than a purely isolated form (Dahm et al., 2025; Dahm & Rieger, 2023). Yet, self-reports were expectably aligned towards the instructed agency and modality. Regarding imagery speed, response times were longer in object-imagery than in self-imagery in mental body rotations. In contrast, response times were shorter in object-imagery than self-imagery in mental paper folding. Therefore, we conclude that mental body rotations benefit from embodied cognition that involves oneself as an actor, whereas mental paper folding becomes more complex by including the own person perspective and kinesthetic aspects. Hence, as assumed previously (Dahm & Sachse, 2025) to effectively solve mental paper folding, a first person perspective is not necessary. Regarding mental body rotations however, agency as oneself benefits response speed. This goes in line with studies showing that people with aphantasia do not make more errors than people without aphantasia, but they do show longer response times (Kay et al., 2024). Together, these findings indicate a task-dependent advantage of embodied processing, rather than a general superiority of self-imagery.

Performance in both tasks did not correlate meaningfully with vividness scores from the Vividness of Movement Imagery Questionnaire, reinforcing previous observations that self-report imagery measures often fail to predict behavioral performance (Czilczer, Sachse, et al., 2026; Moreau et al., 2010; Suggate, 2024). This dissociation challenges the assumption that vividness reflects functional efficiency in imagery-based cognition and underscores the importance of combining both objective, performance-based measures, and subjective, self-report measures, when assessing imagery ability (Czilczer, Moreno-Verdú, et al., 2026; Dahm, 2020).

Small correlations of mental paper folding accuracy during object-imagery with visuospatial span capacity (Kessels et al., 2000) and spatial intelligence (matrices:

Heydasch, 2014) are consistent with demands of maintaining and updating several mental transformations when mentally folding a two-dimensional grid. Such correlations with visuospatial span capacity and spatial intelligence were not observed for mental body rotations, yet, more interference inhibition was weakly associated with shorter response times using self-imagery. Together, these results provide further support for the view that mental paper folding requires more abstract object-imagery abilities, whereas mental body rotations require own-body centered self-imagery abilities (Steggemann et al., 2011).

This study collected data from a range of behavioral tasks, and therefore we decided to reduce the number of trials collected from each task to minimize fatigue and inattention, particularly because data was collected remotely. This represents a limitation, as the number of trials is an important factor for task reliability (Zorowitz & Niv, 2023). However, our study collected data from a sample substantially larger than previously achieved. Therefore, the group-level estimates are more robust to trial-level noise (Cohen, 2013).

Neurophysiological and behavioral evidence indicates that kinesthetic and visual imagery can evoke distinct patterns of activation and classification (Chholak et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2021). Still, imagery may leave short-lived sensory traces that bias subsequent perception (Pearson et al., 2008), a mechanism that may provoke carry-over effects in the absence of an explicit washout period. Further, one may question whether participants engaged in genuinely distinct processes during selfimagery and object-imagery, particularly given the absence of a washout period between conditions. Although participants reported performing different forms of imagery, such reports could reflect the intention to comply with instructions rather than true differences in simulation strategies (Guillot & Collet, 2005). Importantly, instructions were not directly followed by the ratings. Participants first performed all trials of the task, and reported what they had been doing after completing the task.

Body-related stimuli in behavioral tasks may recruit motor-related processing during imagery relatively automatically and thus largely independently of explicit instruction. By contrast, non-body stimuli may not recruit motor-related processing unless it is induced by instruction, and may instead promote a purely visuospatial, non-action-specific strategy (Kosslyn et al., 1998). This distinction is critical when the aim is to measure action imagery ability specifically, because instructions can shape the processing of the stimuli. In addition, strategy-related carryover effects may occur across tasks, such that strategies adopted in earlier tasks influence performance in later tasks (Wraga et al., 2003). Accordingly, when using a battery of behavioral tasks (Czilczer, Moreno-Verdú, et al., 2026; Moreno-Verdú, Waltzing, Caenegem, et al., 2025), researchers should consider not only imagery instructions (Moreno-Verdú et al., 2024) but also task order and prior task context to improve interpretability and comparability across studies.

Overall, the present findings refine current accounts of action imagery by demonstrating that agency and modality can modulate imagery processing. In our study, imagery instructions influenced response times, but not error rates, providing no clear evidence of a speed-accuracy trade-off. The medium cross-task response time correlations under congruent imagery instructions suggest possible instruction-specific commonalities in processing across mental paper folding and mental body rotation.

While this is consistent with evidence for differential recruitment of motor and visuospatial systems (Guillot et al., 2009; Kosslyn et al., 2001), inconsistent correlations for error rates limit conclusions about distinct imagery processes. At the same time, the lack of strong associations with self-reported vividness suggests that subjective imagery experience and objective performance capture dissociable components of imagery ability. The present findings are consistent with a flexible account of imagery in which the contribution of self-related kinesthetic and objectcentered visual processing varies with task demands and imagery instructions. One possible interpretation is that these processing modes draw on different configurations of a partially shared simulation system. Alternatively, the tasks may be solved through partly distinct motor and visuospatial strategies. Self-related processing may therefore be advantageous in some task contexts, while explicit self-imagery instructions are not necessary for successful performance across these tasks. This perspective offers a framework for investigating when object-centered transformations involve action simulations and how perceptual and motor representations contribute to imagery under different task and instructional conditions.
