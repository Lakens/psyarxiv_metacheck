Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Behavioural models are used to forecast outcomes, interpret psychological processes and assess interventions. We develop an account of model warrant centred on the claim being made: a defeasible justification tied to a specified inference, its target conditions and the assumptions connecting a model to behaviour. Predictive, process and intervention warrants identify overlapping evidential obligations rather than a hierarchy or an exhaustive account of understanding. The organising question is whether accounts compatible with the available observations disagree about the conclusion being drawn. An analytical example shows that a binary logit curve can coincide exactly with the choice probabilities of a simple diffusion model, while leaving its temporal commitments untested. We distinguish the additional constraints supplied by response times from the separate requirements of parameter identification, recovery and construct validation. A second constructed example gives two accounts with identical observational choice distributions but different intervention effects. These examples locate the extra assumptions or observations needed to extend a model's interpretation. They also identify conclusions that remain defensible under uncertainty: predictive competence, partial process knowledge and decisions robust across specified alternatives. We propose a concise statement of warrant that connects each substantive claim to its evidence, scope and unresolved alternatives. This approach makes model evaluation more informative for psychological theory without reducing understanding to fit, transparency or an exhaustive description of mechanism.

Introduction

A model fitted to choices may subsequently be used to describe preferences, explain a decision process, or appraise an intervention. A coefficient first introduced to improve prediction can acquire the name of a psychological construct; a parameter that varies across experimental conditions can become a proposed target for changing behaviour. These uses raise different questions about the same fitted object. Accurate forecasts, defensible interpretations of latent processes, and credible estimates of intervention effects are all valuable achievements. Assessing one does not settle the others. The difficulty is to determine which conclusions survive a change in what the model is being asked to do. This difficulty is familiar in several forms. The distinction between explanation and prediction has consequences throughout statistical modelling, including variable selection and evaluation (Shmueli, 2010). Predictive assessment can improve psychological research by requiring models to succeed on observations beyond those used for estimation (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017).

Formalisation makes theoretical commitments explicit, but those commitments still require an empirical connection to their target (Guest & Martin, 2021). Recent discussions of understanding sharpen the question of what a successful model establishes (Shiffrin et al., 2026). The problem therefore concerns the interpretation of success as much as the detection of failure.

Several contributions already address important parts of this problem. Motz et al. (2026) relate understanding to the purpose for which it is sought. Timokhov (2026) distinguishes interpretations of evidence accumulation models, while Bachmann et al. (2026) emphasise the difficulty of carrying laboratory accounts into practical settings. Within theoretical psychology, Vanhasbroeck et al. (2026) argue that neither phenomena nor detailed data should serve as an exclusive basis for validating a theory. Almaatouq and Na (2026) develop ways of integrating explanation and prediction through experimental design, generalisation and interpretation. These arguments give good reasons to resist a single, undifferentiated standard of modelling success.

A related recent preprint by Chuang and Guest (2026) examines the justification of transitions across the computational modelling chain. The need to justify transfers of empirical sup-port is therefore not itself a new claim. Our focus is on the inference sought from a model in a particular domain: accounts can agree with the observations while disagreeing about a process, a construct or an intervention. This focus complements scrutiny of the links between theoretical specification, implementation and empirical assessment.

Our contribution is to organise the evidential obligations associated with particular claims and to show how they change across uses of a behavioural model. We distinguish predictive, process, and intervention warrants. The term warrant refers here to a defeasible justification for accepting a specified claim, given the observations, modelling assumptions and target setting. It does not name an intrinsic quality of an equation or a score that can be assigned to a model once and for all. A model may be well supported for one purpose while an apparently small extension of its interpretation remains unsupported.

The organising question is whether accounts that remain compatible with the available observations disagree about the claim being made. Such disagreement can be exposed analytically, even before new data are collected. A familiar connection between binary logit and diffusion models illustrates how identical choice probabilities leave substantive process commitments unresolved. A second constructed example shows how identical observational associations are compatible with different intervention effects. Alongside these examples, we distinguish statistical identifiability, parameter recovery and construct validity. The purpose is to make clear what additional evidence would change an assessment, rather than simply to counsel caution about models.

The argument applies across mathematical psychology, cognitive modelling and econometric choice analysis. These fields provide different resources for specifying and testing behavioural accounts, and no exclusive association between a field and a warrant is assumed. We also use intervention warrant more narrowly than policy justification. An estimate of what an intervention would do is a scientific input to a decision; the decision additionally depends on whose outcomes matter, feasible alternatives and the consequences of error. Keeping these questions visible allows useful modelling contributions to be recognised without asking a single successful analysis to establish more than it can.

Understanding and the scope of a model claim What a warrant adds

A statement that a model has been validated is incomplete until the object of validation is specified. It might refer to reproducing a distribution, estimating a parameter within a model family, representing a psychological operation, or forecasting a response to an action. These claims can involve the same data and software, but their truth conditions differ. A numerical routine may solve the intended equations correctly while the equations misrepresent the target behaviour.

Conversely, an idealised model may capture a useful dependence without describing every operation that produces an observed response.

We therefore assess a claim together with its scope. Scope includes the population, task, outcome, measurement procedure and conditions to which the claim refers. It also includes the resolution at which the claim is made. A model may describe average choice shares adequately while failing to distinguish individuals; it may reproduce a response-time distribution while misrepresenting sequential dependence. Agreement at one resolution is evidence about that resolution. Treating it as validation of an entire account silently broadens the object being evaluated.

Warrant also depends on assumptions that connect formal quantities to observations. These include the observation model, sampling conditions and restrictions on how parameters vary. Calling a threshold response caution, for example, commits the modeller to more than an algebraic stopping rule. It connects a mathematical feature to a psychological interpretation. Calling a fare coefficient a causal response adds assumptions about why fares vary and what would remain stable if they were changed. Such assumptions can be reasonable, but their status should remain distinguishable from facts established by the fit itself. This use of warrant is deliberately modest. It accommodates different inferential methods and permits support to be partial or revisable. We do not offer a new probability calculus for theoretical claims. Instead, we ask modellers to specify what an analysis justifies and where that justification relies on substantive commitments. Evidence can strengthen a claim without uniquely proving it, and a claim may be useful before all plausible alternatives have been examined. The appropriate conclusion then retains those qualifications.

Understanding the model and understanding behaviour

Understanding is not exhausted by the possession of a warranted forecast. A researcher may know that a forecasting system is reliable without grasping why a behavioural pattern occurs.

Philosophical accounts that emphasise intelligibility and the ability to work with a theory make the user's capacities relevant to understanding (de Regt & Dieks, 2005). Work on idealisation also gives reasons to reject a requirement that an explanatory model reproduce its target in every detail (Potochnik, 2017). These positions caution against equating understanding either with prediction alone or with an exhaustive reconstruction of mechanism.

There is a further distinction between understanding a model and understanding through it. An analyst can derive exactly how a diffusion threshold changes a response-time distribution. This is knowledge of a mathematical system. Applying the result to a person's caution requires evidence that the system captures the relevant aspects of that person's behaviour. The derivation does not supply this connection merely by being exact. Equally, an interpretable regression coefficient may be easy to explain while its connection to the target construct remains uncertain.

Opacity creates a different problem. Difficulty inspecting a model's internal operations can obstruct particular inquiries, but it is not a general proof that the model cannot contribute to understanding. Sullivan (2022) distinguishes opacity from uncertainty about the connection between a model and its target. This distinction matters here: a transparent model can have an inadequately supported target interpretation, whereas a complex model can contribute to a well constrained account when the evidential connection is strong. Transparency can assist scrutiny without substituting for that connection.

Accordingly, predictive, process and intervention warrants are not offered as three necessary and sufficient definitions of understanding. They identify recurring claims made when behavioural models are credited with understanding. Predictive warrant establishes a kind of competence within a specified domain. Process warrant supports an account of how behaviour is organised or generated at a stated level. Intervention warrant concerns the consequences of a specified change. How these achievements contribute to understanding depends additionally on the question being answered and what the researcher can grasp or use. The framework makes the evidential commitments of these attributions explicit.

Selected warrants and shared evidence

The categories in Table 1 are neither exhaustive nor mutually exclusive. Models also support measurement, conceptual exploration, unification and demonstrations of possibility. A simulation showing that a mechanism can generate a pattern may be valuable even before there is evidence that people use that mechanism. It establishes a possibility claim under the simulated assumptions. Its contribution should be assessed accordingly. Measurement is especially important because it connects formal parameters to psychological constructs and can enter each of the three warrants.

Nor do the warrants form an obligatory sequence. A randomised intervention can identify an average behavioural effect while leaving its cognitive pathway unresolved. A process model can explain a qualitative pattern without providing the most accurate individual forecasts. The same experiment can nevertheless bear on several claims: a manipulation may discriminate processes, test out-of-sample predictions and estimate an intervention effect. What changes is the argument linking the observations to each conclusion. Distinguishing warrants therefore supports integration of evidence, provided the connections are defended. an appropriate baseline and examining the distributional features relevant to the intended use makes the predictive claim more informative. The value of predictive discipline lies in a specified test, not in the presence of the phrase cross-validation (Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017).

These issues are connected to the generalisability of psychological results. A claim about a broad population of situations requires a design and analysis that address variation over those situations (Yarkoni, 2022). Precision obtained by repeatedly observing a narrow range of conditions cannot substitute for coverage of the domain to which the conclusion refers. This applies equally to a flexible forecasting algorithm and a compact cognitive model. For both, the validation domain is part of the claim, rather than a detail to be added after accuracy has been reported.

Prediction can be valuable without a process interpretation. It can reveal stable regularities, identify where a theory's predictions fail, and provide a benchmark that a proposed explanation should meet. The contrast between statistical modelling cultures drawn by Breiman (2001) helps expose the costs of evaluating models solely through familiar assumptions. However, pre-dictive success leaves a further question: which features of the target account for that success?

Correlated proxies, genuine causal dependences and compensating errors can each contribute.

The answer requires information beyond an undifferentiated accuracy score.

Discriminating commitments

Process claims concern the organisation of behaviour at a specified level. A sequential sampling model proposes a relation between evolving evidence, a stopping rule and a response. A learning model proposes how experience changes a state that influences subsequent choices. A process account need not specify neural implementation to be informative. To favour one process account over a relevant rival, evidence must bear on a commitment on which they differ. The mathematical detail of a model is useful insofar as it sharpens those commitments.

Goodness of fit becomes theoretically informative through this comparison. Roberts and Pashler (2000) show why agreement with data is insufficient when it is unclear which outcomes a model could accommodate. Work on model selection adds the importance of flexibility and the relation between fit and complexity (Pitt et al., 2002). A more flexible model may fit because it captures a relevant process, because it absorbs omitted structure, or because it can reproduce a broad class of patterns. A fit statistic alone does not distinguish these explanations. Comparing serious alternatives and examining their qualitative commitments gives the numerical result a theoretical setting.

Joint observations can constrain an account more strongly than a single outcome. In decision research, choice and response time connect a stopping process to both what is selected and when selection occurs (Forstmann et al., 2016; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008). Additional measurements help when the competing accounts make different predictions for them and when the measurement model is credible. More recorded variables do not automatically supply more diagnostic evidence: several measures may track the same ambiguity or introduce new interpretive assumptions. The value of a measure lies in the distinctions it enables.

Successful prediction can consequently provide strong process evidence. A prediction made before a diagnostic manipulation, and missed by plausible competitors, may be highly informative. There is no general rule that prediction belongs only to predictive warrant. Conversely, a failed prediction challenges a package of process assumptions, parameter restrictions and measurement choices; it does not uniquely identify the faulty component. Palminteri et al. (2017) emphasise the role of falsification and informative comparison in computational cognitive modelling. The practical objective is to design situations in which the proposed accounts have something consequential at stake.

A logit equation that is silent about response time cannot be refuted by a time pattern it never purported to explain. Rather, times distinguish specified process accounts and assess the extra commitments introduced by a diffusion interpretation. Other process families may still accommodate the enlarged observations. Support for the diffusion account is conditional on the competitors, constraints and measures examined, even if its parameters can be estimated precisely.

Designing a discriminating test

An experiment could make these commitments more informative by varying speed requirements or the quality of information while measuring choices and response times. One candidate account might restrict a manipulation to the boundary; another might predict a change in drift or in the mapping between attributes and evidence. Their jointly predicted patterns provide a basis for comparison. Simply allowing every parameter to change in every condition, and interpreting the resulting estimates after fitting, weakens the connection between the experimental contrast and a particular process claim. Parameter recovery asks a different question. Data generated from known parameter values are fitted, and the resulting estimates are compared with the generating values. This evaluates an estimation procedure under particular assumptions, sample sizes and designs. Recovery can be poor even when parameters are identifiable in principle, because finite observations contain little information, parameters trade off approximately, or estimation is unreliable. Good recovery establishes that the procedure can work in the examined generative setting. It does not establish that human data were generated by that setting (Wilson & Collins, 2019).

Model recovery likewise asks whether candidate generating models can be distinguished by the chosen comparison procedure. It can expose a design in which one model is systematically selected even when another generated the data. Its conclusions depend on which generators, parameter regions and observation processes were examined. Neither a successful recovery exercise nor a more concentrated posterior distribution rules out misspecification outside that exercise.

These checks are useful resources for interpreting an analysis, but the claims they support remain conditional.

Hierarchical estimation adds another distinction. Pooling information can improve estimation when participants have few observations, while the resulting estimates depend on assumptions about the population distribution. A stable group mean need not imply that individual rankings are measured precisely. If the substantive claim concerns individual differences, the uncertainty and stability of those differences must be examined at that level. Precision in the grouplevel model cannot simply be inherited by every interpretation of its participant-level parameters.

What validates a construct

Construct validity concerns whether a formal quantity can support an interpretation in terms of the psychological attribute of interest. This requires engagement with the meaning of the construct and the relations in which it is expected to stand. The nomological approach of Cronbach and Meehl (1955) and the account of validity developed by Borsboom et al. (2004) differ in their commitments, but both make clear that attaching a name to a score does not settle its validity.

Slaney and Racine (2013) further question how construct language is used and what it commits psychologists to. A model parameter does not escape these issues by being inferred from a sophisticated likelihood. A parameter labelled caution, for example, is defined in the model by its role in terminating accumulation. Interpreting it as a person's stable tendency to deliberate requires additional claims about temporal stability, task dependence and the relation between the laboratory measure and the intended attribute. An experimental effect on the parameter can support an interpretation, especially when predicted in advance, but it can also arise because another process changes in a way the model absorbs. Parameter names provide hypotheses about this connection. They do not independently test it.

Validation can combine theoretically targeted manipulations, independent measures and comparisons across specifications. The contribution of each depends on its own assumptions.

Agreement between two measures that share a task artefact is weaker evidence than agreement across methods with different vulnerabilities. A manipulation intended to change one construct may affect several operations. A parameter's stability across model variants is useful only if those variants allow the relevant competing interpretations. The question is whether evidence targets the proposed psychological meaning, rather than merely reproducing the same numerical regularity by another route.

Scale and interpretation in choice models

Econometric choice modelling makes the scale issue especially visible. In a random utility model, multiplying all utilities by the same positive constant preserves the choices. For logit, the estimated coefficients combine systematic utility weights with the inverse scale of unobserved utility differences (Train, 2009). A cost coefficient of larger magnitude in one population can therefore reflect a change in sensitivity, a change in unobserved variation, or both. Flexible representations of heterogeneity do not automatically resolve this ambiguity; Hess and Train (2017) examine the relation between taste heterogeneity, correlation and scale.

Ratios can remove a common multiplicative scale, but this achievement also has a limited scope. Suppose deterministic utility is linear in cost and travel time and both coefficients are negative. The ratio β t /β c is then positive and gives the marginal willingness to pay for a unit reduction in travel time under the maintained utility interpretation. With cost in pounds and time in minutes, its units are pounds per minute. Interpreting this ratio as a welfare-relevant value requires assumptions about preferences, the monetary attribute, functional form and the counterfactual being evaluated. Scale cancellation does not establish those assumptions.

The same observed ratio has another reading in the diffusion construction. With a common boundary and diffusion scale, the coefficients in the logit choice curve are proportional to the corresponding drift coefficients. Their ratio then describes a trade-off in the specified mapping from attributes to drift. The algebra does not decide whether that mapping measures subjective utility, attentional weighting or some other process. A statistically identified ratio can consequently remain psychologically underdetermined. This is precisely where identification within a model and validation of the model's interpretation must be kept distinct.

From observed behaviour to intervention A different target of inference

Intervention warrant concerns what would happen if a specified action were taken. Examples include changing a fare, altering an information display or modifying the incentives of a decision task. The target differs from predicting the behaviour of people who happen to face those conditions. An observed association can depend on the process that allocated people to conditions, whereas the proposed action changes that allocation. Causal accounts therefore require explicit assumptions about the relation between observed variation and intervention (Hernán & Robins, 2020).

This requirement is compatible with interventionist accounts of explanation, which relate causal claims to dependences that support answers to questions about changes (Woodward, 2003). It also explains why intervention warrant overlaps with some process claims without requiring a complete process account. A well designed experiment can estimate an average effect while leaving the cognitive pathway uncertain. Conversely, a plausible account of attention or accumulation need not specify all the circumstances that determine how a practical intervention changes behaviour.

This allocation produces exactly the same observed joint distribution of receipt and choice as the first account. Yet setting A to either value leaves U, and therefore Y , unchanged. The probability of public transport choice under either intervention is 1/2, so the average causal effect is zero.

Table 2 summarises the contrast. Every observed combination of A and Y has positive probability, and concession receipt is possible in both latent groups. The ambiguity is therefore not an artefact of an empty observed cell. Unlimited observations of receipt and choice under the original allocation process would still leave the accounts observationally equivalent. Predictive scores based on these shared observational probabilities are identical for the two accounts. Crossvalidation using only receipt and choice therefore cannot resolve the causal disagreement: the accounts agree on the target that this validation exercise measures.

Additional outcomes distinguish these causal accounts only when they constrain the causal ambiguity. Attaching the same conditional response-time distribution P(R | A,Y ) to both accounts would preserve their equivalence for the expanded joint observations (A,Y, R). This construction does not claim that the accounts have independently validated, identical cognitive mechanisms. It shows why merely collecting more behavioural measures cannot replace assumptions connecting those measures to the assignment and response processes. Randomising concession receipt, or obtaining sufficient covariate information together with justified causal assumptions, changes the evidential problem in a way that extra repetitions of the same observations do not.

Transporting an effect to a target setting

An effect identified in one study can still require further support before it is applied elsewhere.

Transportability concerns the conditions under which causal information from one population or environment informs another. Such reasoning requires assumptions about relevant similarities and differences; it is not warranted by the size of the original sample alone (Pearl & Bareinboim, 2014). The earlier predictive question about a domain reappears here, but the object being transported is an intervention consequence rather than only an observational probability.

In the travel example, a concession tested among existing public transport users might be applied to people with different access to services. A short experiment might precede adaptation in departure times or residential choices. If a large programme changes congestion, crowding or service frequency, outcomes for one traveller can depend on the responses of others. An individual choice equation estimated under a fixed environment then supplies only one component of a system-level forecast. The relevant time horizon, implementation and interactions belong in the target description.

Structural choice models are designed to address counterfactual questions by specifying how attributes, constraints and behaviour relate (Hess & Daly, 2024; McFadden, 2001; Train, 2009). Their value lies partly in making extrapolation assumptions available for inspection.

Those assumptions still need a substantive defence. In the diffusion example, changing a fare attribute in a fitted drift equation assumes that the mapping from fare to evidence remains appro-priate under the proposed intervention. A change in framing, attention or the feasible choice set can challenge that mapping even when the original response-time fit is good.

Intervention warrant can therefore be strengthened by evidence from the relevant perturbation, comparison across settings and sensitivity analysis over disputed assumptions. When interventions cannot be observed directly, the conclusion should distinguish effects supported across plausible specifications from those that depend on a particular extrapolation. Evidence of stability in some environments supports those environments; it does not establish unrestricted invariance.

This is a substantive limitation to report, not a reason to abandon counterfactual modelling.

What survives uncertainty about a model

Claims can be supported at different resolutions

The examples demonstrate limits on particular inferences, but they also identify conclusions that survive those limits. Observationally equivalent accounts assign the same distribution to specified observables. Where predictive adequacy has been established for a target draw under those conditions, unresolved process interpretations need not prevent a warranted forecast. Requiring a unique psychological interpretation before using such a forecast would impose an evidential demand unrelated to that predictive purpose. The scope of the justified claim determines which uncertainties matter.

The same reasoning applies within process modelling. Accounts may disagree about peripheral components while sharing a supported claim about a particular operation. A parameter combination may be identifiable even when its individual components are not. Conversely, precise identification of every parameter within a restricted family can coexist with uncertainty about that family's psychological interpretation. It is therefore useful to ask what aspects of a process are constrained, at what level, and relative to which alternatives. Treating a model as either understood or not understood suppresses these distinctions.

This assessment does not require a catalogue of every possible model. The space of pos- sible accounts is open-ended, and the importance of an alternative depends on the claim under examination.

In the choice example, a change in evidence units is irrelevant to behaviour, whereas a change that preserves choices but alters times matters to a temporal process claim. In the concession example, allocation based on a latent predisposition matters to the intervention claim. An informative comparison explains why its alternatives are serious possibilities and what conclusions they would change. It also acknowledges where unexamined alternatives remain plausible.

Observational equivalence also does not imply equal credibility. Independent knowledge of how a concession was allocated could rule out one of the constructed accounts. Evidence from another task might favour a process restriction that the present choices cannot identify. Such information belongs in the justification for the claim, with its own scope and uncertainty made explicit. The point of the equivalence argument is to locate what the specified observations cannot settle, not to erase knowledge obtained elsewhere. In a Bayesian analysis, for example, equal likelihoods for those observations leave relative support dependent on prior information; they do not require equal posterior probabilities. Restrictions can resolve an ambiguity when they are independently defensible. When they are merely convenient, the resulting conclusion remains conditional on that convenience. Reporting their role prevents substantive assumptions from being mistaken for information extracted from the fitted data.

Exact equivalence is a particularly clear demonstration of insufficiency. Empirical work more often encounters approximate agreement under finite data. There, uncertainty about fit and uncertainty about interpretation must be considered together. Two models can have similar overall performance while differing in a feature central to the intended claim. A small discrepancy in the relevant tail of a distribution may matter more than a larger discrepancy elsewhere. The substantive target determines which differences merit scrutiny; a single ranking of models need not serve every use.

Explanation extends beyond process reconstruction

One objection is that the framework privileges causal mechanisms by separating process from prediction. This would be a problem if process warrant were intended to encompass all explanation. It is not. Philosophical work on distinctively mathematical explanation provides an explicit challenge to reducing explanation to causal process descriptions (Lange, 2013). An account may explain through constraints, structure or relations that do not reconstruct a temporal mechanism.

Such explanations require their own connection to the psychological target, rather than automatic assignment to one of the present categories.

Our examples illustrate why this qualification matters. Showing that a choice function leaves a boundary and drift trade-off unresolved is an informative mathematical result about the limits of an inference. It helps explain why a particular design cannot answer a particular question. It is not a proposed account of how travellers decide. The result remains useful precisely because its explanatory target is clear. The framework permits this contribution while retaining a distinct category for claims about the generation of behaviour.

Idealisation raises a related objection. Since all models omit features of their targets, demanding extra evidence for every transition might seem to make understanding unattainable. The relevant question, however, concerns the consequences of an omission for the claim at issue. An idealisation can isolate a dependence and make it intelligible (Potochnik, 2017). It becomes problematic when a conclusion relies on features the idealisation removes. A fixed environment may be adequate for a local process comparison and inadequate for a forecast in which the proposed intervention changes that environment. The standard is sensitivity of the inference to the omission, rather than completeness of description.

Useful decisions under incomplete knowledge

Uncertainty about precise consequences need not make every decision indeterminate. If the remaining plausible models favour the same action under a stated objective and the relevant constraints, some unresolved differences may be immaterial to that decision. This establishes a conditional robustness claim about the action. It does not establish that the models share a mechanism, estimate the same effect, or exhaust every plausible account. A sound recommendation makes clear both the agreement and the assumptions under which it holds.

The opposite can also occur. Models may forecast baseline behaviour equally well but imply different preferred actions because they disagree about intervention effects or important distributional consequences. The concession example supplies a simple instance: a costly programme could appear worthwhile under one account and ineffective under the other. Averaging their predictions does not remove the underlying identification problem; it adds a choice about how to weight the accounts. Sensitivity to that choice should remain visible to the decisionmaker.

This distinction helps direct further research. An uncertainty that cannot change the proposed action may have little immediate decision value, although it remains scientifically inter- esting.

An uncertainty about who benefits or whether an effect survives implementation may be decisive. The appropriate next study can therefore differ from the study that most improves average predictive accuracy or most refines an internal parameter. Relating the analysis to its intended use allows those priorities to be argued explicitly.

Making model warrants reportable A statement attached to the analysis

The practical proposal is to attach a short, substantive statement of warrant to a modelling analysis. The statement identifies the claim, the target conditions, the evidence that bears on it, and the assumptions on which the interpretation depends. It also identifies a consequential alternative that the evidence has excluded or has left open. This is not a demand for a universal numerical score. Different studies can make different contributions, provided readers can determine what was evaluated and how far the conclusion extends.

For the choice example, a predictive statement would specify which participants or conditions were held out, what information was available for prediction, and which features of the choices were successfully forecast. A process statement would add the temporal predictions and experimental contrasts that distinguished the accumulation account from named alternatives. An intervention statement would specify how the effect of changing a fare was identified and why it was expected to apply in the target setting. These descriptions make different evidential commitments even when they refer to parts of one research programme.

The strongest statements also describe a boundary that would matter in practice. A result might depend on a fixed choice set, an observation window, an assumption about nondecision time, or the absence of interference between participants.

A meaningful limitation explains what inference would become uncertain if that condition changed. General acknowledgements that models are simplifications provide less guidance. Stating a specific boundary allows subsequent work to test it, relax it or deliberately restrict the application. Reporting uncertainty at the appropriate level is equally important. A narrow interval around a fitted coefficient describes uncertainty under the fitted specification and inferential procedure. It need not include uncertainty about construct interpretation, omitted mechanisms or transport to another setting. These sources of uncertainty may call for comparison across models, sensitivity analysis or further conceptual work. They should not be compressed into the numerical precision of a parameter estimate merely because that precision is easy to report.

Consequences for research design and theoretical progress

Once the desired claim is explicit, model checking and data collection can be directed towards the ambiguity that obstructs it. If candidate accounts differ mainly in predicted decision time, collecting further choices alone may be inefficient. If they differ in the assignment mechanism, an observational sample with more precise choice probabilities may leave the causal contrast untouched. If the unresolved issue is the meaning of a construct, another recovery study under the same interpretation may be insufficient. The additional evidence should bear on the particular disagreement, rather than simply increase the volume of observations.

Conceptual clarification is consequently part of empirical design. A construct must be defined well enough for a manipulation or measurement to bear on it. A theoretical claim must be specified well enough for competing outcomes to have different implications. Eronen and Bringmann (2021) emphasise difficulties in building psychological theory that include establishing robust phenomena and relating them to constructs and causes. Scheel et al. (2021) similarly argue for work that develops concepts and phenomena before premature hypothesis testing. These arguments complement formalisation: precision in equations is most useful when the empirical and conceptual questions to which they answer are also precise.

The framework leaves room for exploration. Discovering a regularity, proposing an interpretation and revising a model after inspecting data are legitimate parts of theory development.

The evidential claim should record that sequence. Agreement obtained by adapting a prediction to a known outcome cannot also be treated as an independent test of that prediction. An exploratory parameter interpretation can motivate a study without being presented as an already validated measure. This distinction protects the value of exploratory work by describing what it has actually contributed.

For theoretical psychology, the broader implication is that discussion of modelling success can become more exact without being reduced to a single statistical criterion. The unit of evaluation is a claim made with a model under stated conditions. This permits researchers working with different mathematical representations to compare the questions they answer, the observations that constrain them and the assumptions they require. Disagreement can then concern a particular inferential step that is open to analysis or experiment.

Conclusion

A behavioural model can contribute to understanding by establishing regularities, constraining an account of a process, or supporting reasoning about a change in conditions. The contribution depends on the claim being made and on the connection between the model and its target. Predictive, process and intervention warrants identify several common forms of that connection without supplying an exhaustive theory of understanding.

The analytical examples make the distinction consequential. A logit choice curve can coincide exactly with the choices generated by a simple diffusion model while leaving temporal commitments untested. Two data-generating accounts can produce the same observed association between a concession and travel choice while implying different intervention effects. Parameter estimation introduces a further interpretive step: identifiability and successful recovery within a specification do not validate the psychological construct attached to an estimate.
