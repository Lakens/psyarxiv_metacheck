How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

This module retrieves information from repositories.

Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Skilled adult readers show biases in their visual attention that reflect the direction of their native writing systems (e.g., English readers scan left-to-right while Hebrew readers scan right-to-left), but it not yet clear when these biases develop. We tested the hypothesis that language-specific patterns of behavior precede the ability to read by testing how toddlers (2.5-4 years) exposed to different writing systems allocated their attention in a picture naming task. Across two communities, children tended to name objects in the direction that matched their native language.

Hebrew-learning toddlers labeled objects from right-to-left, while English-and Spanish-learning toddlers showed the reverse pattern, and language-specific tendencies strengthened with age.

These results demonstrate that children have begun to discover directional regularities associated with their language even before they receive literacy instruction and illustrate how cultural conventions exert a direct influence on children's attention from a very young age.

1. Introduction

Learning to read is among the key educational challenges that children encounter.

Individual differences in reading skills tend to endure throughout and beyond formal schooling (Bowey, 2005; Daneman & Carpenter, 1980; Share et al., 1984; Shaywitz et al., 1999), making it crucial to understand the developmental processes that support and reflect emergent reading abilities. Equally importantly, learning to read changes perceptual processes and behavior. In both reading and non-linguistic tasks, skilled readers show biases in their visual attention that reflect efficient strategies for interpreting the script of their native language; English readers tend to scan from left-to-right, while Hebrew readers scan right-to-left (Shaki et al., 2009; 2012). However, it is not yet known when these biases first emerge and whether they precede or reflect literacy instruction. In the current studies, we used a cross-cultural approach to test that hypothesis that toddlers' looking behavior is shaped by the conventions of their native text even before they have formally begun learning how to read.

Children's ability to demonstrate pre-reading skills such as knowledge of sounds or the names of letters of the alphabet is reliably related to their reading abilities (National Reading Panel, 2000; Share et al, 1984), and research has made significant progress in documenting predictors of reading success in children near or at the age of school entry (e.g., Cooper et al., 2002; Marks et al., 2019). However, children display evidence of these foundational skills as young as two to three years (Kenner et al., 2017; McDowell et al., 2007), suggesting that the roots of reading originate even earlier. One recent study found that two-to three-year-old toddlers who had not yet received any formal literacy instruction already showed specialized neural responses for native text, suggesting that toddlers may begin becoming familiar with the appearance of text through implicit learning mechanisms (Kherbawy et al., 2026). Here, we tested whether toddlers would also show evidence that they had learned directional regularities associated with their native writing system prior to learning to read.

Reading is a visual behavior, and eye movements can provide a window into processing.

With practice, eye movements become consistent for skilled readers, while struggling readers often show atypical patterns of fixation when they view text (Dürrwächter et al., 2010; Hutzler & Wimmer, 2004). Proficient English readers fixate on the left part of a word before moving to the next word (Rayner, 1979). Likewise, readers of right-to-left scripts begin on the right, and fixation patterns correlate with reading skill (Deutsch & Rayner, 1999). When children first begin to read, it is a laborious process. They look at each letter individually, moving their eyes in the direction of their language's script (Luca et al., 2010; Zoccolotti et al., 2005). While children's looking patterns are less consistent than adults', the tendency to follow the direction of their text is evident in early stages of reading (Mason, 1980). In addition, like less skilled adult readers, children with learning difficulties show less structured fixations (van der Schoot et al., 2012) and are at risk for reading difficulties (Leppänen et al., 2008). Intriguingly, interventions that train children's fixation strategies can be effective (Francheschini et al., 2013; Holman, 2019), further emphasizing that appropriate visual attention is linked to fluent reading.

With how practiced these movements become, language-specific directional biases are not limited to reading activities. Adults' behavior can reflect the direction of the language they read even when no text is involved (e.g., Maass & Russo, 2003). In one task, stimuli appear in the right or left field of vision. Participants whose language is read left-to-right detect targets better in the left hemifield, while those whose language is read right-to-left show the opposite pattern (Smigasiewicz et al., 2010). Similar biases appear in line bisection tasks, where participants judge the center of a line as closer to the side where they initiate eye movements for reading (e.g., English readers are left-biased; Rinaldi et al., 2014). These biases are already evident in school-age children (Banfi et al., 2017; Chokron & De Agostini, 1995) and correlate with elementary school reading skills (Hoyos et al., 2021). Thus, biases in visual attention derived from reading appear relatively early and are expressed across a range of perceptual tasks. However, it is not yet clear if children develop these biases while they learn to read or before.

Research on the origins of spatial representations associated with numbers has examined the role of educational practices in the development of directional biases, and literate adults' behavior reliably conforms to cultural conventions (e.g., Cooperrider et al., 2017; Dehaene et al., 1993; Göbel et al., 2011; Pitt et al., 2021; Zebian, 2005). However, associations between space and number can also be observed in young children who have not yet entered formal schooling (de Hevia & Spelke, 2009; Opfer et al., 2010), and it is debated whether early biases reflect innate expectations or observational learning (e.g., Bulf et al., 2016; Giorgio et al., 2019; de Havia et al., 2014; McCrink & de Hevia, 2018; Pitt et al, 2021 Pitt et al, , 2023;; Shaki et al., 2009 Shaki et al., , 2012)).

Toddlers' biases appear to be less consistent than those of older children, but by the preschool years, children have a tendency to organize numerical information according to cultural norms (McCrink et al., 2017; Kamawar et al., 2010; Opfer et al., 2010). One possibility is that children begin to develop biases by observing and/or imitating patterns in parents' behavior. For example, evidence from interactions between American and Israeli parent-child dyads supports the view that parents' behavior reflects the direction of their writing system, suggesting that young children could discover conventional patterns through experience (McCrink et al., 2018). In the current study, we presented toddlers with stimuli that were unrelated to number concepts, allowing us to test whether culture-specific patterns of attention could be found in a different domain before children receive formal instruction.

Across two studies, we compared the behavior of toddlers exposed to languages that are read in opposite directions, allowing us to test whether different writing systems shape the way that young children perceive and organize information even before they are taught to read. Toddlers ages 2.5 to 4 years performed a task where they named a series of objects in the order of their choice. Based on evidence that children of this age follow other cultural conventions involving spatial organization (Hoyos et al., 2021; Nuraydin et al., 2024; Tillman et al., 2022; Shaki et al., 2012), we predicted that across both communities, toddlers would be more likely name objects in the direction of their native text. We also expected older toddlers, who have more experience with text-based activities, to show stronger biases.

2. Study 1

In Study 1, we tested toddlers in Israel exposed Hebrew, which is read right-to-left. We also collected measures of participants' pre-reading abilities to test whether our novel measure of visual attention was related to established indicators of early reading ability or experience with text. Pre-reading skills such as phonological awareness, knowledge of links between sounds and letters, and speed of naming are associated with later reading ability (Kirby et al., 2003; Parrila et al., 2004; Schatschneider et al., 2004), and we predicted that children with stronger biases in their visual attention might show more advanced pre-reading skills.

2.1. Methods

>Methods

2.1.1. Participants

Monolingual Hebrew-learning toddlers were recruited from the community surrounding Tel Aviv University and participated either in their homes or at a daycare/preschool. The final sample included 70 toddlers ages 2.5-4.0 years (35 girls, 35 boys, Mage = 3.3 years, SD = 0.47). This slightly exceeded our target sample size of 64 children, determined based on a power analysis (Faul et al., 2007) suggesting that this sample would suffice to give us power <.99 to detect a large (f =.25) within-subjects effect and power = .8 to detect a moderate correlation (r = .3), in line with our plans to test for relations between children's performance across different measures. One additional child was tested but excluded because of a reported communication disorder. The parents of all participating children provided written informed consent, and families received 20 NIS and a small thank you gift for their participation. All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Texas at El Paso.

2.1.2. Picture naming task

>Picture naming task

2.1.2.1. Stimuli.

Visual stimuli consisted of sets of black and white clipart images of three familiar objects presented on laminated cards. All items involved frequent, imageable words that preschool children would be expected to know (e.g., plane, ball, apple). Sets of images were constructed such that all items were easily distinguishable (e.g., multiple animals did not appear in the same set). For Practice trials, images appeared in a vertical line (see Figure 1). For Test trials, images were presented in a horizontal row. All stimuli can be found at https://osf.io/rfs5z/.

2.1.2.2. Procedure.

Children sat at a table across from an experimenter and viewed one set of images at a time. There were two phases: Practice and Test. On each of two Practice trials, children saw a set of objects in vertical orientation (Figure 1A) so that they could become familiar with the task without being encouraged to focus on the left or right side. They were shown a card and asked to name the pictures, and if necessary, the experimenter would prompt the child by pointing to the top object. After the child successfully completed two Practice trials, they were shown 20 Test trials, each with a unique set of images in a horizontal row (Figure 1B), while the experimenter asked them what they saw. Children received neutral feedback on their responses, but if they stopped responding before labeling all three items, the experimenter would prompt them by asking what else they saw. Children's responses were coded by noting the order (first, second, or third) in which the child labeled and/or pointed to each object (left, center, right) in a set. Responses with alternative labels (e.g., woof-woof for a dog) were accepted, as long as it was clear which object the child intended to refer to. Individual trials where the child did not label all items in the set or where a parent or sibling directed the child's attention to a particular object were excluded. On average, children provided usable data on 18.0 trials.

2.1.3. Other measures

>Other measures

2.1.3.1. Pre-reading skills.

Children were tested on a series of tasks assessing preliteracy skills, including their knowledge of letter names and different aspects of phonological awareness (see Supplemental materials on OSF). While we mention these tasks for transparency and completeness, many children were unable to complete all the tasks or at floor in their performance, making it difficult to interpret any associations with our primary measure. Therefore, we report results and correlations in supplemental materials, but do not discuss them further.

2.1.3.2. Home literacy questionnaire.

Parents were asked to fill out the Home Literacy Questionnaire (Van Steensel, 2006, translated to Hebrew) to provide information about their children's exposure to literacy activities (e.g., How often do you read bedtime stories with your child? How often do you visit the library?). Each of 13 items was scored 0 (rarely/never), 1 (sometimes), or 2 (often), and items were summed for a total score of 0-26. On average, total scores were 13.2 (SD = 2.8), indicating that children had fairly regular exposure to literacy activities.

2.1.4. Analytic approach

For each child, we calculated the mean of the rank order (1-3) in which they labeled the left, center, and right objects. We also calculated the proportion of trials in which children named object in a language-consistent order (i.e., right-center-left). Data and analysis scripts are available on OSF.

2.2. Results & Discussion

To test whether children showed systematicity in the order in which they named objects, we used a repeated measures ANOVA to compare the mean rank order in which the object in each position (left, center, right) was labeled and found a significant effect, F(2,138) = 14.91, p <.0001, η² = .18. On average, right object was named earliest (M = 1.81) and the left object was named latest (M = 2.25), consistent with our predictions (see Figure 2). Overall, children named objects in the expected right-to-left direction on an average of 45.6% of trials (SD = 30%), and they named the center object second on 73% of trials (SD = 25%), indicating that on the majority of trials, children named items in a linear sequence and rarely started from the middle.

We then tested the correlation between children's age and the proportion of trials where they gave a language-consistent response and found a significant positive association, r = .46, p <.0001, 95% CI = [.25, .62] (See Figure 3). As children got older, their tendency to label objects in the direction that matched their native writing system increased.

The results of Study 1 provide preliminary support for our hypothesis that toddlers' visual attention is influenced by the direction of their native text before they know how to read. Even when they were presented with a task that involved no text and received no instructions about how to label objects, Hebrew-learning toddlers tended to direct their attention from right-to-left, consistent with how their language is written. In Study 2, we conducted a cross-cultural replication with children exposed to a left-to-right writing system to more rigorously test the proposal that directional biases reflect toddlers' growing familiarity with the conventions associated with their native text.

3. Study 2 3.1. Methods

>Study 2 3.1. Methods

3.1.1. Participants

Forty North American English-and/or Spanish-learning toddlers matched to the age of participants in Study 1 (21 girls, 19 boys, Mage = 3.3 years, SD = .45) were recruited from two local children's museums in a bilingual community along the US/Mexico border. Because our planned analyses were focused on group differences rather than individual differences, this sample size was chosen a priori because we expected it to be sufficient to detect a large effect of object position and an interaction between position and community with power >.8. According to parent report, the majority of children were of Hispanic/Latino descent (83%), and 23% were enrolled in daycare or preschool. Nine toddlers were tested in Spanish, and 31 toddlers were tested in English, and none of the participants included in the final sample had exposure to a writing system that did not use the Latin alphabet. Thirteen additional children were tested but excluded for not providing usable data for at least 10 trials because they were distracted or because parents or siblings interfered. The parents of all participating children provided written informed consent, and children received a small prize for their participation. All study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at [masked].

3.1.2. Stimuli & procedures

All stimuli and procedures were the same as those used for the picture naming task in Study 1. Children visiting the museum were invited to perform the task with an experimenter at a table set up away from exhibits. On average, children provided usable data for 18.4 trials.

Children did not perform any additional tasks testing their preliteracy skills.

3.1.3. Other measures

Parents were asked to respond to a questionnaire asking about demographic information (e.g., child's race/ethnicity, parent education level) and the Home Literacy Questionnaire (Van Steensel, 2006). However, in the museum setting, many parents did not respond to all the questions, so we were unable to meaningfully include this information in analyses. All parents who provided responses (N = 30) indicated that their children were read to at home, and 53% reported that their children were read to daily.

3.2. Results & Discussion

As in Study 1, there were significant differences in the mean order in which items in each location were labeled, F(2,78) = 4.88, p = .01, η² = .11. On average, North American toddlers named the left object earliest (M = 1.82) and the right object latest (M = 2.27), complementing what we observed with Hebrew learners (Figure 2). Participants in Study 2 named objects in the expected left-to-right direction on 50.4% of trials (SD = 37%) and named the center object second on 81% of trials (SD = 21%). An exploratory analysis using a Welch t-test showed no difference in the proportion of language-consistent responses for participants tested in English vs. Spanish, t(69.57) = -0.60, p = .55. As in Study 1, we also found a significant positive association between children's age and their tendency to name objects in a language-consistent manner, r = .48, p = .002, 95% CI = [.20, .69] (see Figure 3).

We then collapsed data across the two communities and performed a mixed ANOVA (Community: Israel vs. North America, Object Location: Left vs. Center vs. Right). This analysis revealed a significant interaction, F(2, 216) = 14.93, p <.0001, η² = .12, meaning that as we predicted, children in the two communities showed differences in the order in which they tended to name objects (Figure 2). Pairwise comparisons (using a Bonferroni correction) showed that there were significant differences in the order in which children in Israel vs. North America named the right and left objects (both p <.0001). However, if we re-coded the data to describe the locations as the expected first, second, and third items for that language (i.e., the right object would be 'first' for Israeli children and 'third' for North American children), there were no significant differences at any location (all p >.67), suggesting children displayed highly consistent behavior across the two communities. 4. General Discussion

This set of studies was designed to test the hypothesis that children begin to develop language-specific biases in their visual attention prior to formal education. Toddlers learning two different writing systems showed that they have internalized the conventions of their native text before they have learned to read. Importantly, because we were able to test toddlers exposed to languages that are read in opposite directions (right-to-left for Hebrew vs. left-to-right for English and Spanish), these results provide compelling evidence that biases develop through experience and illustrate how early sensitivity to regularities in their environment may prepare children for later-emerging behaviors.

It is well documented that adults' attention and behavior is shaped by the languages and writing systems that they know (Eviatar & Ibrahim, 2004; Ge et al., 2015; Pae, 2020; Tan et al., 2003), and prior research has shown that culture-specific patterns of attention can often be found during the preschool years (e.g., Duffy et al., 2009; Kuwabara & Smith, 2012; Köster et al., 2018; Senzaki & Shimizu, 2022). In some cases, cultural differences in attention may emerge in infancy, especially for socially-oriented tasks (Haensel et al., 2020; Heise et al., 2025; Köster et al., 2023; Waxman et al., 2016). Likewise, influences of language experience have been observed in infants and toddlers. For example, Choi (2006) showed that differences in how Korean and English describe spatial relations affect the way that two-and-half-year-olds encode spatial categories. Here, we demonstrated that children's attention is influenced by the written form of their language before they know how to read. These results contrast with studies that emphasize the protracted development of children's expertise with text (e.g., Aghababian & Nazir, 2000; Cantlon et al., 2011; Marks et al., 2026; Treiman, 2013). In one study with Hebrew-learning preschoolers, children under five who were asked to try to write short phrases did not consistently write letters from right-to-left (Tolchinsky-Landsmann, & Levin, 1985), suggesting they were still learning Hebrew conventions. The current results provide new evidence that before they are actually able to read and write, toddlers have discovered directional regularities associated with their writing system and use that information to inform their behavior.

Infants are highly adept at tracking and exploiting regularities in their visual input (Fiser & Aslin, 2002; Kirkham et al., 2002; Saffran & Kirkham, 2018), and they are especially sensitive to patterns associated with human communication (Ferguson & Lew-Williams, 2016; Rabagliati et al., 2019). They also reliably detect and follow the gaze of a social partner (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2021; Farroni et al., 2004; Reid et al., 2004; Senju & Csibra 2008). It could be that over time, children learn that adults in their environment tend to move their eyes in a particular direction, leading them to imitate and eventually develop similar habits (Paulus, 2014). Relatedly, they could notice that adults tend to organize objects and information in a particular direction (e.g., setting the table, laying out toys) and follow these conventions in the same way they follow other cultural norms demonstrated by adults (e.g., Lyons et al., 2007; Keupp et al., 2013; McCrink et al., 2018; Nielsen & Tomaselli, 2010).

Another (not mutually exclusive) possibility is that experience with literacy activities is directly familiarizing children with the relevant conventions. Infants' visual attention is shaped by interactions with their parents, who help them organize their movements to focus on key information (Anderson et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023). Shared reading activities (e.g., pointing, following text with a finger, turning pages, following the reader's eye and head movements) could similarly guide toddlers' attention and body position, and as toddlers gain experience observing and practicing motor movements in a particular direction, they may develop languagespecific attentional patterns. A study by Göbel and colleagues (2018) showed that after reading a storybook, English-and Hebrew-learning children were more likely to count objects in the direction that matched their writing system, demonstrating that literacy activities can directly influence children's attention. In the current study, we did not collect fine-grained measures of children's literacy experience and environments, but future research could test whether children who engage more frequently in certain types of interactions develop earlier or more robust attentional biases. However, the fact that older toddlers showed stronger biases is consistent with the view that infants are using a variety of regularities in their environment to tune their attention (Elsabbagh et al., 2013; Lewkowicz & Ghazanfar, 2009; Mauer & Werker, 2014; Potter & Lew-Williams, 2019) and that effects of prior experience often increase with age (Jaffe-Dax et al., 2023; Thiessen & Saffran, 2003). It is possible that some of the older children in the study were beginning to receive informal exposure to literacy instruction even if they were not in school, and it could be that that experience was helping to train their attention. However, toddlers in Study 1 showed limited evidence of pre-reading skills (see Table S1); on average, they were able to correctly name fewer than 2 of 10 letters of the alphabet that they were shown. This lack of explicit literacy knowledge supports the view that the attentional biases we observed likely emerged through implicit learning.

Even on trials when children did not name objects in the direction of their language, they still tended to start on one side and not in the middle, suggesting that they were not naming objects at random and followed conventions of shifting attention from one side to another. This behavior contrasts with evidence that infants' attention is often drawn to central items and not the periphery (e.g., Van Renswoude et al., 2019) and that when toddlers are interacting with physical objects, they tend to direct their attention toward a central stimulus (Bambach et al., 2016). One possibility is that children use different attentional strategies for two-dimensional vs. threedimensional stimuli, or when they are presented with symbolic vs. non-symbolic stimuli. Indeed, there is evidence that children organize quantitative and non-quantitative information differently and that linguistic labels affect the way that they categorize and allocate attention (e.g., McCrink et al., 2017; Miller & Marcovitch, 2011; Ratterman & Gettner, 1998). Future research involving different types of stimuli could be used to understand the breadth of children's attentional biases and to make predictions about how specific types of experience might be especially formative in guiding children's attention. For example, future studies could test whether children's counting behavior or understanding of the mental number line are related to their tendency to label objects in a particular direction, and cross-cultural research could be especially valuable in tracing the origins and development of different types of biases.

Although directional biases were driven by the direction of children's native text, based on the current study, we do not have evidence that the biases are directly related to literacy knowledge and skills. While we attempted to assess children's pre-reading skills in Study 1, the measures we chose were not sufficiently sensitive to children's knowledge at this age, and there was not enough variability in parent-reported exposure to literacy activities for us to find meaningful associations. While it is possible that a different battery of tests could reveal differences, it could also be that children have not yet developed strong enough pre-literacy skills to find meaningful correlations, and our measure of attention was relatively blunt. In the future, it may be possible to get a more sensitive measure of visual attention, using eye tracking measures or other types of tasks that could also be used to explore longitudinal associations between early attentional biases patterns and later reading skills. For example, even if measures of attention and pre-literacy skills are not related at two years, it could be that children who show stronger directional biases at young ages show more structured eye movements as they actually begin to read, and it would be interesting to test whether early directional biases can predict longer-term individual differences in literacy knowledge such as recognizing letters or even non-visual skills such as phonological awareness or story comprehension. It could also be informative to explore differences in the attentional patterns of children exposed alphabetic vs. character-based scripts to see whether scanning patterns or developmental trajectories reflect other properties of the native text besides its direction. More generally, if it is the case that children's attentional biases are related to concurrent or later literacy skills, future research could test whether attentional patterns could be used to identify children who are ready to read or those who may be at risk for reading difficulties, potentially offering an avenue for intervention.

As we aim to understand the development of important skills and expertise, cross-cultural research allows us to test specific predictions about the role of experience by exploring commonalities and differences in the behavior of children raised in different linguistic and cultural communities (Singh, 2026). In line with studies involving older participants, we found that toddlers in Israel and North America showed complementary and opposite patterns of visual attention, revealing that they conform to cultural conventions associated with their native writing system even before they have been formally taught to do so. The fact that we saw nearly identical effects in the strength of their biases and the degree to which those biases increased with age was striking, given other potential differences in children's experience. For example, toddlers in Israel had no systematic exposure to a language other than Hebrew, but it is nevertheless likely that they encounter other writing systems given the ubiquity of English.

Likewise, the North American participants came from a largely bilingual community, and some prior research has suggested that bilingualism can influence early visual attention and perception (Comishen et al, 2019; Singh et al., 2023), but we found highly similar performance in both communities. The robustness of the patterns we observed highlights the pervasive influence of text on visual attention across different contexts and provides a new illustration of children's ability to uncover regularities that will enable them to process information efficiently in their everyday environments.
