Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Over the past decade, mobile Internet-enabled digital devices have provided ubiquitous access to media content and communication, resulting in unprecedented levels of "always on" media use. As a consequence, many users feel stressed and strained by the new demands of digital media use. A fast-growing field of research has addressed such forms of "digital stress" and its psychological implications. The present chapter provides a review and theoretical integration of the state of digital stress research. After an introduction to the basic psychological mechanisms underlying stress reactions based on the Transactional Model of Stress (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), the chapter reviews and synthesizes existing definitions of digital stress. It then summarizes and systematizes key empirical findings regarding the central predictors, boundary conditions, and effects of digital stress. The review concludes with a discussion of recent developments, open questions, and future challenges for the field of digital stress research. Over the past two decades, developments in information and communication technologies (ICT) have substantially transformed interpersonal communication and social relationships. Thanks to mobile devices and ubiquitous Internet connectivity, media content and digital communication are now permanently available, leaving many users in a state of more or less constant connectedness with the online sphere and with a potentially large and heterogeneous network of social interaction partners (Vorderer et al., 2018). This state of being "permanently online and permanently connected" (POPC; Reinecke et al., 2018, p. 2; see also Vorderer et al., 2016) makes individual users constantly addressable not only within their closer social networks but also for a much broader social sphere (Ling, 2018). This creates new social expectations for availability more generally (Hall, 2017) and the need to renegotiate communication practices within romantic and intimate relationships more specifically (Rieger, 2018). These new forms of digital communication may increase the sense of connectedness (Pouwels et al., 2021). At the same time, the mere amount of communication content, the high frequency of digital social interactions, and the accompanying stream of messages and notifications can be a constant source of distraction in users' everyday lives (Siebers et al., 2022). Given these different demands of digital media environments, concerns have been raised about whether media users can find the right balance between potential beneficial and detrimental outcomes of mobile connectivity (Vanden Abeele, 2021). While the empirical evidence regarding negative long-term implications of digital media use on mental health remains mixed and suggests small overall effects at best (Meier & Reinecke, 2021), more short-term impairments of psychological well-being in the form of ICT-related stress and strain are more clearly documented (Wolfers & Utz, 2022). A continuously growing research field explores the predictors, boundary conditions, and psychological consequences of such forms of "digital stress" that users experience when they perceive digital media demands to exceed their available resources (Khetawat & Steele, 2023; Reinecke et al., 2017; Steele et al., 2020). Our central aim is to provide a concise overview of the state of the field and to summarize its central findings. In the following sections, we will first summarize theoretical conceptualizations and definitions of digital stress. On this basis, we will review empirical evidence regarding the drivers and consequences of digital stress.

We will conclude by discussing more recent theoretical developments in the field and identifying central challenges for future research.

Digital Stress: Concept Explication and Theoretical Foundations

Although the definitions and operationalizations of digital stress vary widely within the field, research in media psychology and communication frequently refers to the transactional model of stress and coping (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) as a theoretical basis for the understanding of stress reactions to digital media use and communication (Hefner & Vorderer, 2017; Wolfers & Schneider, 2021; Wolfers & Utz, 2022). Following the basic assumptions of the model, stress can be conceptualized as "an unfavorable personenvironment relationship" (Lazarus, 1993, p. 8). More specifically, the model suggests that stress occurs when individuals perceive environmental demands (i.e., stressors) as taxing or exceeding the resources available to them. That is, the individual constantly appraises the relevance of current events for personal well-being (Lazarus, 1993). Within primary appraisal, the individual evaluates the relationship between environmental demands and available resources. If the situation is perceived as threatening, challenging, or harmful due to an imbalance of demands and resources, available coping options are evaluated within secondary appraisal (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Depending on the available coping options, the individual may engage in different coping strategies, for example, aiming at resolving the stressor or reducing the negative emotions elicited by the stress response (Wolfers & Utz, 2022). Accordingly, specific aspects of digital media use can be perceived as more or less stressful depending on situational and person-level factors (Hefner & Vorderer, 2017): A high number of received messages may be perceived as stressful by the individual when resources are low (e.g., when performing an important task), but appraised more positively and favorably in situations where more resources are available (e.g., when commuting home in a train). As digital media have been an integral part of work and communication routines within occupational contexts long before entering the personal domain, first attempts to explore digital stress were made in the work context under the label of "technostress" more than four decades ago (La Torre et al., 2019). The concept was initially introduced by Brod (1982) to describe employees' inability to accept and successfully adapt to the demands of ICT in the workplace. Today, the term technostress is used more broadly to describe different forms of stress reactions to ICT-related demands at work, including technooverload (i.e., the need to work faster or longer due to ICT), techno-invasion (i.e., problems with boundary management between work and personal spheres), techno-complexity (i.e., problems due to insufficient computer skills), techno-insecurity (i.e., threats of losing one's job due to ICTs) or techno-uncertainty (i.e., the constant need to adapt to updates and changes in ICT) (La Torre et al., 2019; Tarafdar et al., 2007). These and other forms of technostress in the occupational context have been linked to various negative health and work outcomes (e.g., Borle et al., 2021).

Digital stress outside of the work context has received broader attention after digital communication in the form of personal email, messengers, or social media use has also become an essential part of everyday life more generally (e.g., Misra & Stokols, 2012; Thomée et al., 2007) and further intensified with the advent of mobile Internet connections and devices. Mobile media make digital stress even more prevalent due to the permanence of access, excessive amounts of available content and information, and fast speed of digital communication (Hefner & Vorderer, 2017). In comparison to early conceptualizations of technostress in the work domain, this new field of research has considerably widened the theoretical perspective to include digital stress elicited through all forms of digital media use.

Accordingly, Reinecke et al. (2017) define digital stress as any "stress reactions elicited by environmental demands originating from ICT use" (p. 92). More recently, Freytag and Hefner (2023) have further extended the definition of digital stress to include "stress elicited by the confrontation or interaction with, use of, or cognitive orientation toward digital information and communication," acknowledging that stress reactions may not only be elicited through ICT use per se, but also by cognitive processes or mental preoccupation related to digital media use. Accordingly, whereas earlier definitions tended to be use-centric, relating digital stress primarily to usage behavior (e.g., frequent checking for messages or mediamultitasking), more recent definitions are more user-centric and more systematically integrate idiosyncratic predictors of digital stress (e.g., subjectively perceived levels of connection pressure). Furthermore, digital stress has been addressed both as a situational response to ICT-related stressors (e.g., Gilbert et al., 2022) such as immediate reactions to a large number of notifications , but also as a person-level variable (e.g., Reinecke et al., 2017). A more general impression of being unable to deal with many incoming messages and notifications across a longer period, for example, may represent more chronic forms of digital stress experiences.

While the aforementioned conceptualizations of digital stress build upon classic work on subjective stress experiences (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984) and merely situate the psychological concept of stress in the context of ICT use, other strands of research take different approaches to define digital stress. Some research focuses on digital stress experiences within specific ICT-use contexts, such as "Facebook-related stress" (Beyens et al., 2016) or "social media stress" (van der Schuur et al., 2019). Other conceptualizations treat digital stress as part of a multi-dimensional construct, such as "entrapment," which Hall (2017) defines as "the guilt, anxiety, or stress to respond and be available to others via mobile devices" (p. 148). A third branch of research attempts to conceptualize different subcomponents of digital stress. Steele et al. (2020) and Hall et al. (2021), for example, define a total of five components of digital stress: availability stress, approval anxiety, fear of missing out (FoMO), connection overload, and online vigilance.

The breadth of different definitions of digital stress may come with conceptual difficulties. While narrow, platform-specific conceptualizations-such as Facebook-related stress-may implicitly suggest that the basic stress experience differs fundamentally in different ICT contexts, composites-such as entrapment-make it hard to clearly differentiate the specific role of stress versus other components, such as guilt, as potential mediators between ICT use and outcome variables of interest. Finally, conceptualizations of different sub-components of digital stress seem to refer to different stressors, that is, sources of digital stress, rather than the stress experience per se. In our review of the state of the field that follows below, we therefore discuss these sub-dimensions as potential predictors of digital stress along with other variables, rather than as components or facets of digital stress.

The State of the Field: Predictors, Boundary Conditions, and Effects of Digital Stress

Over the past years, a growing body of research has produced a large number of empirical findings regarding the predictors and boundary conditions of digital stress, as well as the effects of digital stress reactions on different outcome variables, particularly psychological health and well-being (see Khetawat & Steele, 2023, for a recent metaanalysis). In the following sections, we aim at summarizing and integrating the most important findings regarding the predictors and effects of digital stress. For that purpose, we first identify three general clusters of predictors: 1) usage behavior and perceptions of use, 2) cognitive and motivational constructs and processes, and 3) social and interpersonal predictors of digital stress. A second important cluster of work has identified cognitive and motivational predictors of digital stress. This line of research emphasizes that recent changes in the media environment have not only transformed patterns of usage behavior but also led to the development of a psychological state of being permanently online (Vorderer et al., 2016 (Vorderer et al., , 2018) ) in many users that creates self-regulatory challenges in everyday life. Reinecke et al.

(2018) have developed the theoretical construct of online vigilance to refer to the cognitive orientation towards and chronic attention to digital content and communication as well as the motivational disposition to prioritize options for digital communication over other behavioral alternatives. The construct consists of three sub-facets: 1) salience (i.e., the degree of cognitive connection and preoccupation with the online sphere), 2) monitoring (i.e., the individual tendency to check for new online content and to enter the personal online sphere regularly), and 3) reactibility (i.e., the inclination to quickly respond to cues related to digital communication and to prioritize those over other situational demands). Online vigilance has been proposed as a central dimension of digital stress (e.g., Hall et al., 2021) and consistently linked to stress and mental fatigue in previous work (Freytag et al., 2021; Gilbert et al., 2022; Van Gaeveren et al., 2026). Online vigilance contributes to stress in multiple ways: previous research demonstrates an indirect effect of online vigilance on digital stress through increased communication load and multitasking (Freytag et al., 2021; Gilbert et al., 2022). On the other hand, both between-person differences in "chronic" levels as well as situational within-person fluctuations in online vigilance may directly induce stress by reducing the availability of situational resources through cognitive and motivational pre-occupation with digital communication (Freytag et al., 2021; Gilbert et al., 2022; Reinecke et al., 2018). A third pathway that connects online vigilance to perceived stress, goal conflict, is represented by the self-regulatory challenges resulting from constant connectedness. The constant availability of and the immediate gratifications provided by digital media make it hard for many users to resist the temptation to engage in digital media use despite the presence of other goals or obligations (Du et al., 2021; Hofmann et al., 2017). Frequently, mobile devices, social media, and digital communication are used as a tool for procrastination, that is, to dysfunctionally delay other important, yet less hedonically pleasant tasks (Aalbers et al., 2022; Meier et al., 2016 Meier et al., , 2023)). Consequently, digital communication and media use are frequently associated with perceived goal conflict. Both general (Sirois, 2023) as well as media-specific procrastination (Meier et al., 2016) lead to ruminative thought and increased task-related time pressure and have been linked to stress reactions in previous research. Furthermore, perceived goal conflict is strongly associated with negative self-conscious emotions, such as guilt (Reinecke & Hofmann, 2016), and represents a central boundary condition for the emergence of digital stress: in an experience sampling study by Gilbert et al. (2022), perceived goal conflict increased the effects of online vigilance on stress, suggesting that cognitive and motivational pre-occupation with digital communication is particularly stressful when appraised as a threat to other important goals and obligations.

A third and final cluster of studies acknowledges the inherently interpersonal nature of digital communication by addressing different social and interpersonal predictors of digital stress. This line of work refers to the fact that the constant availability of digital communication results in a "mobile connectivity paradox" (Vanden Abeele, 2021, p. 934): while a POPC media environment may provide new avenues for instant need satisfaction in everyday life, it also results in increased social expectation for constant connectedness and availability that may impair psychological well-being.

New opportunities for digital communication have shifted availability norms that represent the social consensus concerning the forms of social interaction and responsiveness that are deemed socially acceptable (Bayer, Campbell, et al., 2016). Many users, and particularly adolescents, experience strong availability expectations from their social environments that are further intensified by technical features, such as "seen functions" implemented in many messenger apps (e.g., De

Groote & Van Ouytsel, 2022; Mai et al., 2015).

As a consequence, social pressure to be constantly available has increasingly become a situational demand and an important source of digital stress (Reinecke et al., 2017; Steele et al., 2020). Such availability pressure may increase stress via different mechanisms. First, availability pressure may drive stress-inducing usage practices, such as communication load (Reinecke et al., 2017). Additionally, social pressure decreases the availability of situational resources by impairing personal autonomy and increases the risk of self-control failure when digital communication is prioritized over other personal goals and needs to meet availability expectations (Halfmann & Rieger, 2019). Ironically, even attempts to successfully selfregulate and shield other goals from availability pressure may result in increased stress.

Findings from Halfmann et al. (2021) suggest that not responding to availability norms to avoid goal conflicts with other important tasks may increase feelings of guilt. Accordingly, availability pressure may increase digital stress both in the case of use and non-use of digital communication.

In addition to social expectations to be constantly available, digital stress may also be a result of perceived social pressure for online self-presentation (Hefner & Vorderer, 2017; Steele et al., 2020). Social media in particular afford unprecedented opportunities to provide insights into all aspects of the self. Younger users in particular may perceive considerable pressure to produce online content and present themselves online frequently (Stevic, 2024). At the same time, social norms for self-presentation in social media tend to follow a positivity bias that puts emphasis on positive forms of self-disclosure (Reinecke & Trepte, 2014; Schreurs et al., 2023). As a result, users may feel pressured to adhere to social expectations for perfection or beauty in their self-presentation (Winstone et al., 2023).

As other users can publicly react to or comment on online postings, many users perceive approval anxiety (Winstone et al., 2023). This fear of negative reactions to online self-presentation is discussed as a further predictor of digital stress (Hefner & Vorderer, 2017; Steele et al., 2020). While previous research has linked self-presentation to increased anxiety (e.g., Hjetland et al., 2024) and other forms of negative psychological well-being (Khetawat & Steele, 2023), empirical studies that provide a direct link to perceived stress are relatively scarce. Beyond approval anxiety, online self-disclosure may also lay the ground for dysfunctional and self-threatening forms of social comparison, which have been proposed as another social predictor of digital stress (Hefner & Vorderer, 2017; Steele et al., 2020).

Similar to the empirical evidence regarding approval anxiety, however, previous research provides ample empirical evidence for a negative relationship between online social comparison and several forms of impaired mental health (Khetawat & Steele, 2023; McComb et al., 2023), yet no clear link to perceived (digital) stress per se. Thus, more studies are needed that directly test the association between approval anxiety or social comparison with stress reactions.

A last social predictor of digital stress that is frequently discussed in the literature refers to the fear of social exclusion. More specifically, the fear of missing out (FoMO) refers to the "pervasive apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent" (Przybylski et al., 2013 (Przybylski et al., , p. 1841)). Social media provide almost constant insights into the social lives of close and distant social interaction partners and are thus considered an amplifier of FoMO (Przybylski et al., 2013). In fact, empirical evidence consistently suggests a positive association between FoMO and digital media use (Akbari et al., 2021). FoMO has thus been proposed as a central component or predictor of digital stress (Steele et al., 2020). FoMO may drive digital stress by encouraging stress-inducing usage practices (higher communication load, more overall digital media use). Additionally, FoMO may make digital communication more stressful per se (Beyens et al., 2016).

Overall, the high number of predictor variables found in previous research underlines the complexity of digital stress and suggests that it is driven by usage patterns, cognitive and motivational aspects, as well as the social dynamics of digital media use. The complexity is further increased by the fact that many predictor variables show dynamic, reciprocal interactions, such as cognitive and motivational factors (e.g., online vigilance) driving stressinducing media use patterns (e.g., communication load, multitasking, procrastinatory media use) and vice versa. Finally, many predictors of digital stress are "Janus faced" (Arnold, 2003, p. 232) as they can drive stress in some situations yet increase the positive effects of digital communication in other contexts. For example, while availability pressure is a clear predictor of digital stress, it was also linked to increased levels of relatedness satisfaction, communication quality, and relationship satisfaction in other research (X. Li & Chan, 2025).

Likewise, while FoMO is associated with a higher risk for digital stress, it may also intensify the positive outcomes of social media use, such as increased affective well-being and decreased loneliness (Karsay et al., 2023). It is thus a central challenge for future research to gain a better understanding of the complex dynamics connecting different predictors of digital stress and of the boundary conditions that help to explain under which circumstances these variables and processes have detrimental versus beneficial effects on stress and well-being.

Individual and Relational Effects of Digital Stress

Beyond the drivers of digital stress, previous research has also made significant attempts to shed light on potential effects of digital stress, both on the individual user and social relationships. With regard to effects on individuals, past research has primarily addressed the consequences of digital stress for mental health and psychological well-being.

The empirical evidence largely mirrors the findings of general stress research that consistently link perceived stress to both impaired mental (e.g., burnout and depression; Cristóbal-Narváez et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020) and physical health (e.g., coronary heart disease; Gaffey et al., 2022). Similarly, in a current meta-analysis by Khetawat et al. (2023), all five dimensions of digital stress proposed by Steele et al. (2020) were associated with lower levels of psychological well-being. Yet, as the analysis did not address perceived stress resulting from digital media use per se, but rather predictors of digital stress, the results do not directly support the role of stress as a central mechanism linking digital communication to impaired psychological health. Numerous other studies, however, provide more direct empirical evidence linking digital stress to impaired psychological health and well-being, both crosssectionally (e.g., Reinecke et al., 2017) as well as longitudinally (e.g., Hall, 2017; Nick et al., 2022; Thomée et al., 2011). A potential mediator of these negative health effects of digital stress is impaired sleep quality. Several studies suggest that both stress and sleep disturbances are outcomes of digital media use (e.g., Thomée et al., 2011 Thomée et al., , 2012)). Longitudinal studies specifically testing a potential effect of digital stress on sleep quality, however, come to diverging results (Koban et al., 2023b; van der Schuur et al., 2019).

In addition to effects on individual well-being, previous research has also explored the relational repercussions of digital stress on dyads and the quality of social interaction. As with individual effects, social or relational effects have often been investigated as consequences of digital stress predictors rather than of the stress experiences proper. Early ideas about how permanent mobile connectivity can alter social relationships (e.g., Katz & Aakhus, 2002; Licoppe, 2004; Ling, 2004) have been applied and developed further to particularly examine how availability enhances or burdens relationship quality. Many findings support both beneficial and detrimental consequences of permanent connectedness on relationship quality (e.g., Coundouris et al., 2021; Taylor et al., 2022), and the reason for these inconclusive results seems to lie in the social norms and expectations about availability (e.g., Bayer, Campbell, et al., 2016; Geber et al., 2024; Ling, 2012). For instance, the expectation to be permanently available to others can foster relationship maintenance, security, and belongingness, and thus relationship satisfaction (Taylor & Bazarova, 2021). At the same time, however, it may reduce autonomous behavior and increase entrapment, thereby decreasing relationship satisfaction (Hall & Baym, 2012). Furthermore, practices such "phubbing" (i.e., attending to the smartphone during face-to-face interactions; Arenz & Schnauber-Stockmann, 2024), can result in attentional conflict, social expectation violations, and experiences of stress and social exclusion and thus impair intimacy and perceived relationship quality (Ni et al., 2025; Vanden Abeele, 2020). Although such complex patterns between the use of digital media and relational outcomes have been repeatedly found in the last years (e.g., Angelini et al., 2023; Angelini & Gini, 2025; Chan, 2018; Fox et al., 2025), the role of the different components of the stress process-that is, demands, resources, appraisals, stress reactions, and coping (see Figure 1)-for such outcomes have not been fully disentangled yet.

Recent Developments and Tasks for Future Research

Our review of the conceptualizations, predictors, and effects of digital stress demonstrates that digital stress research is a prolific and dynamically developing field of research. At the same time, the fast-growing number of theoretical approaches and empirical findings within this area of work pose important challenges for future research.

As outlined above, the field is characterized by a multitude of conceptualizations and a lack of consensus regarding the definition and operationalization of digital stress. Recent conceptualizations have treated digital stress as a subcomponent of multidimensional constructs such as "entrapment" (Hall, 2017) or proposed different subdimensions of digital stress (Steele et al., 2020). Whereas these approaches systematize mechanisms linking the use of digital media to psychological well-being, they may miss out on differentiating digital stressors from the stress response, that is, confound the causes and consequences of digital stress. For a better understanding of digital stress, we thus propose to more narrowly conceptualize digital stress merely as any form of perceived stress subjectively experienced in the context of digital media use. Such an essentialist view on digital stress allows the field to draw from the rich psychological research on general stress reactions and to closely integrate theoretical models of perceived stress, such as the transactional model (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984).

We propose that a closer theoretical integration of psychological stress research may also provide promising impulses for a better understanding of the "mechanics" of digital stress. A first step toward a better understanding of associations between digital stress predictors and reactions may be a more systematic conceptualization of mechanisms along the transactional model of stress (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). In fact, the predictors of digital stress reviewed above can be tracked to all components of the model: some predictors drive stress by increasing situational demands (e.g., communication load) or by reducing situational resources (e.g., online vigilance), thus increasing the risk for situations with an unfavorable person-environment relationship. Other predictors refer to negative subjective experience of digital media use and thus represent forms of primary appraisal that suggest a situational threat to the well-being or self-regulatory goals of the individual (e.g., communication overload, perceived goal conflict). Yet others affect secondary appraisal, for example, by reducing behavioral alternatives and situational coping options through perceived social pressure and decreased autonomy regarding the use or non-use of digital media. We believe that further exploring the relationship between predictors of stress and the mechanisms proposed in the transactional model is a promising task for future research.

A transactional view on digital stress also appears highly promising in addressing a third major challenge of the field: a large number of studies provide empirical evidence for important effects of digital stress on mental health and relational processes. Yet, under which boundary conditions these negative effects occur and when digital media use represents a resource in the everyday lives of users rather than a stressor remains an open question. In this context, we believe that the coping perspective presented in the transactional model of stress may provide important impulses.

In general, media use has often been conceptualized not only as a stressor but also as a coping tool to respond to stressful events (Wolfers & Schneider, 2021). In a conceptual model, Wolfers and Utz (2022) apply the idea of media as stressors, media as resources, and media as coping tools to the field of social media. Such an approach is useful as it does not conflate but differentiates the causes, processes, and boundary conditions in the emergence of stress experiences in a digital environment. It is also evident that multiple steps are necessary to elicit a stress response. Thus, encountering one stressor alone might not be sufficient. For example, encountering many unread messages on the mobile phone results in individually different appraisals in the first place but also-taken individual resources into account-in a second step. Some may find the presence of messages positive and interpret it as a sign of social connectedness; others may find it threatening or challenging if it implies that they need to read and respond to the messages immediately. But even among the latter, the intensity of the stress response should depend on the availability of coping resources (e.g., the skill to communicate effectively).

In the same vein, the IM³UNE (Schneider et al., 2022) assumes that mobile media demands are neither beneficial nor detrimental to well-being per se until they have been evaluated accordingly. Like in the transactional model (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), the appraisal process depends on specific personal resources that empower users to use mobile media to their full advantage (e.g., mindfulness; Bauer et al., 2017; Bayer, Dal Cin, et al., 2016; Lutz et al., 2026). These salutary factors can help appraise potentially demanding situations as challenging rather than threatening (e.g., Crum et al., 2020; Jamieson et al., 2018), thereby altering the satisfaction and frustration users experience while using digital media. For instance, mindfully dealing with availability pressure can result in higher satisfaction and lower frustration of autonomy needs (e.g., Lutz et al., 2026; Schneider et al., 2022). In turn, whether these need experiences contribute to the emergence of stress responses also depends on salutary resources, which may buffer or mitigate the unfavorable effects of need frustrations. For example, experiencing autonomy need frustration while messaging can be less stressful for those who are more mindful (e.g., Lutz et al., 2026; Schneider et al., 2022). Thus, in stark contrast to the dominant pathogenic approach that focuses on the inherently negative aspects of digital stress(ors), the IM³UNE takes a salutogenic perspective-focusing on how we stay healthy instead of what makes us ill (Antonovsky, 1987)-and assumes that environmental demands are omnipresent and inevitable in the human condition; in fact, they are necessary for personal growth, resilience, and wellness.

Finally, future research may greatly benefit from gaining deeper insights into the coping strategies applied by media users in everyday life to find a balance between the demands of digital media use and other personal goals and needs. In this context, a growing body of work explores whether limiting one's digital media use could reduce digital media demands and thus mitigate stress. Whereas tests of digital disconnection strategies have reached mixed results so far, most suggest benefits of disconnection for stress reduction, particularly among heavy media users, in the work context, and in the short term (Gilbert et al., 2026; Q. Li et al., 2023; Plackett et al., 2023). For example, digital disconnection may change how users appraise demands during remote work, thereby reducing perceived stress (Schmitt et al., 2021). Critically, research on digital disconnection highlights how the (non-) use of digital media is embedded in social networks and norms (Van Bruyssel et al., 2023).

The capacity and privilege to disconnect for reducing stress are unequally distributed, with those who are typically assigned the role of caring for their social network (often women) facing difficulties in disconnecting (Kratel, 2023). Similarly, younger age groups may uphold stronger availability norms than others that may limit an individual's opportunity to engage in digital disconnection for stress reduction (Geber et al., 2024). These insights highlight again that digital stress is often a social phenomenon. A relational perspective on media-related stressors, stress responses, and coping will therefore be a fruitful approach to studying digital stress in the future.
