Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. 1.1. Depressive and manic states as ideal types

Depression and mania are often framed as polar opposites in terms of affect and symptoms, but an analyses of the lived experience of both disorders have often pointed to their shared, underlying temporal structure, including living in the eternal now (Gruber et al., 2012; Wyllie, 2005), living an atemporal life between slowing down and speeding up, or between retardation (Fuchs, 2010) and acceleration (Moskalewicz & Schwartz, 2018), living in desynchronization (Fuchs, 2014; Northoff et al., 2018; Ratcliffe, 2015), or coloring of one's reflections on one's past and future experiences (De Haan, 2020), among others.

Nevertheless, the valence of manic and depressive experiences is usually conflicting. It was argued that in mania, the feeling of "eternity" is empowering (Moskalewicz & Schwartz, 2018), whereas in depression, it is a torturous trap (Fusar-Poli et al., 2023). In the former, eternity represents endless possibilities; in the latter, the disappearance of any possibilities. In mania, the person feels limitless energy, while in depression, there is no energy for even a little movement (Frenga et al., 2020). And since bipolar disorder is lived in cycles, its temporal horizon includes the remembered or anticipated extreme poles. We argue that, despite polar extremes, time permeates all these dimensions and intertwines them into a common, integral structure capable of uniting these contradictions and ambivalences. However, the challenge lies in reconciling these "different but same" experiences. It underscores the need to conceptualize bipolar disorder (BD) beyond the boundaries of the medical paradigm alone and incorporate a phenomenological perspective on its experiential aspects.

BD is a psychiatric condition characterized by opposing symptoms across affective, cognitive, psychomotor, and social domains. In the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (American Psychiatric Association, 2022), bipolar and related disorders are located between the chapters on schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic and depressive disorders. Bipolar I is defined by the presence of manic symptoms lasting at least one week, while Bipolar II involves at least one hypomanic episode lasting a minimum of four days and one major depressive episode. Cyclothymic disorder is characterized by periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet the full criteria for a manic, hypomanic, or major depressive episode. BD may involve psychotic features and can occur with anxious distress, mixed features, rapid cycling, and melancholia, among others (DSM-5-TR, 2022, p. 282).1 It is important to note that bipolar disorder presents a continuum of severity from normal to psychotic and a continuum of affect from depression via three bipolar subgroups to mania (Angst, 2007).

Like other psychiatric conditions, BD is lived differently by different individuals, despite the existence of standardized diagnostic criteria. Traditional diagnostic systems, such as the aforementioned DSM or International Classification of Diseases (ICD), conceptualize mental disorders through fixed categories and symptom checklists, often neglecting how individuals experience their illness, what meanings they attribute to it, and how the illness is integrated into their subjective lives. In contrast, the phenomenological and enactivist approaches to psychiatry emphasize the lived experience of the person, and how the illness unfolds within the context of an individual life (De Haan, 2020; Jaspers, 1963).

Phenomenological psychopathology, originally focused on the description of atypical experiences, gradually shifted to the study of structures of subjectivity that underpin self-awareness, embodiment, spatiality, temporality, intentionality, and intersubjectivity (Fuchs, 2009). It thus moves beyond a strictly biomedical paradigm and offers the potential for more nuanced diagnoses. It allows us to take subjective experience in psychiatry more seriously (Kyzar & Denfield, 2023). This is in line with the emerging enactivist approaches to mental disorders, which integrate phenomenology into the broadened scientific model of psychopathology (De Haan, 2020). Instead of single-aspect models in psychiatry that focus mainly on one of the dimensions of psychiatric disorders, whether physiological (biological and neuro-reductionist models), experiential (phenomenological and psychoanalytical approaches), or social (social psychiatry), or even dualist or integrative models (e.g., values-based psychiatry, or the biopsychosocial model), the embodied-enactive approach has a different target: not simply to "add on" an existential or phenomenological dimension to otherwise established models, but to reframe psychiatry in a way that focuses on the body, social interactions, the lived experience of the agent, and targets the whole "brain-body-environment" as one dynamical system.

In this article, we will use the terms depression and mania as ideal types (Jaspers, 1963; Ratcliffe, 2015, p. 252; Schwartz & Wiggins, 1987; Weber, 1949), regardless of the more specific diagnostic subcategories or designations. The use of ideal types makes it possible to compare the abstract structures of experience by disregarding the clinical level of the variety of symptomatic presentations of a disorder. Focusing on the disorder subcategories, on the other hand, would fragment both phenomena in question and make their comprehensive comparison impossible in principle.

The ideal type is neither an empirical generalization or a statistical mean, nor a claim about a shared essence; it is a deliberately idealized construct that functions as a heuristic and analytical tool (Weber, 1949). For this reason, cases that differ from an ideal type do not disprove it. Instead, the type's value comes from making these differences clear and describable. We expect individual cases to differ from the type in specific ways. These differences are informative; they show where the type needs more detail. Our aim is not to disregard diagnostic distinctions, but to employ an idealized model to reveal the experiential structures of manic and depressive states. Understood in this way, depression and mania function less as two heterogeneous conditions than as two poles of a space of variation: the structural features we describe are dimensions along which individual presentations can be located and compared, rather than criteria that each case must satisfy. The ideal types we propose are anchored in both case descriptions, philosophical analysis, and empirical and meta-analysis of data.

The appendices document this heterogeneity in more detail, presenting the range of facets of temporal experience from which the ideal types were abstracted.

The experiences described may be present to some extent in one person but not in another; in some, they may be more pronounced, whilst in others less so. Nor do these phenomena form a fixed set: they may combine in different constellations, so that two individuals may share a diagnosis while differing in which temporal alterations predominate and in how these alterations hang together. This variability also extends to the level of awareness at which such experiences occur: for some individuals, they remain largely pre-reflective, while for others, they are explicitly registered, and this, too, is not static, as prereflective temporal experiences may become reflectively explicit over time. It may also be the case that a single person experiences the phenomena described here at different stages of illness, not all at once. Assessing the extent to which the temporal symptoms we have described are reported by patients and occur in the BD population would require a separate quantitative study. This lies beyond the scope of this article, which focuses specifically on the qualitative aspects reported in published research. Nevertheless, this could be a direction for future research into bipolar disorder.

Our approach aims to advance an integrated phenomenology of bipolarity that bridges clinical psychiatry and the philosophy of time. It also encourages clinicians to look beyond symptoms and categories and work with the patient's lived sense of temporality. Therapeutic implications may include time-sensitive interventions aimed at reestablishing temporal coherence and synchrony, slowing overly accelerated processes, or revitalizing those that have become static. The focus thus shifts from symptoms and categories to the restoration of temporal continuity and meaning.

1.2. Phenomenological approach to bipolar temporality

Lived time has been a central topic in phenomenological psychopathology. Since Ludwig Binswanger's early work on phobia (Binswanger, 1911), four generations of phenomenologically oriented psychiatrists have explored this fundamental dimension of disordered subjectivity in a manner principally distinct from the psychophysical approach. The latter, practiced from Wilhelm Wundt to the present day, presupposes an untensed and binary account of time, to use the analytic distinction (Turetzky, 1998). The phenomenological approach, on the contrary, focuses on the threefold (tensed and "ecstatic," to use Heidegger's (1962) term) and lived (which today often extends toward embodied and enacted (Gallagher, 2017)) experience of time, stretching from its transcendental conditions of possibility, to manifold life-world manifestations and intersubjectivity. As such, it concerns the experience of time in a wider sense than measurable time perception. Phenomenological investigations take into account diverse aspects of temporality such as internal self-awareness of the stream of consciousness, succession, continuity, and duration (Minkowski, 1970), intersubjectivity (Fuchs, 2013), and the embodied dimension (Droit-Volet, 2013), among others. In lived experience, though, these various aspects are interwoven and can only be distinguished through reflection.

The fact that both Mania and depression have a temporal core was early recognized. Eugène Minkowski noticed that mania is a manifestation of "a mental subduction in time" (Minkowski, 1970, p. 296). Erwin Straus observed that depression consists of the disconnection of what he called immanent and worldly time (Moskalewicz, 2017; Straus, 1928). But phenomenological theories differ. Drawing on the Bergsonian concept of duration, Minkowski observed that patients with mania experience a distortion of syntony, rendering their existence instantaneous (Minkowski, 1970). On the other hand, Binswanger's early Heideggerian model posited manic existence as ahistorical and similarly pointed to discontinuities manifested in the flight of ideas (Binswanger, 1964). However, Binswanger's later model interpreted mania as a failure of the implicit temporal constitution of the world. Regarding depression, a widely accepted Fuchs' model posits that experiential changes result from weakening conative-affective life dynamics and involve desynchronization with the biological and social environment (Fuchs, 2001; Salvatore et al., 2012). Ratcliffe focuses on existential feelings and underlines the loss of practical significance of ideas and articulation of long-term projects (Ratcliffe, 2015). There are other, partly overlapping conceptualizations as well. Importantly, many authors have noticed that at the extremes of the manic-depressive spectrum, a standstill of time occurs. Patients report that all is timeless (Straus, 1947), that time is still (Nosachev, 1992), or eternal (Moskalewicz & Schwartz, 2020). In the case of depression, the experiential standstill was termed "McTaggart's syndrome" (Broome, 2005) and "temporal delusion" (Moskalewicz, 2018), while the resulting immobilization of the self in its temporal becoming was posited as the core phenomenal characteristic of psychotic experience (Moskalewicz, 2015).

1.3. Aims of research

The first aim of this paper is to provide an overview of the lived experience of time in depression and mania. While the former has been extensively studied, the latter remains relatively underexplored. Thus, offering an overview of this phenomenon is a valuable contribution in itself.

The second aim is to interpretatively explore the temporal dynamics between depressive and manic states. This issue also remains significantly underexplored, and to approach it, we will use the concepts of implicit and explicit time (Fuchs, 2010). Implicit temporality refers to pre-reflective experience, in which we are not aware of time or the body. It requires two integrated conditions: the synthesis or continuity of consciousness and conation. According to Fuchs (2010), explicit time results from the interruption or negation of implicit time and unfolds across the dimensions of the present, past, and future.

The third aim is to describe the general structure of bipolar temporal experience based on a scoping review of literature and its interpretation. We present core temporal characteristics, including living in an eternal present, oscillating between slowing down and speeding up, experiencing a disruption of the sense of temporal continuity and of synchronization with others, feeling uncoupled from the world, and sensing that one is trapped in one's own body. Afterward, we briefly introduce how these are woven into the experience of the body, affect, sense of self, and relation to others and the world.

Beyond these aims, this inquiry is potentially relevant to psychotherapy. By describing the lived experience of time in bipolar disorder, it invites clinicians to consider temporal disconnection as a core dimension of the disorder rather than a secondary symptom of mood change. In therapeutic contexts, awareness of how patients inhabit time, whether as stagnation, acceleration, or fragmentation, can deepen understanding of their day-to-day experience and inform more attuned interventions.

2.1.Data collection

To identify and synthesize the existing literature on bipolar temporality, we conducted a scoping review and interpreted the findings from a phenomenological perspective. The primary set of keywords focused on bipolar and related disorders. Temporal keywords were used to narrow the search to articles addressing the lived experience of time. Additional terms were included to target studies that specifically describe first-person experiences. Three databases were consulted: in the first phase, Scopus and PubMed, and in the second phase, Google Scholar. First, combinations of three levels of the following keywords were searched for in Scopus and PubMed (using the search in title and abstract option): main keywords: depression, hypo/mania, manic depression, bipolar, affective disorders, mood disorders. temporal keywords: time, time perception, temporality, time experience, time flow, lived time, synchrony.

additional/phenomenological keywords: phenomenology, first-person. This gave a total of 122 different keyword combinations. The searches in both engines returned varying numbers of results depending on the combination used, with some returning thousands of articles, others hundreds, some just two, or none at all. The results were usually very similar across Scopus and PubMed, and the majority were not actually related to the query (phenomenological psychopathology of time experience in bipolar disorder) -they were mostly non-phenomenological, many concerned with neurology. To alleviate this problem, the word "experience" was added to all 122 keyword combinations to make the search results more qualitatively oriented. This narrowed down the search results significantly in both databases (for example the search for "mania+temporal*" in Scopus returned 749 results, using "mania+temporal*+experience returned 53 results, "mania+temporal*+phenomenolog" returned 27 results, "mania +temporal*+phenomenolog*+experience" 12 results). After using the "experience" keyword, all results returned in cases where the total number of results did not exceed 350 positions (if the number of positions was higher, it generally meant that the search results were not related to the topic at hand) and were checked by reading article titles and abstracts. This resulted in a total of 131 articles.

In the second stage, the same keyword combinations were searched in Google Scholar, which does not offer the option to search only titles and abstracts, making it less precise, but it does include entries absent from Scopus and PubMed. The search was conducted in two ways: first, by looking at the most relevant results across all years; and second, by filtering the results to 2018-2025. Generally, search results stop being relevant after 5-10 pages. Many articles were the same as in Scopus and PubMed, but the search nonetheless added 15 articles. The dataset now consisted of 146 relevant articles.

Given the strong and longstanding tradition of phenomenology in German-language discourse, we extended our scoping review to include relevant literature published in German. A similar search using 122 keyword combinations across the Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar databases was conducted by a native German speaker, a coauthor of this article. 17 additional relevant articles were thus added to the final database, bringing the total to 163 for analysis (see Figure 1).

The past holds a special significance in the temporality of depression. In a way, it displaces or fills the present and future. As Gallagher (2012) notes, depressed patients tend to be preoccupied with past events and less focused on present and future ones. However, the outlook on the future is also affected. De Haan (2020) argues that depression affects not only how patients feel (hopeless, worthless, heavy), but also what they perceive. For example, the world appears uninviting and unattractive, even oppressive, as one sees "rejecting, disapproving faces, the phoniness of people's smiles, the litter that has been carelessly thrown onto the streets, the uselessness of activities" (p. 234). This, in turn, influences how depressed people act and interact in the future (such as by avoiding contact with others, or staying indoors).

According to Sofocleous (2023), the sense of anticipation and the belief in the potential for positive change are diminished, leading to a perception of the future as unchangeable, hence resembling the past. That creates a closed temporal loop, in which a person with depression is trapped. The dominance of the past means that life in the present appears to be completely determined by events that can no longer be corrected or dealt with (Küchenhoff, 2023). The relation between these two alterations is not inferential: the closure of the future does not follow from the dominance of the past. Both are expressions of a single structural change. Where the future ceases to solicit, the past is no longer relativized by what might still be done, and comes to occupy the whole of experienced time. Past-fixation and future-closure are thus two aspects of one alteration rather than cause and effect.

In depression the future offers no prospects, value, or interest. It appears closed, static and deterministic (Stanghellini et al., 2017). There is no space for projecting oneself, and the future no longer unfolds as a realm of possibilities. As Ratcliffe (2015) writes, "the world as a whole no longer accommodates the possibility of anything offering support" (p. 60). In ordinary experience, a person is connected to the future. However, in depression, this intentional relation becomes deformed. As Maiese (2018) emphasizes, depression involves a distortion in future-directed intentionality, so that a subject becomes temporally de-situated and cut off from the future. That is, one is cut off from one's potential becoming. The future no longer holds any call or motivation. There is no horizon of change or sense of recovery, no belief in the possibility that "things get better" (Wyllie, 2005).

A clarification about the status of these descriptions is in order. We do not claim that each feature listed above is present in every depressive episode, nor that together they form a checklist. What we take to be invariant is a change in the structure of lived possibility: what is lost is not this or that anticipated event, but the sense that the future holds practical possibilities bearing on oneself. A terminological clarification follows from this. Where we describe the future as "empty," "closed" or "inaccessible," these terms are used structurally rather than evaluatively: they characterize the extent to which the future is experienced as soliciting practical possibilities bearing on the subject, not the subject's appraisal of what it contains. A future experienced as menacing is inaccessible in precisely this sense: what is anticipated bears down on the subject while offering nothing that could be done about it. This structural change may vary between subjects and, within a subject, over the course of an episode: (i) the future as empty, with nothing anticipated at all; (ii) the future as foreclosed, where outcomes are anticipated but fixed in advance, so that anticipation carries no sense of openness; and (iii) the future as menacing, where what is anticipated is further and greater suffering. The third presentation is reported by patients who do not seem to have completely lost touch with the future, but assume that it will only bring greater disaster (Slaby & Stephan, 2012).

The present is not experienced as a living, vibrant moment of being; it does not emerge as something meaningful, but as something slowed down, even stopped (Minkowski, 1970, p. 279). Everything slows down: speech, movements, thoughts, reactions. What once seemed important now feels meaningless. The present is experienced as something empty. If time no longer moves forward, the present is experienced as complete stagnation, as being stuck. It turns into a closed space. A person feels trapped in this "now," devoid of the ability to act, want or feel anything. Some patients experience this as disappearance, some as the unreality of time (Florig, 2016).

The depressed person falls out of sync with the time that everyone else shares. They live in their own (a)temporal dimension. Nevertheless, external, intersubjective time continues to pass them by. This disconnection makes time merely pass for them; they can no longer fulfill and shape it themselves (Fuchs, 2009). The altered experience often also manifests itself as a desynchronization with the environment (Slaby & Stephan, 2012) and other people (Ratcliffe, 2015). Depression hinders one's relation to the world, as the sense-making of the situation gets "stuck"; the depressed person is no longer attuned to the situation, and the suffering is no longer a form of meaningful engagement with the world (De Haan, 2020, p. 246).

Depressive temporality forms a closed temporal loop, from which the only way out might be entering into another temporal loop. The lived experience in bipolar depression can radically shift its vector into manic temporality.

As with depression, we describe a structural pole rather than a uniform clinical picture, and the variation here is considerable. In hypomanic presentations the acceleration is typically experienced as fluent and largely one's own, whereas in full mania it becomes fragmenting and selfpropelling. In dysphoric and mixed presentations, the same acceleration is colored by irritability and dread: the opening of the future is undergone as pressure rather than as possibility, and the past may retain its accusatory weight instead of losing relevance.

The past in mania is experienced as irrelevant, unimportant, and with no sense of continuity or connection to personal present and future (Khadem et al., 2022). It holds no value to be considered in the present or regarding the future; it ceases to be a reference point. What once had a personal significance now appears distant and trivial no longer shapes the direction in which life is unfolding. This lack of anchoring brings a feeling that the world is open and full of potential (Owen et al., 2018). This liberation from the past gives a feeling of physical lightness (Fuchs, 2014). The body is no longer a container for painful memory, guilt or shame; it is full of energy to do whatever will be called, regardless of the limits of the body, relevance, or any other restrictions (Bowden, 2013).

The sense of continuity, that every moment follows another in a meaningful sequence, is disrupted. The person experiences the present as a succession of very small "nows" (Figueira & Madeira, 2011). Everything has relevance and immediately needs to be done. But because this importance applies to everything at once, there is no time for reflection or pause. Thus arises a fragmented temporality, where the flow of time is no longer experienced as continuous but as a series of moments (Bowden, 2013). In this way, mania generates a paradoxical experience: the flood of significance undermines significance itself. As Minkowski (1970, p. 295) put it: "the particular shrinking which the manic person's vital contact with reality undergoes, which, without completely annihilating it, renders this contact particularly superficial, makes it the plaything of the 'now,' always variable, changing from one instant to the next."

A person lives in complete focus on the here and now (Moreira & Bloc, 2012) and the present moment is experienced as accelerated (Fuchs, 2014; Moskalewicz & Schwartz, 2020). Time seems to speed up significantly (Ghaemi, 2007). This manifests itself, among other things, in thoughts and actions that follow one another with little pause. Attention becomes fragmented, jumping between ideas, sensations, and impressions. This acceleration of inner time becomes so intense that it begins to outpace external time. The world appears to be too slow.

According to Moskalewicz and Schwartz (2018), people with mania suffer from an existential leap toward the future, in which the self-abandons normal temporal boundaries. The future is experienced as something real, as if it has already happened. The person seems to live in a mode of preliminary realization: one does not wait for the future, but already acts within it. However, it is accompanied by a loss of volition and agency, along with a feeling of the practical insignificance of action (Bowden, 2013; Farr et al., 2024). There is no conscious planning or consistent movement toward a goal. Instead, it is a continuous reaction to any ideas or impulses. The fear of losing control of oneself in the future also appears (Borda, 2016). Küchenhoff (2023), argues that, contrary to popular belief, mania is not an antidepressant mechanism but an alternative response to existential challenges, which, however, is equally dangerous. The manically minded person also changes the context and interplay of past, present, and future. The past is by no means eliminated in mania, but it loses meaning. The experience of time is completely absorbed by the search for a future that is detached from the past.

3.3. Dialectics of depressive and manic states

Depressive episodes in major depressive disorder and in bipolar disorder satisfy identical diagnostic criteria, and the distinction is made by the presence of (hypo)manic episodes. This is why the two are conflated in clinical practice and, correspondingly, in the phenomenological literature. Comparative symptom research nonetheless reports systematic differences of emphasis: bipolar depression shows higher rates of psychomotor retardation, difficulty thinking, hypersomnia, early morning awakening, diurnal variation of mood, and psychotic features (Cuellar et al., 2005; Forty et al., 2008; Mitchell & Malhi, 2004; Mitchell et al., 2008). One phenomenologically oriented comparison points out that in bipolar depression the dominant affective tone is described as emotional dampening rather than sadness, the lived body appears as a heavy resistance interposed between the intention to act and the act itself, and the stream of thought is felt as advancing against obstruction (Fierro et al., 2015). In unipolar depression, the foreclosure of the future is presented as absolute. In bipolarity, there is embodied knowledge that the state is reversible: the patient may carry a bodily memory of the opposite pole, so that the depressive present is lived as a phase which may be changed.

We shall now explore the relation between depressive and manic states by drawing on the phenomenological concepts of implicit and explicit time. Implicit temporality is pre-reflective and involves a synthesis of a sequence of experiences, integrating them into a unified process (Fuchs, 2010). Implicit temporality rests on two intervening conditions. The first is the continuity of consciousness, which is expressed by Edmund Husserl's concept of "inner time consciousness" (Husserl, 1964), wherein retention (the just-past), presentation (the momentary impression), and protention (the aboutto-happen) are passively synthesized into the flow of lived experience.

The second condition is affective-conative momentum or conation. It builds upon this temporal structure as a fundamental motivational force that integrates emotion and volition, thereby grounding intentional and goal-directed action.

Implicit temporality in depression is characterized by a slowing down of the temporal flow. Without losing temporal continuity, the experiential weight shifts toward retention, while protention becomes diminished. This can be called hyper-retention and hypo-protention. It becomes difficult to maintain a healthy balance between temporal dimensions and envision the future. At the same time, basic motivation and impulse to act are lost. This state can be described as hypo-conation.

In mania, by contrast, the felt flow of time accelerates. The experiential emphasis shifts toward protention, while retention diminishes. The potential future, which is about to come, arises so rapidly that it overwhelms the sense of sequence and control. This can be called hypo-retention and hyperprotention. The timeline may become non-linear and fragmented. Conation in mania, in turn, is characterized by a sense of excessive energy and drive. The individual feels capable of doing everything, perceives all possibilities as attractive and inviting, and is driven to act impulsively. This state can be described as hyper-conation. Sieroka (2016) refers to the work of Ludwig Binswanger, who located the two pathologies of depression and mania against the background of a disturbed perception of time. Binswanger traced the pathology back to disturbances that affect the "a priori structure in the constitutive development of temporal objectivity" (Binswanger, 1960), that is, to disturbances of the internal dynamics of protentions and retentions, as a result of which time cannot be constituted in the usual sense and, consequently, reality cannot be constituted in its intersubjectively usual sense.

Explicit or experienced time, in turn, is a reflective process that requires an active effort of a subject. Usually, for something to become visible and conscious, something unexpected must happen. A person in the state of flow and being "within time" finds themselves conscious of a specific point. In a broader sense, explicit time also refers to the present, past, and future and the ability of a person to create and maintain a narrative about oneself in the continuum of time. This involves active synthesis, which prompts the personal-historical or biographical time (Fuchs, 2010). Fuchs draws on the ideas of Husserl, where implicit time is constituted through passive synthesis (the automatic, pre-reflective flow of retention and protention that requires no effort or attention from the subject), and active synthesis denotes those ego-driven acts through which the subject deliberately recollects, anticipates, and connects otherwise dispersed moments into an ordered sequence. It is precisely this deliberate work of linking that transforms discrete episodes into a coherent narrative.

Explicit time in BD is also transformed. Our claim is not that people with BD lose the capacity to locate events in objective time: chronological ordering, dating, and calendar reasoning are typically preserved, and in euthymia the difficulty we describe need not appear at all. What we argue is that, at the level of reflective self-understanding, the three temporal dimensions lose their mutual coherence: past, present, and future each remain available, but each is articulated in relative independence from the others, so that the biographical continuity that ordinarily binds them is weakened (Bortolan, 2017; Fuchs, 2010; Gallagher, 2007). In depressive episodes, first-person and qualitative reports converge on a past that is over-weighted rather than lost: it retains, and often gains, evaluative authority over the present (Fusar-Poli et al., 2023; Habermas et al., 2008; Stanghellini et al., 2017; Vogel et al., 2018). The future, correspondingly, is articulated not as unknown but as foreclosed, as offering nothing that could count as a project (Maiese, 2018; Owen et al., 2018; Ratcliffe, 2012 Ratcliffe, , 2015)), a shift also registered in the reduced specificity of episodic future thinking (King et al., 2011; Moustafa et al., 2018). In mania, the relation is reversed: the future is articulated as saturated with possibility and as demanding immediate action, while the past figures only thinly, so that earlier commitments, relationships and projects lose their claim on what the person now takes to be worth doing (Binswanger, 1964; Englebert & Stanghellini, 2015; Martin et al., 2018).

We claim that depressive and manic states are not merely opposites in mood or energy, but rather represent two poles in the fundamental structure of temporal experience. Depression is marked by hyperretention and hypo-conation, and an overwhelming sense of the past, while mania is characterized by hyper-protention and hyper-conation, and an overflooded sense of the future. Two qualifications are in order. First, what we describe are ideal-typical structures rather than generalizations over symptom profiles: given the heterogeneity of depressive or manic presentations, no single description holds across all cases, and our claim concerns a structural tendency that may be more or less pronounced. Second, and more importantly, the two dimensions must be kept apart. The claim that depression is dominated by the past concerns implicit temporality (the pre-reflective rhythm of lived time) and not the thematic content of what the subject anticipates. At the explicit level, depressive experience is frequently saturated with worry, catastrophising, or pessimism about what is to come, and these anticipations are indeed future-directed. At the implicit level, however, the future they disclose is not an open field of practical possibility but something already decided, foreclosed, given in advance as a repetition of what has been. Hopelessness, on this reading, is not an absence of the sense of future but the collapse of the future into the already-constituted. What unites both implicit and explicit temporality in depression and mania is their polarity and their tendency to meet limits along different dimensions. It involves a certain asymmetry or disproportion in implicit and explicit dimensions.

By disproportion, we mean that in each pole one temporal ecstasis is hypertrophied at the expense of the others: retention overwhelms protention in depression, protention overwhelms retention in mania, and in both cases the present loses its function as the point at which past and future are integrated. By asymmetry, we mean that the implicit and explicit dimensions are not affected in parallel or to the same degree. The two levels can move in opposite directions: implicit lived time may decelerate while the subject remains explicitly aware of a world-time that races ahead, producing the experience of desynchronization (Fuchs, 2013); conversely, an accelerated implicit conation in mania is not matched by any corresponding capacity for explicit temporal structuring, so that the overflooded future cannot be organized into a plan or a narrative.

What connects these polar states is their cyclicality, as if each state serves as a possible way out of the other, and vice versa. Mania may emerge as an attempt to escape the affective temporal trap of depression. Depression can follow mania as a collapse under the weight of potential opportunities or exhaustion from what has already been done. Rather than isolated disorders, they reflect a dialectical relation: a disturbance of pace, rhythm and synchrony that switches between polarities or limits across multiple levels -time, body, relationships with the world and others.

The enactivist perspective further emphasizes that the disorders of time are not just distortions in subjective experience (consciousness), but breakdowns in embodied sense-making dynamicsdistortions in action. Time is not experienced just in the head, but it shows up in the structure of skills, habits, rhythms, perception of affordances, or interpersonal timing, time enacted in bodily, sensorimotor, affective and social interactions. This has implications for diagnosis and treatment, as the enactivist perspective adds focus on the relation between phenomenology and how disrupted rhythms, pacing, and interactional timing affect and reflect the known psychiatric conditions. The felt temporality is reflected in narrative structures, which in the context of therapy can direct us to the way therapy can be enhanced, by modifying environmental and social affordances and narratives connected to them (Röhricht et al., 2014).

3.4. Vertical temporal structure of bipolar disorder

Using the metaphor of an "internal" clock, manic-depressive temporality involves a shift from one clock to another. It lacks a single-clock perspective. Patients rush from one activity to the next and are thus unable to develop an "inner-historical continuum" (Binswanger, 1960, p. 81). Everything they do remains insufficiently motivated in terms of their life history and also without consequences relevant to their life history. According to Binswanger, manic-depressive patients fall out of the shared world because they live from moment to moment in ever-changing fragments of worlds. They stumble, so to speak, from one present into the next (Sieroka, 2016).

Despite the antagonism between the experiences of Mania and depression, specific temporal characteristics remain consistent. These core characteristics consists of living in the eternal now (Binswanger, 1964, pp. 131-132; Gruber et al., 2012; Wyllie, 2005), also termed atemporal life, between slowing down and speeding up, between retardation (Fuchs, 2010) and acceleration (Moskalewicz & Schwartz, 2018), without a sense of time continuum (Bowden, 2013; Khadem et al., 2022; Ratcliffe, 2012); living in desynchronization with others (Fuchs, 2014; Northoff et al., 2018) and uncoupled from the world (Fuchs, 2014), and with the feeling of being trapped in own body (Aho, 2013; Frenga et al., 2020; Slaby et al., 2013).

One of the core dimensions of bipolar temporal experience is living in what is metaphorically rendered as the eternal now. Depression pulls the individual into the past (Habermas et al., 2008; Maiese, 2018; Minkowski, 1970, p. 335), mania turns them toward the future (Binswanger, 1964; Farr et al., 2024), but both conditions share a common feature: being trapped in the present moment. This opposition, however, concerns the thematic content of experience -what the subject is preoccupied with rather than the temporal form in which experience is constituted. At the formal level, both poles exhibit the same alteration: the present loses its transitional character. Metaphorically speaking, this experience does not unfold within a horizontal temporal progression, but along a vertical one. The ups in manic states and downs in depressive states are both stacked at one temporal point, which evokes the concepts of eternity and atemporality. The present moment stops functioning as a moment in which what has been is retained and what is coming is anticipated, and becomes a standing point that absorbs both. This can be specified in each direction.

In depression, the past is not thematized as a past that could be revisited, reinterpreted or narratively reworked; it is given as unalterable, and it is precisely as unalterable that it saturates the present. What is lived is not "then" but "now, and now cannot be otherwise": guilt, failure and loss are present-tense phenomena. Where the future no longer offers genuine possibility, retrospection is what remains. In mania, conversely, the future is thematically salient but is not lived as a horizon that requires time to mature. Possibility is not projected and then awaited; it is consumed immediately, and the subject moves from one possibility to the next rather than realizing any one of them through duration. In both cases, the mode of givenness of the non-present is present-tense: in depression as a weight that cannot be lifted, in mania as an abundance that need not be traversed. It is this shared structure that the metaphor of verticality is meant to capture. In both states, individuals do not experience time as a continuous process of becoming and unfolding (Minkowski, 1970, p. 294; von Gebsattel, 1954a; Khadem et al., 2022). What varies in bipolar experience is not primarily positioning along this axis but amplitude at a single temporal index; the ups of Mania and the downs of depression are stacked at one point rather than distributed across a sequence, and intensity substitutes for succession. This is also why there are appeals to eternity and atemporality in BD, although the poles realize them differently: depression is experienced as an immobile "now" in which becoming is inhibited, and mania as a saturated "now" in which everything is already available.

Speaking in terms of rhythms, this atemporality manifests through radically altered internal pacing: in depression, time slows down, and almost reaches a standstill (Cavaletti & Heimann, 2020; Weber-Guskar, 2023), while in mania, it accelerates radically (Fuchs, 2014; Moskalewicz & Schwartz, 2018). Rather than progressing through time, there is the experience of loop or trap. One lives in one's own atemporal inner world. This atemporality is not only an absence of time but a suspension of becoming, each moment standing on its own rather than opening onto the next. In depression, this takes the form of an immobile now in which the future is foreclosed in advance; in mania, a saturated now in which each moment is already complete and demands nothing beyond itself. What the two poles share is thus not a common pace but the loss of pace altogether, the collapse of the very possibility of unfolding that would let the present be lived as a passage rather than as a fixed point.

The experience of external rhythms is also altered. Retardation in depression and acceleration in mania both lead to intersubjective desynchronization. In depression, a person may experience a sense of being "too late," whereas in mania a sense of being "too early," both in relation to the social rhythms and the external pace of people and the world as a whole (Fuchs, 2010). The internal temporality becomes desynchronized from objective clocks, others, and social rhythms. One cannot meaningfully participate in shared temporal flow, find attunement and resonance, and becomes uncoupled from the world (Fernandez, 2014; Figueira & Madeira, 2011; Fuchs, 2014). All this leads to a sense of temporal isolation. From an enactivist perspective, mania also involves inflexible habits of behavior and attention, pointing to a mismatch between what agents care about and what they actually do (Maiese, 2022).

Speaking in terms of the relation between past, present, and future, there is no stable continuity or linear development in the experience of time. The explicit dimensions of time are poorly connected, and the boundaries between them are sometimes blurred. Even if a one temporal mode dominates, there is no integration into a coherent temporal narrative (Habermas et al., 2008; Khadem et al., 2022; Landazuri, 2024; Raucher-Chéné et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2021).

3.5. Bodily, affective and relational consequences

These core temporal characteristics correspond to central themes in phenomenology: the body and affectivity, the sense of self, and the relation to others and the world. We briefly outline these domains since they are deeply intertwined with the experience of temporality.

The experience of the body and affect in BD unfolds between poles characterized by shifts in the general sensation of the body and its mobility, movement, and emotions. Depressive states bring heaviness and stiffness, with slow, constrained movements and the feeling that the body is an object (Frenga et al., 2020) or corpus (Fuchs, 2014). Affect is marked by a strong feeling of guilt (Bortolan, 2017; Frenga et al., 2020) and emotional emptiness, while the emotional direction turns inward. In contrast, manic and hypomanic states bring about the feeling of the body characterized by lightness (Fuchs, 2014). Affect is dominated by excitement, euphoria, sometimes even anger and irritation, because people around appear slow (Bowden, 2013). The emotions and attention are directed outward, to bright opportunities to the world and to other people. As Fernandez (2014) argued regarding bipolar affectivity, depressive states should be characterized by a diminished capacity for finding ourselves situated in and attuned to the world, while manic states should instead be characterized by an enhanced or heightened capacity for finding ourselves situated in and attuned to the world. This formulation draws on the idea that attunement is a medium through which the world is disclosed as mattering, and possibilities appear as relevant and available. To say that manic states involve a heightened capacity for situatedness and attunement is therefore to say that the world is disclosed as saturated with practical possibilities and affective significance. The hyper-attunement of mania is itself pathological, since possibilities are disclosed without the selectivity and limitation that would make any of them actionable. This corresponds, in terms of temporality, to the hyper-protention described above.

The experience of self and action is characterized by polarity as well. During depressive episodes, the sense of agency is minimized or completely disappears, and actions feel impossible (Ratcliffe, 2015; Slaby et al., 2013). There is no energy for action and actions seem meaningless. Since the future holds no prospects for change, the present has no meaning. There is a sense of dissolving or losing oneselfa loss of "me" (Everett, 2013). In contrast, mania brings an overwhelming agency that is no more under control: the person may feel capable of anything, yet it is not grounded in personal meaning or previous plans and values. The sense of grandiosity may appear.

The experience of the world and others shifts as well. During depressive phases, the world feels pessimistic and constricted. Socially, the individual may avoid contact and feel unworthy of connection, along with feeling like a burden to others (Fusar-Poli et al., 2023). Their place in the world feels insignificant, out of place. In contrast, during manic states, the perception of the world becomes optimistic, even grandiose, as if the world is filled with endless possibilities (Bowden, 2013). Interaction with others is marked by a strong desire to connect: the individual becomes proactive in social contact.

4. Discussion

4.1. Therapeutic relevance

Knowledge of temporality in BD can be applied therapeutically. In both depressive and manic states, it is crucial to initiate resynchronization processes that impact both the physiological and social dimensions (Fuchs, 2002). Psychotherapeutic interventions (Asarnow & Manber, 2019; Frank et al., 2000), pharmacological treatments (Hutka et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2006), and adjunctive methods such as sleep deprivation (Giedke & Schwärzler, 2002) or light therapy (Tao et al., 2020) are employed. These strategies can be applied, for instance, at a physiological level to guarantee adequate sleep length. In manic states, a close correlation between changes in photoperiod and the onset of manic episodes has been established, as mania occurs more frequently in spring (Geoffroy et al., 2014). Similarly, depressive episodes are more common in winter. This correlation has been exploited by exposing manic patients to 14 hours of darkness at night, which led to an earlier improvement in symptom remission (Barbini et al., 2005).

In psychotherapy, manic patients need a temporal and spatial framework outside of their accelerated functioning (for instance, a framework such as the one offered by De Haan (2020) that integrates phenomenology, sense-making and stance-taking. Patients with depression should be afforded the option to detach from expectations, enabling them to gradually readjust to social timelines without the burden of temporal or social pressure. They require a structured daily routine in order to gradually experience that they are not stuck in the past, but may shape their own future. Although these activities may necessitate effort, they empower patients to establish goals once more. The rhythm in daily life offers patients stability in the face of the relentless passage of time. This simultaneously provides an exit from remanence, being left behind obligations and expectations, by permitting patients to progressively reintegrate into societal responsibilities throughout the therapy process. An illustration of this is interpersonal and social rhythm therapy, a distinct type of psychotherapy for individuals with BD, which aims to enable those affected to regulate their mood through their biological and social routines and reduce interpersonal difficulties (Frank, 2007).

4.2. Limitations and future work

Based on this review and our observation of the underexplored areas, we now outline several limitations and directions for future research. The studies on temporality in mania remain limited, and there is a lack of in-depth research nuancing the differences between manic and hypomanic temporal experience. Even fewer studies have focused on the temporality of mixed states in BD, where symptoms of both depression and mania are present simultaneously. It would also be valuable to investigate temporality in rapid cycling, manic and depressive psychosis, and other unspecified bipolar and related disorders, including cases with comorbid conditions. It is also worth exploring how a person experiences time during euthymia as a stable mood state and the state between depressive and manic phases of the disorder. Another potential area of research is how the experience of temporality changes during the course of therapy, for example, at its early and later stages, and how the experienced temporality affects the perception of action possibilities, habits, and routines.

The main methodological limitation of our approach, meanwhile, is that a heuristic construct can be mistaken for an essence, thereby ignoring variation at the very moment when the type needs revising. Relatedly, if the ideal type is built from paradigm cases and then applied to all cases, poorly fitting presentations may be assimilated into a generalized description, thereby risking the loss of nuances and the diversity of experiences. Grouping can also obscure clinically important differences, such as treatment response or the risk of switching in bipolar depression, so our therapeutic suggestions should be read as orientations rather than protocols.
