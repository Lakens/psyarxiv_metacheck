Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

research_questions As our study does not follow an experimental design approach, we have formulated criteria rather than hypotheses to define the conditions under which content validity is deemed achieved for each item.

Content validity is assumed if the following two criteria are met:

Criteria 1: Both stakeholder groups rate the conceptualization of each item as aligned with the respective target construct.

Criteria 2: Examples provided during the cognitive interviews align with the item definitions formulated by the research team and the items show no problems in being understood by at least 75% of the interviewed group of users.

If one of the criteria for content validity is not met, this is taken as an indicator that further adjustments to the respective item by the research team are necessary.

study_design_overview Our study follows a mixed-methods design by combining quantitative and qualitative data analysis.

In Part 1, quantitative data is collected through an online survey, gathering input from two stakeholder groups: researchers in the field of ER as well as users with experience responding to the SERiEL. Stakeholders are asked for their opinion on whether the conceptualization of each item is significantly aligned with the respective target construct. They may also offer comments and suggest improvements.

In Part 2, qualitative data is collected through cognitive interviews with the user group. In these semi-structured interviews users are asked to think-aloud (Anders Ericsson & Simon, 1993) while answering the SERiEL. In addition, interviewers utilize multiple probes (Prüfer & Rexroth, 2005) to obtain more detailed information regarding the interviewee’s understanding of the items: e.g. category selection probing (“Why did you select a xy scale value? What does a high/ low scale value represent for you?”); information retrieval probing (“Is there anything you didn’t understand about the question?”). These interviews will then be transcribed verbatim and rated by two independent, trained raters to assess whether the examples provided during the cognitive interviews align with the item definitions and if any problems in understanding and/ or answering the items occur. If misalignment or further problems in understanding are observed, the raters specify the exact nature of problem.

Applying a triangulation design of mixed-methods approaches (Greene, Caracelli & Graham, 1989) all results of Part 1 and 2 will equivalently be integrated to revise and optimize the items of the SERiEL-questionnaire. Results are discussed within our research team with the aim of making adjustments to items that exhibit potential content validity issues. These adjustments will be informed by the content of the cognitive interviews as well as the comments and ideas of improvement gathered in the online survey.

data_collection_procedures Researchers were recruited via e-mail. Invitations to the online surveys on SoSci-Survey (Part 1) were sent out to associates of a research initiative at the RPTU Kaiserslautern-Landau, which aimed to study ER through the lens of various psychological disciplines. At the time of the invitation, this initiative had already collaboratively conducted two large ESM studies. Additionally, other German-speaking researchers with at least one publication on emotion regulation were identified through university websites and invited.

Users were recruited through personal contact. To ensure the SERiEL could be used in both clinical and non-clinical settings, we recruited an equal number of users with and without prior psychotherapy experience. To enhance the representativeness of the user group, we ensured that users from each age decade were included, with a minimum age of 18 years and a maximum age of 75 years. The users first gained three days of experience with answering the SERiEL in their daily routines using an app-driven ambulatory assessment tool on their private smartphone named m-Path by KU Leuven (Mestdagh et al., 2023). Users were also provided a handout in which each SERiEL item was presented with additional information (label in m-Path, definition, response format, example from everyday life). Following this, they participated in an in-depth cognitive interview, where they completed the SERiEL using a specific example of their life (Part 2). The cognitive interviews were conducted in person or via (video) call and were recorded for later analysis. It was only after users gained substantial experience in responding to the SERiEL that they rated each of the SERiEL items in the online survey (Part 1).

Stakeholders did not receive any compensation.
sample_size Our target sample size are 15 participants for Part 1 of the study. For Part 2 we decided for a total sample size of 15-17 participants, with the aim to interview at least one person of each age decade in both subgroups (see Data collection procedures).
sample_size_rationale Due to the absence of standardized guidelines for determining sample sizes in item evaluation by judges our total sample size for Part 1 (including both subgroups of researchers and users) was informed by Johnston et al. (2014) who recommend a minimum sample size of N = 15 to detect a large effect size of .8 in the planned analysis (two-tailed Wilcoxon one-sample test, α = .05, power = .8). Preferably, each group should be represented by 15 stakeholders.

Due to differing recommendations regarding sample sizes in the implementation of cognitive interviews (Boateng et al., 2018; Hennink & Kaiser, 2022) we decided in favor of a stricter a priori sampling by following the recommendations of Hennink and Kaiser (2022) who propose a minimum sample size of 9-17 interviews to reach saturation.

measured_variables In Part 1, following the recommendations of Johnston et al. (2014), both researches and users were asked whether they agreed with the conceptualization of each item (dichotomous item: yes/ no), followed by a rating of their level of certainty about their answer (on a scale 0-10, 0 being not sure, 10 being very sure).

In Part 2, cognitive interviews were conducted, recorded, transcribed and analyzed by performing a qualitative content analysis by Mayring (2000, 2014). The interviews were carried out by using pre-defined probes (for further information see Study Design) in order to gain information about the interviewees understanding of the SERiEL items. The responses to the cognitive interviews were then rated by two trained, independent raters based on a codebook. Categories for the codebook were created deductively based on previous research (Mierwald & Junius, 2022) and represent the measured variables. First, the raters decide whether the example given and the thoughts expressed by the users match the respective item definition and if users have problems in understanding or answering the item (dichotomous item: problems / no problems). If there is a problem, the raters specify the nature of the problem in order to gain a deeper understanding: Expressions/ technical terms, differing understanding, incomprehension, response format, uncertainty of the answer and explanation. In line with the qualitative content analysis, three categories were inductively adapted in order to be more specific after both raters had gone through 30% of the interviews: Incomprehension or uncertainty in regard to item formulation, incomprehension or uncertainty in responding to the item. statistical_tests In order to verify the fullfilment of criteria 1 all datasets of the online ratings (Part 1) will be analyzed per item by conducting multiple two-tailed one-sample Wilcoxon tests with adjusted p-values by applying Hochberg Procedure for multiple comparisons (Hochberg, 1988).

To verify the fullfilment of criteria 2 cognitive interviews in Part 2 will be analyzed with a qualitative content analysis by Mayring (2000, 2014) which consists of a multi-step approach to determine if each item reflects the domain of interest to a sufficient extent, if it is understood in accordance with the item definitions provided by the researcher-team and if it shows problems in being understood. The coding process consists of the following steps after the final transcript is available (for transcription process see section Transformation):

(1) Develop deductively a codebook based on previous research (Mierwald & Junius, 2022) and tailored to our research question with distinct categories.
(2) Rate about 30% of all interviews by two independent, trained raters using the in (1) developed codebook.
(3) Extract inductively examples for each category out of the interviews rated in (2).
(4) Readjust and refine the codebook according to findings in (2) and (3) through an exchange between the two raters (e.g. adapt definition of category, add or delete categories).
(5) Rate remaining interviews by two independent, trained raters using the in (4) finalized codebook.
(6) Interpret results and apply quantitative analysis (frequency of each category for every item).
(7) Identify items which show difficulties in understanding by more than 75% of the interviewed group of users.

For Part 2 we plan to analyze the inter-rater reliability between the two independent raters of the cognitive interviews (Cohen´s Kappa).
transformations Part 1: Following the approach of Johnston et al. (2014), we will combine dichotomous and continuous ratings into a single scale. This scale integrates the certainty rating with the valence of the dichotomous rating, indicating whether each item adequately represents the intended construct. The resulting scale ranges from -10 to +10. A value of -10 represents a high confidence in the judgment that the item does not adequately represent the construct, whereas a value of +10 represents a high confidence in the judgment that the item adequately represents the construct.

In Part 2: Cognitive Interviews the audio-recordings of semi-structured interviews will be transcribed verbatim by using the software tool MAXQDA for qualitative data analysis. The resulting text files are then reviewed and revised by a research assistant. The final transcripts are then interpreted and rated by conducting a qualitative content analysis by Mayring (2000, 2014). Thereby we transform qualitative into categorial quantitative data by applying a coding scheme (codebook) (see Statistical Models).
inference_criteria The One-Sample Wilcoxon tests in Part 1 will use adjusted p-values by applying Hochberg Procedure for multiple comparisons (Hochberg, 1988) to determine if the tests suggest that the results are significantly different from those expected if the null hypothesis were correct.

Although there are no clear cutoff recommendations for analyzing cognitive interviews, we followed Boeije and Willis (2013) in setting a predefined criterion: At least 75% of the interviewed user group must demonstrate no difficulties in understanding an item for it to meet criterion 2 of content validity. This 75% threshold was chosen because it represents a substantial deviation from pure chance.

We will use p<.05 as a criterion of significance for the interrater-reliability coefficient.
data_exclusion_criteria In order to reflect differing opinions, we have not excluded any data points. We did not apply any awareness check.
missing_data_handling The online survey was set up in such a way that stakeholders could only get to the next page once they had answered all the questions. This was to prevent stakeholders from accidentally missing an answer to a question. If an interviewee in Part 2 did not provide any data on an item of the SERiEL (e.g. by skipping the item due to given skip logic), it will be marked as missed data and not included in further analysis of the respective item.
exploratory_analyses As exploratory analysis we plan to analyze the comments and suggested improvements given by researches and users in the online survey (Part 1). This information will be taken into account in further adjustments of items which missed one or both of given criteria for content validity (see Hypotheses).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Full text

Abstract

Experience Sampling Methods (ESM) offer a promising approach to capturing emotion regulation (ER), but item validity is often unclear due to ad hoc development and insufficient evaluation. Qualitative approaches can uncover validity issues, yet systematic guidelines remain limited. We examined content validity of the Scale for Emotion Regulation in Everyday Life (SERiEL), a new ESM questionnaire, via mixed-methods triangulation of qualitative (cognitive interviews) and quantitative (online surveys) data from users (n = 16) and researchers (n = 12). Analyses revealed content validity concerns in 15 item wordings, 5 flagged quantitatively and 10 more qualitatively, with qualitative analyses yielding richer, more nuanced insights. Most prominent was construct conflation, including confusion between ER strategies and between strategy employment and perceived success. Our systematic cognitive interview analysis approach and lessons learned may inform standardized best practices across studies; for ER research, clearly distinguishing distinct regulation processes seems important to avoid construct conflation.

Introduction Theoretical Background

Emotion regulation (ER) is defined as the process through which individuals shape their emotional experiences, determining which emotions arise, when they occur, how they experience and express these (Gross, 1998). These processes can operate either automatically or deliberately, and may unfold with or without conscious awareness (Gross, 1998). ER is central to daily life, supporting adaptive functioning (Gross, 2002), and it has also been recognized as a key transdiagnostic factor in mental health problems (Aldao & Dixon-Gordon, 2014; Berking & Wupperman, 2012; Sloan et al., 2017).

On the Complexity of Emotion Regulation

Multiple frameworks have been proposed to capture the complexity of ER. One of the most prominent models, the process model of emotion regulation, describes how strategies unfold across multiple stages, influencing emotions at different points in the emotion-generative process (Gross, 1998; Gross et al., 2019). Empirical evidence indicates that the choice of ER strategies is shaped by a range of factors, among which the valence and intensity of the emotions experienced play a significant role (Hay et al., 2015; Sheppes et al., 2011). Relatedly, from a resilience perspective, effective ER requires context sensitivity, a coping repertoire, and feedback monitoring to flexibly align strategies with situational demands (Bonanno et al., 2024). Complementing these perspectives, goal-based approaches emphasize that ER should be understood in light of its underlying goals and motives (Tamir, 2016). While prohedonic goals, such as increasing positive affect and reducing negative affect, are common, ER can also be driven by broader instrumental and social motives, including enhancing task performance or facilitating interpersonal interactions (Springstein & English, 2024; Tamir, 2016). Together, these theories offer a comprehensive understanding of how ER unfolds across contexts and goals, allowing clinical deficits to be identified more precisely (Sheppes et al., 2015), while also underscoring the challenge of measuring its complexity meaningfully. Despite these theoretical advances, the empirical literature has predominantly focused on the characteristics and regulation of unpleasant emotions such as fear or anger, whereas the regulation of pleasant emotions including happiness and joy has received some (e.g. Heiy & Cheavens, 2014), but comparatively less empirical attention (Boemo et al., 2022; Heininga & Kuppens, 2021). This imbalance toward negative emotions prevents a more comprehensive understanding of ER, as it remains unclear whether existing models generalize equally well to positive emotional experiences or whether distinct mechanisms and functions are involved.

Tracking ER in Everyday Life: Promise and Pitfalls

Over the last years, experience sampling methods (ESM) have gained popularity for capturing ER, as they yield more fine-grained data than retrospective questionnaires by assessing ER in close temporal proximity to its occurrence, thereby reducing recall bias while capturing contextual influences, dynamic fluctuations, and their effects on subsequent emotional experience (Mestdagh & Dejonckheere, 2021; Stone et al., 2023). While ESM may maximize ecological validity (Shiffman et al., 2008), challenges include ad hoc survey development and lack of systematic validation. This is documented empirically by a recent meta-analysis, which showed substantial heterogeneity in item wording (ranging from single words to complex sentences) and in the number of daily assessments across studies (Boemo et al., 2022).

Response formats similarly vary, ranging from dichotomous items to Likert-type scales, as noted in methodological guidance on ESM design (Eisele et al., 2025; Myin-Germeys & Kuppens, 2026).

Existing ESM approaches to assessing ER (e.g., Blanke et al., 2022; Brockman et al., 2017; Koval et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Medland et al., 2020; Nezlek & Kuppens, 2008; Weiss et al., ESTABLISHING CONTENT VALIDITY FOR THE SERiEL 2025) likewise vary considerably in instrument development and assessment focus. Some studies have adapted established global self-report questionnaires for ESM designs (Medland et al., 2020; Weiss et al., 2025), whereas others have developed measures specifically for ESM contexts (Blanke et al., 2022; Koval et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). Similarly, most available ESM instruments assess the use of predefined ER strategies, while some additionally capture contextual information, such as characteristics of the eliciting emotional event (Li et al., 2025) or the intensity of experienced stressors (Blanke et al., 2022). Given this conceptual heterogeneity, it is particularly notable that most studies fail to report psychometric data, limiting the ability to build on previous findings (Eisele et al., 2025; Hall et al., 2021; Trull, 2019; Vogelsmeier et al., 2024). Together, these issues hinder comparability across studies and the accumulation of robust evidence.

Addressing Content Validity in ESM Studies: The Role of Mixed-Method Approaches

Content validity, defined as the degree to which an instrument adequately represents the target construct, underpins subsequent psychometric evaluation (Hinkin, 1995). Although concerns regarding the validity of ESM measures continue to grow within the scientific community, methodological approaches still predominantly rely on quantitative ratings, mostly by experts.

Involving the target population as end users however is considered equally important (Boateng et al., 2018; Brod et al., 2009; Eisele et al., 2025; Schorrlepp et al., 2025). Established quantitative approaches include the discriminant content validity method (Crombez et al., 2020; Johnston et al., 2014), as well as indices such as Cohen´s kappa (Cohen, 1960) and the Content Validity Index (CVI) (Wynd et al., 2003). The CVI quantifies the extent to which multiple raters agree on the relevance of an item to its intended content domain (Wynd et al., 2003). These methods are typically based on expert or respondent ratings obtained through structured surveys (Boateng et al., 2018).

Qualitative methods may substantiate or complement results of quantitative approaches, ranging from focus groups to semi-structured or cognitive interviews and the use of open-ended items in validity research (Schorrlepp et al., 2025). Cognitive interviews are particularly well suited to this purpose, as they provide direct insight into participants' interpretation and response processes. However, their use in content validity research and, in particular, the availability of systematic guidelines for analyzing the resulting qualitative data remain limited (Boeije & Willis, 2013; Eisele et al., 2025; Schorrlepp et al., 2025).

When combined in a mixed-methods design, the triangulation of qualitative and quantitative data can strengthen support for validity (Boateng et al., 2018; Eisele et al., 2025; Truijens et al., 2023), as qualitative data not only enable the systematic identification of validity concerns but also yield fine-grained insights into their specific content (Eisele et al., 2025; Schorrlepp et al., 2025). This combination is particularly critical in ESM research, where longitudinal, and context-sensitive, predominantly single-item measures require careful evaluation to ensure accurate representation of the intended construct (Boemo et al., 2022; Dejonckheere et al., 2022; Eisele et al., 2025; Truijens et al., 2022). While previous studies already combined online surveys with cognitive interviews (Hofmann et al., 2023) or semi-structured interviews (Heron et al., 2023) to support the development and evaluation of the content validity in ESM measures, the literature still lacks standardization and established guidelines for the implementation and analysis of qualitative data, specifically cognitive interviews, in the validation of ESM measures.

The Present Research

The present study aims to investigate and optimize the content validity of the Scale for Emotion Regulation in Everyday Life (SERiEL), a scale designed for ESM use. Drawing on multiple theoretical perspectives on ER (Bonanno & Burton, 2013; Doré et al., 2016; Gross, 1998; Gross et al., 2019; Tamir, 2016), the SERiEL is intended to capture the complexity of ER by assessing regulatory strategies, situational contexts, underlying motives and the perceived success of strategies used in the context of daily ER in relation to both negative and positive affect. To ensure comprehensive coverage, the study employs a mixed-methods approach that triangulates quantitative expert and user ratings with qualitative insights from cognitive interviews with users to examine whether each SERiEL item meets two predefined evaluation criteria, one based on quantitative data and one based on qualitative data. Items meeting both criteria were considered to provide sufficient content-related validity evidence, while others were refined based on the collected information. By integrating these methods, the questionnaire is intended to more accurately identify and assess ER deficits across both clinical and non-clinical populations.

Goals and Overview. Psychological constructs assessed by questionnaires are typically defined based on the scientific literature. Analysis of cognitive interviews.

As the framework of data analysis, we employed structural content analysis (Mayring, 2014 (Mayring, , 2022)), a widely applied method in qualitative research (Sigl et al., 2025). Given the aim of detecting potential validity issues, structural content analysis was considered suitable, as it is underpinned by an interpretivist-constructivist epistemology, while simultaneously accommodating a post-positivist orientation (Cuthbertson et al., 2020). Guided by this epistemological stance, two independent coders evaluated whether users' understanding of the item wordings and the everyday examples they provided, as captured by the interview data, aligned with the research definition in the reference sheet (for reference, see Supplemental Material S1). The response format was also evaluated, including how users interpreted the extreme ratings. Potential validity issues were then systematically coded based on a deductive categorization adapted from previous research (Mierwald & Junius, 2022), which was subsequently expanded and refined inductively throughout the coding process (see Table 2).

By doing so, we aimed to examine for each item wording whether the examples provided by interviewees during the cognitive interviews aligned with the definitions established by the research team, operationalized in Criterion 1 as being rated in category 0 (no problems in understanding and/or responding) by at least 75% of the participants. Both coders had a psychology background (first coder, author, PhD trainee and CBT therapist in training; second coder, Master student in clinical psychology and research assistant), were between 25 and 35 years old, and of European ethnicity. Neither coder was involved in the original development of the scale; however, both were members of the research team responsible for its development and were supervised by senior members of the team. The use of two trained, independent coders ensured coding reliability, resulting in substantial inter-rater agreement (κ = 0.69, 95% CI [0.64, 0.73]).

Part 2: Quantitative Data -Online Survey

Goals and Overview.

An online survey was completed by users who had participated in the cognitive interviews and by researchers specializing in ER. Collecting data from these complementary perspectives allows us to assess additional aspects of items' content validity.

While users provide the most accurate insights into the comprehensibility and relevance of the items, researchers are better suited to rate their theoretical alignment with the target constructs.

Group of Researchers.

Twelve German-speaking researchers with experience in ER were recruited via e-mail. Invitations were sent out to associates of an established research initiative ESTABLISHING CONTENT VALIDITY FOR THE SERiEL with extensive experience conducting collaborative ESM studies (Lischetzke et al., 2022; Mema et al., 2026). Additional German-speaking researchers with at least one publication in the field were identified via university websites and invited to increase the sample's diversity.

Mean age of researchers was 39.08 years (SD = 5.9, range = 31 -49). Further details are provided in Table 4.

Procedure of the online surveys.

Researchers were invited to the online survey via email, while users completed it following their cognitive interviews. The survey was administered using SoSci Survey (Leiner, 2024). Adapting Johnston et al.'s (2014) approach of discriminant content validity, users and researchers rated whether each item wording aligned with its intended construct (dichotomous response scale), followed by indicating their certainty (rated on a 11point slider scale ranging from not sure (0) to very sure (10)). These responses were combined into a single rating ranging from -10 to +10, where -10 represented strong confidence that the item wording does not adequately represent the construct and +10 represented strong confidence that the item wording adequately represents the construct. For the analysis, we initially intended to apply the full discriminant content validity approach proposed by Johnston et al. (2014). However, because of the length of the questionnaire and the need to minimize participant burden, participants were not asked to evaluate the correspondence between each item and every target construct, as specified in the original procedure. In addition to the quantitative ratings, the survey included open-ended questions that allowed both participants and researchers to provide anonymous feedback and suggest revisions to improve the item wording.

Analysis of online surveys. To assess whether users and researchers rated the conceptualization of each item wording as aligned with the respective target construct (Criterion 2), the ratings of researchers and users were analyzed using multiple two-tailed one-sample Wilcoxon t-tests with adjusted p-values, applying the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control for multiple comparisons (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Given that researchers and users provide distinct types of input, their ratings were analyzed separately. Significant positive Z-values indicated that the respective group agreed with sufficient certainty that the item measured the intended construct, while significant negative Z-values indicated disagreement.

Triangulation of data sources

In triangulating multiple data sources, these sources can substantiate, contradict, or complement each other (Greene et al., 1989). Because the present data included different perspectives (users versus researchers) and different assessment methods, we weighed all data available from cognitive interviews (qualitative data) and from online survey (quantitative data) equally. In this process, content validity was operationalized at the item level, such that an item wording was considered valid only if it met both predefined criteria (Criterion 1: see cognitive interviews; Criterion 2: see online surveys).

Revision of item wordings

To facilitate optimization of item wording in a subsequent step, an additional type-building content analysis (Mayring, 2022) was conducted to generate insights beyond the mere identification of content-related issues (e.g., differences in interpretation vs. uncertainty in responding to an item). Type-building content analysis reduces heterogeneous material to a limited set of salient aspects, facilitating the identification of recurring issues in item wording.

To revise item wordings based on user feedback, the type-building content analysis (Mayring, 2022) was conducted on all item wordings identified as potentially invalid by any source. For this purpose, we first defined the theory-based typology dimension of issues per item wording as well as the typology criterion of frequent occurrences to be leading in the further analysis.

The same two independent raters (first coder, author, PhD trainee and CBT therapist in training; second coder, Master student in clinical psychology and research assistant), then inductively developed a typology of frequent content-related issues, with representative quotations, followed by reconciliation in a consensus discussion between the two coders and a senior member of the research lab (see Table 5).

Revisions were conducted iteratively through team discussions, integrating insights from the content analysis as well as feedback from users and researchers responding to open-ended survey questions.

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the content validity of the Scale for Emotion Regulation in Everyday Life (SERIEL), a German-language questionnaire for assessing ER using ESM.

Items were initially developed based on multiple theoretical frameworks of ER (Boemo et al., 2022; Doré et al., 2016; Gruber et al., 2012; Moskowitz et al., 2017; Tamir, 2016) and designed to meet recent ESM quality guidelines (Eisele et al., 2025). Through a mixed-methods approach that triangulated expert and user feedback from both qualitative and quantitative data, 15 items were further optimized. The SERIEL is now available as a comprehensive tool for clinical and non-clinical contexts, measuring ER in both emotionally pleasant and unpleasant situations, while individual items can also be used in future research. The full scale in German (Supplemental Material S3) and an unvalidated version in English (Supplemental Material S4) will be made available upon publication. We next explore how our analytic approach may guide content validation of other ESM instruments and interpret our findings in terms of measuring ER in daily life.

Towards Systematic Analysis of Cognitive Interviews in Validity Research

While quantitative approaches continue to dominate validity research, the present results support previous findings that cognitive interviews extend beyond the identification of general validity issues by providing more fine-grained insights into their underlying nature (Eisele et al., 2025; Schorrlepp et al., 2025; Truijens et al., 2023). In our study, qualitative triangulation revealed content validity problems in ten additional item wordings beyond the five identified quantitatively, highlighting the value of integrating both approaches. However, qualitative interview data require a rigorously developed analytic and reporting framework to fully realize their potential (Schorrlepp et al., 2025; Truijens et al., 2023). Yet standardized guidelines to evaluate and optimize content validity based on cognitive interviews are currently lacking. To address this gap, we developed and transparently reported a multi-step procedure for identifying validity issues in the SERiEL and identified key lessons to inform future validity research, particularly in the integration and analysis of qualitative data.

Within the range of available analytic approaches for qualitative research, we decided for Philipp Mayring's structured and type-building qualitative content analyses (Mayring, 2014 (Mayring, , 2022)), which represent well-established methodological frameworks for the analysis of qualitative data and have been used in validity research before (Sigl et al., 2025). Compared to other purely constructivist approaches, the qualitative content analysis was chosen because it aligns with a post-positivist perspective, assuming that a "correct" construct definition exists as intended by the researcher (Cuthbertson et al., 2020). This allowed for a structured evaluation of whether participants' interpretations corresponded to the intended meaning. We found that establishing clear, conceptually meaningful definitions of the individual constructs a priori was particularly valuable in this process. In addition, qualitative content analysis facilitated frequency-based analyses, enabling the application of cut-off criteria to identify problematic items. However, it should be noted that the chosen threshold of 75% of participants' answers being rated as category 0 (no problems in understanding and/ or responding) was, to some extent, arbitrary and requires further empirical validation.

We initially applied a deductive approach using a predefined coding scheme to analyze the transcripts (Mierwald & Junius, 2022), which provided a useful framework for structuring the large interview dataset. However, as the scheme was originally developed for educational contexts, it emphasized comprehensibility rather than conceptual distinctions. Consistent with previous findings (Panayiotou et al., 2026), comprehensibility was not the primary challenge.

Instead, participants frequently interpreted items differently from their intended meaning, which underscores the importance of empirically assessing participants' comprehension of items rather than relying on self-reported understanding.

Although we iteratively refined the coding scheme inductively, it remained limited to distinguishing broad validity issues (e.g., differing item interpretations vs. response uncertainty) and lacked the granularity needed to guide item refinement.

This is consistent with previous research showing that cognitive interviews can identify problematic items but may provide limited guidance for improving item quality (Lenzner et al., 2023), underscoring the need for rigorous analytic approach. We therefore complemented the structural content analysis with an inductive type-building analysis (Mayring, 2022), systematically comparing recurring patterns across items and participants to derive more specific conceptual problem types. This two-step approach yielded a more nuanced understanding of participants' responses, revealing recurring issues in our emotion-regulation measure, such as conflating strategy employment with regulation success. We therefore recommend extending deductive coding schemes with categories capturing conceptual validity issues. Construct conflation, in particular, may represent a recurring challenge in ESM questionnaire development and beyond. Future research should also examine the generalizability of additional validity categories, thereby supporting the development of standardized procedures for cognitive interview analyses.

While the benefits of incorporating qualitative data into validity research are evident, these approaches remain resource-intensive, requiring substantial time and personnel for data collection, transcription, and analysis. Technological tools, including AI-assisted software such as MAXQDA (Rädiker & Kuckartz, 2020) may help reduce this burden. However, in our experience, automatically generated transcripts still required manual review and correction due to transcription errors. Moreover, identifying subtle, interpretive issues require careful researcher interpretation beyond the capabilities of current AI-assisted tools (Morgan, 2023).

Although these technologies are rapidly evolving, they currently complement rather than replace direct researcher involvement in qualitative data processing and analysis.

In employing cognitive interviews, we deliberately sought to capture an idiographic perspective on respondents' item interpretations and underlying response processes. At the same time, decisions regarding items with potential validity issues were based on aggregated patterns across participants which may be understood as a rather nomothetic approach (see e.g., Scholten et al., 2024). Cognitive interviewing, as a qualitative method, foregrounds the premise that item validity cannot be adequately established through quantitative indicators alone, but instead requires close attention to individuals' subjective meaning-making processes (Eisele et al., 2025; Schorrlepp et al., 2025; Truijens et al., 2022). However, because item comprehension and response processes are likely to vary both between individuals and within individuals across time and contexts (Schorrlepp et al., 2025), evaluating item validity requires identifying patterns that generalize beyond individual cases.

Capturing ER in Everyday Life: Understanding What We Measure

The present findings highlight the challenges of capturing ER in its full complexity. First, our qualitative data indicated that participants appeared to interpret continuous scales assessing the intensity of ER strategy use not only as capturing the extent to which a strategy was implemented but also as reflecting its perceived success, thereby conflating constructs that were intended to be assessed separately. This finding underscores the importance of carefully operationalizing the intensity of ER strategies. Given that previous studies assessed the use of ER strategies either on dichotomous items (e.g. was a strategy employed -yes/no) or continuous scales (e.g., Likert-type scales ranging from not at all to very much) to capture the extent to which individuals report using specific strategies in a given situation (e.g. Grommisch et al., 2020 , Wenzel & Rowland, 2025), this aspect should be investigated further. While dichotomous formats may be more economical, they often lack fine-grained detail. This concern is empirically supported by Wenzel and Rowland (2025), who found that continuous ER items explained additional variance in affective well-being beyond binary frequency types alone, and speculated that this reflects a conflation of strategy occurrence and implementation success, consistent with our own qualitative finding. Therefore, when choosing the wording of items, care should be taken to disentangle these processes. To meet this challenge, we adopted a twostep procedure: participants first indicated whether they had used a given ER strategy (dichotomous item, yes/no), followed by a continuous rating of the extent to which the chosen strategy was employed (slider scale ranging from barely to very much). The qualitative findings further informed refinement of this item by clarifying that it assessed the intensity of strategy implementation independently of its perceived success.

The final wording was: Regardless of whether it actually affected your emotions: To what extent did you [worry]? Second, although the literature acknowledges varying degrees of (un)consciousness in ER (e.g. Gross, 1998; Yuan et al., 2015), our interview data revealed a tendency among participants to assume that ER strategies must be applied consciously and with deliberate intention (P5: "[...] for me it's simply that influencing feelings requires you to actively do so").

Importantly, however, consciousness in this sense should not be equated with interviewees' assumption that ER necessarily requires a deliberate intention. ER strategies may be enacted without an active decision to regulate while nevertheless becoming accessible to awareness either during their implementation or retrospectively. One possible explanation for this tendency to equate ER with deliberate intention may lie in individuals' emotion beliefs, which differ with respect to the perceived controllability of emotions or their perceived value or harmfulness (Peter et al., 2025). As proposed by Ford and Gross (2019), beliefs about the controllability of emotions may shape regulatory behavior. Viewed in light of our findings, it is also conceivable that beliefs about the controllability of emotions influence not only how individuals conceptualize ER but also the extent to which they engage in regulatory efforts. These considerations directly informed our revision of the item wording to encompass both active and passive forms of ER (final wording: "To what extent did your behavior influence your feelings?"). For future research, it may be valuable to assess individuals' emotion beliefs alongside their perception of SERiEL items. Doing so could help determine whether assumptions regarding the controllability of emotions systematically influence how respondents interpret self-report measures of ER and, consequently, which aspects of ER these instruments capture.

Yet, we acknowledge that self-report measures on ER remain limited to consciously accessible aspects, potentially privileging controlled over automatic regulatory processes (Braunstein et al., 2017). Future research may therefore benefit from complementing self-report with additional data sources capable of capturing implicit processes related to ER, for instance through passive sensing via wearables or smartphones (for an overview, see Bettis et al., 2022).

Several physiological indices have already been discussed as potential markers of implicit ER processes, including heart rate variability (Guendelman et al., 2024; Williams et al., 2015) and blood pressure (Dan-Glauser & Gross, 2011). However, evidence regarding physiological markers of implicit ER remains heterogeneous and lacks standardization, highlighting the need for further research (Zaehringer et al., 2020).

Limitations and Constraints on Generality

Our study has several limitations. First, because the study involved in-depth interviews with a relatively small number of participants, the generalizability of our findings is limited. Although we sought to include equal numbers of men and women, with and without prior psychotherapeutic experience, across each age decade, our user sample was comparatively highly educated. The researcher sample, in turn, was necessarily highly educated and embedded in academic setting due to the study design. Furthermore, our user sample was relatively heterogeneous with regard to ethnic and cultural background, as it included only individuals of European decent whose first language was German. Therefore, the generalizability of our results to individuals from other ethnic or cultural backgrounds, individuals whose first language is not German, and individuals with a broader range of educational backgrounds may be limited. Second, among researchers, we did not reach the preregistered target of 15 experts.

Third, we did not follow the full procedure of the discriminant content validity approach (Johnston et al., 2014), as we assessed each item only against its intended construct rather than all constructs represented in the scale. Fourth, the use and analysis of qualitative data are inherently susceptible to subjective interpretation, as ratings cannot be entirely free from rater bias. Although the two raters were not involved in the original development of the scale, both were members of the same research team. Consequently, the possibility of a favorable evaluation bias cannot be completely excluded, despite efforts to ensure objective and independent coding. Fifth, we did not account for potential variability in how users understood and conceptualized items over time due to increased familiarity with the items or contextual factors affecting responses (Schorrlepp et al., 2025), a characteristic that may be inherent to ESM data given its repeated-assessment format. Our current study did not address this potential for intraindividual changes in item interpretation. Future research could examine this issue by conducting follow-up cognitive interviews with the same participants after they have engaged with the SERiEL questionnaire over a defined period. Finally, we did not subject the refined items to the same validation procedures applied to the initial version of the SERiEL. A systematic re-evaluation of these revised items may therefore represent a valuable avenue for future research.

Conclusion

The final version of the SERiEL can be regarded as a content-valid instrument for measuring ER in everyday life. Our results indicate that qualitative data collected through cognitive interviews can effectively complement quantitative methods in validity research. Nonetheless, a standardized best-practice analytic framework for analysing cognitive interview data is lacking. To develop a shared deductive coding scheme for detecting item-validity problems, we propose extending other existing coding schemes focusing on comprehension issues (Mierwald & Junius, 2022) to additionally include conceptual validity issues (e.g., construct conflation).

For ER research, our findings highlight the importance of ensuring that participants distinguish between individual process components, such as strategy employment and its perceived regulation success, when answering ESM items.

Additionally, given that response patterns might be influenced by emotion beliefs, future research might benefit from assessing individual differences in emotion beliefs at study onset, prior to the assessment period. Doing so could help render visible whether and how such beliefs systematically shape the way respondents interpret and answer ESM items. Furthermore, our results underscore the potential of complementing everyday self-report measures with additional psychophysiological assessments to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of ER processes.
