Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Autism spectrum disorder involves differences in social communication, interaction and behavioral flexibility, yet conventional single-response Theory-of-Mind tasks rarely capture the trial-by-trial dynamics of social adaptation. We examined how autistic and non-autistic young people organized recent choice history, how they adjusted it across opponents, and how these patterns related to task performance. Twenty-seven autistic children and adolescents and 24 healthy controls completed the same two-choice hideand-seek task under social and non-social framing against artificial opponents of graded sophistication. We compared aggregate performance, Bayesian model-based estimates of latent strategy use, and model-free history kernels measuring how current choices depended on recent self-actions and opponent actions. History kernels showed that autistic participants switched away from their previous choice more strongly against every opponent, whereas controls added lag-2 and opponent-coupled structure when the opponent adapted to them and reconfigured their kernels across opponents more. Within the same dataset, replaying participants' kernels against empirically fitted opponents reconstructed accuracy (r = .86) and the direction of the group difference against the first-order opponent, against which switching provided less benefit than against the zero-order opponent. Against the first-order opponent, controls were more often described by first-order opponent modelling (influence learning) and autistic participants by heuristic policies. These findings suggest that autism-related differences in repeated interaction are expressed in how choice history is organized and reconfigured across opponents, beyond categorical differences in inferred strategies.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a lifelong neurodevelopmental condition defined by persistent differences in social communication and reciprocal interaction, together with restricted, repetitive, or inflexible patterns of behavior [1, 2, 3]. Understanding the cognitive processes underlying these differences requires examining how autistic people respond, learn, and adapt during interaction [4]. Repeated interaction poses a particular challenge: participants must use another agent's recent behavior while adjusting their own choices as that agent responds to them. Behavioral tasks can provide repeated, time-resolved measurements of the cognitive processes involved [5, 6]. A central question is how autistic young people organize recent experience to guide their choices, and how this organization changes across interaction demands. Theory of Mind (ToM), including the representation of others' beliefs, intentions, and strategies, has been central to cognitive accounts of autism [7, 8, 9, 10]. Cognitive tasks such as Happé's Strange Stories probe the interpretation of others' mental states [11], whereas repeated games allow these processes to be examined as choices unfold. Game-theoretic and Bayesian approaches have formalized how agents infer and respond to others' strategies [12, 13], and have been applied to interactive behavior in 2 15 20 25 30 35 40 45 autism [14, 15]. Heuristic, reinforcement-learning, influence-learning and recursive ToM policies can each be fitted to trial-by-trial behavior to estimate which candidate policy best explains an individual's choices [13, 14, 16]. Influence learning specifically models how a player's own actions affect an opponent's subsequent behavior [16].

These approaches have helped distinguish cognitive components of interaction. In a repeated trust game, Xiang and colleagues linked inferred depth-of-thought to distinct neural learning signals and found that its distribution in healthy players differed with the partner group they encountered [17]. In autism, computational games have likewise helped separate aspects of strategic cognition. In a cooperative Stag Hunt game, Yoshida and colleagues separated inference about another player's strategic sophistication from iterative planning: the former was associated with autism symptom severity, whereas the latter was associated with intellectual ability [15]. In competitive hide-and-seek, Forgeot d'Arc and colleagues characterized the recursive depth and flexibility of participants' inferred strategy repertoires. Autistic adults showed reduced adjustment to social framing, yet outperformed controls against the zero-order opponent [14]. In a non-clinical sample, Craig and colleagues further found that higher autistic traits were associated with less cooperation with an adaptive ToM agent but more cooperation with a fixed-strategy agent [18]. This combination makes the opponent's behavior central to interpreting group differences: a strategy can be effective in one interaction and less effective in another. These studies motivate asking how such context dependence is expressed in the temporal structure of autistic participants' choices.

Choice history offers another behavioral window on adaptation. In competitive tasks, using recent experience can help anticipate an opponent, while regularities in one's own choices can make behavior predictable [19]. Moreover, the influence of previous choices and outcomes can depend on opponent identity [20]. In perceptual decisionmaking, people adjust the strength and direction of choice-history biases to environmental statistics; in environments with repetition or alternation regularities, greater adjustment was associated with better performance [21]. A tendency to alternate or repeat choices describes one aspect of behavior; changing its strength, or incorporating the opponent's actions over additional lags, describes another. Linking these patterns to performance helps assess their functional significance. This distinction also connects to accounts of recursive ToM and supervisory control, which consider how predictive confidence and task demands regulate the selection and configuration of processes guiding behavior [22, 23].

History-kernel, or Volterra-style, analysis describes how current choices depend on previous self-actions and opponent actions across multiple lags [24, 25, 26]. The selfhistory kernel indexes repetition, switching, and persistence in one's own action sequence, whereas the opponent-history kernel indexes coupling to the opponent's 3 55 60 65 70 75 80 85 recent behavior. This representation does not assume that participants implement Bayesian ToM, reinforcement learning, or another specific cognitive algorithm. Alongside strategy estimates, it describes the direction, strength, and temporal extent of dependencies in observed choices. Earlier work in autistic adults also included modelfree choice-sequence analyses [14]. Here, the unresolved question concerns how these temporal dependencies are organized in autistic young people, how they differ across opponents, and whether they can account for opponent-dependent performance. Prior evidence of reduced strategic flexibility in autistic adults [14] raises the possibility that choice-history structure is less reconfigured across opponents in autistic young people.

The repeated hide-and-seek paradigm provides a compact setting for addressing this question. Participants repeatedly choose between two options while competing against artificial opponents of graded sophistication. A biased opponent follows a fixed preference; a zero-order opponent tracks the participant's action frequencies; and a first-order opponent models the participant as a learner attempting to anticipate it [13, 14]. Both learning opponents adapt to the participant, but differ in how they use the interaction history. Comparing them tests whether temporal choice structure changes with the form of adaptation encountered. The same formal decision problem can be framed socially, as interaction with a human-like strategic opponent, or non-socially, as interaction with a slot machine. This allows sensitivity to framing to be examined alongside adjustment to opponent behavior.

We therefore asked how autistic and non-autistic young people organize self-and opponent-generated information over time, how this organization changes across opponents, and how it relates to task performance. Twenty-seven autistic children and adolescents and 24 non-autistic controls completed the task under social and non-social framing. We analyzed the same choice sequences at three levels: aggregate performance, Bayesian model-based estimates of strategy use, and model-free history kernels. We compared kernel differences across opponents with differences between repeated blocks of the same condition to distinguish opponent-dependent reconfiguration from within-condition variability. We then replayed each participant's kernel as a simulated agent against models of the task opponents to test whether the estimated temporal dependencies reconstructed individual accuracy and opponentdependent group differences within the same dataset. A secondary cross-validated classification analysis assessed the group-related information carried by each representation. Together, these analyses connect strategy estimates, temporal choice organization, and performance to characterize adaptation in autism.

Participants

Participants were drawn from the cohort described in [27, 28], recruited through the outpatient clinic of Roozbeh Psychiatric Hospital (Tehran). Children and adolescents in the autism group (ASD; aged 8-18 years) had received a diagnosis of high-functioning autism spectrum disorder (mild to moderate; DSM-5) from an expert child and adolescent psychiatrist, and diagnosis and severity were confirmed with the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS ≥ 25.5). Healthy control (HC) participants were matched to the ASD group on age and sex, had no personal or family history of psychiatric conditions according to parental report, and, like the ASD participants, had an intelligence quotient in the normal range as assessed with Raven's Progressive Matrices. Following the criteria of the parent cohort, participants with comorbid neurological or psychiatric conditions (e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, epilepsy, bipolar disorder, depression) or with any severe immune-system disease were excluded.

From this cohort, valid behavioral task data were obtained for 27 ASD and 24 HC participants, who constitute the analyzed sample of the present study.

Experimental task

Each participant completed a repeated two-alternative hide-and-seek game, adapted for children from the paradigm previously used in autistic adults [14]. The task followed a factorial design crossing stimulus framing with opponent type. The framing factor distinguished social trials, in which the game was presented as an interaction with a human-like strategic opponent, from non-social trials, in which the identical formal decision problem was presented as a gambling device (a slot machine). Because the formal action space was the same across the two framings, any behavioral difference between them can be interpreted as sensitivity to the social meaning ascribed to the interaction rather than to a change in the low-level decision structure.

The opponent factor comprised three artificial opponents (described below): a biasedrandom opponent, a zero-order Theory-of-Mind (0-ToM) opponent, and a first-order Theory-of-Mind (1-ToM) opponent. Crossing the two framings with the three opponents yielded six task situations per participant. The present paediatric protocol was restricted to opponents up to first-order mentalizing. Second-order recursive opponents were omitted to limit the total task length and cognitive demand for the paediatric sample.

On each trial the participant selected one of two options within the available response window. The artificial opponent generated its action according to its predefined strategy, and the trial outcome depended on whether the participant's choice was successful against that opponent. Feedback showed the outcome and rewarded option. Trials were organized in repeated blocks within each framing-by-opponent situation.

Artificial opponents

The three opponents differed in how they exploited the history of the participant's past actions to anticipate the participant's next choice; the reward was then placed so as to defeat that anticipation. The biased-random opponent followed a predetermined pseudo-random sequence with a fixed bias towards one option and did not adapt to the participant, providing a non-mentalizing reference condition. The 0-ToM opponent did not attribute mental states to the participant but tracked the evolving frequency of the participant's actions and hid where it predicted the participant would not seek, an approach equivalent to a Bayesian form of fictitious-play learning. The 1-ToM opponent was endowed with limited artificial mentalizing: it treated the participant as a strategic (0-ToM) agent attempting to anticipate it, and recursively updated its beliefs about that agent's parameters, so that it could outwit opponents who adapt without mentalizing. The opponents were implemented as in [14]; their recorded behavior, which did not differ between the two groups, is summarized in Supplementary Note 13.

To characterize temporal dependence in the choice sequence, we fitted a first-order (linear) Volterra-style lag-kernel logistic regression in which the current choice was predicted from the participant's own previous actions and from the opponent's previous actions over lags 1-8: (2) where y {-1, +1} denotes the participant's current action and a {-1, +1} the ₜ ∈ ₜ ∈ opponent's action, with the two options coded symmetrically.

Under this coding, a positive self-history coefficient indicates repetition of the participant's own previous action at that lag and a negative coefficient indicates switching, whereas for opponenthistory terms a positive coefficient indicates attraction towards, and a negative coefficient avoidance of, the opponent's previous action. Because the previous outcome equals the product of the previous own and opponent actions under this coding, winstay/lose-shift corresponds to a positive lag-1 opponent-history coefficient and outcomeindependent switching to a negative lag-1 self-history coefficient, so the lag-1 terms represent previous action and its interaction with the previous outcome. Models were fitted separately for each participant, framing, opponent type, and block using ridge-regularized logistic regression. Trials without a valid response were treated as timeouts and excluded from kernel estimation, and a trial entered the kernel design only if it and the preceding eight trials had received a valid response; no block-level threshold was applied (Supplementary Note 9). From each fitted kernel we extracted the signed lag coefficients and cumulative absolute-magnitude summaries, the latter providing a direction-independent index of overall history dependence. Subject-level features were obtained by averaging block-level summaries across available blocks and situations, while recording the number of valid kernel blocks and the mean number of responded trials as quality-control variables. Because the number of retained blocks reflects timeout frequency, these variables were not used as classification features. Cumulative lag windows from 1-2 through 1-8, together with early windows (1-2 and 1-3), characterized the temporal localization of the signal; wider (1-16) and optimized lag windows were examined only as supplementary analyses, given the sample size.

The experimental opponent set was restricted to biased-random, 0-ToM, and 1-ToM opponents, but the participant-level fitted model space was deliberately broader. History kernels reveal differences in early temporal dependence For each participant and task situation, a ridge-regularized logistic model predicted the current choice from the participant's and the opponent's actions on the previous eight trials (Methods). This yielded two separable temporal kernels: a self-history kernel, indexing how strongly participants' own previous choices carried forward into the current choice, and an opponent-history kernel, indexing how strongly choices were coupled to the opponent's previous actions. The fixed lag 1-8 window captured short-range and moderately extended dependence while limiting the number of features relative to the sample size.

Across conditions the largest absolute coefficients occurred at lag 1, with weaker and less consistent effects at later lags, so current choices were shaped by recent trial history, not by a uniformly extended memory. But the ASD-HC contrast was not a simple global increase or decrease in history dependence. ASD participants showed stronger immediate self-history dependence (switching away from the previous choice) against every opponent, whereas HC participants showed stronger lag-2 self-history and opponent-history dependence in first-order opponent conditions. Full subject-level fixed lag 1-8 kernel features and group contrasts are provided in Supplementary Data 3 and 4.

To localize these differences, we examined the cumulative lag 1-2 and lag 1-3 models separately by framing and opponent type (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 2); the ASD-HC difference lay mainly in the first two lags. ASD participants showed stronger lag-1 selfhistory dependence in all six conditions (lag 1-2 window, d = 0.97-1.28, all FDR q < .05). In the first-order opponent condition, where the opponent's behavior depended on the participant's recent choices, HC participants additionally showed stronger lag-2 self-history dependence under social framing (d = -0.95, q = .006) and stronger lag-2 opponent-history dependence under non-social framing (d = -0.90, q = .012). Of these effects, only the lag-2 self-history difference depended on framing: HC participants increased lag-2 self-history dependence under social framing whereas ASD participants 14 405 410 415 420 425 did not (group × framing interaction in the first-order condition, d = -0.89, permutation p = .003). The lag-1 and lag-2 opponent-history differences did not differ between framings (Supplementary Note 13). The group difference was thus not a broad extension to lag 3 but a condition-specific difference in how the first two trials of history shaped the current choice. The early-window and lag-expansion analyses are shown in Supplementary Figures 8 9 10and Supplementary Note 6, with numerical outputs in Supplementary Data 11.

The full fixed lag 1-8 model (Fig. 3) confirmed that the pattern was not driven by a broad long-range ASD elevation across the whole history window. The largest splitcondition effect in the full lag 1-8 profile occurred in non-social self-history at lag 1, where ASD participants showed stronger absolute dependence than HC participants (ASD = .538, HC = .343, d = 0.94, permutation p = .005, FDR q = .040); the same lag-1 difference was present under social framing (ASD = .510, HC = .291, d = 0.95, q = .021). Later self-history effects were smaller and less consistent, and some later opponent-history effects instead favored HC participants, including non-social opponent-history lags 6 and 8 (Supplementary Table 3). The fixed lag 1-8 profile points to a local self-history effect, not a generalized long-range increase in history dependence in ASD. Signed coefficients showed that this reflected switching: the lag-1 self-history coefficient was negative in both groups and more negative in ASD participants in all six conditions (d = -0.57 to -1.06, all FDR q < .05). The difference exceeded a permutation noise floor that was equal in the two groups, was not confined to switching after failures (ASD participants showed higher switching probabilities after both successes and failures), and was unchanged after adjusting for the higher timeout rate of ASD participants or when only blocks without timeouts were analyzed (Supplementary Note 12). By contrast, the lag-1 opponent-history coefficient, which indexes win-stay/lose-shift, was larger in HC participants against the zero-order opponent (d = -0.88 and -0.78, q = .024), in line with the higher expected frequency of WSLS in HC participants in the non-social zero-order condition.

Taken together, the model-free analyses locate the strongest group differences in early self-history and opponent-history dependence. ASD participants were neither simply less responsive to the opponent's previous actions nor uniformly stronger in long-range dependence. Their choices were instead more dominated by switching away from their immediately preceding choice, regardless of opponent, while HC participants recruited stronger two-trial and opponent-coupled structure in first-order conditions where adaptive tracking of the opponent was required. This pattern is consistent with stronger context-dependent recruitment of opponent-sensitive processing when the interaction becomes adaptive.

History kernels account for performance against the opponents

Finally, we asked whether the temporal structure captured by the kernels accounted for task performance. Within each zero-and first-order condition, kernel summaries explained 40-46% of between-participant variance in accuracy, and the direction of the association depended on the opponent: stronger lag-1 self-history dependence went with higher accuracy against the zero-order opponent and lower accuracy against the first-order opponent (Supplementary Note 10). In the social first-order condition, where the only Holm-corrected accuracy difference occurred, the kernel summaries statistically accounted for most of the group difference (indirect effect -7.8 percentage points, 95% bootstrap interval -14.4 to -3.2; Supplementary Table 4). Because kernels and accuracy were estimated from the same games, this is a statistical decomposition rather than evidence of causal mediation.

Agent simulations were consistent with this structure producing the observed pattern (Supplementary Figure 14; Supplementary Note 10). Agents that differed only in their lag-1 self-history weight showed a crossover: switching away from the previous choice raised accuracy against the zero-order opponent but not against the first-order opponent, which learns and exploits such regularities, as agent-based models of recursive Theory of Mind predict [22]. Replayed against empirically fitted opponents, 21 565 570 575 580 participants' own kernels reconstructed accuracy within the same dataset (r = .86 across 204 participant-by-condition observations in the zero-and first-order conditions; r = .83 after removing condition means and .58-.92 within single conditions) and reproduced the ASD disadvantage against the first-order opponent (simulated -5.6 and -5.9 percentage points under non-social and social framing; observed -7.7 and -9.9); the k-ToM agents simulated with default toolbox parameters reproduced its direction but not its size. Because participant kernels and empirical opponent replicas were fitted to the same games, this is a reconstruction rather than an independent predictive validation; correlations with individual accuracy against the canonical first-order opponents were weak (r = .05 and .23; Supplementary Note 10). The ASD pattern is consistent with a lag-1 switching policy whose value depends on the opponent, whereas the additional lag-2 and opponent-coupled structure in HC kernels was associated with performance against the first-order opponent.

Two further analyses tested these interpretations directly (Supplementary Note 14). First, controls reconfigured their policy more across opponents: their kernels differed more between the first-order and the other opponents than those of autistic participants (d = -1.00, permutation p = .001), whereas variability between the two repeats of the same condition was equal in the two groups; the difference lay mainly in the increase of lag-2 self-history dependence against the first-order opponent (d = -1.07). We detected no group difference in kernel change across repeats of a condition; this does not rule out learning within conditions. Second, autistic participants' choices were more predictable from their own two previous choices (conditional entropy 0.77 versus 0.88 bits, d = -0.73, p = .008), but this general predictability was not related to accuracy against the first-order opponent (r = .04), so it was the specific lag-1 switching structure, rather than predictability as such, that tracked performance.

Discussion

Our findings link autism-related differences in repeated interaction to the temporal organization of choices and their reconfiguration across opponents. Autistic participants switched more strongly away from their previous choice across opponents, whereas controls reconfigured their kernels more and showed additional lag-2 and opponentcoupled structure against the first-order opponent. These patterns were related to task performance. In the observed data, stronger switching was associated with higher accuracy against the zero-order opponent and with lower accuracy against the firstorder opponent; in agent simulations, switching raised accuracy against the zero-order opponent but not against the first-order opponent. Replaying participants' kernels against empirically fitted opponents reconstructed accuracy and the direction of the group differences within the same dataset. Bayesian model selection provided a complementary description, with greater support for first-order influence learning in controls and heuristic policies in autistic participants against the first-order opponent.

These findings refine the interpretation of social-adaptation differences in ASD. The signal here was not simply a shallower inferred mentalizing level or a categorical shift from one strategy class to another; it was concentrated in the earliest lags of the choice sequence and depended on opponent structure. ASD participants showed a stronger tendency to switch away from their own previous choice against every opponent, whereas HC participants showed stronger lag-2 self-history and opponent-history dependence when the opponent required adaptive tracking. Such a signature is compatible with broader theories of atypical social prediction and precision-weighting in ASD [31] and with evidence that autistic adults overestimate environmental volatility, which places more weight on the most recent outcomes [32], but here it appears as a directly measurable property of the choice sequence, not as a latent model label.

The findings also clarify the methodological distinction between computational explanation and computational phenotyping. Generative models are the appropriate tools for asking what cognitive process may have produced behavior. However, the features that best explain behavior at the group level are not necessarily those that best describe how individuals differ. In short behavioral tasks, information about how behavior produces performance may reside in temporal structure that model comparison compresses away. History-kernel analysis preserves that structure and, as the agent simulations suggest, can link it to performance, providing an interpretable bridge between raw behavior and its consequences.

The model-based and history-kernel results describe adaptation to the opponent from two angles. Simple policies were common in both groups in the biased and zero-order conditions. In the same paradigm, autistic adults outperformed controls against the zero-order opponent [14]; our zero-order accuracy contrast under non-social framing was in the same direction (.709 versus .655) but did not survive correction (Table 2).

Against the first-order opponent, HC behavior was more frequently explained by firstorder influence learning, alongside stronger lag-2 self-history and opponent-history dependence, whereas ASD behavior was more frequently explained by heuristic policies. The model-based contrast centers on first-order influence learning, which tracks how one's own choices change the opponent's behavior, and fits the observed differences in temporal reconfiguration. Together, these findings suggest a group difference in how strongly behavior is reconfigured when effective prediction must accommodate an opponent that models the participant's own learning. The agent simulations are consistent with this account: lag-1 switching, which distinguished ASD choices, raised simulated accuracy against the zero-order opponent but not against the first-order opponent, and in the observed data stronger switching was associated with lower accuracy against the first-order opponent. In agent-based models, the advantage of higher-order Theory of Mind rests on lower-order players being unable to play unpredictably [22], and autistic individuals generate less random sequences [33]; a strong lag-1 regularity is the kind of structure an adaptive opponent can exploit. In our 23 630 635 640 645 650 655 660 data, autistic participants' choices were indeed more predictable from their own history, although only the specific lag-1 switching structure, not predictability as such, tracked accuracy against the first-order opponent. This parallels evidence that individuals with higher autistic traits cooperated more with a non-adaptive agent but less with an adaptive one [18], and that autistic players are more often described by fixed strategies that are updated less flexibly during interaction [15], and suggests that the difference lies in reconfiguration across opponents rather than in a uniformly less effective policy.

A possible cognitive explanation concerns how feedback regulates the influence of opponent models on choice. In reciprocal interaction, successful predictions can increase confidence in higher-order models when simpler policies provide less predictive value [22]. Monitoring whether an ongoing policy remains effective may help regulate when opponent-sensitive processes are recruited and sustained [23]. The ASD-HC pattern may reflect differences in feedback-guided reconfiguration of behavior as the demands on opponent modelling increase. In the terms of Shallice and Cooper [23], the autistic pattern resembles a routine policy maintained across situations, whereas controls' greater reconfiguration is compatible with task setting when routine schemas become less effective. Consistent with greater reconfiguration, controls' kernels differed more between opponents than those of autistic participants, beyond the variability between repeats of the same condition; the supervisory processes themselves were not measured. Because the lag-1 differences did not depend on framing, and the accuracy difference, although significant only under social framing, did not differ significantly between framings, the main effects were not demonstrably specific to social framing. This is compatible with a domain-general account but does not establish it. The one framing-dependent effect was that controls, but not autistic participants, increased lag-2 self-history dependence under social framing.

The same participants previously completed two established Theory-of-Mind tasks, Happé's Strange Stories and the moving-shapes animations, allowing direct comparison with conventional mentalizing measures in the same cohort [27]. All three tasks probe Theory of Mind, but they differ in format. The Strange Stories and animation tasks ask participants to interpret a vignette or attribute intentions to moving shapes and report that interpretation, yielding a considered judgement for each item. By contrast, the hideand-seek game requires repeated, outcome-driven choices against an opponent and so yields a trial-by-trial sequence of decisions instead of a single attribution.

Across the three tasks, group differences depended on task demands. In the earlier tasks, autistic and non-autistic participants were indistinguishable in the low-demand control conditions, the random animations and non-mentalizing story blocks, and differed specifically in the Theory-of-Mind-loaded conditions. The present task shows a related pattern: accuracy was broadly comparable across most conditions, and the only accuracy difference that survived correction occurred in the socially framed first-order 24 670 675 680 685 690 695 700 opponent condition (Table 2), the situation in which the opponent adapts to the participant and success requires representing its strategy. That difference did not, however, differ significantly from the corresponding non-social difference, so in this task what mattered most was whether the opponent adapted, not how the interaction was framed.

The pattern of adaptation also converged across tasks. In the animation task, autistic participants were unimpaired when no mind had to be inferred and diverged once goals and intentions had to be attributed. In the game, their choices depended more strongly on their own immediate action history, against every opponent, whereas non-autistic participants engaged in more opponent tracking once the opponent became adaptive. These patterns link the group difference to demands for representing and tracking another agent.

The two paradigms differ, however, in what each can resolve. The earlier tasks yield a single summary score per condition and so operate entirely at the level that the present study calls aggregate performance. The game reproduces that finding at the aggregate level but adds a layer that the story and animation tasks cannot provide: the trial-by-trial temporal structure of choices, which here accounted for performance against the adaptive opponent. The tasks also differ in their verbal demands. The Strange Stories task measures verbal, narrative Theory of Mind, whereas the game is almost entirely non-verbal and is therefore largely silent on that component. The overlap between the two approaches is principally the non-verbal intention-tracking axis, on which the earlier animation results and the present game results broadly agree.

The present findings also speak to earlier genetic work conducted in an overlapping subset of this cohort. We previously reported that OXTR rs2268498 and rs53576 dissociated along a verbal/non-verbal Theory-of-Mind axis [27]. The earlier work used narrative and animation-based tasks, whereas the present study used a repeated competitive game; the studies also differ in their level of comparison. The genetic associations were estimated principally within diagnostic groups, whereas the present analyses contrast between them. Nevertheless, both suggest that Theory of Mind in this population is better understood as a set of separable components than as a unitary faculty. Whereas the earlier work fractionated Theory of Mind into verbal and non-verbal components with partially distinct genetic correlates, the present analysis fractionates the behavioral signal into inferred-strategy and temporal-dynamic components and locates the variation that accounts for performance in the latter. Both findings weigh against a unitary Theory-of-Mind-deficit account of autism and favor a componential account, even though the specific components foregrounded by the two studies are not identical.

Clinical diagnosis remains essential and cannot be replaced by a short behavioral task. Scalable behavioral experiments may nonetheless complement existing diagnostic 25 710 715 720 725 730 735 740 pathways by supplying objective, low-cost, quantitative markers of social adaptation. In the present sample, task-derived representations separated the groups modestly (balanced accuracy .63-.66, against .98 for clinical variables), so such use would require validation in larger samples. A repeated hide-and-seek task can be administered quickly, requires minimal verbal explanation, and produces trial-by-trial data suitable for computational analysis. The kernel-based features used here are also computationally lightweight, requiring standard regression rather than iterative model inversion or full Bayesian model comparison.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample size was modest, and classification used a matched subset of 41 participants. Of the 202 refitted inversions, 66 remained unsuccessful; low recursive-ToM frequencies therefore depend partly on the treatment of numerical failures. The sensitivity analyses support the direction of the principal group contrast, but not the absence of recursive reasoning. Representation comparisons are descriptive and do not establish statistically reliable superiority; they were underpowered (Supplementary Note 11) and depended on the kernel inclusion rule and the sample analyzed, whereas the kernel group differences did not (Supplementary Note 9). The simulated opponents reproduced the recorded opponent behavior only approximately, and the mediation estimates are cross-sectional (Supplementary Note 10). The robustness of these results should be evaluated in an independent cohort. Although leave-one-subject-out cross-validation is appropriate for small samples, it can still produce optimistic estimates when the feature space is rich relative to the number of participants. Future work should test the kernel features in larger and more heterogeneous samples, examine test-retest reliability, and evaluate whether the same temporal signatures generalize across age, sex, cognitive ability, language level, and clinical comorbidity. It will also be important to compare taskderived features with standardized clinical measures and to test whether they predict functional outcomes beyond diagnostic status.

Our findings characterize ASD-HC differences through both inferred policy use and temporal choice dynamics. Against an opponent that adapted to them, controls more often tracked that adaptation, in both their inferred strategy and their choice history, whereas autistic participants maintained a lag-1 switching policy across opponents. These differences may partly reflect how feedback regulates the recruitment and continued use of opponent modelling, extending the interpretation from which strategies participants use to how their use is monitored and controlled.

History-kernel estimation

To characterize temporal dependence in the choice sequence, we fitted a Volterra-style lag-kernel logistic regression in which the current choice was predicted from the participant's own previous actions and from the opponent's previous actions over lags 1-8. The two options were coded symmetrically as -1 and +1. Under this coding, a positive self-history coefficient indicates repetition of the participant's own previous action at that lag and a negative coefficient indicates switching; for opponent-history terms, a positive coefficient indicates attraction toward, and a negative coefficient avoidance of, the opponent's previous action.

Models were fitted separately for each participant, framing, opponent type and block using ridge-regularized logistic regression. Trials without a valid response were treated as timeouts and excluded from kernel estimation, and every trial whose own response and preceding eight trials had all received a valid response (an uninterrupted eight-trial sequence) entered the kernel design, without any further block-level threshold; a block was fitted when it contained at least one such trial and both choice options. From each fitted kernel we extracted the signed lag coefficients and cumulative absolute-magnitude summaries, the latter providing a direction-independent index of overall history dependence. Subject-level features were obtained by averaging block-level summaries across available blocks and situations, while recording the number of valid kernel blocks and the mean number of responded trials as quality-control variables, which were not used as classification features. Cumulative lag windows from 1-2 through 1-8, together with early windows (1-2 and 1-3), characterized temporal localization of the signal; wider (1-16) and optimized lag windows were retained only as supplementary audit-trail analyses given the sample size.

Model-based analysis and Bayesian model selection

For each participant, framing, opponent type and block, we fitted a structured family of candidate computational models to the trial-by-trial choice sequence. The candidate set included biased-random (Nash-like) responding, Win-Stay-Lose-Switch, reinforcement learning, influence-learning models and recursive Theory-of-Mind models. Model evidence was summarized by variational free energy and stored in subject-level F-matrices. Before group-level selection, these tables were sanitized to ensure consistent participant, diagnostic-group, framing and opponent labels and finite model-evidence values, and evidence was summed across repeated blocks within each participant and situation. Inversions of the fitted ToM2 and ToM3 models flagged by the evidence threshold in an earlier version of the analysis were refitted, and inversions that still failed kept their low evidence (Supplementary Note 15).

Random-effects Bayesian model selection was applied separately by diagnostic group and task situation using the VBA toolbox. The primary group-level output was expected model frequency (Ef), the estimated proportion of participants in a group best described by each model while allowing for between-subject heterogeneity. Exceedance probability (XP) was retained as a supplementary diagnostic because of its sensitivity to small evidence differences, sample size and model-set structure. The main model-based representation comprised relative model-evidence summaries, model-probability summaries and winningmodel counts for the nine fitted models, together with mean entropy, mean maximum model probability and mean ToM-model probability, computed from the corrected evidence (Supplementary Note 15).

Supplementary Note 1. Normative simulations

To contextualize the Bayesian model-selection results, we simulated the candidate computational models in the same repeated hide-and-seek environment used in the empirical task. Each model was paired against the other candidate models over repeated runs, and average payoff was computed. Simulations were conducted under deterministic and noisy policies. The deterministic simulations estimate the payoff structure implied by each model pairing, whereas the noisy simulations test whether this structure is preserved under stochastic choice.

The simulations showed that no candidate strategy dominated across all opponents. Instead, performance depended on the match between the agent's strategy and the opponent's generative structure. The task therefore creates a context-dependent payoff landscape, rather than a single globally optimal policy.

Supplementary Note 11. Sensitivity power analysis

We estimated the smallest effects detectable with 80% power at two-sided α = .05 in the two analyzed samples. With all participants (27 ASD, 24 HC), group contrasts were sensitive to d ≥ 0.80 (d ≥ 1.28 at a Bonferroni-corrected α for the 90 lag 1-8 kernel contrasts, a conservative bound for the false-discovery-rate correction used) and correlations to |r| ≥ .38. In the matched classification sample (22 ASD, 19 HC), the corresponding values were d ≥ 0.90 (1.46) and |r| ≥ .43. The lag-1 self-history differences (d = 0.97-1.28 in the lag 1-2 window) and most kernel-accuracy correlations in the zero-and first-order conditions (|r| = .32-.66) lie above these bounds, whereas the weaker associations with accuracy against the biased opponent (r = -.24 and -.28) lie below them and should be read as not detected rather than absent.

With all participants, the required separations were 1.51, 1.61 and 2.05. Supplementary Note 12. Direction, outcome dependence and timeouts of the lag-1 effect

Direction and noise floor. Signed lag-1 self-history coefficients from the lag 1-8 kernels were negative in both groups, indicating switching away from the previous choice, and more negative in ASD participants in all six conditions (ASD -0.32 to -0.54, HC -0.10 to -0.29; d = -0.57 to -1.06; permutation p ≤ .044, FDR q ≤ .044). To test whether larger absolute coefficients reflected estimation noise, each block's responses were randomly permuted 20 times and the kernel refitted, preserving choice counts, timeouts and the design while removing temporal dependence. The resulting noise floor of |β| was similar in the two groups (0.25-0.28; d = -0.34 to 0.15, all p > .24), and the absolute lag-1 coefficient in excess of this floor remained larger in ASD participants in all six conditions (d = 0.59-0.94, FDR q ≤ .043).

Outcome dependence. With options coded ±1, the previous outcome is o(t-1) = y(t-1)•a(t-1), so win-stay/lose-shift, y(t) = o(t-1)•y(t-1), equals a(t-1). The lag-1 opponent-history coefficient therefore indexes outcome-dependent staying and the lag-1 self-history coefficient outcome-independent staying or switching, and these lag-1 terms represent previous action and its interaction with the previous outcome. Model-free switching probabilities agreed with the kernels: ASD participants switched more after failures in all six conditions (0.77-0.86 versus 0.63-0.78; d = 0.58-1.19, FDR q ≤ .044) and also after successes (0.38-0.57 versus 0.31-0.48; d = 0.29-0.72; uncorrected p < .05 in three conditions), supporting a switching tendency that was not confined to responses after failures. The lag-1 opponent-history coefficient did not differ between groups overall (group difference -0.05, 95% bootstrap interval -0.17 to 0.09) but was larger in HC participants against the zero-order opponent (non-social d = -0.88, social d = -0.78, FDR q = .024).

Timeouts. ASD participants timed out more often (4.7-10.1% versus 1.8-6.6% of trials; d = 0.58-0.86) and therefore contributed fewer usable lag-8 histories per block (24.9-31.4 versus 29.9-36.5). In regressions of participant-by-condition coefficients on group and condition (participant bootstrap, 2,000 resamples), the group difference in the signed lag-1 self-history coefficient was -0.28 (-0.71 SD; 95% interval -0.43 to -0.12) without covariates, -0.32 (-0.82 SD) after adjusting for timeout rate and -0.32 (-0.80 SD; -0.50 to -0.15) after adjusting for timeout rate and usable histories. Restricted to blocks without any timeout (50 ASD and 121 HC blocks), the difference was -0.35 (-0.72 SD; -0.61 to -0.10), and among participants whose timeout rate did not exceed the 75th percentile of controls (8 ASD, 18 HC) it was -0.37 (-0.96 SD; -0.59 to -0.13). Because ridge shrinkage is stronger when fewer trials are available, fewer usable histories would, if Supplementary information | 34 anything, reduce rather than inflate the coefficients of ASD participants. Supplementary Note 13. Opponent behavior and framing interactions

Opponent behavior. The opponents were those of Forgeot d'Arc et al. (main-text reference 14); their recorded behavior is summarized here. The zero-order opponent kept its hiding location after 99.5-100% of unsuccessful searches and after 38-47% of successful ones, and hid at the participant's last search location on 25-31% of trials. The first-order opponent kept its location on 53% of trials (48% after a successful and 58-60% after an unsuccessful search) and hid at the participant's last search location on 45-46% of trials. Supplementary Note 14. Reconfiguration across opponents and predictability of choices

Reconfiguration. Lag 1-2 kernels (self-and opponent-history coefficients at lags 1 and 2) were fitted separately to each block. For each participant and framing, we computed the Euclidean distance between the first-order kernel and the zero-order and biased kernels (between-opponent distance) and the distance between the two repeats of the same condition, scaled by 1/√2 (within-condition distance, a noise baseline). Between-opponent distances were larger in controls (1.02 versus 0.78; d = -1.00, permutation p = .001), whereas within-condition distances did not differ (0.70 versus 0.70; d = 0.03, p = .93), so the reconfiguration index (between minus within) was larger in controls (0.32 versus 0.09; d = -0.90, p = .002). The difference lay mainly in the absolute lag-2 self-history coefficient, which increased from the zero-order to the first-order opponent in controls but not in autistic participants (+0.30 versus +0.03; d = -1.07, p = .001); the shift in the signed lag-1 coefficient did not differ (p = .50). Changes from the first to the second repeat of each condition, in individual coefficients, in the overall kernel and in accuracy, did not differ between groups for any opponent (all p ≥ .10), so the detected group difference concerned reconfiguration between opponents; these null results do not exclude learning within a condition.

Predictability. The conditional entropy of each choice given the participant's two previous choices (Miller-Madow corrected) was lower in autistic participants (0.77 versus 0.88 bits; d = -0.73, p = .008), significantly so in four of six conditions (non-social zero-order, first-order and biased, and social biased; FDR q ≤ .049). Cross-validated self-prediction (an own-history lag 1-3 model fitted on one repeat and tested on the other) showed the same direction but was significant only in the non-social zero-order condition (gain 0.24 versus 0.02 bits per trial; d = 0.82, q = .022; overall p = .23). Across participants, lower conditional entropy was associated with higher accuracy against the non-social zero-order opponent (r = -.60, p < .001) but was unrelated to accuracy against the first-order opponent (r = .04 and .04) or the biased opponent. General predictability therefore did not account for the first-order deficit; the lag-1 switching structure, which is negatively related to first-order accuracy (Supplementary Note 10), did. Outputs are in the analysis repository (results/reported/robustness/).

Supplementary Note 15. Flagged k-ToM inversions and corrected model selection

Problem. Block-level model evidence was estimated by variational Bayesian inversion in the VBA toolbox. In an earlier version of this analysis, the two highest-order k-ToM models (fitted ToM2 and ToM3, displayed as 1-ToM and 2-ToM) were inverted with a reduced iteration budget (at most 8 iterations, free-energy tolerance 0.1) and in some blocks returned free energies of about -200 to -1,500, against about -35 for a model at chance on 50 trials. The aggregation step set evidence below -200 to missing for that model only and summed the two repeats of each situation with missing values omitted. A model that failed in both repeats therefore received a total of 0, the highest possible evidence, and a model that failed in one repeat was scored on one block while the other models were scored on two. Of 306 participant-by-situation cells, 8 contained a zero and 66 a single-block total, in 41 of 51 participants; these cells favored the failed model and inflated the expected frequencies of the recursive ToM models.

Refits. We refitted both repeats of every affected model in every affected cell (202 inversions in 102 cells) with the toolbox default convergence settings and, where these failed, with up to 64 iterations, a tolerance of 10 ³ and up to 8 Gauss-Newton iterations per step. A fit was classed as failed when its free energy was ⁻ non-finite or below T log 0.5 -max(50, T) for T responded trials (about -85 for 50 trials); fits above this threshold in this study lie between about -20 and -70 per block. 136 inversions returned evidence above the threshold (ToM2 48 of 61, ToM3 88 of 141); the optimizer's convergence state was not stored, so failure is defined by this threshold, and repeated attempts on a failed block returned identical evidence. Failed refits were more frequent in HC than in ASD participants (37 of 98 versus 29 of 104), mainly for fitted ToM3 (31 of 70 versus 22 of 71; ToM2 6 of 28 versus 7 of 33). Because a failed fit counts against its model under the primary rule, this asymmetry could lower recursive ToM frequencies more in HC participants; the sensitivity analysis that gives failed fits typical evidence removes this penalty. Both repeats passed the evidence threshold in 43 cells, one in 49 and neither in 8; 2 cells had a single block.

Rules and results. In the primary analysis a failed inversion kept its low evidence (non-finite values were set 1,000 below the threshold), so that the model lost that block, every model was summed over the same blocks, and all 51 participants were retained in every situation. Two sensitivity analyses excluded a participant from a situation whenever any model had failed (15-26 participants per group and situation) or gave failed fits the median per-block evidence of the other models. These failure-handling rules test sensitivity to numerical failures; they do not turn unsuccessful inversions into valid evidence against recursive reasoning. The dominant model was the same under the three rules in all 12 group-by-situation cells; the largest difference from the primary analysis in any model or family frequency was .037 when failed fits received typical evidence and .127 when they were excluded (Nash in HC participants, non-social firstorder condition: .265 versus .138), and every group difference larger than .05 in main-text Table 3 kept its sign. Compared with that earlier analysis, the expected frequency of the highest displayed ToM level fell from .09-.35 to at most .04 in every situation, with corresponding increases for WSLS against the zero-order and Nash against the biased opponent, whereas the group difference against the first-order opponent (firstorder influence learning in HC participants, Nash and WSLS in ASD participants) was preserved.
