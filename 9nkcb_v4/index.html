Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

Abstract

Social networks emerge from individual decisions to create and remove connections. How can these decentralized decisions produce efficient networks when individuals observe only part of the evolving structure and the first link costs its sponsor more than it returns? Here, we develop an agent-based model of strategic network formation in which agents combine local network information, optimistic expectations about unseen connections, and probabilistic choice. Across groups of 10, 50, and 100 agents, we identify conditions under which nearoptimal collective welfare emerges from isolation, despite individual incentives favoring an initially disconnected network. When agents could see only their immediate neighbors, collective welfare peaked at intermediate optimism. A wider view generally broadened the range of optimism compatible with high welfare. Comparisons of individual decisions suggest why: optimism can make the first link attractive, while greater visibility helps agents recognize when a link preserves access and when another route makes it redundant. The starting network also mattered. Some policies that yielded high average welfare from dense random networks yielded lower welfare from both isolation and connected trees. The same fixed decision rule can therefore support both initial linking and subsequent reorganization as the evolving network changes what agents see and which actions they judge worthwhile.

Introduction

Social networks evolve through individual decisions to create, maintain, or remove connections. Scientific collaborations, professional contacts, and communication networks influence how information spreads, innovations diffuse, and resources circulate [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] . Establishing these connections requires time, effort, or material resources, while their benefits depend partly on the relationships that others have already formed. A connection that is unattractive between isolated individuals may therefore become valuable once a wider network develops. Individuals rarely know the full structure of the networks they might join. Understanding how costly individual decisions made with incomplete information generate collectively valuable networks is a central problem linking economics, network science, and cognitive science.

Strategic network-formation models formalize this tension between individual incentives and collective outcomes [6] [7] [8] . Here, we consider a two-way-flow environment in which one individual pays to establish a connection, but both connected individuals gain access to one another and, through indirect paths, to others in the network. We define an efficient network as one that maximizes collective welfare: the total benefits of access to others minus the total costs of maintaining connections, summed across all individuals. Because access benefits decrease with network distance, efficiency requires balancing shorter paths against the costs of additional links.

Under the payoff conditions studied here, the empty network is a strict Nash equilibrium: that is, no isolated agent benefits from initiating a connection. Collective welfare is maximized by a star-shaped network in which one individual connects directly to all others and each link is paid for only once 7 . Existing networks can also require reorganization to improve access or reduce costs. Human experiments show that groups can construct high-welfare networks in this strategic environment 9 . These observations raise a central question: how do individual decisions to create, retain, or remove connections enable groups to build efficient networks, whether starting from isolation or from existing relationships? Evaluating a connection requires estimating how it changes access through a network that individuals may only partly observe. Research on bounded rationality suggests that people address such demands through restricted attention, simplified representations, and approximate decision rules [10] [11] [12] . Network experiments show selective use of structural information and increasing reliance on simpler strategies as informational demands grow [13] [14] [15] . Evidence of compressed cognitive maps likewise suggests that people can preserve useful relational structure without representing every connection explicitly 16 . Partial knowledge creates two distinct demands: evaluating connections through observed paths and assigning value to potential partners whose wider connections remain unknown.

Previous models establish that cognitive constraints and individual heterogeneity can alter emergent network structure. Restrictions on strategy complexity can support nonempty networks in settings where fully rational players remain disconnected 17 , while memory constraints and heterogeneous link utilities can generate differences in network density, topology, and individual position 18, 19 . Stochastic network-evolution models also show that departures from deterministic best response can change the structures reached through decentralized adjustment, although these structures need not maximize welfare 8, 20 . The challenge is to identify decision policies that support initial linking while preserving sensitivity to the changing benefits and costs of existing connections. Optimism about the benefits of unseen connections can encourage useful new links, but excessive optimism may sustain unnecessary linking costs. Limited visibility can also prevent agents from recognizing which existing links are valuable and which could be removed or replaced.

We develop an agent-based model in which agents revise their connections one at a time.

Their decisions depend on three parameters: visibility radius determines how far agents observe relationships through the network, outside-link optimism determines the value assigned to possible access through unseen connections, and choice temperature determines how strongly estimated action values guide choices. The policy also incorporates prospective rewiring, allowing agents to evaluate whether a new connection could replace an existing sponsorship. Agents retain fixed parameter values throughout each simulation, allowing us to examine whether changes in the network itself can support exploration and consolidation without an explicit switch between decision policies.

Across groups of 10, 50, and 100 agents, we find that local decisions can generate nearoptimal collective welfare from isolation. Analytical comparisons suggest how this becomes possible: optimism about unseen connections supports initial linking, while sufficient visibility and precise choices help agents retain useful links and remove those whose costs outweigh their benefits. Simulations starting from dense random networks and connected trees show that initial connectivity does not guarantee high welfare: the conditions supporting efficiency also depend on the network's starting structure. Under the experimentally matched payoff schedule, larger groups require broader visibility to sustain high welfare. The main welfare patterns persist when agents differ in their decision parameters. Initially identical agents develop different network positions, with some becoming highly connected hubs. When agents retain different decision parameters across repeated simulations, they tend to occupy similar relative payoff positions.

Together, our results show that efficient networks can emerge through optimistic search and informed link revision, as evolving connections reshape individuals' information and opportunities without requiring an explicit change in their decision rules.

Groups of n agents face a decentralized network-formation problem. Sponsors pay for directed links, whereas undirected access benefits extend along paths to other agents. Because these benefits decrease with graph distance, forming a valuable network requires enough links to provide access without retaining sponsorships that become redundant once indirect paths exist (see "Network formation game" in Methods). We study whether a boundedly informed revision process can solve this problem from an initially empty network. At each revision opportunity, one agent is selected uniformly at random and observes the sponsorship network induced by agents within graph distance ρ. Using this local view, the agent scores four types of action shown in Figure 1(a): making no change, deleting one sponsored link, adding a link to a visible agent, or choosing a generic outside-link action.

The three parameters govern what the agent sees, how optimistically it values the outside-link action, and how action values translate into choice probabilities (Figure 1(c)). The sampled addition, deletion, or non-change defines the next network state; repeating this cycle produces a sequence of network states. We measure time as t = s/n, where s is the number of revision opportunities; one unit therefore corresponds to one opportunity per agent in expectation. The Methods subsection "Local-view revision policy" gives the formal definition of the policy.

This revision process must escape an inefficient equilibrium without global information or coordinated action. For the game-payoff parameters adopted from Choi et al. 9 , the empty graph is a strict Nash equilibrium and a minimally sponsored star maximizes welfare, with the periphery-sponsored star (PSS) as the strict-Nash orientation among the welfare-equivalent stars (Appendix A). The eventual hub tends to sponsor or receive a link early

A PSS gives the hub a distinct position. It is directly connected to every other agent but sponsors no link, while each peripheral agent pays for one. This asymmetry raises a further question about which agent becomes the eventual hub.

At the parameter setting with the highest mean terminal welfare for each treatment, we ran 512 additional episodes. Every episode ended in a PSS, whose hub was the unique highest-degree agent. All agents followed the same policy with the same parameter values. We ordered agents by when they first sponsored a link and by when they first received one; passing did not count as acting.

For each treatment, the probability that the eventual hub appeared among the first k agents generally exceeded the random-ordering benchmark in both orderings, and the two cumulative curves closely tracked one another (Figure 4). Mean hub ranks were below the reference expectation (n + 1)/2 for both orderings in every treatment after Holm correction (all adjusted p < 10 -4 ). Within each treatment, the paired rank comparison found no statistically significant difference between the orderings (all Holm-adjusted p ≥ 0.1962). Early sponsorship or link receipt was therefore associated with eventual hub identity at these selected settings. This association among initially identical agents is consistent with endogenous symmetry breaking, but does not establish a causal effect of early involvement.

Agents tend to hold similar payoff ranks across episodes

The preceding hub analysis used homogeneous agents at the highest-welfare parameter setting for each group size to relate early network events within an episode to the identity of its eventual hub.

Here we examine all configurations with defined tie-corrected concordance in the heterogeneousparameter sweep, excluding configurations in which every agent tied in every episode. Within each configuration, each agent has an agent-specific policy vector (ρ, ω, τ ) that remains fixed across 32 repeated episodes. We denote Kendall's coefficient of concordance by W K to distinguish it from social welfare. The coefficient depends on agents' within-episode payoff ranks rather than their payoff magnitudes and assesses whether the same agents occupy similar relative positions across episodes. At W K = 1, the same ordering of agents, including any ties, recurs in every episode. At W K = 0, the rankings vary so that no agent ranks systematically higher or lower than the others across episodes. We compared the observed W K values with those obtained after shuffling agent identities within each episode.

The observed W K distributions were shifted toward larger values than the identity-shuffled distributions (Figure 5). Using the 99th percentile of each treatment's pooled identity-shuffled reference as a descriptive benchmark, 41.46% of configurations with defined coefficients exceeded it for n = 10, 91.94% for n = 50, and 99.29% for n = 100. Fixed agent-level policy differences were therefore associated with persistent differences in agents' relative payoff positions across episodes.

We replaced one payoff-guided agent with an agent that follows a fixed rule without evaluating payoffs. On each activation, it adds one sponsorship until it reaches a preset target. It then switches permanently to withdrawing one of its own sponsorships at random on each activation and passes once none remain. We call this agent the scripted link builder.

The sampled construction share h ∈ [0, 1] sets the integer sponsorship target k = ⌈h(n -1)⌉, with effective target share k/(n -1). During construction, the builder chooses uniformly among available visible additions and the single generic outside-link action. We sampled h together with (ρ, ω, τ ). Discussion

Efficient network formation requires local decisions to address both the creation of useful connections and the reorganization of existing ones. Under the incentives studied here, isolation presents a clear equilibrium barrier: initiating a link remains privately unprofitable even with complete information. The nonempty-start comparisons reveal a broader challenge, however, because an initially connected network can also fail to reach high welfare under the same revision policy within a given period. Our simulations identify conditions under which a local revision policy nevertheless generates near-optimal collective welfare. Optimistic valuation of unseen connections supports initial linking, while sufficient visibility and precise choices permit useful access to be retained and links whose costs outweigh their benefits to be removed. These findings complement equilibrium and dynamic accounts of network formation 7, 8 by distinguishing escape from an inefficient equilibrium from the broader problem of reaching efficient network structures through local revision.

Our model extends the explanatory scope of immediate-payoff best response in the networkformation framework of Bala and Goyal 7 . Under the incentives studied here, an isolated individual loses by creating the first link, so choosing only immediately profitable changes leaves the network empty. Human groups achieved high terminal welfare under the same payoff schedule 9 . In each paid round, participants could form links during a one-minute unpaid trial before the five payoff-relevant minutes began. This leaves open how participants would initiate links if costs applied from the first revision. In our simulations, optimistic valuation of unseen access can encourage initially unprofitable connections, while sufficient visibility and value-sensitive revision support the retention and reorganization of useful access. Our results identify conditions under which these requirements can be reconciled within a fixed decision policy. Comparisons across initially empty and connected networks further show that its success depends on the structure in which it operates. This extends the account from whether a network resists individual changes to how local decisions can generate collective efficiency.

The analytical comparisons suggest that successful formation depends on reconciling exploration with consolidation. Expectations about unseen access can encourage agents to establish connections before their benefits are apparent. As connections develop, broader visibility helps agents recognize the access that existing links provide. Optimism can help sustain early growth by favoring further additions over deletion, but excessive optimism can also prolong costly linking after it becomes unnecessary. Wider visibility eases this tension by revealing both the benefits supported by existing bridges and alternative routes that make some sponsorships dispensable. Expectations and information thus play complementary roles in formation. These comparisons provide a candidate explanation of the welfare landscape, rather than evidence that every successful trajectory passes through distinct exploratory and consolidating phases.

Coordination can emerge because the connections agents create or remove change the choices available to others. A new link can improve access to other individuals and bring previously unseen relationships within an agent's view; removing a link can reduce access or make those relationships harder to observe. Consequently, the same decision rules can produce different choices as the network evolves. Earlier actions thus influence later behavior through the structure they leave behind, without requiring a shared plan or memory of who contributed. Even a highly centralized network can emerge through decentralized decisions: an individual can become a hub because others connect to them, without directing those connections.

The comparisons between starting networks show why this evolving structure matters for collective efficiency. Holding the other simulation settings fixed, dense random networks and connected trees retain the broad welfare patterns but change which combinations of decision parameters perform well. For 100 agents with a visibility radius of four, dense starting networks exhibit a pronounced region of high welfare at low choice temperature that is largely absent when starting from isolation or a connected tree. Because a tree already connects every individual and each link is paid for only once, connectivity alone cannot explain this difference. Dense networks offer opportunities to reduce costs by removing links while preserving useful access, whereas trees may require new connections to shorten paths and replace existing links. The results are consistent with these different requirements, although they do not establish which revisions account for the observed welfare differences. They describe performance over the simulated duration, rather than demonstrating that inefficient outcomes are permanent or that the nonempty starting networks are Nash equilibria.

Our results connect evidence on partial network knowledge to its consequences for collective efficiency. Empirical studies show that knowledge of social networks is localized 21 and that people use compressed representations of social relationships 16 . Our model complements these findings by identifying conditions under which limited network information supports efficient formation and reorganization. Under the experimentally matched payoff schedule, larger groups required broader visibility to sustain high welfare. This pattern reflects the combined effects of population size and the associated payoff conditions. The information available within a given visibility radius also depends on network position: at radius one, a star's center observes the entire population, whereas a peripheral agent observes only itself and the center. Visibility therefore describes access to network information rather than cognitive burden directly. Together, these results show how the consequences of partial observation depend on the structure agents collectively create.

Formation history and persistent policy differences also shape who benefits from the resulting network. Initially identical agents can acquire different positions, with early action or link receipt associated with eventual hub formation. When individual decision parameters remain fixed across episodes, agents tend to occupy similar relative payoff positions. These findings complement accounts linking network position to differences in endowments, linking costs or benefits 19, 22 , while locating persistent heterogeneity in the decision policy. Mixed-policy simulations further show that the consequences of random linking depend on how many agents follow that policy and how frequently they act. A scripted agent that first builds links without evaluating payoffs and subsequently withdraws them lowered mean welfare in small groups but raised it in the larger groups. In favorable larger-group configurations, others benefited while the builder bore substantial costs. Because construction or withdrawal can remain incomplete at the endpoint, these gains do not establish that earlier costs are eventually repaid.

Formation history provides information about the decision policies underlying collective outcomes. Within the model, welfare trajectories improved parameter recovery relative to terminal welfare alone, indicating that similar final outcomes can conceal differences in the policies that generated them. This result supports recording network development rather than measuring only final performance. Recovery was assessed using observations averaged across repeated simulations and concerns inference within the assumed model. Recent work demonstrates how experimentally estimated individual decision parameters can inform predictions of collective diffusion and the effectiveness of network interventions 23 . Our framework addresses the complementary problem of decisions that create and reorganize the network itself, identifying candidate policy parameters whose behavioural relevance can be tested against individual link-revision histories.

These conclusions concern unilateral sponsorship, fixed policy parameters and performance over finite simulation horizons. Prospective rewiring and the representation of unseen targets through one pooled search action are substantive ingredients of the policy; the present analyses do not isolate their contributions from those of visibility, optimism and choice precision. Parameter recovery also showed remaining limitations in uncertainty calibration. Finally, efficiency refers to total access benefits net of linking costs, rather than other collective objectives such as resilience to disruption.

Our findings connect bounded rationality with the emergence of efficient collective organization.

Previous models show how restrictions on strategy complexity and locally informed revisions can shape connectivity, balance and hierarchy 17, 24 . Here, we identify conditions under which optimistic search, partial observation and value-sensitive revision jointly support useful linking and subsequent reorganization. Efficient networks can emerge without centralized planning or an explicit change in individual decision rules. Collective efficiency thus depends on how those rules interact with the evolving structure they create.

Utilitarian social welfare, hereafter simply welfare, is the sum of individual payoffs, W (⃗ g) = i∈N π i (⃗ g). A pattern of sponsored links is socially efficient if it maximizes W . Local-view revision policy

We derive the local-view revision policy from a complete-information rule in which agents revise at most one sponsored link at a time. Under complete information, an active agent observes the entire network, compares retaining its current sponsorships with every feasible addition or deletion of a single link, and chooses the option that maximizes its payoff. Our policy changes this rule in four ways. First, the agent observes only agents within graph distance ρ, together with the links among them. Second, opportunities to link beyond this visible neighborhood are represented by a single outside-link action. Its heuristic value interpolates between crediting only the target and also crediting distance-two access to every other agent outside the visible neighborhood.

The weight ω controls this additional credit; these endpoints do not bound the realized marginal payoff. Third, when evaluating an addition to a visible agent, the agent considers whether the new link could replace one of its existing sponsored links. This prospective swap affects the score of the addition, but only the new link is added immediately; the replaced link may be deleted at a later revision opportunity. Finally, the agent samples among the available actions using a Boltzmann choice rule, with choice temperature τ controlling choice noise. Thus, ρ, ω, and τ govern visibility, outside-link optimism, and choice.

We match the group sizes and payoff parameters that Choi et al. 9 used in their experiment.

Implementation

The simulator was written in Python. Graphs were represented and manipulated using the Python package igraph.

Action values were evaluated jointly by a compiled C kernel. Using a bit-set representation of the visible access network, the kernel reused a single distance precomputation to evaluate all feasible deletions, additions, and prospective rewirings, rather than recomputing shortest paths for each alternative. Boltzmann choice probabilities were computed with a max-shifted softmax for numerical stability.

The parameter-recovery analysis used mixed neural posterior estimation in version 0.26.1 of the sbi toolbox 25, 26 . The main estimators used group size and either the welfare trajectory or terminal welfare, averaged across 32 episodes. Appendix G describes the inputs, architecture, training, held-out evaluation, and exploratory posterior-rank diagnostics.
