Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In many social situations, self-interest conflicts with social considerations such as fairness and reciprocity. Previous research suggests that self-interested behavior may be an automatic response whose regulation requires cognitive control. Consistent with this view, findings from neuroeconomics implicate the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (rDLPFC) in regulating self-interested behavior and implementing social preferences. Whereas prior neuromodulation studies have shown that inhibition or disruption of the rDLPFC increases self-interested behavior, studies examining the effects of enhancing its activity have yielded inconsistent results. Moreover, it remains unclear whether such enhancement promotes responses only to intentional unfairness or also to inequality resulting from an intention-free process. In the present study, we applied anodal transcranial direct-current stimulation (tDCS) over the rDLPFC to reexamine its causal role in regulating self-interested behavior among ultimatum game responders. In addition, we tested whether this manipulation promotes responses to both intentional unfairness and inequality resulting from an intention-free process. Anodal stimulation increased rejection rates particularly for the most unequal offers, and this stimulation condition by fairness interaction was observed separately within the human-proposed and computer-generated offer conditions. These findings suggest that enhanced rDLPFC activity promotes otherregarding considerations even when unequal outcomes cannot be attributed to another person's intentions. Alongside prior findings demonstrating the opposite effect of anodal stimulation applied to an adjacent lateral prefrontal region using the same paradigm, the results point to distinct and complementary roles of lateral prefrontal regions in managing the conflict between self-interest and social preferences. We discuss implications for dual-process accounts of social decision-making.

Introduction

In many daily social situations, and specifically in social exchange situations, individuals face a conflict between selfish motives and social considerations.

Researchers from various domains (economics, mathematics, philosophy, psychology, brain science, education, sociology, marketing, etc.) apply unique research methodologies to understand how people make decisions in such mixed-motive situations and to shed light on the factors and mechanisms that underlie this conflict.

A prominent tradition in economic theory adopts the homo economicus assumption that individuals maximize their own material outcomes without an intrinsic concern for others' outcomes (Edgeworth, 1881; Olson, 1965; Rubinstein, 1982).

Nevertheless, accumulating evidence indicates that people systematically deviate from the predictions of models based on this pure self-interest assumption and that they often base their decisions on various social considerations such as fairness and reciprocity (Camerer, 2003). People favor outcomes that are consistent with their fairness expectations and show dislike for outcomes that do not match those expectations (Loewenstein et al., 1989; Roth, 1995). For example, in the consumer domain, consumers tend to seek additional alternatives when they suspect a vendor mistreats them. This behavior often causes businesses and firms to avoid raising prices, even when demand and supply in the market justify such an increase, if they suspect consumers will perceive this as unfair (Kahneman et al., 1986).

Theories of social preferences attempt to address this fairness behavior. Two of the most prominent models of social preferences are inequality aversion and reciprocal fairness, which emphasize different underlying motivations, particularly with respect to outcome distributions versus the processes that generate them.

Inequality-aversion theories suggest that people are sensitive to inequities in outcomes, both against them and in favor of them (although to a lesser extent).

According to these theories, individuals care less about their absolute payoff and more about their relative share (Bolton & Ockenfels, 2000; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999).

Alternatively, reciprocal fairness theories assume that people care not only about outcomes but also about the fairness of the process through which they are determined. According to this view, people tend to reciprocate kind acts with kindness and unkind with unkindness, even if it entails a personal cost (Blount, 1995; Dufwenberg & Kirchsteiger, 2004; Rabin, 1993).

In recent years, there has been an ongoing debate concerning whether the nature of social preferences is automaticallyfoot_0 driven (whereas deliberate actions are necessary to ensure economic self-interest) or whether it requires cognitive control (suggesting that self-interest is the primary motive, which needs to be constrained by inhibition). According to the automatic social preferences hypothesis (Halali et al., 2014), automatically driven emotional processes are essential factors in social preferences (Halali et al., 2017; Harlé & Sanfey, 2007; Pillutla & Murnighan, 1996; Sanfey et al., 2003; Tabibnia et al., 2008; for review, see Sanfey & Chang, 2008).

Thus, when these preferences conflict with self-interest motives, they must be suppressed through cognitive control mechanisms (i.e., emotion regulation) for individuals to maintain their selfish motives and personal gains (Achtziger et al., 2016; Calvillo & Burgeno, 2015; Cappelletti et al., 2011; De Neys et al., 2011; Grecucci et al., 2013; Halali et al., 2013 Halali et al., , 2014 ; ; Katzir et al., 2021; Slater et al., 2025; Van't Wout et al., 2010).

In contrast to this view, the deliberate social preferences hypothesis (Halali et al., 2014) suggests that social preferences are deliberative processes, and egoistic tendencies are the dominant responses that need to be overridden to foster social considerations. This approach also receives considerable support from behavioral (Achtziger et al., 2015 (Achtziger et al., , 2018;; Halali et al., 2013; Katzir et al., 2021), evolutionary (Jensen et al., 2007), and developmental evidence. The latter, for example, indicates that self-interested behavior is the dominant motivation at an early age, whereas social preferences tend to develop later (Bereby-Meyer & Fiks, 2013; Kogut, 2012).

Additional evidence supporting the deliberate social preferences hypothesis (Halali et al., 2014) comes from neuroeconomics findings suggesting that the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (rDLPFC) is involved in inhibiting self-interested behavior (Baumgartner et al., 2011; Hu et al., 2022; Knoch et al., 2006 Knoch et al., , 2008 Knoch et al., , 2009;; Speitel et al., 2019; Van't Wout et al., 2005). The rDLPFC is a brain region that is known for its involvement in various executive functions (Chan et al., 2008; Fecteau et al., 2007; Miller & Cohen, 2001), and specifically in regulating and inhibiting reward-seeking behaviors such as cravings for sweet foods (Kekic et al., 2014), smoking (Fregni et al., 2008), and alcohol use (Boggio et al., 2008). In light of this, the current work aims to further examine the causal role of the rDLPFC in overriding self-interested responses, such as earning as much money as possible in the ultimatum game, a mixed-motive social exchange situation, despite fairness concerns.

Importantly, inequality aversion and reciprocal fairness are inherently confounded in the standard ultimatum game. To address this limitation, the current study uses a modified version of the game to examine whether the involvement of the rDLPFC in regulating self-interested responses is restricted to situations involving intentional unfairness or extends to inequality generated by an intention-free process.

The ultimatum game (Güth et al., 1982) is an established two-player game that models the tension between selfish motives and social considerations. In this game, one of the players needs to propose how to split a sum of money between the two players. The other player, the responder, has two choices: to accept or reject the proposed allocation. If the responder accepts, then the sum is split as proposed.

However, if the responder rejects, then both players receive none. Although models based on pure self-interest predict that the responder will accept any positive amount, which illustrates economic self-interest, a substantial proportion of responders tend to reject offers below 40% of the total sum. Numerous studies have replicated this deviation from the predictions of pure self-interest models (for an overview : Camerer, 2003), including games played for large stakes (Cameron, 1999; Hoffman et al., 1996) and in different cultures (Henrich et al., 2001; Roth et al., 1991).

In the context of the ultimatum game, the rDLPFC has been shown to be engaged in situations involving a conflict between self-interest motives and fairness considerations. Specifically, unfair, compared with fair, offers are associated with increased activation in the rDLPFC (Baumgartner et al., 2011; Halko et al., 2009; Sanfey et al., 2003). Furthermore, follow-up studies using neuromodulation have provided evidence for a causal role of rDLPFC activity in controlling self-interested motives. Temporary disruption using repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulationfoot_1 (rTMS; Baumgartner et al., 2011; Knoch et al., 2006; Van't Wout et al., 2005), and temporary inhibition using transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS; Knoch et al., 2008; Speitel et al., 2019) foot_2 , resulted in decreased rejection rates of unfair ultimatum game offers. For example, Knoch et al. (2006) found that rTMS over the rDLPFC decreased rejection rates of unfair offers relative to placebo stimulation (from 44.7% to 9.3%), bringing participants' behavior closer to the predictions of pure self-interest models. Stimulation over the left DLPFC, however, did not appreciably affect behavior.

More recently, studies aspired to establish the causal role of the rDLPFC in regulating self-interested behavior also by enhancing (rather than inhibiting/disrupting) its cortical activity, expecting it would result in the opposite effect (i.e., increased rejection rates compared to the control condition; Hu et al., 2022; Speitel et al., 2019). However, the findings were inconsistent across these two studies, with Hu et al. (2022) finding an effect of the cortical activity enhancement, whereas Speitel et al. (2019) did not. Moreover, beyond this empirical inconsistency, it remains unclear whether enhancing rDLPFC activity promotes both inequality aversion and reciprocal fairness or is limited to reciprocal fairness.

Considering the inconsistent findings regarding the possible effect of enhancement of the rDLPFC activity among ultimatum game responders, our first objective in the current study was to reexamine whether anodal stimulation (i.e., cortical activity enhancement manipulation) would have an effect of decreasing selfinterested behavior. Bearing in mind the suggested role of the rDLPFC in downregulating self-interested behavior, we expected that rDLPFC enhancement would decrease self-interest, resulting in increased rejection rates of unfair offers.

Our second objective was to discern whether the enhanced activity of the rDLPFC might promote both inequality aversion and reciprocal fairness. To address this question, it is necessary to distinguish between these two motivations, as they are inherently confounded in the standard ultimatum game, where rejection of unfair offers can reflect both inequality aversion and reciprocal fairness. According to outcome-based inequality aversion models (Bolton & Ockenfels, 2000; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999), people are sensitive to their relative share, rather than solely to their absolute payoff. Accordingly, ultimatum game responders are willing to reject unfair offers to minimize inequality, even at the price of reducing their own monetary payoff. Alternatively, procedural-based models of reciprocal fairness (Dufwenberg & Kirchsteiger, 2004; Rabin, 1993) assume that people are sensitive to the intentions underlying the allocation. Consequently, responders may reject unfair offers to punish the proposer for their unkind behavior, despite the financial cost entailed in this decision.

To address this confound, we employed Blount's (1995) paradigm, which introduced a condition in which offers are generated by a random mechanism rather than by a human proposer. In this condition, any allocation, including an unfair one such as a 10:90 split, is randomly determined. As such, it is the product of an intention-free process that cannot be attributed to intentional unfairness by the proposer, and is therefore procedurally fair (or at least perceived as impartial), even though the resulting outcome may be unequal.

Consistent with this reasoning, Blount (1995) found that rejection rates of unfair offers were significantly lower when allocations were generated by a random mechanism rather than by a human proposer. This pattern suggests that removing intentionality reduces reciprocity-based responses, while the persistence of some rejections indicates a continued role for inequality aversion. Building on this framework, if enhanced rDLPFC activity promotes both inequality aversion and reciprocal fairness, increased rejection rates should be observed in both the humanproposed and the random-proposed (computer-generated) conditions. In contrast, if rDLPFC activity primarily promotes reciprocal fairness, such an effect should be observed only in the human-proposed condition, where intentionality is present, but not in the computer-generated condition, where no intentionality is involved.

The only neuromodulation work that attempted to disentangle these two motivations and focused on the role of the rDLPFC involved a temporary disruption of this area, which inhibits (rather than enhances) its cortical activity (Knoch et al., 2006). The authors found that disrupting rDLPFC activity decreased reciprocal behavior (i.e., increased self-interested behavior), but did not affect inequality aversion per se, in a condition in which offers were randomly generated by a computer. However, rejection rates in this condition were very low overall (Knoch et al., 2006), raising the possibility of a floor effect (or a ceiling effect in terms of acceptance rates). Specifically, since individuals tend to tolerate inequality when it is determined by an intention-free mechanism and rarely reject such offers, it may be difficult to further reduce rejection rates in this context. In contrast to this disruption manipulation, which inhibited rDLPFC activity, enhancing its activity is expected to increase rejection rates of unfair offers (i.e., reduce self-interested behavior).

Therefore, cortical activity enhancement may provide a better opportunity to examine whether the rDLPFC promotes not only reciprocal behavior but also inequality aversion in situations where inequality results from an intention-free process. To the best of our knowledge, this possibility has not been examined using cortical activity enhancement of the rDLPFC.

Method

Participants. Fifty-five first-year native Hebrew-speaking psychology students participated in the experiment (69.1% women; mean age = 22.5, SD = 1.8) in exchange for course credit. Additionally, to incentivize authentic behavior in the ultimatum game, we randomly selected four participants and paid them according to their actual gains in one randomly selected trial of the game. We informed participants about this procedure in advance. The sample size was determined using an a priori power analysis with the participant-level repeated-measures ANOVA originally planned for the study. This analytic strategy was consistent with that used in our previous work employing the same behavioral paradigm (Slater et al., 2025).

We conducted the analysis in G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009), specifying a repeatedmeasures within-between interaction, five repeated measurements, and two stimulation groups. Assuming a medium effect of ηP2 = .06 (f = .2526)foot_3 , α = .05, a correlation of .50 among repeated measures, and a nonsphericity correction of 1, the required sample size ranged from 32 participants for 95% power to 56 participants for 99.9% power. Accordingly, we aimed to recruit approximately 60 participants (30 per condition). In total, we recruited 55 participants and randomly assigned them to one of two tDCS experimental conditions (stimulation, n = 26; control, n = 29). In line with tDCS safety guidelines (Antal et al., 2017), we verified that all participants had no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, substance abuse or dependence, or use of central nervous system-active medication. In addition, we verified that participants had not undergone brain surgery.

Materials. Ultimatum Game.

We used a computerized version of the ultimatum game, which was based on Halali et al.'s (2014) procedure. In this game, participants received one-shot monetary offers from multiple independent proposers. Participants were informed that their partners in the game were students from other universities who participated in this study in a different session. Each offer involved a split of 100 New Israeli Shekels (NIS; approximately $30). In half of the trials, the participants' partners made the offers ('partner' condition), while in the other half, as in Blount's (1995) paradigm, the computer program decided how to divide the money between each dyad ('computer' condition). In each one of these two conditions, participants received 22 offers that varied in terms of fairness level, ranging from fair to highly unfair offers : 50% (n=4), 40% (n=4), 30% (n=5), 20% (n=5), and 10% (n=4) of the stake. The number of offers per fairness level was not fully balanced, reflecting common distributions of offers in ultimatum game settings and allowing for sufficient variability in responses across levels of unfairness. We randomized the assignment of faces to offers so that each participant received the offers with different faces and in a different order. In each trial, as shown in Figure 1, participants first saw a fixation point (+) in the middle of the screen for 500 ms, followed by a picture of the current partner that was presented for 3 s. The displayed pictures were selected from a database used in a different behavioral study (Ganel & Goshen-Gottstein, 2004) and included male and female students with neutral facial expressions. The assignment of faces to offers was fully counterbalanced, such that 50% of the trials involved male partners and 50% involved female partners. This balance was maintained within each proposer's condition (partner vs. computer) and across all fairness levels. Next, in the partner condition trials, an enlarged version of the partner's picture was presented for an additional 2 seconds, indicating that these partners made the following offers.

Participants then saw the proposed offer (e.g., "Sharon keeps 70 Shekels for herself and gives you 30 Shekels") and had to decide within 10 seconds whether to accept or reject the offerfoot_4 . After deciding, the outcome of their decision was presented for 4 s and was followed by the next offer.

In the computer condition, a picture of a die replaced the partner's picture, indicating that the subsequently proposed money allocation would be randomly generated by the computer. The proposed allocation was then formulated accordingly (e.g., "The computer program gives 70 Shekels for Sharon and 30 Shekels for you").

We counterbalanced the response-key arrangement (left [z] /right [M] key to indicate 'acceptance' / 'rejection') between participants.

tDCS.

A constant current flow of 1.5 mA was delivered for 20 minutes by a battery-driven stimulator (DC-Stimulator Plus, NeuroConn) through a pair of salinesoaked sponge electrodes. We applied a relatively small electrode of 3 cm × 3 cm (anodal electrode: 9 cm 2 ; current density: 0.166 mA/cm 2 ) and a relatively large reference electrode of 5 cm × 7 cm (cathodal electrode: 35 cm 2 , current density: 0.04 mA/cm 2 ) to improve stimulation focality (Luca et al., 2020; Nitsche et al., 2007), while maintaining safety guidelines for current density (Bikson et al., 2016). In addition, to illustrate the expected current distribution in the targeted brain region, we used ROAST (Huang et al., 2019), a MATLAB toolbox that simulates current flow based on current density and tissue characteristics such as skull, CSF, and brain (see figure 2). The anodal electrode was located over the rDLPFC corresponding to the F4 site (Tayeb & Lavidor, 2016) of the 10-20 EEG system, and the reference (cathodal) electrode was located over the contralateral supraorbital area (Ditye et al., 2012). A C B To examine whether the active and sham stimulation conditions produced different levels of subjective discomfort that could potentially cue participants to their condition assignment, as well as to assess stimulation tolerability, we asked participants after completing the study whether they had experienced pain or aversiveness during the stimulation. We also asked participants who reported such discomfort to rate its intensity on a scale ranging from 1 to 10. We assigned an intensity score of zero to participants who reported no discomfort.

Procedure. We informed participants that they would receive stimulation for 20 minutes. Participants in both conditions began with a gradual increase in stimulation for 30 seconds ('ramp up') until it reached 1.5 mA. Participants in the anodal stimulation condition received a constant current of 1.5 mA throughout the session, whereas participants in the sham condition received stimulation for 30 seconds followed by a gradual decrease to zero intensity (30 seconds of 'ramp down'). This sham stimulation protocol is considered an effective blinding technique (Nitsche et al., 2008; Peled-Avron et al., 2019), as it produces sensations similar to active stimulation without affecting cortical activity (Gandiga et al., 2006). All participants were blinded to their stimulation condition.

We instructed participants to begin reading the ultimatum game instructions only after two minutes of stimulation. Thus, participants started the game after approximately four to five minutes of stimulation. By that time, the effects of the stimulation were expected to have begun (Bikson et al., 2009). After participants completed the ultimatum game task, we informed them that, within 24 to 48 hours after the experiment, they would be requested to complete an online questionnaire. This survey included several demographic questions and a couple of additional questionnaires (for exploratory reasons).

Data Analysis.

The originally planned analysis was a participant-level repeated-measures ANOVA based on aggregated rejection rates. For the final analyses, we instead adopted a trial-level generalized estimating equation (GEE) approach. This approach accommodates the binary nature of the accept/reject decisions and their repeated-measures structure while retaining trial-level information that would be lost by aggregating decisions into participant-level rejection rates.

The analyses included 2,406 binary decisions made by the 55 participants, in response to one-shot monetary offers in the Ultimatum Game. Participants made up to 44 decisions each; 14 trials were missing because participants did not respond within the allotted time. We coded rejection as 1 and acceptance as 0. We estimated GEE models in SPSS that included stimulation condition (anodal vs. sham), proposer (partner vs. computer), fairness level (50%, 40%, 30%, 20%, 10%), and their interactions as predictors. We mean-centered fairness and effect-coded the categorical predictors (condition and proposer) as -0.5 and 0.5. The models specified a binomial distribution with a probit link function and an autoregressive AR(1) working correlation structure, with observations clustered by participant. Results were robust across alternative correlation structures. Similar analytic approaches have been used in prior work on repeated decision-making (e.g., Nakashima et al., 2017).

To examine the robustness of the findings across alternative analytic approaches, we also analyzed rejection behavior using the participant-level repeatedmeasures ANOVA originally planned for the study and a trial-level logistic generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a participant-level random intercept.

The complementary analyses produced broadly consistent directional and substantive patterns, although the statistical significance of some of the focal stimulation effects differed across approaches. A logistic GLMM also models the binary trial-level decisions but estimates effects conditional on participant-level random effects and therefore addresses a subject-specific rather than a population-averaged inferential question (Agresti et al., 2000; Zeger et al., 1988). Thus, GEE and GLMM are both applicable to correlated binary data, but they estimate differently defined effects.

Because our hypotheses concern average differences in rejection behavior across the experimental conditions, we retained GEE as the analysis most directly aligned with our research questions. We report the full results of the complementary analyses and their model-specific sensitivity analyses in the Supplementary Materials and discuss the differences in statistical significance across approaches in the Discussion.

Sensitivity Analyses.

We first conducted a sensitivity analysis in G*Power using the final sample size and the specifications of the originally planned repeatedmeasures ANOVA. This analysis indicated that the study had 80% power to detect a within-between interaction of ηp² = .022 (f = .1490). Because this ANOVA-based calculation does not directly quantify sensitivity under the trial-level GEE, we also conducted a simulation-based sensitivity analysis corresponding to the final model.

The simulations preserved the observed allocation of participants across stimulation conditions, trial structure and pattern of missing responses, fitted outcome probabilities, and within-participant serial dependence. We generated binary responses using a probit model with AR(1) dependence calibrated to the serial dependence observed in the data. We then analyzed every simulated dataset using the same binomial GEE, probit link, AR(1) working correlation structure, and robust standard errors used in the primary analysis. We used two-sided Wald tests with α = .05 and evaluated the final candidate effect sizes using 5,000 simulated datasets each.

The design provided approximately 80% power to detect a stimulation condition × fairness interaction of |B| = .015 and a stimulation condition × proposer × fairness interaction of |B| = .029 in the hypothesized direction.

Results

Manipulation check.

We performed a chi-square test of independence to compare the proportion of participants who reported pain or aversiveness during the stimulation in the anodal (11/26 = 42.3%) and sham (13/29 = 44.8%) stimulation conditions. The difference between conditions was not significant,  2 (1, N = 55) < 1, p = .852. Further, there was no significant difference in the average reported pain or aversiveness (anodal stimulation: M = 1.46, SD = 2.1; sham stimulation: M = 1.52, SD = 2.0), F(1,53) < 1, p = .921. Taken together, these findings indicate that the stimulation conditions produced comparable levels of subjective discomfort, suggesting that pain or aversiveness was unlikely to provide a differential cue to condition assignment.

Decision behavior.

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of fairness, B = -.070, SE = .005, Wald χ²(1) = 183.26, p < .001, indicating that lower (more unfair) offers were more likely to be rejected and replicating the robust and widely documented effect of fairness on rejection behavior in the Ultimatum Game.

Importantly, the interaction between stimulation condition and fairness level was significant, B = -.023, SE = .010, Wald  2 (1) = 4.89, p = .027, indicating that the effect of anodal stimulation over the rDLPFC varied as a function of offer fairness. As illustrated in Figure 3, rejection rates were generally higher under anodal than sham stimulation for more unfair offers, consistent with a fairness-dependent modulation of behavior. This interaction was not further moderated by proposer identity, B = -.010, SE = .009, Wald  2 (1) = 1.21, p = .271, suggesting that the modulation of rejection behavior by stimulation across fairness levels generalizes across both human and computer proposers.

Follow-up analyses indicated that the effect of stimulation was driven primarily by responses to the most unfair offers. Specifically, for the 10%/90% offers, stimulation had a significant effect, B = .671, SE = .296, Wald  2 (1) = 5.13, p = .023. This effect did not significantly vary as a function of proposer identity at this fairness level, B = .361, SE = .272, Wald  2 (1) = 1.76, p = .184. No significant effects of stimulation emerged at higher fairness levelsfoot_5 , all Wald χ²s(1) ≤ 1.45, ps ≥ .228.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the effect of anodal stimulation over the rDLPFC on rejection behavior was most pronounced for highly unequal offers.

Consistent with prior findings (Blount, 1995; Slater et al., 2025), the analysis also revealed a significant main effect of proposer, B = .240, SE = .087, Wald  2 (1) = 7.62, p = .006, reflecting higher rejection rates when the human partner was responsible for the offer than when the offer was generated by the computer. The interaction between fairness and proposer was also significant, B = -.009, SE = .004, Wald  2 (1) = 4.38, p = .036, indicating that the difference in rejection rates between the human-partner and computer-generated conditions increased as offers became less fair. This difference was largest for the most unequal 10:90 offers (81.4% vs. 67.6%) and substantially smaller for equal 50:50 offers (1.8% vs. 0.5%). To further examine this interaction, we analyzed the two proposer conditions separately. Fairness strongly predicted rejection decisions both when offers were generated by the computer, B = -.063, SE = .005, Wald χ²(1) = 148.83, p < .001, and when they were proposed by a human partner, B = -.073, SE = .006, Wald χ²(1) = 162.76, p < .001. Moreover, as can be seen in Figure 3, the stimulation condition × fairness interaction was significant in both the computer-generated condition, B = -.021, SE = .010, Wald χ²(1) = 4.12, p = .043, and the human-partner condition, B = -.028, SE = .012, Wald χ²(1) = 5.98, p = .015. Additional analyses examined the potential role of gender. Because the sample included only 17 male participants, we did not include participant gender as a factor in the full factorial model, as the resulting interaction estimates would have had limited precision. Instead, we first included participant gender as a covariate in the primary model. Participant gender was not significantly associated with rejection decisions, B = -.319, SE = .242, Wald χ²(1) = 1.74, p = .187. Second, we included partner gender as an additional factor, along with its interactions with stimulation condition, proposer identity, and fairness. None of the interactions involving partner gender was significant, all Wald χ²s(1) ≤ 1.21, ps ≥ .271. However, a significant main effect of partner gender emerged, indicating higher rejection rates in trials involving male rather than female partners, B = -.110, SE = .047, Wald χ²(1) = 5.59, p = .018.

Finally, we estimated a model that included both partner gender as a factor and participant gender as a covariate. The main effect of partner gender remained significant, B = -.111, SE = .047, Wald χ²(1) = 5.51, p = .019, whereas participant gender remained nonsignificant, B = -.321, SE = .242, Wald χ²(1) = 1.76, p = .185, and none of the interactions involving partner gender was significant, all Wald χ²s(1) ≤ 1.24, ps ≥ .267. Across all three additional models, the inferential conclusions for every other effect included in the primary model remained unchanged: all effects that were significant in the primary model remained significant, and all effects that were nonsignificant remained nonsignificant.

Reaction time.

Reaction time analyses revealed no significant main effect of stimulation condition, B = 254.87, SE = 166.85, Wald χ²(1) = 2.33, p = .127. A stimulation condition × proposer × fairness interaction emerged, B = -9.83, SE = 4.93, Wald χ²(1) = 3.98, p = .046. However, follow-up analyses conducted separately at each combination of proposer and fairness did not reveal a significant effect of stimulation, all Wald χ²s(1) ≤ 3.08, ps ≥ .079. Thus, the reaction-time analyses provided no consistent evidence that stimulation altered response speed.

Discussion

The current work provides additional experimental evidence for the causal role of the rDLPFC in overriding self-interested behavior in social exchange situations in favor of other-regarding considerations. Specifically, we found that noninvasive anodal stimulation over the rDLPFC increased rejection of unfair offers among responders in the ultimatum game. Whereas several studies have reported the opposite effect following inhibition (cathodal tDCS; Knoch et al., 2008; Speitel et al., 2019) or temporary disruption (rTMS; Baumgartner et al., 2011; Knoch et al., 2006; Van't Wout et al., 2005) of the rDLPFC, the only two studies that examined the effect of enhancing activity in this region yielded inconsistent results (Hu et al., 2022; Speitel et al., 2019). The current findings help resolve this inconsistency and provide further support for the causal role of the rDLPFC in reducing self-interested behavior and promoting other-regarding considerations in social exchange situations.

Consistent with prior findings (Blount, 1995; Slater et al., 2025), the fairness by proposer interaction indicated that the difference in rejection rates between humanproposed and computer-generated offers increased as offers became more unequal.

This pattern is consistent with reciprocal fairness providing an additional motivation for rejecting unfair offers when the partner is responsible for the allocation, beyond aversion to the unequal outcome itself. Against this behavioral backdrop, to the best of our knowledge, the current work is the first tDCS study to examine whether the behavioral effect of enhancing rDLPFC activity is restricted to intentional unfairness or extends to inequality per se, that is, inequality resulting from an intention-free process. Our findings revealed that anodal stimulation over the rDLPFC increased rejection rates of unfair offers both when they were intentionally proposed by a human partner and when they were randomly generated by a computer. Thus, the influence of enhanced rDLPFC activity was not restricted to situations involving intentional unfairness. This finding is consistent with recent evidence that inequality per se, even when it results from an intention-free process, is aversive and subject to regulatory processes (Slater et al., 2025). Two interpretations of the present pattern remain possible. Because unequal outcomes were present in both proposer conditions, the effect may primarily reflect enhanced sensitivity to inequality operating across both conditions. Alternatively, rDLPFC activity may support a broader implementation of social preferences that encompasses both inequality aversion and reciprocal fairness.

The absence of evidence that the stimulation effect varied as a function of proposer identity differs from the pattern reported by Knoch et al. (2006), which, to the best of our knowledge, is the only prior neuromodulation study of rDLPFC activity among ultimatum game responders to use a paradigm designed to disentangle reciprocal fairness and inequality aversion. Specifically, for unfair offers proposed by human partners, the authors observed an effect opposite to that found in the present study, namely, a decrease in rejection rates of unfair offers (as opposed to the increase observed here). This pattern is nevertheless consistent with the current findings, given that the manipulation in that study involved disrupting rDLPFC activity, whereas in the present study, we enhanced its activity. However, for unfair offers that were randomly generated by a computer and were therefore intention-free, the authors did not find any differences between conditions. One possible explanation for this discrepancy concerns statistical sensitivity.

Neither the overall three-way interaction nor the follow-up analysis focusing on the most unequal 10:90 offers provided evidence that the stimulation effect varied as a function of proposer identity. The sensitivity analysis indicated, however, that the present study was adequately powered to detect only larger three-way effects. Thus, the findings cannot distinguish between the absence of a proposer-dependent stimulation effect and the presence of a smaller effect that the study was not sufficiently sensitive to detect. At the same time, Knoch et al. (2006) detected an interaction between treatment group and proposer type with a slightly smaller overall sample (N = 52), divided among three relatively small treatment groups (right-DLPFC n = 19, left DLPFC n = 17, sham n = 16). This finding suggests that limited statistical sensitivity may not fully explain the discrepancy. Nevertheless, power cannot be compared solely on the basis of sample size, because it also depends on the magnitude of the underlying effect, the experimental design, the distribution of responses, and the statistical analysis.

A second possible explanation concerns the relatively low baseline rejection rates in the computer-offer condition. If disrupting rDLPFC activity reduces participants' ability to override the self-interested tendency to accept an offer, its predicted effect in this condition would be a further reduction in an already relatively low rejection rate. The limited room for such a reduction may have made the effect difficult to detect in Knoch et al. (2006). In contrast, enhancing rDLPFC activity, as in the present study, was expected to increase rejection rates, leaving considerably more room for an observable change from the same low baseline. Thus, the difference between the studies may partly reflect a floor constraint on the behavioral effect of disruption rather than a fundamental difference in the processes engaged by humanand computer-generated offers.

A third possibility is that the range of offers used in Knoch et al. (2006) did not include sufficiently extreme levels of inequality (with the most unequal offers corresponding to 20/80 splits), whereas the current study included more extreme disparities (i.e., 10/90 splits), where the effect of stimulation was most pronounced.

Finally, it is also possible that enhancing cortical activity, as in the present study, and disrupting it, as in Knoch et al. (2006), differentially affect the underlying mechanisms. Specifically, enhancement may amplify sensitivity to social considerations, whereas disruption may reduce the ability to override self-interested responses. Meta-analytic evidence suggests that modulation effects of tDCS (i.e., anodal vs. cathodal) are not always symmetrical, with anodal stimulation often producing more robust and consistent effects (Jacobson et al., 2012). This raises the possibility of a broader asymmetry between enhancing and inhibiting neural activity, beyond the specific techniques used (i.e., tDCS and TMS).

The current findings, along with previous neuromodulation studies demonstrating the role of the rDLPFC in modulating the drive to earn money (Baumgartner et al., 2011; Hu et al., 2022; Knoch et al., 2006 Knoch et al., , 2008 Knoch et al., , 2009;; Speitel et al., 2019; Van't Wout et al., 2005), are consistent with the notion that self-interest may operate automatically, or as a default tendency in such contexts. Specifically, in line with developmental (Bereby-Meyer & Fiks, 2013; Kogut, 2012), evolutionary (Jensen et al., 2007), and behavioral (Achtziger et al., 2015 (Achtziger et al., , 2018;; Halali et al., 2013; Katzir et al., 2021) findings, and in accordance with the deliberate social preferences hypothesis (Halali et al., 2014), it seems that egoistic tendencies are a default automatic response in social situations. Within this framework, the rDLPFC may serve as an inhibitory mechanism that down-regulates self-interested motives, thereby enabling the implementation of social considerations. This interpretation is further supported by evidence from affective neuroscience suggesting that reward-seeking is primary, spontaneous, and requires little conscious intent (Panksepp, 2000 (Panksepp, , 2004)), and is associated with activity in the mesolimbic dopaminergic system (Alcaro et al., 2007). Neuroimaging findings further indicate that the rDLPFC is involved in regulating dopaminergic activity (Guo et al., 2014; Van Den Bos et al., 2015), suggesting a mechanism through which prefrontal control may modulate reward-driven behavior. Taken together, these findings suggest that the mesolimbic dopaminergic reward-seeking system may underlie the selfinterested tendencies often attributed to the 'rational player'. This proposition is consistent with neuroeconomics findings linking reward-related activity to self-interested decision-making (Breiter et al., 2001; Grecucci et al., 2013; Harlé et al., 2012; Tabibnia et al., 2008; Van den Bos et al., 2009).

Notably, the current findings highlight a functional dissociation between the rDLPFC and the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (rVLPFC), two adjacent regions in the prefrontal cortex. Despite their proximity, these regions are anatomically distinct (the rDLPFC encompassing Brodmann areas 46 and 9, and the rVLPFC encompassing areas 44, 45, and 47) and appear to serve different regulatory functions.

Specifically, previous work has shown that enhancing rVLPFC activity increases selfinterested behavior at the expense of fairness considerations (i.e., increased acceptance rate of unfair offers; Slater et al., 2025), consistent with its established role in emotion regulation and the downregulation of affective responses (Berkman & Lieberman, 2009; Lieberman, 2007 Lieberman, , 2011;; Riva et al., 2015). In contrast, in the current study, as well as in Hu et al. (2022), enhancing rDLPFC activity produced the opposite effect, reducing self-interested behavior in favor of other-regarding considerations (i.e., increased rejection rates of unfair offers).

Intriguingly, the notion of distinct regulatory roles for the rDLPFC and the rVLPFC in managing the frequent conflict between self-interested motives and social preferences may help reconcile the two seemingly contradictory perspectives regarding whether social preferences are automatically driven or require deliberative control. Specifically, findings indicating that both selfish and social motives are downregulated by distinct cognitive control systems suggest that both self-interest and social preferences may operate automatically, as proposed in a recent dual-control framework (Halali, 2026). This proposition is consistent with findings and theoretical models in the neuro-affective field suggesting that there are two segregated limbiccortical pathways (Damasio et al., 2000; Liotti et al., 2000; Liotti & Tucker, 1995; Phan et al., 2002), namely the ventral and dorsal streams, which may lead to two distinct prefrontal control regions (Liotti et al., 2000). Moreover, this account aligns with contemporary approaches to dual reasoning suggesting that what has traditionally been conceptualized as competition between an automatic "System 1" process and a deliberative "System 2" process may instead reflect competition between two or more automatic "System 1" processes (Bago et al., 2021; De Neys, 2022; De Neys & Pennycook, 2019; Katzir et al., 2021; Pennycook et al., 2015).

Limitations and Future Research

The current study holds several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as is common in studies relying on undergraduate psychology samples, the sample included substantially fewer male than female participants. The small number of men precluded a sufficiently precise examination of participant gender as a factor in the full factorial model. Prior research has not yielded consistent evidence for gender differences in responder behavior in the Ultimatum Game (Croson & Gneezy, 2009; Niederle, 2016), and gender was not a central focus of the present study.

Nevertheless, we included participant gender as a covariate, and this adjustment did not change the inferential conclusion for any effect in the primary model. Partner gender was fully counterbalanced within participants, and its main effect remained significant after adjusting for participant gender. Furthermore, none of the interactions involving partner gender was significant. However, the present sample does not allow us to determine with sufficient precision whether responses to male and female partners vary as a function of participant gender. Future research using larger and more balanced samples may further examine this possibility.

An additional limitation concerns the sensitivity of the focal findings to the analytic approach. The complementary participant-level ANOVA and trial-level logistic GLMM produced directional and substantive patterns that were broadly consistent with those obtained using the primary GEE. However, the statistical significance of the stimulation condition by fairness interaction and the stimulation effect on the 10:90 offers differed across approaches. These differences may partly reflect the distinct effects estimated by the models: the GEE estimates populationaveraged effects, whereas the GLMM estimates subject-specific effects conditional on participant-level random effects (Agresti et al., 2000; Zeger et al., 1988). The modelspecific sensitivity analyses further suggest that limited statistical sensitivity under the GLMM specification may have contributed to these differences. Within their respective model specifications, the GEE showed substantially greater sensitivity than the GLMM to effects of the magnitude observed in the present sample (see Supplementary Materials). Thus, the nonsignificant GLMM results may reflect limited sensitivity to effects of the magnitude observed in the present sample rather than a qualitatively different behavioral pattern. Future studies planning to estimate subject-specific stimulation effects using GLMMs should conduct simulation-based a priori power analyses corresponding to the intended model and recruit larger participant samples, particularly when stimulation condition is manipulated between participants.

Another limitation of the current design is that stimulation was applied to a single target region without the inclusion of an active control site. While stimulating an additional brain region could, in principle, strengthen claims regarding anatomical specificity, identifying a truly neutral control region is challenging given the distributed nature of neural processing. As a result, active control sites may introduce unintended functional effects. Instead, we employed a sham-controlled design, which allows for a cleaner comparison by isolating the effect of stimulation to the target region without modulating additional neural substrates. Future research may extend this approach by incorporating multiple stimulation sites to further examine anatomical specificity.

An additional direction for future research would be to apply formal computational models to further decompose the underlying social preferences at the individual level. For example, models such as Fehr and Schmidt (1999) and Bolton and Ockenfels (2000) provide parametric accounts of inequality aversion, whereas models such as Rabin (1993) or Dufwenberg and Kirchsteiger (2004) capture reciprocity based on the perceived intentions of others. However, these models rely on different theoretical assumptions and are not easily integrated within a single framework, particularly in designs that explicitly manipulate intentionality, as in the present study.

Importantly, the current experimental approach offers a conceptually distinct method for examining these processes by directly manipulating the motivational structure of the task (i.e., intentional vs. intention-free unfairness), thereby allowing for a more direct test of the causal role of the rDLPFC in regulating behavior under these conditions. Future research may benefit from integrating such experimental manipulations with computational modeling approaches to provide a more comprehensive account of the mechanisms underlying social decision-making.

Concluding remarks

To conclude, in the current work, using tDCS, a technique that allows the modulation of cortical activation, we provide further evidence for the prominent and causal role of the rDLPFC in regulating the often-existing conflict between selfinterest and social preferences. Specifically, in light of previously inconsistent findings, our findings provide further evidence that enhanced rDLPFC activity increases the regulation of self-interest, as reflected in increased rejection rates of unfair ultimatum game offers.

Importantly, extending prior work, our findings demonstrate that this effect is not limited to responses to intentional unfairness but is also observed when unequal outcomes are generated by an intention-free process. These results suggest that the rDLPFC supports the implementation of other-regarding considerations across situations that differ in whether inequality can be attributed to another person's intentions.

Additionally, when considered alongside previous findings demonstrating the causal role of the rVLPFC in increasing self-interested behavior (Slater et al., 2025), the current findings further support the notion of distinct and complementary functional specialization within the lateral prefrontal cortex. This pattern is consistent with the dual-control framework, which proposes differentiated roles for the rDLPFC and rVLPFC in managing the conflict between self-interest and social preferences through the regulation of reward-seeking and socio-emotional motivations, respectively (Halali, 2026). Future research should directly compare the roles of these regions within a unified experimental design to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying this functional dissociation.
