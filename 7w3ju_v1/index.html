Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. 1. Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a frequent and debilitating consequence of trauma exposure. It is associated with major problems in daily functioning, social relationships and overall quality of life (Jellestad et al., 2021). Current clinical guidelines recommend trauma-focused psychotherapies (e.g., prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, trauma-focused cognitive-behavioural therapy) as first-line treatment options, supported by strong empirical evidence and efficacy in different trauma populations (Foa et al., 2013; Rothbaum & Watkins, 2025). PTSD is still a challenging condition to treat for many patients, especially those with chronic, complex or severe trauma-related symptoms (Karatzias et al., 2019). Many patients benefit from empirically supported psychotherapies, but the same limitations recur across studies. Full remission is not achieved by many people, dropout rates are high, especially with exposure-based therapies, and relapse is common even in those who respond initially (Bradley et al., 2005; Najavits, 2015; Schottenbauer et al., 2008). This implies that the problem may not be in the delivery or enactment of therapy. It may also indicate a more profound fundamental limitation in how existing therapies try to modify trauma-related memories and beliefs (Rothbaum & Watkins, 2025; Vervliet et al., 2013).

Part of this limitation stems from the fact that most trauma-focused therapies are primarily based on extinction learning. Extinction does not erase the original traumatic memory. In contrast, it produces new inhibitory learning that diminishes anxiety reactions under favourable conditions (Lebois et al., 2019; Schiller & Delgado, 2010). As the original representation of the threat is retained, traumatic reactions may be reactivated under conditions of stress, in different contexts or when environmental stimuli evoke the original trauma.

Treatments that rely on inhibitory learning, rather than changing the unpleasant memories themselves, might lead to relapse (Monfils & Holmes, 2018; Vervliet et al., 2013).

Memory reconsolidation is a different paradigm. It implies that a consolidated memory may become labile for some time when reactivated, so that it can be updated before it is reconsolidated (Lee et al., 2017; Nader, 2015). This would allow a change in the original traumatic memory, rather than creating a conflicting inhibitory memory. However, the reliability and the limitations of reconsolidation-oriented therapies are still a matter of lively discussion (Phelps & Hofmann, 2019). Scholars do not agree on the mechanisms of memory destabilisation, the conditions that promote reconsolidation and the process by which correction information is integrated into a reactivated memory trace (Beckers & Kindt, 2017; Bui & Milton, 2023; Kida, 2020; Treanor et al., 2017). A recent narrative review likewise concludes that reconsolidation may operate as a shared mechanism of change across psychotherapies, while noting that its clinical use depends on timing, emotional regulation, and contextual boundary conditions (Ismaya et al., 2026).

One framework that might shed light on how memories are altered during reconsolidation is predictive processing. From this point of view, the brain works as a stratified system that generates predictions. It constantly updates its internal models by minimising differences between its expectations and the data it receives. These mismatches are called prediction errors (Clark, 2013; Friston, 2012; Friston & Stephan, 2007). The central tenet of this approach is precision weighting, which determines the influence of prior beliefs on new sensory information. Excessive precision in maladaptive threat expectations can shape the interpretation of experiences and sustain trauma-related symptoms, despite fresh experiences that may contradict them (Chamberlin, 2019). This notion is becoming significant in computational psychiatry and in predictive-processing frameworks of PTSD (Kaye & Krystal, 2020; Kube et al., 2020).

Predictive-processing frameworks elucidate the persistence of maladaptive expectations, while reconsolidation theories delineate the conditions under which memories may be revised (Lee et al., 2017). Nonetheless, neither model adequately explains how corrective experiences should be incorporated into traumatic memories in therapy. To address this gap, the present manuscript proposes Trauma Processing Therapy (TPT), a conceptual therapeutic approach that integrates memory reconsolidation and predictive processing.

Preliminary case-level documentation of TPT exists for prolonged grief disorder treated in a single session (Ardian, 2025) and for complex PTSD with treatment-resistant depression treated over six sessions (Octaviani et al., 2026). TPT focuses on the creation of precisionweighted prediction discrepancies during the period of reconsolidation. The aim is to increase the likelihood that the corrective information alters the original trauma representation, rather than being stored separately. This is based on the data that prediction error is essential for destabilisation of fear memories (Sevenster et al., 2013). TPT is a flexible closed-loop system in which each intervention is shaped by ongoing patient feedback.

TPT is not empirically supported. The only clinical reports to date are these two uncontrolled single cases (Ardian, 2025; Octaviani et al., 2026), which are preliminary and cannot establish efficacy or mechanism. It is a theory driven, operationalised framework which generates testable hypotheses for future research. Its main argument is that lasting trauma recovery may require systematic manipulation of precision-weighted prediction errors during reconsolidation windows to update maladaptive trauma memories. TPT is thus an extension of previous reconsolidation-based methods. It offers a model for clinicians to use, links mechanisms of memory updating with the theory of predictive processing, and is empirically falsifiable and refinable.

1.1 The Free Energy Principle and Predictive Processing Theory

The free-energy principle suggests that organisms survive by proactively minimising uncertainty about their environment (Friston, 2012). Organisms cannot directly access the hidden causes of their sensory inputs, and instead must use internal generative models to infer what is happening in the world (Friston et al., 2013; Friston & Stephan, 2007; Pezzulo et al., 2024). The brain is, in this view, a prediction-making system. It is always predicting sensory events and comparing these predictions with incoming information (Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Clark, 2013; Friston, 2012). Thus, survival requires more than a reaction to stimuli. It also depends on maintaining and updating internal models that predict environmental conditions well (Friston et al., 2010; Friston et al., 2013).

Rather than recording external reality, perception is an active process of inference used for predictive processing (Clark, 2013). The brain constantly hypothesises the causes of sensory experience (Friston et al., 2016). It uses prediction errors, the differences between expectations and sensory evidence, to update and refine these hypotheses (Feldman & Friston, 2010; Hohwy, 2012). The brain is not able to compute surprise per se, as this would require knowledge of the probability of a sensory input under all its possible causes (Friston et al., 2010). Uncertainty, which is surprise averaged over time, cannot be calculated directly. The theory instead takes a computational stance, regarding variational free energy as a statistical quantity that the brain can compute from its own model (Friston et al., 2010). Surprise is upper bounded by variational free energy, so low free energy also implies low surprise (Friston, 2012; Friston et al., 2010). There are two ways in which free energy can be reduced when there is a prediction error. The brain can either update its internal model so that it predicts better, or it can act on the world to change its sensory input so that the input fits the model's predictions (Friston, 2012; Friston et al., 2010). Perception and learning are the first route; the second is action. All three can therefore be seen as examples of the same basic process of free-energy minimisation (Friston, 2012; Friston et al., 2010).

In this model, perception is a never-ending loop between predictions and prediction errors. Predictions shape perception and prediction errors correct predictions when they do not fit the sensory evidence (Clark, 2013; Hohwy, 2012). This exchange enables the brain to improve its models of the environment (Clark, 2013). In a more general sense, cognition can be thought of as a process that is centred on prediction, updating models and reducing uncertainty (Pezzulo et al., 2024).

Changing priors is costly, so they cannot be changed very often. Thus, the brain must decide whether an observation should or should not change a prior. This is done by estimating how much each prediction error should update the current model. This is called precision weighting. It decides which mismatches are significant signals for updates and which are noise.

Precision weighting may be a key mechanism through which trauma distorts predictive inference as it regulates the trade-off between keeping a model stable and revising it.

1.2 Precision Weighting

For predictive processing, perception is not just a matter of detecting sensory inputs.

The brain also evaluates the importance of each input for inference. Sensory signals vary in reliability, so prediction errors carry different amounts of evidence and cannot all be treated the same way (Feldman & Friston, 2010; Friston, 2022). Thus, the brain estimates the confidence or precision of each prediction error before deciding whether to update its generative model (Friston, 2022; Friston et al., 2016). More formally, precision is the inverse variance of a signal. It captures the expected reliability of incoming information, and the strength of prediction errors in updating belief (Feldman & Friston, 2010). Precision weighting enables high-precision prediction errors to drive learning and revision of the model, and low precision errors are considered noise (Feldman & Friston, 2010).

In this framework, precision acts as a gain-control mechanism that determines the salience of mismatches between prediction and sensation (Feldman & Friston, 2010). Precision must be allocated selectively among competing signals; it is not evenly spread (Friston et al., 2016). This leads to attenuation of less relevant signals, and the inference is dominated by signals of higher precision (Friston, 2022). This selective allocation provides a predictive-processing account of attention, which can be understood as optimising precision to particular sources of information (Feldman & Friston, 2010; Hohwy, 2012). Sensory attenuation works in the opposite direction: it is the active down-weighting of signals that are less informative or less relevant to behaviour (Idei et al., 2022). Perception is thus a function of both the information available and the weighting of that information (Hohwy, 2012).

One consideration in setting the precision weighting is statistical reliability, but so is the cost of being wrong. Threat, harm and physiological integrity signals are given more weight from an adaptive point of view, because errors in these domains may endanger survival (Van den Bergh et al., 2020). Thus, threat-related cues often receive a higher weighting than their reliability alone would warrant, and have a stronger influence on perception, learning and behaviour (Chamberlin, 2019). This bias is a functional trade-off: the cost of missing real danger is greater than the cost of over-estimating it (Van den Bergh et al., 2020).

Seen in that light, trauma is easier to understand. Trauma-related disturbances may arise when threat predictions are resistant to updating and continue to influence perception, attention and behaviour long after the original threat has passed (Kube et al., 2020; Lyndon & Corlett, 2020). That is because of two features of predictive processing. First, threat predictions may be more precise than the evidence on which they are based (Van den Bergh et al., 2020). Even when there is evidence of safety, fear of danger pervades perceptions (Chamberlin, 2019).

Second, at all levels of the cortical hierarchy, free energy is minimised (Edwards et al., 2012).

Thus, minimising prediction error at one level may conflict with expectations and goals at a higher level, and this conflict underlies resistance to model revision (Van de Maele et al., 2024).

This implies that a belief can be maintained despite contradictory evidence, since accepting that evidence would increase error elsewhere in the hierarchy (Krupnik, 2020).

1.3 Hyperprecise Priors

Neural defence systems adapt when they predict danger accurately, but overly exact expectations of threat may persist after the threat has passed, and maladaptive beliefs may arise (Meacham & Bergstrom, 2016). For predictive processing, a hyperprecise prior is a belief that is held more confidently than the evidence warrants (Krupnik, 2020; Lawson et al., 2014).

Belief updating depends on the relative precision of priors and prediction errors, so a hyperprecise prior might resist updating when new evidence is given lower precision (Feldman & Friston, 2010; Friston, 2022). Danger expectations may thus continue to dominate even when safety information contradicts them. In this way, the precision-weighting mechanisms that normally support learning could also safeguard maladaptive beliefs from correction (Lawson et al., 2014; Wilkinson et al., 2017). This resistance may help explain why some trauma-and fear-related symptoms are hard to treat. If threat-related priors are hyper-precise, extinction learning may be less effective because strongly held expectations of danger may override safety information (Fujimoto, 2026; Vervliet et al., 2013). Thus, approaches based on reconsolidation have been proposed as an alternative to update maladaptive threat memories (Beckers & Kindt, 2017; Ecker & Bridges, 2020; Schiller et al., 2009). Their clinical use is still challenging, as memory destabilisation appears to depend on specific boundary conditions and results have not always replicated (Chalkia et al., 2020; Milton et al., 2023; Phelps & Hofmann, 2019).

Traumatic memories may be especially susceptible to this kind of resistance. They are encoded under conditions of high emotional and physiological arousal, which tends to increase the vividness and confidence of memory (Kensinger, 2009). They often have a sense of nowness about them: the event is experienced as if it is happening now rather than being recalled as past (Brewin et al., 2010). In predictive-processing terms, this feature may reflect predictions with unusually high precision (Chamberlin, 2019; Fujimoto, 2026). Traumatic memories are also broad, multimodal representations that integrate sensory details, bodily sensations, emotions, context, and personal meaning (Kearney & Lanius, 2022). This breadth, coupled with high precision, might make them immune to change by ordinary experience.

The hierarchy gives one more reason. A prediction error updates a representation mainly at the level at which that prediction is formed (Friston, 2022). Thus, at the cognitive level, corrective information may leave lower-level sensory, interoceptive or affective predictions unchanged, and the two levels may operate relatively independently (Fujimoto, 2026). A person can know they are safe and still feel unsafe. This may be why cognitive reassurance fails, as it does not address the prediction errors that feed lower-level threat expectations (Fujimoto, 2026). Thus, the difference between "knowing" and "feeling" is just a difference in the level at which revision has occurred in the hierarchy (Kaye & Krystal, 2020; Kube et al., 2020; Wilkinson et al., 2017).

These points suggest the main proposal of this framework: the modification of a hyperprecise traumatic prior may require corrective prediction errors that match the original representation in content and strength, along the dimensions in which it is encoded (Krupnik, 2020). Because traumatic priors are sensory, emotional and meaning-based, corrective experiences that are too narrow, too weak or at the wrong hierarchical level may not challenge the representation as a whole. The rest of the parts not addressed will still be precise and continue stabilising the larger trauma model. We term this a clinical 'lock-in' whereby the trauma representation persists despite repeated evidence to the contrary.

1.4 Memory Reconsolidation

From a predictive-processing perspective, one reason that trauma can be difficult to treat is that traumatic memories might serve as highly precise and broadly distributed predictive models, rather than as simple associations (Lyndon & Corlett, 2020). Memory is not the only factor that makes trauma hard to process, but it is the focus of this paper. These models can continue to shape perception, emotion, and bodily responses long after the original threat has gone (Kaye & Krystal, 2020; Wilkinson et al., 2017). If this is true, extinction-based treatments may alleviate symptoms without significantly changing the traumatic prior (Lebois et al., 2019; Monfils & Holmes, 2018), and permanent change may require changing the original threat representation (Wilkinson et al., 2017).

One explanation may lie in the mechanism of extinction learning. Extinction does not seem to erase a fear memory. It creates a new inhibitory safety association that competes with the original threat memory, which is preserved (Lebois et al., 2019; Schiller & Delgado, 2010; Sosa, 2022). The clinical improvement may thus be the temporary predominance of the safety association (Beckers & Kindt, 2017; Schiller & Delgado, 2010). The threat representation is preserved, allowing it to maintain its precision and to be reactivated later (Lebois et al., 2019; Vervliet et al., 2013). This may explain why relapse happens. Extinction learning is strongly context dependent and therefore fear responses may return through renewal, spontaneous recovery or reinstatement when circumstances change (Vervliet et al., 2013). Threat-related evaluations may also be resistant to extinction even when overt fear responses do decrease (Luck & Lipp, 2015). Stress may also bias the system more toward threat by increasing the salience and precision of danger signals (Chakraborty et al., 2021). Under stress or uncertainty, the old threat can become the dominant one again. It was suppressed but not updated. In this view, the main limitation of extinction might be more about its mechanism rather than the amount of exposure (Lebois et al., 2019; Monfils & Holmes, 2018; Vervliet et al., 2013).

The Predictive Processing Model of Psychotherapeutic Interventions (3PI) was recently

proposed as an integrative framework: common factors create a safe therapeutic context, and specific factors facilitate the updating of dysfunctional generative models (Villiger, 2024). 3PI provides a clear but general, taxonomic account of the inferential goals of psychotherapy. For instance, it describes exposure as revision of the model by accumulation of disconfirmed negative expectations (Foa & McLean, 2015). The account does not do full justice to the neurobiological difference between extinction learning and direct prior modification. If extinction creates a parallel, context-dependent safety prior that competes with the threat representation (Maren et al., 2013), then broad predictive-processing frameworks may fail to fully explain relapse via spontaneous recovery and renewal (de Jong et al., 2018; Foa & McLean, 2015). Thus, a more explicit mechanism of therapeutic updating may be useful.

Like 3PI, TPT assumes that psychopathology is sustained by overly precise priors and that model updating is required for therapeutic change. TPT, unlike 3PI, distinguishes between extinction learning and memory reconsolidation. In TPT, uncalibrated exposure alone may not be sufficient to change a hyperprecise, multidimensional traumatic prior, because the corrective information may not match the prior in scope or hierarchical level. The system can then resolve the mismatch by creating a secondary safety prior that lives alongside the original threat model.

TPT has a specific procedure for performing this updating. Instead of building prediction error through repetition, it deliberately creates an inferential crisis. It compresses the traumatic prior into a Dominant Affective State (DAS), identifies and strengthens a Corrective Affective Prediction (CAP), and then activates both simultaneously within the short window in which the prior is thought to remain labile. The aim is to update the source representation directly and to translate principles of predictive processing into a concrete clinical procedure that can be tested.

Reconsolidation is another pathway because it affects the original memory representation. When a memory is reactivated under appropriate conditions it can enter a short unstable state in which it can be modified prior to restabilisation (Kida, 2020; Nader, 2015).

Destabilisation seems to necessitate prediction error, a discrepancy between what the memory forecasts and what actually occurs, as opposed to just retrieval (Lee et al., 2017). This could lead to a revision of the original threat model at its source which may lead to a reduction in the precision of the traumatic prior and change the experience of the memory (Beckers & Kindt, 2017; Lee et al., 2017). If reconsolidation is successful, the feeling of present-moment threat may be reduced, temporal context may be restored, and recall may be less vivid and emotionally charged (Monfils & Holmes, 2018; Phelps & Hofmann, 2019). This has two clinical implications for therapy: to induce prediction errors that are proportional with the extent and specificity of the trauma representation, and to provide corrective information within the narrow time window when the memory is labile (Beckers & Kindt, 2017; Bui & Milton, 2023; Milton et al., 2023).

2. The Protocol

Mechanistically, this framework suggests that trauma therapy might involve three core tasks: reactivating the traumatic prior, generating prediction errors of sufficient magnitude, and delivering corrective information while the prior is still labile. TPT attempts to achieve this via a closed-loop adaptive process. With each therapeutic pass, feedback in the form of changes in distress, emotional state, or resistance is used to modify the next intervention. In this sense TPT is more a decision-making algorithm than a fixed sequence of steps, and its stages are recurring components.

Calibration

The protocol controls timing via its sequence, pace and collision parameters. An adaptive branch only deals with obstacle beliefs when they occur.

2.1 Stage One: The Dominant Affective State

Dominant Affective State (DAS) is a very important operation in the protocol. This operation is meant to respond to the problem that this framework defines as the central one: the scope of the traumatic past. From this point of view, trauma is not so much a single memory as a high dimensional network in which sensory details, interpersonal information, bodily states, temporal features, and inferred meanings are conjoined and specified (Kearney & Lanius, 2022). Trying to rework the entire composite at once can overwhelm the patient, and one corrective experience is unlikely to hit every dimension at once. The DAS tries to make the traumatic past manageable by reducing its dimensional complexity.

This can be thought of as a kind of dimensionality reduction, in which the protocol collapses the broad prior into a single, all-encompassing affective-cognitive node. The node is multimodal and can integrate interoceptive states, language and conceptual meaning, for example a somatic state of constriction together with the belief "I am powerless" (Lindquist et al., 2015; Oosterwijk et al., 2012). It provides the therapist with a target against which a commensurate prediction error can be generated.

The therapist can access the DAS through a dominant feeling or dominant core belief, using questions such as "What is the dominant feeling right now?" or "What do you believe about yourself in that situation?" Sometimes patients answer questions about feelings with a belief like "I am worthless" or "I am powerless." These are not names of feelings, but are still considered the DAS. In TPT, feelings and beliefs are two access points to one affectivecognitive configuration, thus attending to one may activate the other. This is consistent with evidence that body feelings and thoughts share distributed neural networks (Oosterwijk et al., 2012).

Another advantage of compression is serialisation. The DAS is one integrated affectivecognitive node at one level of processing. So, each treatment cycle works on one node and the patient does not have to hold the whole traumatic composite in awareness at once. This may mitigate the risk of defensive precision spikes and permit the updating to progress through manageable, closed-loop iterations. As the prediction error at one DAS node is resolved, conditional probabilities across the wider network may change and the next highly weighted dimension is then more likely to become salient, for example a schema of worthlessness that emerges after fear subsides. The protocol aims to iteratively revise the entire traumatic representation across treatment, but each cycle is manageable.

Distress is measured by Subjective Units of Distress (SUD) on a scale of 0 to 10 Gurwitch & Warner-Metzger, 2022). The SUD rating is not the intensity of the DAS, but the overall distress in the memory being processed. In TPT, SUD is a stand-in for the precisionweighted prediction error, experienced as interoceptive or affective surprise. This is consistent with interoceptive accounts of emotion, whereby emotional experience is described as a reflection of the brain's inferences about bodily states (Seth, 2013). Prior precision is a latent construct that is not available to therapist or patient. Repeated SUD ratings across cycles are used to check whether the precision of the traumatic prior is being corrected over time. Distress is expected to increase briefly during collision events as prediction error is generated and this increase should not be read as clinical deterioration. This is why the SUD is rated only at the end of the activation sequence. A decreasing SUD over cycles may be indicative of revision.

A high SUD indicates an unresolved cycle, which might indicate an inert response (insufficient updating) or a resistant response (defensive impedance). There are two limits. SUD is a reflective, reportable judgement and so only provides indirect access to subpersonal predictionerror dynamics. Nor, as an ordinal clinical scale, does it estimate the formal variational quantities of computational theory.

2.2 Stage Two: The Unmet Affective Prediction

Having isolated the Dominant Affective State (DAS), the next task is to determine the direction from which a prediction error can be generated and to identify a candidate corrective prediction. The protocol does this with two operational devices: the Unmet Affective Prediction The aim is to map the inferential structure of the active traumatic prior, and to locate where corrective information can enter.

Typically, a UAP is elicited by prompts such as "What does this feeling require?" The stated need is not addressed by the protocol. Asking what the feeling "wants" is a way of finding the conceptual boundary of the DAS, the dimension along which the affective state is defined by contrast with its opposite. This is the axis that is likely to be most in conflict with the current prediction. It is thus the direction in which the prediction error is expected to be generated.

The RSQ then asks about the affective state that would occur if the UAP were already fulfilled, with prompts such as "If [the UAP] were fulfilled, how would you feel?" The answer is a candidate CAP. The RSQ describes the corrective state as one that is already in force. The objective of this framing is to reduce the likelihood of instant rejection of the corrective state "by habitual cognitive filters." The patient need not believe the state is true, only to access its affective consequences. This is a proposed mechanism of the protocol, not an empirically observed effect.

The RSQ also decides if a collision is allowed to go ahead. If it produces an affective state that is significantly different from the DAS, the protocol moves to build and fortify the CAP. If it produces no change, or simply reproduces the original affective state, this is taken as a sign that in this direction there is no usable prediction error.

Then the protocol does not generate a collision. It looks back at the UAP and looks for another way. Protective-supportive (soothed, accompanied, defended, helped) and agentic (fight back, brave, capable, empowered) are two broad directions of the UAP and RSQ. The protocol utilises both. If the work remains only on the protective-supportive side, a reduction in SUD may be only downregulation: the patient feels calmer, but the prior has not been corrected. The inclusion of the agentic side is to ensure that a reduction in SUD can also indicate correction via prediction error. Because SUD cannot tell us which of these has happened, correction is best judged by symptom outcomes, as lasting improvement may reflect the generalisation of a revised prior.

If the patient cannot name a UAP, the therapist may offer candidates. To reduce the risk of leading the patient, the therapist always offers contrasting options, never options in the same direction. For example, the therapist may provide a protective and an agentic choice, or a choice that focuses on the outside world (other people or the situation) and one that focuses on the patient's inner state. The patient chooses the option that fits or rejects all of them and RSQ tests the chosen option.

2.3 Stage Three: The Corrective Affective Prediction

The RSQ is contradicted by the expectation evoked by the CAP. When the RSQ and this expectation are brought into contact, the resulting conflict is expected to generate the prediction error needed for revision. The CAP is not fixed and can be improved or replaced, provided that it continues to evoke an expectation that contradicts the DAS. What matters is not its specific content, but whether it challenges the dominant prior.

The UAP, RSQ and CAP are not always based only on the patient answers.

Alternatively, they may be generated in the course of discussion between patient and therapist.

The patient's answer may be derived from the same prior that gives rise to the symptoms and a CAP based only on that answer may then fail to contradict the DAS. In these instances, the therapist may need to guide the CAP, for example from the protective-supportive end to the agentic end, or the reverse.

The RSQ often first yields a weak CAP. Due to the low precision, it may not be able to compete with the high precision DAS that has long-organized experience (Lawson et al., 2014).

The protocol does not therefore go directly to collision. First, it uses amplification, which progressively increases the precision and experiential weight of the CAP until it is strong enough to generate prediction error. Amplification includes imagery that is not therapist-guided (Holmes et al., 2008), recall of congruent autobiographical experiences, and self-talk. The goal is to transform the CAP from a tentative possibility into an experientially salient prediction that can hold its own against the established prior.

Imagery is developed only after the RSQ has identified a CAP that is truly different from the DAS. A corrective prediction that is weak, imprecise, or not consistent with the target prior is likely to be dominated by the DAS and produce insufficient prediction error to be revised. Amplification is meant to lower this risk. The collision is more likely to challenge the dominant prior than to amplify it.

2.5 Stage Five: Cycle Evaluation

Evaluation of a cycle is based on three parameters: change in SUD, change in type of dominant affect, and presence/absence of obstacle beliefs. Together, they classify each cycle as either a revision or one of the two forms of non-revision. These indicators require clinical judgement to read. The most obvious sign of revision would be a drop in SUD, but this could be due to down-regulation (see Stage Two). Changing the type of DAS does not have a fixed meaning and is read according to context. A shift from one negative affect to another may point to a hidden part of the broader affective configuration that is coming into being through serialisation. In patients with emotional numbing or dissociation, the appearance of a specific, identifiable affect for the first time may in itself be a sign of progress, in that it may indicate that an attenuated affective channel has regained sufficient precision to enter conscious awareness (Vaage-Kowalzik et al., 2024).

The per-cycle revision criterion is permissive by design: in most cases either a decrease in SUD or a change in affect type suffice to satisfy it. It has a functional purpose: it provides information that determines the next cycle. The protocol also includes a mechanical safety. The protocol is never applied ad infinitum with no signs of updating, because if the SUD does not decrease for three consecutive cycles, the therapist stops the algorithmic application and starts direct inquiry.

In TPT they are interpreted as signs that revision has made the prior less precise, rather than by suppression alone.

2.6 Obstacle Beliefs as a Contingent Target

Obstacle beliefs are a contingent target, not part of the routine sequence of stages. They seem to be a resistant form of non-revision: the system is activated by prediction error but does not assimilate it, and the patient denies the corrective state in a coherent way (Yon et al., 2018), with responses like "that could never happen" or "it would not be allowed." Conversely, an inert response does not register a meaningful mismatch. Obstacle beliefs therefore actively object.

An obstacle belief becomes a therapeutic target only when it is actively blocking revision, either by preventing a workable corrective prediction from forming or by stopping a collision that is already underway. Beliefs that do not interfere with updating are left untouched. In TPT, a blocking belief is considered to be a different hyperprecise prior at a higher hierarchical level than the trauma representation being processed. Revision of trauma history may threaten coherence of identity, disrupt an established affective function, or conflict with a belief that renders the corrective state seemingly forbidden (Ecker & Bridges, 2020; Miller et al., 2022).

The obstacle belief itself is a prior, and is processed with the same architecture as the original target. Instead of intensifying the collision, the therapist stops it, for continued pressure against a defended prior can reinforce the defence. The obstacle belief is then compressed into a separate DAS and goes through the same closed loop (DAS → UAP → RSQ → CAP → Collision). When the belief no longer hinders revision, the protocol reverts to the initial target.

2.7 Prerequisites, Contraindications, and Safety

TPT is applied rapidly, so that prediction error can be delivered when memory is assumed to be labile during the reconsolidation window. For some patients this pace may be unbearable. So, overwhelm needs to be differentiated from resistance. Processing stops immediately and treatment returns to the previous phase which was not finished. The vignette in Section 3 describes a stabilised patient with an uncomplicated course, so the protocol's safety and efficacy cannot be generalised to unstabilized, complex, developmental or dissociative cases. In a resistant reaction, the patient coherently denies the corrective state, remaining present, reflective and open to dialogue. Overwhelm is a failure of regulatory capacity. It can take the form of affective surges that cannot be settled, re-experiencing rather than recollection, or loss of dual awareness, when the patient can no longer be with the memory and anchored in the present at the same time (Beutler et al., 2022; Corrigan et al., 2010; Ford & Courtois, 2009).

The protocol handles the resistant response with the obstacle belief as the new target.

Overwhelm is managed outside of the protocol: The processing stops and the treatment is returned to grounding and stabilisation until the patient is back within his/her window of tolerance (Beutler et al., 2022; Corrigan et al., 2010; Ford & Courtois, 2009). This distinction needs to be made in the moment as holding the material, as is done for resistance, may exacerbate dysregulation in a patient who is overwhelmed.

Overwhelm indicates that the prerequisites for processing are not yet in place. TPT is an intervention in the processing phase of a phase-oriented model of trauma treatment, in which safety, affect-regulation capacity and a secure therapeutic alliance are established first (Cloître et al., 2011; Karatzias et al., 2019). Before utilising TPT, the clinician assesses whether the patient's regulatory capacity is sufficient to process at that time. Two types of contraindications are shown in Table 3.

3.1 Presentation

Ms. A was an adult female who had intrusive re-experiencing of a past trauma. The memory felt as though it were happening in the present, and it was accompanied by an abiding sense of worthlessness. After she finished the stabilisation phase of trauma treatment, we began the processing. The test used a neutral cue that had become associated with the event, not the threatening scene itself (to have the client imagine the threat again could trigger the old prediction and risk re-traumatization).

The session met the termination criteria of the framework: SUD was zero, there was no negative affect, and the memory was less vivid and more clearly time-stamped, so that the event was experienced as having happened in the past, rather than a present re-experience.

These changes are consistent with the reconsolidation account, but do not confirm it. Two other signs were the VoC rating and the future pacing test, which indicated that the associated belief had weakened and that the change had generalised to a related cue. Both were observed within the session. The generalisation in everyday life has to be judged in the follow up.

3.4 Synthesis

SUDs went down in steps for 4 cycles (10 → 7 → 5 → 3 → 0). Dominant affect shifted (fear → sadness → powerlessness → anger → unfairness → worthlessness). This is the serialisation of a broad prior. In each cycle an affective dimension was picked out, paired with a corrective prediction, and the two were brought into collision. Then came the next dimension.

The case has three aspects that fit the framework. First, the inert response is evidenced by the failure to form a CAP in Cycle 2: the prediction error was misdirected and thus, no revision happened and the UAP was re-navigated. Second, the obstacle belief in Cycle 3 shows the resistant response: the system received prediction error but a defensive increase in precision was produced and the belief was treated as a contingent branch. Third, the end combination of reduced vividness and time-stamping is consistent with the proposal that successful reconsolidation may alter the precision of the prior itself (Monfils & Holmes, 2018). The immediacy of the memory was lost, as would be expected if the representation had been edited at the source (Nader, 2015). For comparison, a published case of prolonged grief disorder reported lower grief and depression scores after a single TPT session, maintained at 3 and 6 months (Ardian, 2025), and a case of complex PTSD with treatment-resistant depression reported a PCL-5 fall from 71 to 5 over six sessions (Octaviani et al., 2026). Like the present vignette, these reports cannot separate reconsolidation from expectancy or alliance effects.

The above case did not involve overwhelm. If the patient becomes overwhelmed during processing, the therapist stops the processing and brings the client back to the stabilisation phase. Processing only resumes when the patient is back within their window of tolerance.

4. Discussion

This paper presents Trauma Processing Therapy as a closed-loop protocol designed to manipulate precision-weighted prediction error in destabilized trauma representations. The discussion compares TPT with related reconsolidation-informed and imagery-based therapies and outlines its limitations and future research needs.

TPT differs from Coherence Therapy primarily in its therapeutic target. Coherence Therapy targets implicit memory, where emotional learnings exist outside awareness (Ecker & Bridges, 2020; Ecker et al., 2024). TPT assumes that the traumatic prior can be accessed via the episodic memory in which it was created, where emotion and belief are reactivated together.

This assumption is in line with evidence that reactivated episodic memories undergo reconsolidation and can incorporate new information. It does not mean that working at the episodic level is more effective. The two approaches may be suited to different clinical presentations.

TPT also uses client-directed imagined scenarios, as does imagery rescripting (IR) (Arntz, 2025). In IR, the intervention is the re-scripted scenario. In TPT, the intervention is the collision, and imagery is just one way to evoke the CAP. The same function can be served when a congruent autobiographical memory or self-talk evokes the CAP to a greater degree (Stage Three). Reconsolidation research distinguishes between the potential updating of existing threat memories and the establishment of competing inhibitory memories (Clem & Schiller, 2016; Monfils & Holmes, 2018; Schiller et al., 2009). The development of TPT is based on the first route. Whether TPT does this more often than IR has not been tested. Memory vividness and time-stamping are indications of revision, but neither of these alone can be taken as evidence for reconsolidation (Lee et al., 2017; Treanor et al., 2017).

Another comparison is eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), an established treatment for PTSD with proposed mechanisms of working memory taxation and orienting responses (Landín-Romero et al., 2018; Rothbaum & Watkins, 2025). TPT offers a predictive-processing account of simultaneous activation, in which a reactivated trauma representation collides with an incompatible corrective prediction. This interpretation is based on evidence that prediction error can influence memory destabilisation and reconsolidation (Sevenster et al., 2013), but has not been examined in TPT. It is not presented as an account of how EMDR works, nor does it assume that simultaneous activation necessarily leads to reconsolidation.

The difference from emotion-focused therapy (EFT) is a basic assumption about emotion. Constructionist and interoceptive views suggest that the brain constructs emotions based on bodily signals, past experience and concepts (Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Lindquist et al., 2015). TPT takes this position, so it does not assume that an emotion is a real need to be satisfied. The UAP is a metaphor that serves to evoke the CAP (Stage Two). This is a theoretical choice and there is no evidence that it leads to better outcomes than working with emotional needs as EFT does.

This overlap leads to a fair question: should TPT be viewed as a distinct protocol? What might be distinctive is that it couples a particular unit of analysis (the DAS), a structured therapist role, a contrast gated collision and closed-loop calibration that discriminates inert from resistant responses. When we refer to TPT as a different protocol, we only mean this organization. It doesn't mean that TPT is more effective than existing approaches, or that it operates through a new mechanism.

4.2 Limitations

There are no controlled outcome data for TPT, only one composite vignette to support the proposed protocol, and efficacy and mechanisms are not established. The vignette illustrates the order of the protocol, but cannot separate the proposed reconsolidation mechanism from expectancy, therapeutic alliance, or other common factors. This mirrors a more general problem of translating reconsolidation research into clinical interventions (Beckers & Kindt, 2017; Lee et al., 2017). The two published TPT case reports (Ardian, 2025; Octaviani et al., 2026) are likewise uncontrolled single cases, and their authors call for broader studies and randomised trials.

The protocol also relies on subjective indices: SUD, vividness of memory, nowness, and felt pastness. These have not been demonstrated to be reliable or specific markers of memory reconsolidation (Phelps & Hofmann, 2019). Treating SUD as a proxy for precisionweighted prediction error is a theoretical link between self-report and an unobservable process.

Thus, changes in distress or in the experience of the memory cannot in and of themselves distinguish memory revision from extinction, expectancy, or demand characteristics (Lee et al., 2017; Treanor et al., 2017). A further risk of using the free-energy principle as a descriptive framework is that clinical phenomena may be redescribed using predictive-processing terms post hoc, without providing any additional explanatory value (Bruineberg et al., 2021).

Therapist judgement is also part of the protocol, especially when the therapist offers candidate UAPs. This step can help the patient. Offering contrasting UAP options (Stage Two) is intended to mitigate this risk, but this has not been tested. Imaginal interventions are also susceptible to distortion and confabulation of memories, particularly if the events are uncertain or disputed (Otgaar et al., 2023).

Therefore, at the end of each cycle the patient returns to the original memory as remembered, and SUD is re-rated against that memory, not against the imagined scenario. The aim of this step is the prevention of imagined material replacing the original event, but its protective effect has not been tested and cannot remove suggestibility or memory distortion.

Finally, how well TPT generalises is unknown. It has not been tested across trauma types or populations, especially those with complex trauma, developmental trauma, or dissociative presentations. These conditions may be associated with different patterns of emotion regulation and memory processing that make trauma-focused work more difficult (Beutler et al., 2022; Cloître et al., 2011), or may be less useful for them. Controlled studies are required to test outcomes, mechanisms and safeguards and to define when TPT can be used.

4.3 Directions for Research

Research on TPT could proceed in phases: early-phase studies first, followed by studies testing effectiveness. The first step is a treatment manual that describes each stage, the decision rules and safety procedures, as well as measures of therapist adherence and fidelity. Then a training program can be developed and tested to see if therapists can learn the protocol and deliver it as intended. Feasibility studies may assess the delivery of the protocol in routine settings including recruitment, retention, session length and adverse events. Acceptability should be evaluated from two perspectives: clients' tolerance and utility of the procedure, and therapists' usability and compatibility of the procedure with their practice. Interviews with both groups may suggest what aspects of the protocol need to be changed before larger trials. The case reports to date used a single session (Ardian, 2025) and six weekly sessions (Octaviani et al., 2026), so feasibility studies should record session number and dose alongside outcomes, and later trials should compare brief with multi-session delivery.

If these early studies support feasibility and acceptability, pilot trials and then randomised controlled trials may compare TPT with established trauma focused treatments, with long term follow up. These assessments could include the use of reinstatement and contextual-renewal paradigms to determine whether changes induced by TPT are resistant to symptom recurrence, as durability may serve to dissociate reconsolidation-based updating from inhibitory learning (Beckers & Kindt, 2017; Vervliet et al., 2013). The role of simultaneous activation might be explored in dismantling studies, e.g. by comparing rapid versus deliberately slowed delivery (Bui & Milton, 2023). Sessions can be coded in process studies to test whether independent raters can reliably classify unsuccessful cycles as inert or resistant, and whether

CAPs that are matched to the DAS lead to greater improvement than unmatched ones (Sevenster et al., 2013).

Mechanistic studies follow because they require a stable, manualized protocol. We can test whether improvement after TPT is associated with changes consistent with memory reconsolidation using psychophysiological and neuroimaging studies, and whether reduced vividness and time-stamping predict lower physiological reactivity and lasting improvement.

However, reliable human markers of memory destabilisation and reconsolidation are still difficult to establish (Milton et al., 2023; Phelps & Hofmann, 2019). These studies would test the central proposal of TPT that long-lasting change depends on calibrated, precision-weighted prediction error during the reconsolidation window, and whether existing learning processes can account for the same changes.

5. Conclusion

This paper starts with two issues in trauma treatment: the re-emergence of symptoms after extinction-based approaches (Vervliet et al., 2013), and the lack of established conditions for reliable memory updating in reconsolidation research (Phelps & Hofmann, 2019). Trauma Processing Therapy (TPT) is a predictive-processing-informed protocol conceptualising trauma as a hyperprecise prior and therapeutic change as calibrated, precision-weighted prediction error while the memory is labile. It does this in a closed loop of identifying the dominant affective state, evoking a corrective prediction, activating both together, and evaluating change against the original memory. TPT employs existing therapeutic procedures and arranges them around this loop, with explicit criteria for when to continue and when to stop. Its efficacy and mechanisms of action have not yet been determined.

The next step is early-phase research on the manual, therapist training, feasibility and acceptability to clients and therapists. Only then can controlled trials determine whether TPT effects are lasting and mechanistic studies determine whether those effects reflect memory updating or processes already explained by existing learning accounts. Until then TPT remains a clinical proposition and its value depends upon this evidence.
