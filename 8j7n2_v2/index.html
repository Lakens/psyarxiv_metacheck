Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Attitudes and behaviors on complex societal challenges are driven by multiple psychological processes, yet interventions often target just one process at a time. Three preregistered randomized experiments (N = 12,823 U.S. adults) examined whether combining distinct psychological interventions into ensembles improves effectiveness. In a megastudy, strategically constructed ensembles outperformed their constituent single-mechanism interventions on policy support. The leading ensemble-combining psychological distance reduction, future generations thinking, and perceived governmental outcome efficacy-yielded the largest point estimates: 41.5% greater climate-policy support and 43.0% greater donations than an informed control. This ensemble, spanning individual, social and governmental considerations, also outperformed a single-mechanism intervention matched in length. A nine-component "kitchen-sink" ensemble offered no additional benefit over smaller ensembles. An ensemble also reduced partisan animosity by 10.0% and support for undemocratic practices by 31.9% relative to control. Strategically combining psychological interventions can outperform single mechanisms, whereas indiscriminate bundling of more intervention content does not yield additional benefits.

1 Introduction

Key societal challenges require broad public action, whether in the form of championing climate policies, protecting democratic institutions in the face of rising division, rapidly changing behavior during health crises, or navigating the adoption of emerging technologies. In response, behavioral science has increasingly tested interventions at scale, often through megastudy approaches aimed at identifying interventions that are most effective in driving behavior change 1 . Yet, the results have frequently been sobering. Even widely studied and seemingly promising interventions often produce effects that are small, inconsistent, or insufficient for the rate and extent of change required. In one global megastudy, none of the expert-designed climate interventions increased pro-environmental behavior 2 . A megastudy of the ten most-cited climate message interventions similarly found that no intervention could reliably increase pro-environmental charitable donations 3 . Meta-analytic evidence likewise suggests that the effects of behavioral interventions (e.g., labeling, framing, social norm messaging) in driving sustainable food consumption 4 and the effect of social norm interventions in driving health-related behaviors 5 may be modest and may disappear once publication bias is taken into account. These findings raise a pressing question for behavioral science: is identifying the single best intervention the optimal strategy to drive meaningful behavior change? Few, if any, consequential behaviors are determined by a single psychological process and the processes that shape these behaviors vary across different people 6 . Thus no single intervention is likely to provide a path towards behavior change for all individuals. Support for public policies, collective action, and costly prosocial behavior, for example, rarely depend on a single belief or motive, but are shaped by multiple determinants operating together, including knowledge of effectiveness, fairness, social norms, emotional engagement, and agency 7, 8, 9 . Just as each of these processes can facilitate behavior change, each can also act as a barrier, constraining action even when other enabling factors are present 10 . Moreover, the psychological levers that matter are not the same for everyone, reflecting treatment effect heterogeneity. The same intervention may resonate with some groups while leaving others unmoved. For example, learning about the scientific consensus increases climate action among liberals, but does not motivate conservatives 11 , and in health domains, intervention effects often vary across populations, with some strategies proving more effective for particular groups, such as at-risk individuals 9, 12 . Similarly, individuals with different cultural worldviews face different psychological barriers to energy conservation 13 , and ethnic groups differ in the extent to which they perceive various societal challenges such as health inequities, poverty, and environmental issues to be inter-connected 14 . Yet behavioral intervention research has largely proceeded by isolating these determinants and testing interventions that target one mechanism at a time. Given the multiplicity and diversity of the psychological processes that underlie action on societal challenges, such interventions may fail to address all of the barriers constraining a given individual and may appeal only to select segments of the population. Thus, although single-mechanism interventions have generated important insights into the processes that underpin behavior, they leave unresolved a central challenge: how should effective interventions be designed when action is shaped by multiple, diverse psychological processes and corresponding barriers at once? A promising way forward may lie in strategically combining effective interventions rather than continuing to evaluate them in isolation 15 . These resulting ensembles of interventions may be able to activate multiple psychological processes at once, allowing them to address several barriers simultaneously within individuals while also appealing to a broader range of individuals who resonate with different aspects of the issue. Other disciplines like medicine, public health and community-based social marketing have long recognized the value of combining approaches to overcome the limits of any single strategy 16, 17, 18 . Recent simulations, such as those in misinformation and public health likewise suggest that combinations of structural and motivational tools may prove particularly effective 19, 20, 21.

In contrast, behavioral intervention research has more commonly compared isolated interventions to identify the single most effective mechanism 22 . Beyond the field's theoretically-motivated desire for greater mechanistic clarity, concerns about combining interventions may reflect longstanding psychological theories about how people process, respond to, and resist influence attempts. First, combinations of interventions may overtax recipients' cognitive and attentional capacity 23 . Cognitive load theory, for example, holds that working memory is sharply limited in its ability to process novel information simultaneously 24 . Exposing recipients to multiple distinct persuasive mechanisms may therefore saturate these finite resources, causing each component to be processed less deeply. Interventions that engage multiple psychological processes may also dampen motivation by inadvertently activating multiple and potentially competing goals, which, according to goal systems theory, can weaken commitment to each 25 . Another class of apprehensions about combining interventions stems from classic concerns in persuasion research. More overt and multifaceted influence attempts can heighten perceptions of threat to autonomy, thereby causing reactance 26 . And even in the absence of motivated resistance to influence, adding weaker arguments may dilute the impact of a stronger message 27 . From this perspective, behavioral science's emphasis on identifying the single best intervention may reflect a theoretically grounded expectation that combining interventions is more likely to yield diminishing returns than stronger effects. Yet empirical work points to an alternative possibility: combinations of interventions can outperform single approaches when they target distinct constraints in complementary ways. Indeed, factorial designs have long been used in behavioral science to test both the individual and interactive effects of two intervention components 28.

For example, in exercise interventions, pairing motivational content with implementation intentions produced substantially larger effects than either component alone 29 . Similarly, interventions combining growth-mindset and stress-can-be-enhancing messages improved adolescents' stress responses, well-being, and academic outcomes more than interventions targeting either mindset alone 30 . In a recent megastudy, the intervention most effective at driving climate advocacy-encompassing political, financial, and lifestyle behaviors-combined efficacy and emotion pathways 31 . And recent work on social influence finds that combining scientific and social consensus signals is more effective in motivating action across environmental, health, and technology issues than communicating either signal alone 32 . Although factorial designs are valuable for estimating component effects and interactions, as the number of candidate components increases, the number of conditions required for a fully crossed design grows rapidly, creating practical and statistical constraints 28 . Consequently, factorial studies often focus on a relatively small number of components (mostly two), leaving open questions about how larger intervention ensembles perform.

Behavioral science still lacks an understanding of how multi-component interventions should be designed and evaluated. Existing studies often test a small number of interventions (usually two components) in factorial designs or they test intervention "packages" as indivisible wholes, making it difficult to determine why they are effective and whether they are reliably more effective than each of their constituents 33 . As a result, the logic of combination remains underspecified. It is still unclear how interventions should be combined from a large repertoire when exhaustive testing of each combination is impractical. Namely, whether interventions should be combined on the basis of practical evidence about which individual components perform best, theoretical accounts of the key mechanisms that ought to be targeted, broader multi-level perspectives on behavior change, or some combination of these approaches. The field also has limited guidance on dosage: how many components should be combined to maximize impact without overloading recipients cognitively or making interventions too costly and difficult to deliver in practice. Thus the principles that should govern which components to combine, and how many, remain largely unresolved.

In the present research, we advance an ensemble-based approach to behavioral intervention design. We define an intervention ensemble as a deliberate combination of theory-grounded psychological modules delivered together while remaining conceptually distinct. Rather than treating ensembles as ad hoc bundles of interventions, we test three explicit design principles for creating them (see Figure 1). First, outcome-optimized ensembles combine the interventions that have been shown to perform best individually on the target outcome. This reflects a pragmatic logic: build upward from what already works. Second, mechanism-coverage ensembles combine interventions chosen to collectively target the theorized psychological determinants of the outcome, even when some components are modest in isolation. This logic prioritizes causal arguments over standalone effect size. Third, multi-level ensembles combine psychological interventions whose focal content concerns processes operating at different levels of influence: individual-level considerations such as one's own experiences and consequences; collective-level considerations such as what is normative in one's social group; and system-level considerations such as governmental capacity to enact solutions. This logic is motivated by theories suggesting that durable collective action is often simultaneously influenced by perceptions of personal, social, and system-level factors 34, 35, 36, 37 . We use "multi-level" to refer to the level of the process represented in the intervention contentthe referent of the targeted belief or perception-rather than the level at which the intervention itself is delivered. Indeed, all modules in the present studies are communications delivered to individual participants. Across the three design principles, the interventions we combine in different ways were each shown to increase support for a personally costly climate policy-congestion pricing-relative to a control condition providing baseline information about the policy in a recent megastudy (see Methods Table 1). Study 1 evaluates these three design principles in the context of costly climate action, testing whether intervention ensembles can increase both support for a personally costly climate policy and real, incentivized donation behavior to a climate advocacy organization. We focus on climate actions that have been particularly difficult to motivate in prior studies because they require individuals to accept immediate personal costs in service of broader, longer-term collective benefits 38 . In our study, the focal policy was a federal carbon fee: a policy that places a price on carbon-intensive fossil fuels and other sources of carbon emissions and is widely regarded as capable of reducing emissions 39 , but that is also likely to raise consumer energy costs-a feature that substantially limits public support 40 . The domain of costly climate action provides a demanding test for intervention ensembles because support is constrained by multiple barriers at once, including beliefs about effectiveness, fairness, motivation, and willingness to bear personal sacrifice 10 . Moreover, climate-related beliefs and support for climate policy are strongly divided along partisan lines in the United States. Climate skepticism and resistance to climate policy are particularly associated with Republican political identity 41 . The same communication frame can also appeal to one political faction while backfiring for the other 42 . This partisan context provides a stringent setting in which to test intervention ensembles: if combining multiple persuasive appeals heightens resistance or reactance, such effects should be especially likely to emerge in this context, where messages are often resisted by key political constituencies and filtered through partisan identity 43, 44.

Study 2 tests whether leveraging multiple mechanisms in an ensemble approach is more effective than more extensively developing a single-mechanism intervention. We compare the best-performing ensemble from Study 1 with an extended version of the strongest single-mechanism intervention, providing more in-depth information and argumentation about the same construct. Both interventions were matched on word count and reading ease. Study 3 provides a cross-domain test of whether the optimal ensemble-construction approach identified in Study 1 can be used to strengthen intervention effects beyond the climate context. We turn to a distinct societal challenge: antidemocratic attitudes. We use a second, published megastudy-a large-scale test of interventions designed to reduce partisan animosity and antidemocratic attitudes among U.S. partisans 45 -as a new intervention repertoire from which to construct an ensemble. We combine component interventions that were each among the top performers in reducing anti-democratic attitudes in this megastudy, and test the effectiveness of this resultant ensemble in reducing partisan animosity, defined as hostility toward members of the opposing political party, and support for undemocratic practices. In doing so, Study 3 evaluates whether ensemble-based intervention design can produce value across domains, intervention sets, and outcomes. As such, across three studies and a range of quantitative analyses and analyses of open-ended reasoning, we ask three design-oriented questions. Do intervention ensembles outperform single-mechanism interventions in shifting policy support for a federal carbon fee and incentivized donation behavior in support of that policy? Do different principles of construction yield different levels of effectiveness? And does the effectiveness of ensembles transcend the climate domain to other societal domains as well as outcomes? By answering these questions, we aim to shift behavioral intervention science from the task of ranking isolated interventions toward the task of designing coordinated combinations of interventions, equipped to effectively drive consequential behavior on complex societal challenges.

4 Study 3 Results

Study 3 utilized the multi-level ensemble-construction approach from Study 1 in a new intervention repertoire and societal domain. Drawing on a published megastudy of interventions to reduce antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity among U.S. partisans 45 , we combined the top-performing interventions targeting individual-level considerations (Utility of Outparty Empathy), collective-level considerations (Correcting Democracy Misperceptions), and system-level considerations (Democratic Collapse Threat) into a multi-level ensemble. In a pre-registered experiment, we randomly assigned N = 2,549 U.S. adults, quota-balanced by political party affiliation (Republican or Democrat) and gender, to a no information control, one of the three component interventions, or the multi-level ensemble, which delivered all three interventions in randomized order. Our outcomes were support for undemocratic practices (the primary pre-registered outcome) and partisan animosity. Although our pre-registered analyses specified unadjusted linear models in which each outcome was predicted solely by condition, we report covariate-adjusted models in the main text to remain consistent with Voelkel et al. 45 , whose intervention conditions this study replicates and conceptually extends. As in Voelkel et al., these models adjust for gender, age, race, partisan identity, and political party. The pre-registered unadjusted models are reported in Supplement Sections 25.4.5 and 25.5. Support for undemocratic practices was measured as agreement with four antidemocratic actions on 0-100 scales. This outcome showed strong floor effects: responses were highly right-skewed and modal at the lower bound, with 13.7% of participants scoring at the minimum of the scale (Supplement 25.4.1). These distributional features compromised the appropriateness of the pre-registered linear model because the floor of the scale compressed variation among participants with very low latent support, making mean differences on the observed 0-100 scale a conservative test of intervention effects 49 . As such we conducted two complementary post-hoc analyses better suited to the observed data, while still reporting the pre-registered linear model in the supplement (Supplement 25.4.5). First, we conducted floor-effect-corrected Welch tests 49 . This approach treats values at the lower bound as potentially censored observations on an underlying latent continuum as participants at the floor may differ in their latent support for undemocratic practices, but the observed scale cannot distinguish values at or below zero. Second, we conducted dichotomized threshold analyses to capture substantively meaningful shifts in whether participants expressed any support for undemocratic practices.

In these analyses, the multi-level ensemble showed the most consistent reduction in support for undemocratic practices. Floor-corrected Welch tests showed that while the collective-level, system-level and ensemble interventions each significantly lowered support for undemocratic practices, the multi-level ensemble produced the lowest point estimates of support for undemocratic practices (Complete contrasts in Supplement 25.4.2). In threshold analyses, the multi-level ensemble was the only condition that significantly reduced the likelihood of expressing any support for undemocratic practices relative to control (Complete models and contrasts in Supplement 25.4.3). Patterns of results were largely similar in models not adjusted for covariates: the floor-corrected Welch tests were unadjusted by design, and unadjusted versions of the threshold analyses similarly found that the ensemble condition was the only condition that lowered the proportion of participants expressing any support for undemocratic practices. Thus, across all specifications, the ensemble reliably reduced support for undemocratic practices relative to control. However, it did not significantly outperform all its components.

5 Discussion

Behavioral science has long sought to identify which interventions work best at motivating behaviors like exercising 50 , supporting governmental policies 22 , or risky choice preferences 51, 52 , often by pitting mechanisms and framings against one another in tournament-style comparisons in search of the single most effective lever. The present findings suggest that this approach may be incomplete for most outcomes shaped by multiple psychological processes and barriers at once-a category that includes most, if not all, efforts needed to tackle contemporary societal challenges. Across three megastudy-informed experiments, the central result was clear: strategically designed ensembles outperformed the single interventions from which they were built.

In Study 1, ensembles achieved the largest point estimates in consequential outcomes including increasing support for a personally costly climate policy and donations to climate advocacy. And across three design principles (outcome optimization, mechanism coverage and multi-level), ensembles outperformed the informed control as well as their constituent single-mechanism interventions on policy support (when this comparison was possible). Study 2 showed that the ensemble advantage cannot be attributed solely to message length. The leading ensemble from Study 1 outperformed a length-matched intervention providing a more in-depth treatment of its most effective component-governmental outcome efficacy-on both policy support and donation. Notably, governmental outcome efficacy was the strongest single-mechanism intervention in Study 1, consistent with past meta-analysis underscoring its central role in shaping policy support 8 . Study 3 further demonstrated that ensembles can be persuasive even on a contentious issue such as partisan animosity, achieving the lowest point estimates of outpartisan hostility and showing most consistent evidence for lowering support for undemocratic practices. However, simply adding more intervention content via the "kitchen sink" condition did not outperform strategically designed smaller ensembles, despite the former containing more persuasive material. The findings point to a more complex conclusion than "more is better": ensembles can be effective, but their benefits depend on the strategic combinations of components.

Together, these findings empirically counter the skepticism around the potential effectiveness of ensembles, stemming from psychological theories about reactance to persuasion 26 , bounded cognitive resources 24 and split motivation between competing goals 25 . These concerns may not apply uniformly: strategically coordinated combinations can outperform single interventions, whereas indiscriminate bundling may still produce diminishing returns. The kitchen-sink ensemble-which added modules regardless of their status as established determinants of policy support-did show diminishing returns relative to the best smaller ensembles, consistent with concerns about cognitive overload and signal dilution. Attrition points the same way. Dropout during the intervention rose steadily with its length, from 0.2% in the control to 5.4% in the nine-module condition in Study 1 and from 0.6% to 13.2% in Study 3 (Supplement 22). Participants who abandon a long intervention are plausibly those least receptive to it, so this selection should, if anything, flatter the longest conditions; yet even with that advantage, and even when dropouts were assigned outcomes far worse than their condition's mean, the five-and nine-module bundles did not outperform the three-module ensembles, and their deficit only grew under such corrections. Larger bundles thus demanded more attention and lost more of their audience without returning more persuasion. As such, the key differentiator of the success of ensembles appears to be whether their combination is designed to address complementary barriers without redundancy or incoherence. This distinction also matters for scalability. Because large-scale interventions are time-and resource-intensive, and real-world audiences may devote far less attention than paid study participants, identifying efficient combinations is crucial.

Exploratory analyses further clarify the nature and practical value of these ensemble gains. Across four ensembles for which each constituent component was also tested individually, ensembles generally achieved larger gains than the average of their single-mechanism components and the strongest observed single-mechanism component across outcomes, although not all of these differences were statistically significant. However, ensemble effects were consistently sub-additive such that the effect of each ensemble fell below the sum of its constituents' standalone effects relative to control (Supplement 20; Figure S32). Some degree of sub-additivity may be expected because the constituent single-mechanism interventions were all designed to advance the same downstream outcome and therefore likely overlap in the considerations they activate and the variance they explain. Once one mechanism has shifted support, there may be less remaining scope for another mechanism to produce its full standalone effect; indeed, for the larger ensembles, simply summing the constituent effects produced predictions that exceeded the upper bound of the outcome scale. The practical value of ensembles may therefore lie less in effects that exceed the sum of their parts and more in their potential robustness across outcomes and recipients. The strongest single mechanism varied considerably across outcomes: Governmental Outcome Efficacy produced the largest single-mechanism effect on policy support, Psychological Distance on incentivized donation, and Binding Morals on willingness to pay. By contrast, the same three-component outcome-optimized ensemble-Governmental Outcome Efficacy + Psychological Distance + Future Generations-produced the largest point estimates across all three outcomes. A similar logic may apply across individuals. Decades of persuasion research show that the effectiveness of different kinds of appeals depends on recipient characteristics; for example, people higher in need for cognition are more responsive to strong, elaboration-requiring arguments, whereas those lower in need for cognition tend to rely more heavily on simpler or peripheral cues 53, 54 . An ensemble combining evidence-based, socially grounded, and more affective or value-based routes may therefore increase the likelihood that at least one component addresses the considerations most important to a given recipient. As such, ensembles present a practical advantage for intervention designers who often cannot know in advance which outcome will ultimately matter most or which psychological route will be most persuasive for a heterogeneous audience. This work prompts key future directions for theoretically-grounded inquiry. In Study 1, across design principles, the ensembles that produced the largest point estimates also featured content pertaining to individual-level (e.g., Psychological Distance), collective-level (e.g., Considerations about future generations or prevalent social norms), and system-level (e.g., the government's effectiveness) processes. The shared multi-level composition of the leading ensembles is consistent with socio-ecological perspectives that emphasize the joint relevance of individual, collective, and systemic influences on societal challenges 34, 35, 36 . Our findings suggest that combining messages targeting psychological considerations associated with these different levels of influence may be a promising approach to ensemble construction. Future research can directly test whether combining content across individual, collective, and systemic processes confers an additional advantage over ensembles that combine the same number of mechanisms within fewer levels. More broadly, our findings speak to discourse that contrasts individual-focused (i-frame) and system-focused (s-frame) approaches to societal challenges 55 . Rather than treating mechanisms at these levels as mutually exclusive, our results demonstrate that communications can jointly engage personal, social, and systemic considerations within an effective intervention ensemble. Importantly, our system-focused modules are not themselves s-frame interventions because they do not alter institutional rules or structures. Instead, they communicate information about systemic processes to individuals.

Why might combining messages across different influences outperform single-mechanism approaches? One possibility is that complementary messages make different considerations salient, allowing people to connect a decision to the multiple goals and values they already hold. Prior research has shown, for example, that pro-environmental choices can increase when vehicle fuel efficiency is presented in multiple ways-miles per gallon, annual fuel costs, and greenhouse gas ratings. Although these measures describe the same underlying attribute, each highlights its relevance to different concerns, such as saving money or reducing emissions 56 . Similarly, by leveraging different mechanisms, for example communicating about personal, social, and institutional dimensions of a policy, ensembles may make different motivations for supporting it salient, rather than simply providing more arguments. Although our exploratory analyses of post-decision explanations cannot establish this process, future research could directly test whether activating complementary considerations helps explain the incremental effectiveness of ensembles.

Across our studies, we examined effects on a range of outcomes, including policy attitudes, stated willingness to pay, incentivized donation decisions, partisan animosity, and attitudinal endorsement of undemocratic practices. These outcomes should not all be treated interchangeably with behavior or collective action, but they are consequential in different ways. Climate policies like a federal carbon fee cannot deliver their intended environmental benefits unless they are first enacted and then sustained in the face of potential public opposition.

Yet public acceptance of policies that impose immediate personal costs in exchange for longer-term collective benefits is often difficult to secure, reflecting the social dilemmas central to collective action challenges such as climate change 57 . For example, a 2025 national poll found that only about three in ten Americans were willing to pay an additional $10 or $20 per month on their energy bills as part of a carbon fee 40 . For policymakers considering such a policy, the level and distribution of public support can shape perceptions of its political feasibility and ultimately decisions on whether to enact it 58, 59, 60 . Thus, shifting public support for policies is a meaningful target for interventions. Partisan animosity is likewise consequential as research links hostility toward political outgroups to opposition to bipartisan cooperation, strained social relationships and discrimination beyond explicitly political settings 45, 61 . Our incentivized donation measures, meanwhile, provide evidence of financially consequential allocation decisions, with Study 2 enacting every participant's decision.

The incremental benefits of ensembles varied across these outcomes. In Study 1, the three-component ensemble combining Governmental Outcome Efficacy, Psychological Distance, and Future Generations Thinking produced the largest observed gains in both policy support and donations. However, while it significantly outperformed the strongest single-mechanism intervention for policy support (Governmental Outcome Efficacy), its advantage over the strongest component for donations (Psychological Distance) was not statistically significant.

In Study 2, the ensemble significantly outperformed both control and a length-matched, extended Governmental Outcome Efficacy intervention on both outcomes. These findings raise the possibility that the incremental benefits of ensembles may depend on the nature of the outcome being targeted. For instance, ensembles addressing individual, social, and institutional considerations may offer particular advantages for decisions concerning policies whose implementation requires coordination across multiple actors, relative to more direct individual decisions such as allocating funds. However, the present studies do not directly test this explanation. Future research should systematically examine whether and how outcome characteristics moderate the incremental effectiveness of ensembles over single-mechanism interventions.

All nine intervention modules in Study 1 were adapted from a single prior megastudy on congestion pricing, and were selected because that repertoire uniquely met the requirements of our design principles and included a vast range of mechanisms that other studies find to be consequential for climate policy support and attitudes 8, 2, 31 . The module's relative effectiveness was consistent across the two policy domains, as evidenced by the broadly consistent rank ordering of single-mechanism effects across the two policies. However, the present studies cannot establish how far these particular modules generalize to climate policies with different cost structures or affected populations. Study 3 addressed the dependence on one source by drawing on an independently developed repertoire in a different domain. Another limitation of this work is that all outcomes were measured immediately after intervention exposure.

We therefore cannot establish whether ensemble effects persist over time, translate into repeated behavior outside the survey context, or ultimately influence policy adoption and implementation. Future research should examine these questions longitudinally and in field settings, including whether experimentally induced changes in policy support predict subsequent advocacy or other consequential actions. Future research can also further test the psychological mechanisms explaining ensemble effectiveness, in particular the question of whether ensembles outperform individual interventions by addressing multiple psychological barriers at once within individuals or due to treatment effect heterogeneity, whereby providing a diverse range of arguments increases the likelihood that at least some part of the intervention will appeal to recipients who differ in their concerns. Additionally, with more granular experiments focused on deconstructing any one ensemble, future studies can also illuminate whether ensemble advantages arise from additive or multiplicative effects and how they differ across individual differences, behavioral domains, and cultural contexts.

Despite limitations and room for future inquiry, the current work established that strategically combined interventions targeting multiple processes may offer benefits across diverse societal challenges. What does the success of intervention ensembles imply for the tradition of intervention tournaments and related comparative approaches in behavioral science? It suggests that such efforts remain essential, but incomplete on their own. Identifying which single mechanisms, framings, or interventions perform best is a necessary foundation for theory building. Yet for outcomes constrained by multiple barriers at once, the field should not stop at ranking and promoting isolated processes and their associated interventions. Instead, insights from these comparative approaches should be used to design ensembles that combine effective elements in theoretically informed ways across levels of analysis.

The question of which intervention works best to motivate specific socially important behaviors has driven a productive era of behavioral science. 6.2 Participants

Participants were recruited from Prolific using quotas designed to approximate the U.S. adult citizen population on age, sex and party affiliation (see Supplement 1 for sample characteristics and Supplement 6 for sampling quotas).

The preregistered recruitment plan targeted a total sample of N = 10,904. An a priori power analysis indicated that, with approximately 699 participants per condition, the study would provide 80% power to detect small pairwise differences between conditions (d = 0.15, ε = .05) 64 . We planned to oversample by approximately 20% to account for anticipated exclusions due to data quality checks 65 , therefore arriving at our target N = 10,904.

In practice, however, recruitment on Prolific slowed substantially after approximately 7,000 responses. We therefore filed an update to the preregistration specifying an end date for data collection, one month after that update was recorded. Data collection was then stopped on the prespecified end date, for a total of 8,917 participants prior to exclusions.

Consistent with the preregistration, participants were excluded if they met any of three criteria: (a) they failed an attention check, defined as giving inconsistent responses across two administrations of the same item asking what day of the week it was; (b) they completed the survey in less than one-third of the median response time; or (c) they were flagged by Roundtable.ai as high risk for fraudulent or bot-driven responding, defined as a risk score greater than 70. Of the 8,917 participants recorded in the data, 8 were removed for not providing consent. Of the remaining 8,909 randomized participants, 245 were excluded for failing a data-quality check: 173 completed the survey in less than one-third of the median response time, and 163 did not provide two matching responses to the attention check, either because they exited the survey before answering both administrations of the item (135) or because their two answers disagreed (28); 91 participants met more than one of these criteria. A further 176 were excluded for exceeding the bot-detection risk threshold, yielding a final analytic sample of N = 8,488 (median completion time = 12.7 minutes).

Post-assignment attrition, defined as leaving the survey before answering all four primary outcomes, was 3.3% overall and increased with intervention length, from 1.8% in the informed control to 6.9% in the nine-module ensemble; it was concentrated during the intervention itself (odds ratio per additional minute of intervention material = 1.26, 95% CI [1.18, 1.33]). Attrition in the two best-performing three-module ensembles (3.7% and 2.9%) was roughly half that of the nine-module condition. A sensitivity analysis assigning dropouts outcomes 20% worse than their condition mean left the three-module ensemble's advantage over control essentially unchanged on all four outcomes (policy support: 20.1 to 19.7 points), and it remained 18.3 points when dropouts were assigned the scale minimum; the nine-module condition's advantage shrank more under every such correction (Supplement 22).

Power analyses indicated that this final analytic sample still afforded high statistical power. Specifically, N = 8,488 provided a power of over 95% to detect a small omnibus effect in a one-way ANOVA (partial ϑ2 = .02, ε = .05), and 95% power to detect small pairwise differences between conditions (d = 0.20, ε = .05) 64.

Because all reported pairwise contrasts were Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) corrected within each research question's contrast family (6 contrasts for RQ1, 10 for RQ3, 12 for RQ5, 28 for RQ4, and 45 for RQ2; Supplement 8), these conventional estimates overstate the power available for any individual contrast. We therefore conducted a simulation-based sensitivity analysis that incorporates the correction. This test simulated contrast estimates from observed condition means and cell sizes (n ↔ 653 per condition; see Supplement 21). The rejection threshold of the BH procedure is adaptive, depending on how many condition means differ within a family, so power is reduced only modestly when many contrasts reflect genuine effects, as was the case here. Across 40,000 simulations, power to detect a pairwise difference of d = 0.20 under BH correction was 0.94 to 0.95 across the five contrast families under the observed configuration of condition means, compared with 0.95 uncorrected. We also tested the most conservative configuration, in which all conditions other than the focal pair share the control mean. In this extreme case, simulated power ranged from 0.78 to 0.90 across contrast families. The study was thus well powered for differences of d ↓ 0.20, which characterize our primary comparisons. 6.6 Secondary Measures

Secondary measures assessed theorized mediators and related constructs. All items were measured on 0-100 slider scales. Scale reliability estimates are reported in Supplement 3.

Perceived fairness. Participants reported perceptions of how fair the policy was (a) in general, (b) for themselves personally, and (c) for low-income individuals. With adequate internal consistency, these responses were going to be averaged to create a composite fairness measure.

Perceived effectiveness. Participants reported how effective they thought the policy would be in (a) combating climate change and (b) reducing carbon emissions. With adequate internal consistency, these responses were going to be averaged to create a composite effectiveness measure.

Communication characteristics and manipulation checks.

To better understand how participants experienced the interventions, we assessed message communication characteristics. Results for these measures are reported in Supplements 10. Communication characteristics included perceived credibility 75 , interest 76 , understanding, and perceived threat to freedom 77 . We did not observe many notable pairwise differences across conditions on these communication-characteristic measures overall, suggesting that ensembles remained as engaging and credible as single-mechanism designs despite containing more content and arguments. Notably, the two top-performing three-intervention ensembles, which combined individual (psychological distance), collective (social norms or future generations), and systemic (governmental outcome efficacy) components, were perceived as most believable, accurate, interesting, easy to pay attention to, and informative. By contrast, the kitchen-sink intervention ranked highest on perceived pressure. These patterns suggest that strategically combined three-intervention ensembles may be especially engaging, whereas less strategic kitchen-sink approaches risk feeling more forceful or overbearing.

6.7 LLM-Assisted Coding of Open-Ended Responses

We coded all 8,289 non-empty responses using an LLM-assisted content-coding protocol. The protocol follows emerging practice for LLM-assisted content analysis, in which a language model applies a human-authored codebook and is validated against human coders on held-out data 78, 79 , and adds several safeguards: a human-derived category system, module contrasts defined from the verbatim stimulus text, a coding prompt finalized before validation, held-out validation with a two-coder adjudicated gold standard, and a reliability floor set before validation. The full protocol and all validation results are reported in Supplement 19; the coding prompt, codebook, coded outputs, validation materials, and analysis code are available with other data in the OSF project repository.

Codebook development. The 26-category codebook was derived from the data by two trained human coders, blind to condition. Both coders independently open-coded a stratified derivation sample of 130 responses, identifying 751 reason phrases. Phrases were then grouped into candidate categories by a pinned LLM, and both coders independently audited every assignment (94% final-placement agreement, zero direct conflicts); saturation analysis showed no new categories in the second half of the derivation sample. Each category carried a written definition, a stance rule distinguishing taking a position from merely mentioning a topic, and verbatim examples.

Two low-base-rate categories required by the planned module tests were added on theoretical grounds and are disclosed as such.

Coding procedure. All coding used a pinned model snapshot (gpt-5.5-2026-04-23) with structured JSON output. Every response was coded in three independent passes, and a category was assigned only when at least two passes agreed. The coding prompt embeds the complete codebook and was refined in three documented iterations against a 60-response development set, then finalized before validation and used unchanged thereafter.

In the full deployment, all three passes agreed exactly for 72% of responses, and independent duplicate runs of the validation sample yielded identical category sets for 85% of responses.

Validation. The finalized LLM coder was evaluated on a held-out test set of 150 responses, disjoint from all development data, coded independently by two trained human coders whose 162 disagreements were resolved in a joint adjudication session blind to model output. Against this gold standard, the model achieved 97.2% agreement with a median category Cohen's ω of .82; 23 of 26 categories met the reliability floor of ω ↓ .60 that we specified before validation began. Human-model agreement was comparable to or higher than between-human agreement (median ω = .66 before adjudication). The three below-floor categories were excluded from all analyses; one of them (policy_will_work) removes a directional contrast from the Outcome Efficacy module test, as reported in the Results.

Analysis approach.

All open-ended analyses are exploratory. The themes and mechanisms coded are not mediators explaining intervention effects. This is because explanations were elicited after the support rating. We report the number of distinct reasons provided across the 13 experimental conditions. We used Poisson regression to test whether the number of reasons varied with the number of intervention modules received. We also examined differences in the number of reasons between each intervention condition and Control.

To examine the valence of expressed reasoning, we used two complementary measures. We first calculated the proportion of participants in each condition who provided at least one pro-policy reason and the proportion who provided at least one anti-policy reason, comparing the Control, three-module ensemble, and nine-module ensemble conditions using Fisher's exact tests. We then assessed the overall balance of pro-and anti-policy reasons within each response using a valence ratio, calculated as (N pro ↑ N anti )/(N pro + N anti ), where N pro and N anti represent the number of distinct pro-and anti-policy categories identified in each response. This ratio ranges from ↑1 (entirely anti-policy) to +1 (entirely pro-policy). Responses containing neither pro-nor anti-policy reasons were excluded from this analysis, as the ratio is undefined for these responses. We compared mean valence ratios across the Control, three-module ensemble, and nine-module ensemble conditions using OLS regression, applying Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the three pairwise comparisons.

We also conducted additional supplementary analyses including intervention-content correspondence tests, lexical-echo sensitivity checks, and coding reproducibility checks (see Supplement 19).

6.4 Procedure

After providing informed consent, all participants first read baseline information about the policy, its stated goals, and its likely consumer costs, which served as the informed control. Participants were then randomly assigned to either the informed-control condition or to one of 12 intervention conditions. Those in the intervention conditions then read their assigned materials at their own pace. All intervention texts were presented in a standardized visual format and page structure.

Following exposure, participants completed the primary outcome measures, followed by secondary measures and demographic items.

6.5 Outcome Measures

Primary Outcomes This study included four primary dependent variables. Support for a carbon fee (continuous). Participants indicated their level of support for a federal carbon fee using a continuous scale ranging from 0 ("Strongly oppose") to 100 ("Strongly support"). The question specified that, if passed, the policy would increase the average household's monthly energy costs (including electricity, heating gas, and gasoline or diesel) by $20.

Willingness to pay for a carbon fee (continuous). Participants reported the maximum amount their household would be willing to pay each month in additional energy costs for a federal carbon fee to combat climate change. Responses were provided on a continuous scale ranging from $0 to $100.

Support for policy implementation (binary).

Participants indicated whether they supported the actual implementation of a federal carbon fee to combat climate change (Yes/No). The question again specified that, if passed, the policy would increase the average household's monthly energy costs by $20.

Incentivized donation behavior (continuous).

Participants completed an incentivized allocation task in which they allocated a $10 bonus between themselves and a nonpartisan nonprofit organization that advocates for the passage of a federal carbon fee to reduce carbon emissions, Citizens' Climate Lobby. The dependent variable was the dollar amount (from $0 to $10) donated to the nonprofit. Participants were informed that 100 participants would be randomly selected and that their allocation decisions would be implemented as stated. In total, this task led to the research team making a donation of $414.60 to the Citizens' Climate Lobby on behalf of 100 randomly selected participants and disseminating $585.40 in bonuses to those participants via Prolific. 8.4 Outcome Measures

Partisan animosity. Partisan animosity was measured with a two-item composite: thermometers toward outpartisan voters and politicians each on a scale of 0 (Very cold or unfavorable feeling) to 100 (Very warm or favorable feeling). The items were reverse-coded so that higher values indicate colder feelings. And given their high spearman-brown reliability (0.89), they were composited into a partisan animosity score. Support for undemocratic practices. Participants indicated their agreement with four items, each measured on a 0 (Strongly disagree) to 100 (Strongly agree) scale, with higher scores indicating greater support for undemocratic practices: (1) "[Inparty members] should reduce the number of polling stations in areas that support [outparty members]"; (2) "[Inparty] governors should ignore unfavorable court rulings by [outparty]-appointed judges"; (3) "[Inparty] governors should prosecute journalists who accuse [inparty] politicians of misconduct without revealing sources"; and (4) "[Inparty members] should not accept the results of elections if they lose." Items were averaged into a composite.

As additional exploratory measures, we measured participants' support for seven policy positions (e.g., increasing environmental regulations, reducing access to abortion; See Supplement 25.7) and gathered socio-demographic information.

These patterns nevertheless highlight an important trade-off between intervention effectiveness and the resources required to field larger interventions. In Study 1, median time on intervention material ranged from 1.4 minutes for the shortest single-mechanism condition to 9.1 minutes for the nine-module ensemble. The practical design question is therefore not simply whether ensembles outperform single interventions, but when the incremental benefit of adding mechanisms justifies the additional time required to deliver them. Three-component ensembles may offer a promising middle ground: at 4.3-4.6 minutes of reading time, they produced gains from combining complementary mechanisms while avoiding the considerably greater duration of the 5-and 9-intervention ensembles. Nonetheless, duration differences between ensembles and single-mechanism designs are likely to matter even more in field settings, where audiences are not paid to attend. Field experiments comparing the uptake, completion, and effectiveness of ensembles against shorter single-mechanism interventions are therefore an important next step. Encouragingly, Study 2 shows that an ensemble still outperforms its single-mechanism constituent even when the two interventions are matched on length and duration. Therefore, it is possible that communications addressing societal challenges, such as climate change or inter-group conflict, could be made more effective within the same time and attention demands by leveraging multiple psychological mechanisms rather than focusing primarily on one.

extended the applicability of ensembles to independently developed interventions addressing a different societal challenge, the origins of these interventions may have limited the additional benefits of combining them. Whereas we developed the single-mechanism components in Studies 1 and 2 to minimize mechanistic and content overlap, Study 3 used interventions developed for a competitive tournament in which intervention developers were incentivized to prioritize intervention effectiveness. Consequently, interventions targeting one mechanism sometimes also incorporated appeals relevant to another. For example, the System-level intervention (Democratic collapse threat) encouraged understanding and constructive engagement with political opponents, overlapping with the target of the Individual-level intervention (Utility of outparty empathy). Similarly, the Collective-level intervention (Correcting democracy misperceptions) could reinforce perceptions of shared democratic commitments, overlapping with the System-level intervention's common-values appeal. Such overlap may have reduced the incremental benefits of combining interventions into an ensemble, as individual interventions may have already addressed aspects of other components' intended mechanisms.
