Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Young people with chronic pain are routinely asked to carry out multicomponent selfmanagement plans with limited day-to-day support. ADHD traits are common, and often unrecognised, among young people attending paediatric pain services, yet little is known about how an ADHD profile shapes the way they act on these plans. This conceptual review argues that some apparent non-adherence in this group reflects a knowing-to-doing gap: a mismatch between the demands of treatment plans and the executive and motivational profile associated with ADHD, rather than a lack of knowledge or concern. Drawing on ADHD, prospective memory, paediatric chronic illness, and health-behaviour research, we identify where this mismatch is most likely to occur: remembering to act at the right time, starting without a prompt, monitoring progress, and resuming after interruption. As direct evidence is lacking, we set out testable propositions: executive differences predict enactment beyond knowledge and intention; the gap widens when plans require independent organisation and narrows with external structure; and inconsistent enactment contributes to later pain-related disability.

Clinically, services should identify where enactment breaks down before attributing it to low effort, and adapt plans through simpler actions, routines, prompts, and coordinated homeschool-clinic support.

1. Introduction

Chronic pain, defined in the ICD-11 classification as pain that persists or recurs for longer than three months (Treede et al., 2019), affects approximately one in five children and adolescents (Chambers et al., 2024). Beyond the experience of pain itself, chronic pain can disrupt sleep, schooling, and social participation, and affected youth experience substantially elevated rates of anxiety and depression compared with their pain-free peers (Dudeney et al., 2024). Chronic pain management extends beyond medical treatment alone, and young people are often expected to participate actively in its day-to-day management. This may involve taking medication as prescribed, pacing physical activity, and practising relaxation and coping strategies. Successful self-management therefore depends not only on receiving appropriate information and treatment recommendations but also on translating these recommendations into consistent self-management behaviours in everyday life (Caes et al., 2021).

These demands are especially relevant to young people with ADHD, because ADHD and chronic pain frequently co-occur. A scoping review reported elevated rates of ADHD among youth with chronic pain and elevated rates of chronic pain among youth with ADHD (Battison et al., 2023), and a lifespan scoping review found that greater ADHD symptom burden was associated with greater pain severity and pain-related impairment (Kasahara et al., 2026).

Population studies point in the same direction: in a US national survey of 84,117 children, parent-reported chronic pain was about twice as common among children with ADHD as among those without (11.8% vs 5.5%; Chou et al., 2026), and in a Swedish birth cohort, 10-11-year-olds screening positive for combined-type ADHD symptoms more often reported weekly pain (52.5% vs 36.2%; Berggren et al., 2024). Neurodivergent young people also appear to be over-represented, and often unrecognised, in specialist pain services. Among 146 young people referred to a tertiary paediatric pain clinic, 19.9% had parent-rated ADHD symptoms and 13.7% had autistic traits in the clinically significant range, yet only 4.8% had a previous diagnosis of either condition (Wiwe Lipsker et al., 2018). These figures were based on screening questionnaires rather than diagnostic assessment. Longitudinal evidence further suggests that ADHD symptoms may precede poorer pain outcomes: in young adults prescribed opioids for acute pain, greater baseline ADHD symptoms predicted greater pain interference six months later (Brown et al., 2025), although ADHD was assessed by brief self-report and attrition was substantial.

Carrying out pain self-management behaviours places demands on executive functioning, a family of top-down cognitive processes that support goal-directed behaviour, of which working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility are considered core components (Diamond, 2013). In pain management, working memory may be needed to remember to take medication and follow treatment instructions, whereas inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility are likely to support the use of non-pharmacological coping strategies, such as relaxation and shifting attention away from pain (Caes et al., 2021). These executive capacities continue to mature across adolescence and may therefore influence how consistently young people engage with pain-management recommendations (Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023). This link is formalised in the Cyclical model Of Pain, Executive function, emotion regulation and Self-management (COPES; Caes et al., 2021), which proposes that executive and emotionregulation difficulties reduce adolescents' capacity for pain self-management, and that persistent pain in turn further constrains these capacities. Available evidence suggests that youth with chronic pain may experience difficulties across several executive domains, although findings have been inconsistent across studies using everyday behavioural ratings and performance-based neuropsychological measures (Beckmann & Jastrowski Mano, 2021).

More broadly, health-behaviour research demonstrates that positive intentions do not reliably translate into action, a discrepancy commonly described as the intention-behaviour gap (Conner & Norman, 2022). From this perspective, pain self-management may be understood not simply as a matter of knowing what to do, but also as an executive challenge involving the initiation, organisation, monitoring, and sustained implementation of recommended behaviours over time.

ADHD is particularly relevant to this executive challenge. It is characterised by persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that affect many areas of daily life (Faraone et al., 2021) and is frequently associated with executive-functioning difficulties, although their extent and pattern vary and they are not present in all young people with ADHD (Townes et al., 2023; Wilens et al., 2024).

Evidence that the gap between intending and acting may be wider in ADHD comes mainly from research on prospective memory and everyday executive functioning. Adults with ADHD did not differ from controls on laboratory prospective-memory tasks but carried out fewer of their own everyday intentions (Altgassen et al., 2019), and a meta-analysis of 36 studies of young people found that differences between those with and without ADHD were considerably larger on ratings of everyday behaviour than on performance-based tests (Ceruti et al., 2024).

In Altgassen et al.'s study, participants with ADHD most often attributed uncompleted intentions to not feeling like doing them, suggesting that motivational as well as executive processes contribute. The gap also appears sensitive to how action is structured: if-then plans linking a goal to a specific cue improve goal attainment (Sheeran et al., 2025) and tended to be more effective in samples of children with ADHD, although most of these studies used laboratory tasks (Breitwieser & Reinelt, 2026). To our knowledge, no study has yet directly compared this gap for health behaviours in young people with and without ADHD.

In the context of pain care, these difficulties could make it harder to remember multistep instructions, initiate routines without prompting, adjust strategies when symptoms change, and maintain treatment behaviours across settings. Evidence from autistic adults suggests that similar difficulties may also arise in interactions with health services. In a survey of 507 autistic and 157 non-autistic adults, autistic respondents more often reported difficulty booking appointments by telephone, communicating with their doctor, and feeling understood. Many also reported difficulty making appointments in advance, prioritising health concerns, and remembering appointments. These barriers were associated with self-reported adverse outcomes, including late presentation and untreated conditions (Doherty et al., 2022). That study involved a self-selected online sample of autistic adults rather than young people with ADHD. However, executive difficulties in daily life are shared across these groups (Ceruti et al., 2024), and ADHD symptoms and autistic traits frequently co-occur among young people attending pain services (Wiwe Lipsker et al., 2018). A neurodiversity perspective therefore cautions against locating the problem solely within the young person. The "double empathy problem" describes communication breakdowns as arising between people who perceive and communicate differently, rather than from a deficit in one party (Milton, 2012). By analogy, the gap between knowing and doing may be better understood as a mismatch between a young person's executive profile and the way services deliver recommendations: telephone booking, verbal instructions, and home practice that must be organised independently. This shifts the question from whether young people with ADHD are adherent to whether treatment plans are designed so that they can be enacted.

Evidence that ADHD and chronic pain co-occur does not, by itself, explain how ADHD may affect pain management once chronic pain is established. Existing reviews have mainly synthesised epidemiological associations, clinical phenotypes, proposed neurobiological mechanisms, and preliminary evidence on pharmacological treatment, with little attention to how ADHD-related executive differences shape day-to-day self-management, or how pain services accommodate them (Battison et al., 2023; Kasahara et al., 2026). This conceptual review addresses this omission. It examines how a mismatch between ADHD-related executive differences and the demands of treatment plans may create a "knowing-to-doing" gap in chronic pain self-management among young people, and considers the implications for clinical assessment, service design, and future research. We refer to this proposed framework as the knowing-to-doing account. To our knowledge, this is the first account to specify how this mismatch may interfere with the enactment of pain self-management recommendations in young people.

2. Approach to the Literature

This article is a conceptual rather than a systematic review. The selection and interpretation of evidence were guided by an explicit organising framework: the sequence of processes required to move from knowing about a pain-management recommendation to enacting it, namely forming an intention and then planning, remembering when to act, initiating the behaviour, monitoring it, adjusting it when circumstances change, and resuming it after interruption (Kliegel et al., 2006; Sheeran & Webb, 2016). Literature was identified through targeted, iterative searches in Google Scholar combining terms related to ADHD, executive functioning, chronic pain, self-management, treatment adherence, prospective memory, and the intentionbehaviour gap. Studies were included when they informed at least one stage of this sequence in young people with ADHD or chronic pain or, where direct evidence was lacking, in adults with ADHD, other neurodivergent groups, or young people managing other chronic conditions with comparable self-management demands. For each source, we considered the population, whether executive functioning was assessed through performance-based tests or ratings of everyday behaviour, whether the outcome concerned knowledge, intention, or actual enactment, and its methodological limitations. The aim was to develop and critically examine a testable account rather than to provide an exhaustive synthesis.

The knowing-to-doing account also rests on an explicit view of how ADHD relates to executive functioning. Taxometric and behavioural-genetic evidence suggests that ADHD traits are distributed dimensionally in the population rather than forming a discrete category (Coghill & Sonuga-Barke, 2012). However, ADHD cannot simply be equated with the lower end of an executive-functioning continuum. A meta-analysis of 83 studies found medium-sized differences in executive functioning between groups with and without ADHD, but these differences were not present in all individuals with ADHD, leading the authors to conclude that executive difficulties are neither necessary nor sufficient to cause all cases of ADHD (Willcutt et al., 2005). The dual pathway model proposes that, alongside an executive pathway, a second motivational pathway, characterised by altered reward processing and aversion to delay, contributes to ADHD (Sonuga-Barke, 2003). This account therefore treats executive differences as one important route, but not the only one, through which ADHD may affect treatment enactment. Pain self-management often requires effortful action now for benefits that are delayed and uncertain, a demand that may be particularly challenging for young people who experience strong delay aversion. Executive and motivational pathways may therefore converge on the same observable outcome: recommendations that are understood but not enacted. This is consistent with the motivational attributions reported by Altgassen et al. (2019), described above.

A further complication is that chronic pain itself affects cognition. A meta-analysis of 24 studies of adults found moderate working-memory difficulties in people with chronic pain compared with healthy controls, although all included studies were at high risk of bias (Berryman et al., 2013), and executive difficulties have also been reported in adolescents with chronic pain (Caes et al., 2022). Pain may therefore make ADHD-related difficulties more apparent, or produce similar difficulties in young people without ADHD. This account does not assume that executive difficulties observed in young people with chronic pain reflect ADHD. Instead, it treats distinguishing longstanding executive differences from pain-related ones as a central task for future research.

3. Executive Functioning as a Bridge Between Pain-Management Knowledge and Action

The knowing-to-doing account distinguishes three related but separable stages of pain selfmanagement: understanding a treatment recommendation, intending to follow it, and enacting it consistently in everyday life (Modi et al., 2012; Sheeran & Webb, 2016). The proposed gap arises when a young person possesses adequate treatment knowledge and expresses an intention to act, but has difficulty organising and sustaining the behaviours required to implement that intention. In the knowing-to-doing account, ADHD-related executive differences may affect several points in the enactment sequence (Kliegel et al., 2006; Sheeran & Webb, 2016) The proposed sequence draws partly on broader research on the enactment of health-related intentions, which shows that goal pursuit requires not only forming an intention but also recognising suitable opportunities, initiating the behaviour, and protecting it from competing demands (Sheeran & Webb, 2016). Evidence that executive functioning supports this translation is mixed: in university students, self-reported executive functioning did not independently predict physical activity or diet once intentions were considered, and its moderating role varied by behaviour (Frye & Shapiro, 2021).

Executive functioning can be assessed through performance-based tasks, which estimate specific capacities under structured conditions, or through ratings of everyday behaviour. The two correspond only modestly, and concerns remain about the validity of both (Kofler et al., 2024; Soto et al., 2020). This distinction matters for pain self-management, as a brief, structured assessment may not reflect how a young person initiates and sustains treatment behaviours in everyday life. In a small pilot study, adolescents with chronic pain performed less well than pain-free peers on tests of inhibition, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, but self-reported executive difficulties, rather than test scores, were more consistently associated with pain interference and quality of life (Caes et al., 2022). An account of pain selfmanagement should therefore not assume that either approach alone captures the knowing-todoing gap.

Evidence from other paediatric chronic conditions is mixed, but the pattern is informative.

Executive difficulties have been associated with poorer adherence to demanding regimens, such as a restrictive gluten-free diet in coeliac disease (n = 34; Sady et al., 2025) and multicomponent care in cystic fibrosis (n = 19; Borschuk et al., 2020). In the latter study, the authors proposed that treatment burden may overload executive capacities that otherwise fall within the typical range. In contrast, executive difficulties did not predict electronically monitored medication adherence over 24 months in 97 adolescents and young adults with chronic kidney disease (Eaton et al., 2020). The positive findings come from very small samples and rely largely on parent report for both executive functioning and adherence; in the coeliac study, executive ratings were unrelated to clinician-rated adherence. Together, these studies suggest that executive functioning may matter most when regimens are complex and must be organised independently. Pain-management plans, which typically combine activity pacing, relaxation practice, sleep routines, and medication, resemble these more demanding regimens rather than medication-taking alone.

Type 1 diabetes offers a close analogue to chronic-pain self-management, because both require young people to carry out several daily behaviours with increasing independence. Here, the evidence suggests that the impact of executive difficulties depends on who carries the organisational load. In interviews with six adolescents with co-occurring ADHD and type 1 diabetes, all described difficulty establishing management routines, particularly under stress.

They also reported criticism from parents and clinicians when glucose control was poor, and asked for easier access to healthcare support during difficult periods (Lindblad et al., 2017). A four-year Dutch cohort study of 174 young people with type 1 diabetes, aged 8-15 years at baseline, provides convergent quantitative evidence. Greater parent-reported executive difficulties predicted poorer glycaemic control over time, particularly when young people had taken on more responsibility for management tasks and parents less, although shared responsibility was not uniformly protective among younger children (Vloemans et al., 2019).

Neither study measured treatment enactment directly, and the cohort study did not assess ADHD. Together, however, they suggest that executive difficulties may become most consequential when responsibility for management is transferred before young people have the capacity to carry it, and when lapses are met with criticism rather than support.

Prospective-memory research distinguishes four stages in carrying out a delayed intention: forming the intention, retaining it, initiating it, and executing it (Kliegel et al., 2006). In children with ADHD, some evidence suggests that difficulties may emerge mainly under demanding conditions. In a small laboratory study, children with ADHD were less accurate than controls when working-memory demands increased or the relevant cue appeared outside their focus of attention, but not in a simpler condition or when performance was rewarded (Costanzo et al., 2021). This suggests that the gap may widen as treatment demands increase and narrow when tasks are simplified or rewarded, consistent with the executive and motivational pathways described earlier. Failures at different stages may therefore require different forms of support, even when they produce the same observable outcome: inconsistent self-management.

Time-based prospective memory may be particularly vulnerable in ADHD. In a virtual-reality task, children with ADHD less often carried out intended actions at the specified times; this was accounted for by less strategic monitoring of time as the target approached, not by checking the clock less often (Seesjärvi et al., 2025). Although pain self-management was not examined, acting on treatment knowledge may similarly depend on knowing when to act, a demand that alarms or fixed routines may help to reduce. This account so far has treated executive differences as relatively stable. However, if enactment depends on processes such as initiation and monitoring, anything that temporarily reduces these capacities should widen the knowing-to-doing gap. Sleep disruption is a plausible candidate because it has been linked to executive difficulties in both chronic pain and ADHD, as the following studies illustrate. In 85 adolescents receiving an eight-week internet-delivered cognitive-behavioural intervention for chronic pain, more severe insomnia symptoms were associated with greater self-reported executive difficulties, which were in turn associated with lower engagement in self-management; greater engagement was associated with greater improvement in pain-related disability (Law et al., 2024). The study was single-arm, relied on self-report, and did not assess ADHD. In 56 children with ADHD, longer actigraphy-measured sleep-onset latency was associated with poorer working memory and cognitive flexibility (Liang et al., 2022), although the sample was small and the design cross-sectional. Together, these findings raise the possibility that the gap is dynamic rather than fixed, widening during periods when pain disrupts sleep and executive capacity is lower. Treatment plans could therefore anticipate poorer days, for example by specifying a minimal version of the routine and how to resume it, rather than assuming consistent capacity. This proposed interaction among ADHD, sleep, and enactment has not yet been tested directly.

4. Positioning the Knowing-to-Doing Gap Within the COPES Model

The COPES model (Caes et al., 2021), introduced above, provides the closest theoretical foundation for the knowing-to-doing account. It proposes a cycle in which immature or impaired executive functioning and emotion regulation reduce adolescents' capacity for pain self-management, increasing pain-related disability, while persistent pain is proposed, largely on the basis of adult evidence, to drain cognitive resources and further constrain executive functioning. Flexible parental support is hypothesised to offset some of these effects. However, COPES is a theoretical model, and the evidence on which it was based was largely crosssectional and self-reported. It also conceptualises executive functioning as a single continuum from impairment to optimal functioning, and its authors identified adolescents with cooccurring conditions such as ADHD and autism as a priority for future research (Caes et al., 2021). The present account responds to this call. It does not claim that the link between executive functioning and pain self-management is new. Instead, it extends COPES in three ways: by treating ADHD as involving longstanding executive and motivational differences rather than only a position on an executive continuum; by specifying the stages between intending and enacting at which these differences may matter; and by locating the gap in the fit between the young person and the demands of the treatment plan.

The proposed account can also be situated within the broader paediatric self-management framework developed by Modi et al. (2012). That framework conceptualises self-management as a multidimensional process shaped by interacting influences at the individual, family, community, and healthcare-system levels and emphasises that these influences may change across developmental transitions. From this perspective, executive functioning represents one individual-level capacity operating within a wider ecology of treatment demands, caregiver involvement, social circumstances, and healthcare support. The knowing-to-doing account therefore does not replace a multilevel understanding of paediatric self-management. Instead, it specifies one potential mechanism through which an individual difference may interact with contextual demands: ADHD-related executive differences may make it harder to translate treatment knowledge and intentions into action, particularly when recommendations are complex and external support is limited. Within this account, structured routines, appropriately calibrated caregiver involvement, and accessible healthcare communication are expected to reduce the executive demands placed on the young person. Modi et al.'s framework was not developed specifically for ADHD or chronic pain and does not demonstrate the proposed executive pathway; its relevance here is therefore conceptual rather than direct empirical support.

Importantly, the proposed gap should not be understood as a fixed characteristic located solely within the young person. This account proposes that whether executive differences result in inconsistent treatment enactment may depend partly on the fit between the demands of the selfmanagement task and the support available in everyday contexts. In a secondary qualitative analysis of interviews with 21 adolescents receiving treatment for chronic musculoskeletal pain and 21 parents, participants linked the perceived effectiveness of physical interventions to adolescent autonomy, a trusting therapeutic relationship, meaningful participation, and personalised care (Joslin & Roberts, 2024). However, the original interview guide was not designed specifically to address the question examined in the secondary analysis, and healthcare professionals' perspectives were not included. Complementary participatory research involving three separate workshops, with adolescents with long-lasting pain, with parents, and with healthcare and education professionals together, identified interactions across family, healthcare, and school contexts as important to pain management. Fragmented communication and limited coordination between healthcare and education were described as barriers to collaborative care and to obtaining and implementing support in school settings (Guddal et al., 2025). Interpretation should remain cautious because the workshops represented the perspectives of relatively small groups and only four parents participated in the parent workshop. Building on these findings, this account proposes that external prompts, shared routines, and coordinated support may reduce the executive demands associated with pain selfmanagement, whereas poorly structured or inconsistent environments may widen the knowingto-doing gap. These proposed context-dependent pathways have not yet been tested directly in young people with co-occurring ADHD and chronic pain. ADHD-related executive and motivational differences are hypothesised to impede the translation of treatment knowledge and intentions into consistent enactment, particularly at the stages of planning, remembering at the right moment, starting, monitoring, adjusting, and resuming after a lapse. Alternative explanations, including readiness, treatment burden, and emotional and physical barriers, should be assessed alongside executive differences. All pathways are provisional and require direct testing.

5. Testable Propositions of the Knowing-to-Doing Account

The knowing-to-doing account generates four propositions. For each, we specify the evidence that motivates it, a design and analytic model that could test it, and the result that would count against it (Table 1).

Proposition 1: Executive differences predict enactment beyond knowledge and intention.

Among young people who understand and intend to follow a pain-management recommendation, greater ADHD-related executive differences should predict less consistent enactment. ADHD symptoms are closely associated with everyday executive difficulties (Ceruti & Marzocchi, 2025), but, to our knowledge, no study has linked these to pain-treatment enactment. A prospective cohort could assess knowledge (for example, by asking young people to explain or demonstrate a strategy) and intention at baseline, and then record enactment of specific recommendations daily over several weeks using brief diaries or ecological momentary assessment. Multilevel models, with days nested within participants, would test whether executive measures predict enactment after adjusting for knowledge, intention, pain intensity, mood, and sleep. The proposition would be weakened if executive measures added no explanatory value beyond these variables.

Proposition 2: Everyday measures of executive functioning predict enactment better than structured laboratory tasks. Performance tests and behavioural ratings of executive functioning correlate weakly in children with ADHD (Olsen et al., 2024), and ratings vary across informants (Andersen et al., 2025). Within the same cohort, hierarchical regression could test the incremental validity of everyday ratings and naturalistic prospective-memory tasks over laboratory tests. To reduce shared-method variance, executive functioning and enactment should be reported by different informants or measured objectively, for example through app logs. The proposition would be weakened if laboratory tasks predicted enactment as well as, or better than, everyday measures. Proposition 3: External structure narrows the gap, particularly for young people with greater executive differences. Children's organisational difficulties have been associated with poorer parental implementation of behavioural treatment strategies for ADHD symptoms (Hawkey et al., 2024), and organisational skills can be improved through training (Huang et al., 2025). A micro-randomised trial, in which prompts are randomly delivered or withheld at many decision points within each participant, can estimate the immediate effect of a support component and test whether that effect varies across people and over time (Klasnja et al., 2015). The critical test is an interaction: prompts should increase enactment more for young people with greater executive differences. The proposition would be weakened if prompts helped all young people equally, or helped those with greater executive differences less.

Proposition 4: Inconsistent enactment links executive differences to later pain-related disability. Because this is a mediational claim, testing it requires at least three waves of measurement of executive functioning, enactment, pain, and disability (Cole & Maxwell, 2003; Maxwell et al., 2011). A random-intercept cross-lagged panel model is preferable to the traditional cross-lagged panel model, which confounds stable between-person differences with within-person change (Hamaker et al., 2015). This model would test whether, within individuals, poorer executive functioning precedes less consistent enactment and less consistent enactment precedes greater disability, while simultaneously estimating reverse paths from pain to executive functioning. The proposition would be weakened if enactment did not predict later disability, or if associations ran only from pain to executive functioning.

6. Clinical and Service Implications: Assessing and Reducing the Enactment Gap

If future research supports the knowing-to-doing account, services should first identify where enactment breaks down, rather than attributing inconsistent self-management to a lack of effort, non-adherence, or simply to ADHD. For a given recommendation, clinicians could ask whether the young person understands it, intends to carry it out, and at which stage enactment fails: planning, remembering at the right moment, starting, monitoring, adjusting, or resuming after a lapse. Treatment burden, distress, physical limitations, and limited support should be considered alongside these stages. This extends existing recommendations for comprehensive biopsychosocial assessment in paediatric chronic pain (World Health Organization, 2021).

Given that executive ratings vary across informants and correspond only modestly with performance-based tests (Andersen et al., 2025; Kofler et al., 2024; Soto et al., 2020), assessment should combine reports from the young person, caregivers, and, where possible, school, with brief diaries or ecological momentary assessment of actual implementation (Napvan der Vlist et al., 2021). Where enactment difficulties occur alongside longstanding attentional or organisational difficulties across settings, unrecognised ADHD or autism may warrant consideration, given how rarely these conditions have been identified in young people attending pain services (Wiwe Lipsker et al., 2018). However, inconsistent self-management is not itself evidence of ADHD: pain, poor sleep, anxiety, and depression can produce similar difficulties, and executive difficulties are neither necessary nor sufficient to account for all cases of ADHD (Willcutt et al., 2005). This account also shifts attention from changing the young person to changing the plan.

Recommendations could be broken into a small number of specific actions, linked to stable routines and clear cues, and supported by written or visual schedules, reminders, and prepared materials. Plans should specify a minimal version of each routine for poorer days and how to resume after a lapse, so that a missed session is treated as expected rather than as a failure of commitment. Services can also reduce the demands of their own processes, for example by providing written alternatives to verbal instructions and offering online or text-based booking; autistic adults have reported preferring online or text-based booking and the option to email the reason for a consultation in advance (Doherty et al., 2022). Parental prompting and monitoring may compensate initially but should be adjusted collaboratively to preserve autonomy, and coordination with schools may be essential where treatment behaviours occur during the school day (Guddal et al., 2025; Joslin & Roberts, 2024). Organisational-skills training can improve everyday functioning in children with ADHD (Huang et al., 2025), but, to our knowledge, none of these adaptations has yet been evaluated for pain self-management in this group.

Digital tools could reduce prospective-memory, planning, and monitoring demands, but evidence of sustained benefit is limited. A review of 22 studies of digital self-management tools for adolescents and young adults with chronic conditions, which excluded interventions for pain management, found them acceptable and usable, but engagement often declined over time and evidence of effectiveness was limited (Li et al., 2024). A review of digital interventions for young people with ADHD found the strongest feasibility and acceptability for co-produced tools, but only preliminary evidence that text-message reminders improved medication refills, and no evaluation of pain self-management (Gudka et al., 2025). Reminders and monitoring tools should therefore be co-designed with young people, kept minimally burdensome, and tested rather than assumed to compensate for executive differences.

7. Boundary Conditions and Alternative Explanations

The knowing-to-doing account does not assume a single causal direction. As noted earlier, chronic pain may itself reduce executive capacity, directly and possibly also through disrupted sleep (Berryman et al., 2013; Law et al., 2024), and COPES proposes a cyclical relation among pain, executive functioning, and self-management (Caes et al., 2021). Pain repeatedly interrupts attention and imposes a competing priority to escape it (Eccleston & Crombez, 1999), so it may draw on the same attentional and executive resources that self-management requires. The combined effect of ADHD and pain could therefore be additive, with each independently reducing enactment, or interactive, with pain having a disproportionate effect on young people whose executive and attentional resources are already more limited. The knowing-to-doing account tentatively proposes the latter, which could be tested by examining whether the association between pain intensity and enactment is stronger among young people with more ADHD traits. Inconsistent enactment may therefore reflect the effects of executive differences on self-management, the effects of pain on executive functioning, or both.

Proposition 4 and the analytic approach outlined in Table 1 are intended to separate these pathways.

Inconsistent enactment also has explanations other than executive differences, and this account is useful only if it can be distinguished from them. Expectations and readiness matter. In a follow-up of multidisciplinary pain recommendations, adherence ranged from 47% to 100% across recommendation types, and parental expectations, attitudes, and previous treatment experience were associated with subsequent engagement (Simons et al., 2010). In a digital pain intervention, expectancies and readiness predicted online engagement through perceived helpfulness, but readiness only marginally predicted reported offline use of skills (de la Vega & Palermo, 2023), underlining that exposure to treatment is not the same as enactment. A motivational-interviewing intervention improved study-specific readiness ratings but did not change validated measures of acceptance or later functional outcomes relative to standard care, although admission length was shorter (Smith et al., 2025). Treatment burden offers a second explanation: some young people with chronic conditions deliberately postpone or modify recommendations when their psychosocial costs outweigh their benefits, a pattern described as "rationalised non-adherence" (van Schelven et al., 2023). Adolescents beginning intensive pain treatment also describe physical, emotional, and social barriers to recovery, including low confidence and avoidance (Jacobs et al., 2021). Most of these studies were small, single-site, or qualitative, and none assessed ADHD or executive functioning. Together, however, they point to a key distinction: a young person who intends to act but fails to remember, start, or resume differs from one who deliberately adapts or declines a recommendation. Tests of this account should therefore measure readiness, perceived burden, and emotional and physical barriers alongside executive functioning, and should distinguish unintentional from deliberate non-enactment.

Context sets further boundaries. A systematic review of 119 studies identified family factors, including parental mental health, responses to the child's pain, and family functioning, that were associated with pain and disability outcomes, although most studies were cross-sectional and at moderate or high risk of bias, and the review focused on pain and disability rather than enactment (Martí et al., 2025). A review of 33 studies similarly found that depression, anxiety, catastrophising, and parenting were consistently associated with functional disability (Sinclair et al., 2016), and a meta-ethnography of 43 qualitative studies described pain management as a family-centred process embedded in healthcare, school, and community contexts (Silveira Bianchim et al., 2024). This account predicts that well-calibrated parental and service support may compensate for executive differences, whereas inconsistent or poorly matched support may widen the gap; this should be tested using measures that distinguish supportive scaffolding from overcontrol. More broadly, this account is likely to be most applicable when a young person understands and intends to follow an accessible recommendation but must organise and sustain it independently. It is likely to be less explanatory when enactment is constrained mainly by inaccessible or unacceptable treatment, severe physical limitations, low readiness, family burden, or inadequate support from services. Whether it generalises across ages, pain conditions, ADHD presentations, and socioeconomic and cultural contexts remains to be tested.

8. Methodological Priorities for Testing the Account

Beyond the specific tests summarised in Table 1, several design features apply across studies.

Samples should include young people with a diagnosis of ADHD as well as those with elevated ADHD traits who have not been diagnosed, given that ADHD is under-recognised in girls (Young et al., 2020), in some minoritised ethnic groups in the United States (Coker et al., 2016), and among young people attending pain services (Wiwe Lipsker et al., 2018). Pain type, duration, and severity should be characterised, and co-occurring conditions, sleep difficulties, and medication status documented. Co-occurring neurodivergent conditions, particularly autism, warrant specific assessment, given that ADHD symptoms and autistic traits frequently co-occur among young people attending pain services (Wiwe Lipsker et al., 2018) and that chronic pain is also more common in autistic children (Chou et al., 2026). Comparison groups with chronic pain only, ADHD only, and neither condition would help separate the effects of ADHD-related differences from those of pain. Executive differences vary within ADHD, so analyses should treat inattention, hyperactivity-impulsivity, and specific executive processes dimensionally. Recruitment should also extend beyond tertiary clinics, whose patients may have more severe and complex presentations.

Enactment should be measured separately for each recommendation, because medication use, graded activity, relaxation practice, and sleep routines involve different opportunities and barriers. Self-and caregiver reports should be supplemented by diaries, ecological momentary assessment, app or device records, or attendance data, in line with evidence-based recommendations for paediatric adherence assessment (Quittner et al., 2008). Two measurement issues also warrant attention. Asking participants whether they carried out a behaviour can itself act as a prompt (Klasnja et al., 2015), so monitoring procedures should be as low-burden as possible and their potential cueing effects reported. In addition, attrition and missed assessments should be examined for associations with executive or ADHD-related characteristics, rather than assumed to be random. Studies should be adequately powered for moderated and longitudinal models, preregister their hypotheses and analyses, report null and mixed findings, and develop procedures with young people, caregivers, and clinicians.

9. Limitations of the Present Review

This review has several limitations. It is a conceptual rather than systematic review: although study selection was guided by an explicit framework, searches were limited to Google Scholar and were not preregistered, and evidence was not quantitatively weighted, so relevant studies may have been missed. Direct evidence on self-management in young people with co-occurring ADHD and chronic pain is very limited, and the knowing-to-doing account integrates findings from adjacent literatures, including studies of adults, autistic populations, and other chronic conditions. Support for individual components therefore does not show that the full pathway operates in the target population. The evidence is also heterogeneous: executive functioning has been measured with tasks and rating scales that are not interchangeable (Kofler et al., 2024; Soto et al., 2020), and adherence, engagement, and self-management have been defined inconsistently. Much of it is cross-sectional, reliant on self-or caregiver report, and drawn from small or clinically selected samples, so shared-method variance may have inflated some associations and null findings may be under-reported. Finally, this account has not yet been developed with young people who have ADHD and chronic pain, their families, or clinicians.

Their input will be essential to judge whether it reflects lived experience, whether its language is acceptable, and whether the proposed adaptations are feasible.

10. Conclusion

This review proposes that some apparent non-adherence among young people with ADHD and chronic pain may reflect a knowing-to-doing gap: a mismatch between the executive and motivational demands of pain-management plans and the profile commonly associated with ADHD, rather than a lack of knowledge or concern. The knowing-to-doing account specifies the stages at which this mismatch may arise, extends the COPES model to a group its authors identified as a priority, and locates the gap between the young person and the way services deliver care. Current evidence is insufficient to show that this pathway operates: direct studies are scarce, and pain, sleep, distress, readiness, treatment burden, and family and service context may all contribute. The central empirical questions are whether executive differences predict enactment beyond knowledge and intention, whether external structure narrows the gap, and whether inconsistent enactment contributes to later disability. If future research supports this account, services should ask not only whether a young person is adherent, but whether the plan they have been given can be enacted.
