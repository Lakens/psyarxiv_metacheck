What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. When Risk Factors Compete: A Machine Learning Approach to Identifying Antecedents of Adolescent delinquency Across Domains, Development, and Gender

Delinquency in adolescence has been studied extensively in developmental science, yet it remains only partially explained by any single account of its origins. A substantial body of longitudinal research demonstrates that delinquent involvement does not arise from a single cause but from the accumulation of risk across multiple developmental domains, each carrying independent and interactive significance for behavioral adaptation across childhood and adolescence (Azeredo et al., 2019; Loeber & Farrington, 2000; Murray & Farrington, 2010).

Comprehensive reviews have catalogued risk and protective factors across the individual, family, school, and wider ecological contexts, and called for research capable of weighing these influences against one another rather than examining them separately (Ayano et al., 2024; Farrington, 2000; Ullman et al., 2024; Zúñiga et al., 2024). Decades on, the domain-specific literatures have continued to expand largely in parallel, and the question of which factors carry the greatest developmental significance remains unresolved. This gap is conceptual as well as empirical. Developmental psychopathology has long held that maladaptation emerges from the organization of influences across levels rather than from any level considered in isolation, and that the same outcome may be reached by different developmental routes (i.e., equifinality; Cicchetti & Rogosch, 1996; Masten & Cicchetti, 2010).

Evidence consistent with this has been available for some time, with distinct clusters of risk factors shown to precede similar externalizing outcomes (Deater-Deckard et al., 1998). If that premise holds, the relative contribution of any given domain would be expected to vary according to the developmental period at which it is assessed. Estimating relative contribution is therefore a means of examining a proposition the field has more often assumed than quantified.

Establishing relative contribution first requires establishing what each domain contributes on its own terms. The existing evidence cannot yet supply this comparison. Risk factors have largely been examined domain by domain, in different samples and at different ages, so how much each domain contributes relative to the others, and whether that contribution changes across development, remains unestablished. The present study addresses this gap. Its aim is to establish the relative prospective importance of risk factors for adolescent delinquency when predictors from five domains and four developmental periods are evaluated concurrently, and to determine whether that importance is stable across development or reconfigures between early childhood and mid-adolescence. A subsidiary aim is to examine whether the resulting ordering differs between boys and girls. The current evidence is considered in turn below, spanning personality and temperament, behavioral and emotional problems, family and parenting, school and social adjustment, and neighborhood, housing, and socioeconomic context.

Risk and Protection Across Developmental Domains

Within the individual domain, psychopathic personality dimensions, comprising grandiose-deceitful, callous-unemotional, and impulsive or sensation-seeking traits, have been consistently linked to the onset and persistence of conduct problems from early childhood, with the full three-dimensional construct outperforming callous-unemotional traits alone and differentiating risk more strongly for boys than for girls (Andershed et al., 2018; Fanti et al., 2018; Frogner et al., 2018). Temperamental fearlessness, characterized by reduced sensitivity to threat and punishment, distinguishes children following stable high conduct problem trajectories from preschool age and predicts antisocial behavior across a window exceeding 15 years (Galán et al., 2020; Klingzell et al., 2016).

Behavioral and emotional problems constitute a second well-documented source of risk.

Early conduct problems are arguably the most robustly established predictor of later delinquency in this literature, with risk rising alongside greater severity, greater chronicity, and earlier onset (Assink et al., 2015; Byrd et al., 2012). Attention-deficit hyperactivity symptoms carry prospective significance that is amplified by co-occurring conduct problems, with delinquency in young adulthood best predicted by their combination with conduct disorder symptoms rather than by diagnosis alone (Breuer et al., 2022). Internalizing symptoms have received comparatively less attention as predictors of delinquency, yet network analyses identify anxious and depressed symptoms as central nodes predicting later increases in externalizing clusters, with bridging symptoms differing between boys and girls (K. Liu et al., 2023).

The family and parenting domain has generated an extensive evidence base. Positive parenting, encompassing warmth, praise, and involvement, is protective across cultural contexts (Lansford et al., 2014 (Lansford et al., , 2025;; Pinquart, 2017). Conversely, harsh and inconsistent discipline carries reliable prospective associations with conduct problems and delinquency that strengthen with severity and persist after adjustment for parental characteristics predicting disciplinary use (Farrington & Malvaso, 2025; Joyner & Beaver, 2022; Pan et al., 2024). Beyond discipline, the emotional quality of the parent-child relationship carries prospective risk, with within-individual increases in parental hostility associated with higher psychopathic traits, and increases in warmth with lower psychopathic traits among justice-involved adolescents (Backman et al., 2021).

Variation in that relational quality is itself partly attributable to parental characteristics, as parental interpersonal-affective psychopathic traits are associated both with adolescent callousunemotional traits and with harsher and less warm parenting (Chetcuti et al., 2021; Dotterer et al., 2021). Caregiver mental health carries risk by a further route, with maternal depression and victimization predicting membership in more severe early externalizing subgroups at age four, which in turn predicts delinquency at age 16 (Schmidt et al., 2021).

School and social adjustment constitutes a fourth domain, operating through adolescents' institutional bonds, normative engagement, and capacity to manage social relationships. Higherquality teacher-child relationships assessed at age 10 predict fewer delinquent acts as much as seven years later (Obsuth et al., 2023), individual-level school attachment carries a larger protective association with offending trajectories than school-level ethos (Higgins et al., 2020), and configurations of low teacher support and unfavorable school perceptions in early adolescence predict elevated delinquency across mid-to-late adolescence (Cashman et al., 2026).

In relation to delinquency, social competence functions as a protective factor both directly and through reduced susceptibility to deviant peer affiliation (Müller & Minger, 2013; Wallner et al., 2020). It is also modifiable, with meta-analytic evidence from randomized evaluations establishing effects of social skills training on antisocial behavior (Beelmann & Lösel, 2006).

Neighborhood, housing, and socioeconomic conditions form the more distal context within which these proximal processes unfold. Their influence is understood to operate largely indirectly, with socioeconomic disadvantage associated with delinquency substantially through family functioning, neighborhood characteristics, and children's cognitive functioning rather than through a direct pathway (Bronfenbrenner, 1986; Piotrowska et al., 2019). This positioning has consequences for how such conditions should be expected to perform when placed alongside more proximal predictors. Relative importance therefore indexes proximity to the outcome as well as strength of influence, and a weaker influence cannot be taken as evidence that a condition is unimportant for how delinquency develops (Shmueli, 2010).

The Problem of Relative Importance

The evidence reviewed above establishes breadth rather than relative importance. Metaanalytic syntheses identify significant effects across most of the risk domains examined for persistent offending (Assink et al., 2015), and the number of candidate factors catalogued across this literature extends to well over a thousand (Gubbels et al., 2024). What has not been established is how these domains stand in relation to one another. The literature has to date been unable to determine whether family processes carry more weight than dispositional characteristics, whether either outweighs school or neighborhood context, or whether any such ordering holds from early childhood into adolescence. This is attributable to two limitations. The first is analytical. Studies combining predictors from several domains within a single regression framework have typically been restricted to a small number of indicators per domain, in part because established measures of relative importance become difficult to implement beyond five predictors (Johnson, 2000). While such designs establish that each domain contributes unique variance, they cannot determine which factor carries the greatest weight once several dozen candidates are considered together (Logan-Greene et al., 2011). This is due to the fact that the intercorrelation among predictors undermines the interpretation of regression weights as indices of relative contribution (Nimon & Oswald, 2013) and introduces instability that further constrains what may be concluded (Romero et al., 2025). The common alternative, summing risk factors into a cumulative index, demonstrates that risk accumulates across temperament, family, and school domains (Stoddard et al., 2012; van der Laan et al., 2010). However, it assigns equal weight to every constituent factor by construction, which is precisely the assumption that requires a comparative test to be evaluated.

The second reason is one of scale. A recent three-level meta-analysis classified 1,850 candidate protective factors reported across 305 primary studies into 77 conceptually distinct categories, and identified reliable protective effects for 50 (Gubbels et al., 2024). The corresponding literature on risk factors is comparably extensive (Assink et al., 2015; Ayano et al., 2024). The problem of distinguishing which among many intercorrelated risk factors carry independent influence has long been recognized (Farrington, 2000), and the continuing effort to classify and consolidate the evidence indicates that this challenge remains unresolved (Gubbels et al., 2024). Furthermore, because each factor has been examined in a different sample employing different covariate sets, the effect sizes attached to them are not mutually comparable, and no narrative synthesis can resolve them into a single ordering (Westreich & Greenland, 2013). Producing such an ordering requires that a large number of correlated predictors be evaluated simultaneously, within a single sample and a single model. Machine learning methods such as random forest meet this requirement, as they rank predictors by their contribution to prediction while accommodating nonlinear associations and interactions that need not be specified in advance (Berk & Bleich, 2013; Breiman, 2001; Yarkoni & Westfall, 2017).

Developmental Timing and the Expectation of Change

A further consideration concerns whether any ranking of factors would be stable. The relative importance of predictors should not be expected to remain constant across development, and an ordering established over a pooled developmental window would therefore describe no single period accurately. Developmental periods are not interchangeable windows onto the same process. The manifestations of antisocial behavior and some of its risk factors are age-dependent, with certain risk factors peaking at particular ages (Loeber, 1990). Each period also poses its own developmental tasks, which shape what behavior is normative and which circumstances matter most for a child (Loeber, 1990). The same risk factor may therefore carry different meaning and weight depending on when it is measured. Examining the importance of predictors across adolescence, fixed-effects modeling demonstrated that changes in risk-seeking, parental and peer influences, and attitudes were each associated with self-reported delinquency, and that their relative salience shifted across mid-to-late adolescence (Childs et al., 2010).

Developmental cascade accounts imply a similar instability. If early individual and family risk does not act directly upon delinquency but initiates a sequence of intermediate processes, including school disengagement and peer marginalization, that culminate in delinquent involvement (Savolainen et al., 2015), then what carries predictive weight at any point depends on how far that sequence has progressed. The developmental taxonomy distinguishing life-course-persistent from adolescence-limited antisocial behavior points in a similar direction (Moffitt, 1993). Because the two pathways are attributed to different causesneuropsychological vulnerability and family adversity in the former and peer processes and the maturity gap in the latter-the factors associated with delinquency at a given age depend on which pathway predominates at a given stage of development (Moffitt, 1993). Accounts of critical periods add that influence may concentrate at particular ages, notably early attachment to caretakers, and the rapid increase in nonaggressive antisocial acts in early adolescence, although the evidence for such periods remains incomplete (Loeber, 1990). Such age-graded expected changes carry a methodological implication. A single model estimated across a pooled developmental window establishes which predictors carry greatest weight overall, but cannot distinguish a factor that is unimportant at every period from one that is important early and subsequently superseded by more proximal influences (Savolainen et al., 2015).

Gender and the Composition of Risk

Yet another question concerns whether the ordering of factors differs between boys and girls. Current evidence is suggestive but unsettled, with gender-specific predictors reported in some samples (Sui et al., 2025; Václavíková et al., 2020), while others find uniform factors operating for both sexes and differing only in strength or prevalence (Fagan et al., 2007; Moffitt & Caspi, 2001). For example, family relationships have been found more important for girls, and peer and school factors more important for boys in analyses of protective factors against antisocial behavior (Václavíková et al., 2020). More specific differences have also been reported.

Among boys, perceiving schoolwork as meaningful moderates the association between adverse home conditions and delinquency (Sandahl, 2016), and psychopathic personality constructs differentiate continued risk more strongly (Frogner et al., 2018) while symptoms bridging internalizing and externalizing psychopathology also differ between boys and girls (K. Liu et al., 2023). Much of this evidence nonetheless rests on descriptive comparisons of estimates across groups rather than on formal tests of heterogeneity, and observing an association in one group but not the other does not establish that the groups statistically differ (Eliot et al., 2023; Gelman & Stern, 2006). The extent to which apparent gender differences exceed sampling variability is therefore rarely established, and formal tests of gender moderation are themselves frequently underpowered (Craig Aulisi et al., 2025). Where such tests do return null results, these are in turn frequently reported as showing no difference, when they establish only that the data do not provide strong evidence of one (Tomek et al., 2026). Thus, whether the composition of risk differs between boys and girls remains unresolved.

Taken together, these three sources of variation-relative importance, developmental timing, and gender differences-set the requirements for any design intended to establish order of influence among multiple factors across domains. Predictors from all domains must compete within a single model, given that relative importance cannot be recovered from estimates produced in different samples under different adjustment sets. Models must then be estimated at each developmental period separately, with the outcome held constant, as pooled models cannot distinguish a factor that is unimportant throughout from one that is important early and subsequently superseded. Models must also be estimated separately for boys and girls, with any resulting differences tested rather than inspected. The first two designs answer fundamentally different questions: a pooled model indicates what best indexes concurrent risk, whereas periodspecific models indicate what would need to be known earlier in development in order to anticipate it. The latter is closer to the question early prevention poses, as establishing which influences carry weight before others identifies the earliest points at which intervention could be directed. This does not establish that earlier influences operate through later ones, which would require a formal test of mediation, but it indicates where in development such sequences would need to be examined.

The Present Study

The overarching aim of the present study was to establish the relative prospective importance of risk factors for adolescent delinquency, and to determine whether that importance is stable across development or reconfigures as children move from early childhood into adolescence. A subsidiary aim was to examine whether that ordering differs between boys and girls, and if so where in development such differences are most pronounced. These aims were pursued using 111 predictors spanning personality and temperament, behavioral and emotional problems, family and parenting, school and social adjustment, and neighborhood, housing, and socioeconomic context, measured across four developmental periods from early childhood (3-5) through mid-adolescence (13) (14) (15) (16), for self-reported delinquency in mid-adolescence within a Swedish general population cohort. Three questions were addressed: 1.

When predictors spanning all five domains and all four developmental periods compete within a single model, which emerge as the most important prospective predictors of adolescent delinquency? 2. When each developmental period is modeled separately, does the ordering of predictors reconfigure from early childhood through mid-adolescence, or does it remain stable? 3. Does that ordering differ between boys and girls when formally tested, and at which developmental periods are any such differences most pronounced? Answered together, these questions establish whether the organization of risk is fixed across development or reconfigures as children age, and whether any such reconfiguration proceeds differently for boys and girls.

Method Participants

Data were drawn from the Social and Physical Development, Interventions, and Adaptation (SOFIA) study, a longitudinal cohort following three birth cohorts (2005, 2006, 2007) from early childhood into adolescence. The target population comprised all children enrolled in preschool in a mid-sized Swedish municipality at the start of the 2010-2011 school year (N = 2,542). Parents of 2,121 children consented to participate, corresponding to a nonresponse rate of 16.6%. The analytic sample was 46.6% girls and 53.4% boys. Race and ethnicity were not recorded, as Swedish official statistics classify population background by country of birth (Emilsson & Ulceluşe, 2026). Parents accordingly reported the child's country of birth in nine regional categories, together with age on arrival in Sweden where applicable. As 97.8% of participants were born in Sweden (n = 36 born elsewhere, 2.2%), cell counts across the remaining eight categories were too sparse to support stable estimation, and neither variable was retained in analyses. At baseline the municipality had approximately 85,000 inhabitants and was demographically comparable to similarly sized Swedish municipalities with respect to age, sex, education, employment, and rural-urban composition.

A random subsample of nonparticipating caregivers completed a supplementary telephone interview; no differences emerged with respect to child conduct problems, internalizing problems, caregiver socioeconomic status, or central parenting dimensions, although mothers in the nonparticipating group were more likely to have been born outside Sweden and to report lower levels of affection and praise towards the child.

Four assessment periods were selected to represent theoretically meaningful developmental periods: early childhood in 2010 (M = 3.86 years, SD = .86; 47.0% girls; N = 2,121), late childhood in 2015 (M = 8.86 years; 47.0% girls; n = 1,826), early adolescence in 2018 (M = 11.33 years, SD = .92; 47.4% girls; n = 1,709), and mid-adolescence in 2021 (M = 14.33 years, SD = .93; 47.8% girls; n = 1,506). Teacher-and parent-reported measures were collected at each period and served as prospective predictors of self-reported delinquency assessed in mid-adolescence.

Measures

A total of 111 predictors spanning five domains were assessed: personality and temperament, behavioral and emotional problems, family and parenting, school and social adjustment, and neighborhood, housing, and socioeconomic context. Full instrument descriptions, item counts, and response scales are reported in Supplementary File A. Descriptive statistics for every predictor at every period are presented in Supplementary File B, and intercorrelations among all predictors at each period in Supplementary File C. With the exception of school adjustment, which was not assessed during early adolescence, every predictor was measured across all four periods. Predictors were drawn from two independent informants, teachers and parents, while the outcome was self-reported by the surveyed adolescents.

Personality and temperament comprised psychopathic trait dimensions and fearlessness. Psychopathic traits were rated by teachers using the Child Problematic Traits Inventory (Colins et al., 2014), which yields three dimensions: grandiose-deceitful traits, covering interpersonal manipulation and deceitfulness; callous-unemotional traits, covering affective callousness and lack of empathy; and impulsivity-need for stimulation, covering impulsive behavior and sensation-seeking. Fearlessness was assessed using the teacher-rated Child Fearlessness Scale (Somma et al., 2016), covering greater levels of fearfulness and boldness in social and physical situations.

Behavioral and emotional problems comprised attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder symptoms, conduct problems, and internalizing symptoms. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder symptoms were rated by teachers using the DuPaul ADHD Rating Scale (DuPaul et al., 1998), which follows DSM-IV-TR criteria and yields two nine-item subscales: inattention, covering difficulties sustaining attention, following instructions, and organizing tasks; and hyperactivity, covering restlessness, fidgeting, and difficulty remaining seated or waiting.

Conduct problems were assessed using a 10-item scale based on diagnostic criteria for conduct disorder and oppositional defiant disorder (Colins et al., 2014), covering aggression toward others, rule violations, property destruction, and defiance of authority. Internalizing symptoms were assessed using a two-item measure developed for the SOFIA study, one item capturing anxiety and worry and the other depressed mood and withdrawal. Conduct problems and internalizing measures were each rated independently by parents and teachers, and the two sets of ratings were retained as separate predictors rather than combined.

Family and parenting comprised parent reports of parenting practices and of the wider circumstances in which parenting took place. Positive parenting and parental involvement derived from the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire (Essau et al., 2006), capturing warm and affirming behaviors such as praising and complimenting the child, and active engagement in the child's daily life and emotional well-being. Disciplinary practices comprised corporal punishment, inconsistent discipline, nonviolent discipline drawn from the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire and the Parent-Child Conflict Tactics Scales (Straus et al., 1998), and psychological aggression from the Parent-Child Conflict Tactics Scales, covering physical discipline, variability in disciplinary response, calm explanation or withdrawal of privileges, and verbally aggressive responses such as shouting. Four further measures developed for the SOFIA study captured the parenting context: parental well-being measuring general life enjoyment and on stress experienced in coping with the child; relationship with partner regarding the frequency of dissatisfaction or conflict; perceived social support measuring access to informal support networks and knowledge of where to seek help, and parental concern regarding the child's development spanning physical health, learning, language, emotional development, mental health, social development, and behavior.

School and social adjustment comprised two teacher-rated measures developed for the SOFIA study alongside one measure rated by both parents and teachers. School adjustment captured the degree to which the child appeared to enjoy the school environment and demonstrated trust toward the educator, and the teacher-child relationship captured both the affiliative and conflictual dimensions of that relationship, asking whether the child sought out the teacher for comfort and support and whether the teacher found the child difficult to manage. Social competence was rated independently by parents and teachers (Frogner et al., 2022), covering prosocial and interpersonal skills such as greeting others appropriately, maintaining eye contact during conversation, responding politely when addressed, and helping out at home and at school. School adjustment was the only predictor unavailable at one period, having not been assessed during early adolescence.

Neighborhood, housing, and socioeconomic context comprised four single-item measures developed for the SOFIA study. Economic strain asked whether the family had experienced difficulties covering current expenses such as rent or installments during the preceding 12 months, with the response scale distinguishing how persistent that difficulty had been. Housing satisfaction captured satisfaction with the dwelling itself, and neighborhood satisfaction recorded, as a binary item, whether the family wished to remain living in their current residential area. Teacher concern about the child's home conditions captured concern regarding parenting ability, security, and housing, and was the only measure in this domain not reported by a parent.

Outcome

Delinquency was assessed in mid-adolescence using 22 self-report items drawn from the Swedish National Biannual School Survey on Crime (Swedish National Council for Crime Prevention, 2022), covering theft (13 items), vandalism (4 items), and violence (5 items) committed during the preceding 12 months. Participants reported how often they had engaged in each behavior on a six-point scale ranging from "never" to "more than 20 times". Each of the three domains were dichotomized into a binary indicator of any engagement, followed by the three indicators being combined into a single binary outcome coded 1 where at least one act of theft, vandalism, or violence was endorsed, and 0 where none were endorsed.

Demographics

Demographic characteristics were accounted for by the design rather than by statistical adjustment. Gender was handled through stratification, with separate models estimated for boys and girls at each period. Socioeconomic circumstances were represented within the predictor set by economic strain, housing satisfaction, and neighborhood satisfaction, each assessed at every period. Age was held constant by the period-specific structure, as all participants were assessed within the same developmental window at each wave.

Analytic Strategy

Random forest classification models (Breiman, 2001) were estimated using the ranger package (Wright & Ziegler, 2017) in R version 4.6.0. Missing data were addressed using the missForest algorithm (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012), as it accommodates continuous and categorical variables simultaneously, requires no distributional assumptions, and performs well where variables of unequal scale and type are related nonlinearly, conditions that characterize the present predictor set. Complete case analysis was infeasible, reducing the sample from 2,121 to 90 individuals. Each dataset was partitioned into training (75%) and held-out test (25%) sets by stratified random sampling. Model discrimination was evaluated on the held-out test set using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which served as the focal effect size and was reported with 95% confidence intervals from 1,000 bootstrap resamples.

Analysis proceeded in two stages. In the first, a full combined model entered all 111 predictors from all four periods simultaneously using 1,000 trees, establishing overall importance when every predictor competes concurrently. In the second, models were estimated separately for boys and girls at each developmental period, yielding eight models that each predicted delinquency in mid-adolescence from the predictors available at a single period. This permitted examination of how importance shifts across development and whether it differs by gender.

Variable importance was quantified using permutation importance computed on out-of-bag samples, preferred over mean decrease in impurity as less susceptible to bias (Strobl et al., 2008).

Mean absolute Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) values provided a convergent validity check, and individual-level SHAP values indicated the direction of contribution (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). Intercorrelation among predictors affects importance estimation in random forests: although the algorithm is not destabilized by multicollinearity, correlated predictors can split predictive signal between them and depress the apparent importance of each (Gregorutti et al., 2017). A conservative reference threshold of r = .80 was therefore adopted for flagging intercorrelations, and all predictors were retained on theoretical grounds. Flagged pairs, and the full intercorrelation matrices for each period, are reported in Supplementary File C. Associations were characterized using accumulated local effects (ALE) plots, which visualize how predicted probability changes across the range of a predictor. These were preferred to partial dependence plots, which average predictions over combinations of predictor values that rarely occur together and therefore distort the result when predictors are correlated (Apley & Zhu, 2020). Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) served as the primary performance metric, with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals computed from 1,000 resamples for AUC estimates and their differences, and from 1,000 resamples for feature-SHAP correlations. Hyperparameter tuning, cross-validation, and a logistic regression benchmark are reported in the main text; importance stability across 100 random seeds is reported in Supplementary File E; and sensitivity analyses comprising complete case analysis, rebalancing using the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE; Chawla et al., 2002), alternative partitioning, threshold variation, and confusion matrix comparison are reported in Supplementary File F.

Results

Model Performance

The full combined model demonstrated acceptable discrimination on the held-out test set (AUC = .787, 95% CI [.747, .826]), and 10-fold cross-validation confirmed stability (M = .742, SD = .043). A logistic regression benchmark estimated on identical predictors and partitions performed less well (AUC = .697, 95% CI [.651, .741]), with lower accuracy (.682 compared with .735) and sensitivity (.812 compared with .889). This difference was reliable (ΔAUC = .091, 95% CI [.056, .125], from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the test set) and exceeded the average advantage of random forest over logistic regression reported in a benchmark across 243 datasets (Couronné et al., 2018). Given moderate class imbalance in the outcome (41% delinquent), rebalancing using SMOTE was examined alongside an alternative 80/20 partition and variation of the classification threshold, none of which materially altered performance or importance rankings (Supplementary File F). Variable importance rankings were also highly stable across 100 random seeds, with narrow confidence intervals relative to mean importance throughout (Supplementary File E). Discussion

This study set out to establish the relative prospective importance of risk factors for adolescent delinquency, and to determine whether that importance is stable across development or reconfigures as children move from early childhood into adolescence. A subsidiary aim was to examine whether the resulting ordering differs between boys and girls. Three questions followed from these aims, and the evidence bears on each to a different degree. The first asked which factors emerge as most important when predictors from all domains and periods compete concurrently. Proximal behavioral and dispositional characteristics carried the greatest weight, with parent-rated social competence as the strongest protective factor and parental well-being being the only family context predictor, as distinct from parenting practices, among the leading ranks, an ordering that held across both permutation importance and SHAP attribution. The second asked how relative importance shifts when each developmental period is modeled separately. The composition of factors of importance changed across development, caregiver emotional functioning dominating early childhood before being superseded by individual behavioral characteristics as adolescence approached. Notably, no single ordering described all four periods. The third asked whether the factors of greatest importance differ between boys and girls, and at which periods any differences are most pronounced.

Proximal Behavioral Functioning Relative to Broader Domains of Risk

Adolescent behavioral characteristics dominated the full combined model. This is consistent with previous research identifying conduct problems among the strongest predictors of delinquency (Assink et al., 2015; Farrington & Malvaso, 2025; Murray & Farrington, 2010). The present findings extend that literature by demonstrating that the established ordering is preserved when these characteristics compete against a substantially wider predictor set than has previously been modeled concurrently (Li et al., 2026; Romero et al., 2025; Sui et al., 2025). Nevertheless, previous studies have also attributed predictive weight to early as well as concurrent conduct problems. In the present study, the strongest predictors were, with few exceptions, measured in mid-adolescence, the same period in which delinquency was reported. Parent-rated conduct problems ranked first, and behavioral and dispositional characteristics assessed at that age occupied most of the leading positions, with parental well-being being the only family context predictor, as distinct from parenting practices, among them. Conditions measured earlier in the family, school, and neighborhood domains were retained in the model but consistently outranked, which contradicts previous findings (Assink et al., 2015; Farrington & Malvaso, 2025; Murray & Farrington, 2010).

One interpretation of this ranking is developmental, in which the apparent dominance of proximal behavior reflects its position at the end of a developmental sequence rather than an independent contribution to risk. Developmental cascade models describe this arrangement, in which early risk shapes intermediate adaptation, which in turn shapes later conduct (Masten & Cicchetti, 2010). Applied here, behavior in adolescence would carry forward the influence of earlier conditions, such that conduct problems at mid-adolescence summarize processes already unfolded (Savolainen et al., 2015; Watts, 2018). Family, school, and neighborhood conditions measured in childhood would then exert their influence indirectly, through the behavioral adaptation they shape, and their contribution would already be represented in the proximal predictors that outrank them. Longitudinal evidence supports such indirect pathways. Sequences of risk spanning ages 8-10, 12-14, and 16-18 predicted later criminal convictions (Basto-Pereira et al., 2024). The most predictive sequences typically started with poverty, parenting problems, or risk-taking at ages 8-10, and the risk of conviction increased further when these were followed by low IQ or delinquent peers at ages 12-14, and by school and occupational problems or drug dependence at ages 16-18 (Basto-Pereira et al., 2024). Likewise, adverse childhood experiences occurring before age 11 predicted late-adolescent delinquency through sleep problems in early adolescence and low self-control in middle adolescence (Fava et al., 2023), and early attachment insecurity moderated subsequent socialization, with insecure dyads following a coercive sequence from child anger proneness through power-assertive discipline to rule-breaking conduct (Kochanska & Kim, 2012). Absorption of this kind appears partial rather than complete, as a direct association between early adversity and later delinquency has been found to persist once mediating pathways are accounted for (Fava et al., 2023).

A second interpretation attributes part of this ordering to measurement rather than to development. Parent-rated conduct problems and self-reported delinquency were assessed within the same developmental window and measure overlapping constructs, with aggression, rulebreaking, and defiance appearing in both, so some of the weight assigned to conduct problems may reflect similarity between the measures rather than prediction across development. Two considerations count against this, however. The first concerns who reported the measures. The two measures were completed by different informants, and meta-analytic estimates across 169 studies place agreement between parent and youth reports of behavioral problems at low to moderate (Huang, 2017), meaning shared informant variance is unlikely to account for the association. The second concerns what the measures capture. Confirmatory factor analysis in the Pittsburgh Youth Study indicates that conduct problems and delinquency are related but separable constructs rather than a single construct assessed twice (Pardini et al., 2006).

Measurement proximity may nonetheless inflate the margin by which conduct problems outranks predictors assessed years earlier. The present findings therefore suggest that behavioral functioning in adolescence indexes concurrent risk more strongly than any other domain assessed, while the size of that advantage remains conditional on measurement timing (Card, 2019; Taylor & Card, 2024).

The Reconfiguration of Risk Across Development

To address the possibility that temporal proximity inflated the influence of proximal predictors, and to establish if the relative importance of domains and individual factors shifted from early childhood through mid-adolescence, models were estimated separately at each developmental stage. Barring mid-adolescence, no predictor was measured at the outcome period, meaning that risk factors were estimated at a fixed developmental distance from delinquency in mid-adolescence. Two patterns emerged across these models. First, parent-rated conduct problems were predominantly the leading predictors at every period and for both genders. This finding is consistent with longitudinal population studies in which behavioral problems assessed in childhood are consistently reliable predictors of behavioral problems later in development (Oldehinkel & Ormel, 2023). Second, however, this finding was not stable across development, with caregiver emotional functioning possessing the greatest predictive weight during early childhood prior to being displaced by dispositional and behavioral characteristics as adolescence approached. This reordering provides a basis for distinguishing between the two interpretations of the full combined model outlined above.

Caregiver emotional functioning demonstrated a distinct developmental pattern. In the full combined model, it was the only family context predictor among the leading ranks, and was subsequently outperformed by behavioral characteristics assessed in mid-adolescence. In the early childhood model where no contemporaneous predictor was available, it demonstrated the greatest predictive weight of any factor. Its influence being superseded in the combined model may therefore reflect the presence of more proximal predictors rather than limited predictive value. Caregiver emotional difficulties are themselves an established risk factor for later behavioral problems, which is more consistent with the developmental interpretation than with the measurement interpretation addressed above. For example, there is meta-analytic evidence demonstrating an association between parental depressive disorder and child conduct problems (Aaron et al., 2024), and longitudinal research demonstrates both direct and indirect associations between parental emotional distress and adolescent delinquency (D. Liu & Vazsonyi, 2024).

Hence, if caregiver emotional functioning exerts influence during early childhood, and that influence is subsequently expressed through the child's dispositional and behavioral characteristics, it would be expected to demonstrate greatest importance before those characteristics have consolidated, and to be displaced thereafter. Prior longitudinal research supports transmission of this kind. Maternal depression has been shown to predict adolescent delinquency indirectly, through psychologically aggressive parenting (Marçal, 2021), and early adversity to predict later delinquency through intervening changes in the child rather than through a direct association (Fava et al., 2023; Kochanska & Kim, 2012).

One aspect of the present findings sits awkwardly with this account, however. Caregiver mental health has typically been positioned as a distal background condition operating through parenting practices, such that the parenting behaviors themselves should carry the greater proximal weight (Dotterer et al., 2021; Schmidt et al., 2021). At early and late childhood, however, parental well-being outranked the disciplinary and involvement measures through which its influence is presumed to operate. One interpretation is that caregiver emotional functioning captures variance in the household environment that discrete behaviors do not, encompassing the affective climate, predictability, and emotional availability that a caregiver under sustained strain may be less able to maintain irrespective of the strategies they report using. This is consistent with evidence that family profiles characterized by co-occurring adversities carry markedly greater risk than any single factor in isolation (LoBraico et al., 2020).

These findings do not establish such a pathway and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Our analyses demonstrated that continuity and change occur concurrently, rather than as isolated risk factors displacing one instead of the other. This is consistent with developmental accounts in which an early risk factor continues to exert influence, while the form that influence takes changes as the child develops (Masten & Cicchetti, 2010). For example, even though the predictive weight of caregiver emotional functioning declined across development, it is plausible that caregivers experiencing emotional difficulties initiate a cascading effect, whereby a parent's emotional state shapes a child's dispositional and behavioral characteristics, which in turn more strongly predict delinquency in adolescence. This reconfiguration was not confined to caregiver emotional functioning, but was evident, to a lesser degree, across the predictor set as a whole. At early and late childhood, the leading predictors were drawn predominantly from the family domain; by early adolescence, dispositional characteristics had begun to displace them; and by mid-adolescence, the ordering converged on behavioral characteristics and social competence for both genders. Prior work on shifting salience has demonstrated that the relative importance of risk-seeking and of parental and peer influences changes across mid-to-late adolescence (Childs et al., 2010). The present findings are consistent with this and suggest that such shifts are confined neither to adolescence nor to a narrow range of risk factors. Reconfiguration was already emerging between early and late childhood and involved the family domain giving way to the child's own characteristics rather than a reordering of predictors within the family domain itself.

Furthermore, this finding has consequences for the interpretation of cumulative risk approaches, in which each risk exposure often is dichotomized and summed into a single index, wherein the number of risks a child faces is quantified in place of modeling each factor individually (Evans et al., 2013). Such approaches have consistently demonstrated that accumulated risk predicts antisocial outcomes across development, from serious delinquency in early adolescence to violent behavior across the high school years, and do so over and above the individual factors comprising the index (Stoddard et al., 2012; Thomsen & Homel, 2024; van der Laan et al., 2010). However, by tallying factors rather than weighing them, such indices preserve the amount of risk a child is exposed to while obscuring which risks matter at a given age and how much they matter relative to one another. Cumulative risk research has been criticized for designating risk arbitrarily, discarding information on risk intensity, and precluding interaction between constituent factors (Evans et al., 2013), leading to dimensional alternatives being proposed in their place (McLaughlin & Sheridan, 2016). The present findings identify yet another limitation, which is developmental rather than psychometric. In the present study, the factors carrying greatest weight at ages three to five were not those carrying greatest weight at ages thirteen to sixteen. Our findings regarding caregiver emotional functioning are illustrative, as an index weighing this factor equally across periods would understate its contribution during early childhood and overstate it thereafter. Fundamentally, future cumulative risk indices may be strengthened by utilizing period-and domain-specific weighing, particularly where they are applied across samples of differing age composition.

Gender Differences in the Ordering of Risk

To address whether the factors carrying greatest weight, and the periods at which they did so differ between boys and girls, gender-stratified models were estimated at each developmental period. Findings suggested that at early childhood, parental well-being ranked first for boys and second for girls. During late childhood, parental well-being ranked highest for girls and parentrated conduct problems for boys. Differences were most apparent at early adolescence, where the interparental relationship ranked second for girls but ninth for boys, and grandiose-deceitful traits first for boys but ninth for girls. Callous-unemotional traits and neighborhood satisfaction were among the leading predictors for girls only, while parental well-being and impulsivity-need for stimulation were for boys only. By mid-adolescence however, parent-rated conduct problems and parent-rated social competence occupied the first two positions for both genders. The direction of these differences aligns with some prior findings but not others. Parental monitoring has been reported to have a more pronounced protective effect for girls than for boys (L. Liu & Miller, 2020), whereas lack of parental monitoring and parental rejection has been found to predict delinquency among boys but not among girls (Vujovic, 2024). In the present study, formal testing did not support these patterns as reliable heterogeneity, with generalized random forest calibration returning nonsignificant differential predictions at every period, and no predictor demonstrating a bootstrapped importance difference excluding zero. Given that some degree of divergence would ordinarily be expected, this null result requires interpretation.

First, both procedures were low-powered at the available gender-stratified sample sizes (girls n = 988; boys n = 1,133), with wave-specific training samples smaller still following attrition across follow-up and partitioning. Tests of whether an association differs between groups are substantially less powerful than tests of the association itself, and across 159 studies published in high impact journals, the median power to detect an interaction fell well below conventional standards (Sommet et al., 2023). For the calibration test used here, one applied evaluation has estimated that approximately 1,050 participants are required to reach 80% power against a moderate effect (Aktary et al., 2026). Simulation work further indicates that estimates of group differences stabilize only at sample sizes far larger than those commonly available, and that in small samples a substantial proportion indicate a difference in the opposite direction to the one present in the population (Castillo et al., 2026). The inconsistency between gender-stratified findings noted above may partly reflect these constraints rather than genuine variation in how risk operates. Therefore, these null results are interpreted as absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence (Altman & Bland, 1995; Tomek et al., 2026). The observed ranking differences may reflect sampling variability rather than distinct risk processes and are thus reported here as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

This omission is itself informative for a literature in which gender is most often entered as a covariate rather than used as a stratification strategy. Adjusting for gender estimates a difference in mean level while constraining the effect of every other predictor to be identical for boys and girls, so heterogeneity of the kind examined here cannot be observed at all. Where models are stratified, differences are frequently inferred by comparing estimates across them without formal testing, although concluding that two effects differ because one reaches significance and the other does not, is not a test of their difference (Gelman & Stern, 2006; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2011). The differences may be genuine but modest, with the present samples too small to detect them, a possibility supported by the wide confidence intervals and the numerical instability of the generalized random forest estimates at later periods. Alternatively, the rankings may be substantially equivalent across gender, with the core influences on delinquency operating similarly for boys and girls. The present data cannot distinguish these possibilities, and gender-specific risk processes therefore remain an open question requiring larger samples and confirmatory designs beyond the scope of the present study.

The Threshold Structure of Developmental Risk and Implications for Prevention

Risk factor research generally assumes that more risk means more delinquency, and that the association takes the form of a straight line. Higher callous-unemotional traits are associated with more severe conduct problems (Longman et al., 2016), antisocial behavior increases in line with accumulated risk (Thomsen & Homel, 2024), and a longitudinal test of whether cumulative risk operates as a threshold or as a gradient found support for the gradient, with no point beyond which further risk ceased to matter (Appleyard et al., 2005). Studies such as these have generally been taken to indicate that no meaningful boundary exists, and that risk therefore rises steadily across the range of a given measure (Haslam et al., 2020). The present findings indicated that this does not necessarily hold for individual risk factors in isolation. Specifically, predicted probability rose sharply across a narrow band of each leading risk factor and then plateaued well below the highest scores observed, suggesting that higher scores beyond this point were associated with little further increase in predicted delinquency. For example, parental well-being and parent-rated social competence crossed from risk-elevating to risk-reducing at mid-range scores on measures ranging from 1 to 4, in both cases below the cohort mean. The present findings may suggest that a locatable transition is present, and that it falls within the ordinary range of a community sample rather than at the extremes on which clinical samples are typically selected. This accords with evidence that children below diagnostic thresholds carry substantial risk, with subthreshold attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder associated with elevated rates of juvenile delinquency, family dysfunction, and school difficulty comparable to those observed at full threshold (Kirova et al., 2019). If the level at which risk changes lay among ordinary scores, the children to whom preventive effort would apply are not those whom clinical services typically reach.

Three implications follow for practice. First, the predictive weight carried by caregiver well-being at early and late childhood indicates that caregiver emotional functioning across childhood should be considered as a target for delinquency prevention in its own right, rather than solely as a background condition. This is actionable, since services reaching caregivers of young children already exist.

Second, social competence was the strongest protective factor in the full combined model. By mid-adolescence it occupied one of the two leading positions in both the boys' and girls' models, one of the few points at which the gender-stratified rankings converged. A target that operates similarly across gender is more readily addressed through universal prevention than one requiring separate approaches for boys and girls. This identifies it as a favorable target, and meta-analytic evidence from randomized evaluations indicates that social skills training produces gains that are maintained at follow-up (Beelmann & Lösel, 2006).

Third, where risk is distributed as a threshold rather than a gradient, the group that would benefit is identifiable rather than diffuse. Screening and referral typically select children showing high or clinically elevated levels of a behavior, on the assumption that risk increases with severity and that those furthest along the distribution have most to gain. Teachers identify severe conduct and internalizing problems accurately, but are considerably less likely to judge children with moderate or subclinical symptoms as requiring services (Splett et al., 2019), and underidentification of this kind contributes substantially to unmet need (Soneson et al., 2020). The present findings question the assumption behind this practice. Our results indicate that children whose scores fall just below conventional referral criteria, and therefore would be missed by provision organized around severity, likely would benefit from interventions. Comparable limitations have been demonstrated directly, with a randomized trial across 14 schools finding that universal screening identified substantially more adolescents with depressive symptoms, and led to more treatment initiation, than the usual practice of referring only those whose behavior prompted concern (Sekhar et al., 2021).

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the highest ranked predictor in the full combined model was assessed contemporaneously with the outcome and captures behavior conceptually proximal to delinquency, such that part of its weight may reflect construct overlap.

This is partly addressed by the period-specific models, on which the substantive conclusions rest, but future research should consider assessing delinquency at follow-ups subsequent to all predictors and employ cross-lagged or residual change specifications capable of distinguishing prediction of new involvement from behavioral continuity.

Second, no peer-level constructs were tested, despite deviant peer affiliation being among the most potent proximal predictors of adolescent delinquency (Huijsmans et al., 2021). Peer measures were available within the cohort, but only through adolescent self-report and only at the later assessment periods. Their inclusion would have introduced a third informant on the predictor side, so that associations between adolescent-reported predictors and adolescentreported delinquency would no longer be estimated across informants, and would have restricted the developmental span across which predictors could be compared. Social competence and attentional difficulties are theoretically proximal to peer processes (Müller & Minger, 2013; Savolainen et al., 2015), so some peer signal is plausibly represented indirectly, but the rankings reported here establish relative importance within the domains assessed rather than across the full space of documented predictors. Establishing whether peer processes would displace the predictors identified here requires measures reported by an informant other than the adolescent, or a design in which within-reporter and across-reporter associations are compared directly. This is an area that should be further investigated in future studies.

Third, several predictors were highly intercorrelated, notably impulsivity-need for stimulation with hyperactive symptoms across all periods. Although random forest is not destabilized by multicollinearity, intercorrelation may split signal between correlated variables and depresses the apparent importance of each (Gregorutti et al., 2017). Convergence between permutation importance and SHAP values indicates the broad ordering is robust, but importance within correlated clusters should be interpreted cautiously, and conditional permutation importance (Strobl et al., 2008) or grouped measures would be of value in future research.

Fourth, the current sample was drawn from a single demographically homogeneous municipality, and several predictors showed pronounced distributional restriction, including a severe ceiling for corporal punishment and floors for the psychopathic trait dimensions and the ecological measures. The modest contribution of the ecological domain may therefore partly reflect limited variability rather than limited developmental relevance, and replication in samples with greater socioeconomic and residential heterogeneity is required. A further boundary concerns developmental scope. The present analysis spans preschool age to mid-adolescence and cannot establish whether the reconfiguration observed continues into adulthood. Establishing when across childhood and adolescence the configuration of risk reorganizes is nonetheless a precondition for asking whether it persists. The children in the SOFIA-study have been followed into late adolescence and are entering emerging adulthood, making that extension directly testable within the same sample rather than requiring inference across studies.

Fifth, the tests of gender heterogeneity were low-powered. Both the generalized random forest calibration test and the bootstrapped importance difference procedure are demanding of sample size, particularly across numerous predictors per period, and the gender-stratified samples available here are likely insufficient to detect genuine but modest differences.

Generalized random forest estimates additionally demonstrated numerical instability at the later periods. The null heterogeneity results should accordingly be read as absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence (Altman & Bland, 1995; Tomek et al., 2026). Equivalence testing through the two one-sided tests procedure would distinguish genuine equivalence from inconclusive evidence, but requires an a priori bound on what magnitude of difference in variable importance would be substantively meaningful. No such benchmark is currently established for permutation importance, and a bound chosen post hoc would not support the inference the test is designed to license (Tomek et al., 2026).

Sixth, cumulative missingness across 111 predictors necessitated imputation, which assumes data are missing at random conditional on observed predictors; systematic attrition related to unmeasured characteristics would not be accommodated. Multiple imputation with importance pooled across imputed datasets, alongside sensitivity analyses under departures from this assumption, would strengthen future work. Finally, variable importance quantifies contribution to predictive accuracy rather than effect magnitude, and neither permutation importance nor SHAP attribution supports causal interpretation.

From the APQ, positive parenting (3 items) assessed warm and affirming parenting behaviours such as praising and complimenting the child, and parental involvement (3 items) assessed active engagement in the child's daily life and emotional wellbeing. Both subscales are rated on a five-point scale ranging from1 = "never" to5 = "very often", with higher scores indicating more frequent engagement in these behaviours.

Corporal punishment (1 item) assessed physical disciplinary behaviour such as slapping the child when misbehaving, and inconsistent discipline (1 item) assessed the degree to which disciplinary responses varied across situations. Both are rated on a five-point scale ranging from1 = "almost every time" to5 = "never", and were reverse coded prior to analysis so that higher scores indicate more frequent use of these behaviours.

Nonviolent discipline (5 items) was assessed using items drawn from both the APQ and the CTSPC nonviolent discipline subscale, covering responses to misbehaviour such as calmly explaining why the behaviour was wrong, ignoring the behaviour, or withdrawing privileges, rated on the same five-point scale, and was reverse coded prior to analysis so that higher scores indicate more frequent use of these behaviours.

Psychological aggression (2 items)

was assessed using the CTSPC psychological aggression subscale, covering verbally aggressive responses to the child such as shouting or using profanity, rated on the same five-point scale, and was reverse coded prior to analysis so that higher scores indicate more frequent use of these behaviours.

Parental wellbeing was assessed using a 2-item parent-rated measure covering general life enjoyment, rated on a four-point scale ranging from1 = "very good" to4 = "not good at all", and the degree of stress experienced in coping with the child over the preceding six months, rated on a four-point scale ranging from1 = "no" to4 = "yes, often". Both items were reverse coded prior to analysis so that higher scores consistently indicate better parental wellbeing.

Relationship with partner was assessed using a single parent-rated item asking how often the parent experienced dissatisfaction or conflict with their partner, rated on a five-point scale ranging from1 = "never" to5 = "very often". The item was reverse coded prior to analysis so that higher scores indicate a more positive partner relationship.

Parental perceived social support was assessed using a 2-item parent-rated measure capturing access to informal support networks and knowledge of where to seek help with financial, relational, or health-related problems, rated on a four-point scale ranging from1 = "does not apply" to4 = "applies very well", with higher scores indicating greater perceived support.

Parental concern regarding the child's development was assessed using a 7-item parent-rated measure capturing the degree of worry experienced across physical health, learning, language, emotional development, mental health, social development, and behaviour during the preceding six months. Items are rated on a five-point scale ranging from1 = "not at all worried" to5 = "very worried", with higher scores indicating greater parental concern.

School and social adjustment: School adjustment was assessed using a 2-item measure developed specifically for the SOFIA study, rated by teachers on a four-point scale ranging from1 = "does not apply" to4 = "applies very well". Items assess the degree to which the child appears to enjoy the school environment and demonstrates trust toward the educator. Higher scores indicate better school adjustment. School adjustment was not assessed during early adolescence.

Teacher-child relationship was assessed using a 2-item measure also developed for the SOFIA study, rated by teachers on a five-point scale ranging from1 = "never" to5 = "very often". Items assess the degree to which the child seeks out the teacher for comfort and support, and conversely the degree to which the teacher finds the child difficult to manage, capturing both the affiliative and conflictual dimensions of the relationship. Higher scores indicate a more positive teacher-child relationship.

Social competence was assessed using a 5-item measure rated independently by both parents and teachers (Frogner et al., 2022), covering prosocial and interpersonal skills such as greeting others appropriately, maintaining eye contact during conversation, responding politely when addressed, and helping out at home and in school. Items are rated on a four-point scale ranging from1 = "does not apply" to4 = "applies very well", with higher scores indicating greater social competence.

Neighbourhood, housing, and socioeconomic context: Economic strain was assessed using a single parent-rated item asking whether the family had experienced difficulty covering current financial expenses such as rent or instalments during the preceding 12 months. The item is rated on a five-point scale ranging from1 = "no" to5 = "yes, for 9-12 months", with higher scores indicating more persistent economic strain.

Housing and living conditions were assessed using two separate parent-rated single items.

Housing satisfaction was rated on a four-point scale ranging from1 = "not at all satisfied" to4 = "very satisfied". Neighbourhood satisfaction was a binary item indicating whether the family wished to remain living in their current residential area (1 = yes,2 = no), reverse coded prior to analysis so that higher scores indicate greater neighbourhood satisfaction.

Teacher concern about the child's home conditions was assessed using a single teacher-rated item capturing concern regarding parenting ability, security, and housing, rated on a four-point scale ranging from1 = "not at all worried" to4 = "very worried", with higher scores indicating greater teacher concern.

Outcome

Delinquency was assessed during mid-adolescence using 22 self-report items drawn from the Swedish National Biannual School Survey on Crime (Swedish National Council for Crime Prevention, 2022), covering three domains: theft (13 items), vandalism (4 items), and violence (5 items). Participants reported how often they had engaged in each behaviour during the preceding 12 months on a six-point scale ranging from1 = "never" to6 = "more than 20 times". Each domain was dichotomised into a binary indicator (0 = no engagement,1 = any engagement), and the three domain indicators were subsequently combined into a single binary outcome variable coded 0 if the individual reported no engagement across all three domains and 1 if they endorsed at least one act of theft, vandalism, or violence.

Supplementary File D. Generalized Random Forest Analyses of Gender Heterogeneity

To formally test whether gender differences in predictor effects on delinquency risk reflect statistically reliable heterogeneity rather than sampling variability, two complementary analyses were conducted. First, generalized random forest (GRF) models were estimated separately at each developmental period using the grf package in R (Athey et al., 2019). GRF extends the random forest framework to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects, in this case operationalising gender as the treatment variable (girls = 1, boys = 0) and testing whether the effect of gender on delinquency risk varies as a function of the predictor variables included in the model. Calibration was assessed using the test_calibration function, which fits a linear model regressing the outcome on the mean forest prediction and the differential forest prediction. A significant differential forest prediction coefficient indicates that the model captures reliable heterogeneity in treatment effects -in this context, that predictor effects on delinquency risk differ meaningfully across gender beyond what would be expected by chance.

Second, to directly test whether specific predictors of theoretical interest showed reliably different importance for boys versus girls, bootstrapped importance difference scores were computed for seven predictors across all four developmental periods: parental wellbeing, relationship with partner, parent social competence, parent externalising problems, inconsistent discipline, grandiose-deceitful traits, and teacher internalising problems. These variables were selected on the basis of showing the most pronounced gender differences in importance rankings in the primary analysis. For each bootstrap iteration (n = 1,000), gender-stratified training sets were resampled with replacement, a random forest model was estimated for each gender, and the difference in permutation importance between girls and boys was computed for each variable. Bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals were computed from the empirical distribution of importance differences, with a confidence interval excluding zero indicating a statistically reliable gender difference in predictor importance.

GRF results are presented in Supplementary Table F1. The mean forest prediction was significant at early childhood (p = .008), confirming that the model captures meaningful overall signal at this developmental period, but was non-significant at late childhood, early adolescence, and mid-adolescence, suggesting limited overall model signal at these waves under the GRF framework. The differential forest prediction was non-significant at all four developmental periods (p = .979, 1.000, .286, and .059 at early childhood, late childhood, early adolescence, and mid-adolescence respectively), indicating no statistically reliable evidence of gender heterogeneity in predictor effects at any developmental period. The mid-adolescence result approached but did not reach conventional significance (p = .059), consistent with the pattern of more pronounced gender differences in importance rankings observed at this developmental period in the primary analysis.

Bootstrapped importance difference results are presented in Supplementary Table F2. No variable showed a statistically reliable gender difference in permutation importance at any developmental period, with all 95% confidence intervals including zero. The largest mean differences were observed for parental wellbeing at early childhood (mean diff = -.015, 95% CI -.033-.003, favouring boys) and early adolescence (mean diff = -.011, 95% CI -.025-.002, favouring boys), and for grandiose-deceitful traits at early adolescence (mean diff = -.016, 95% CI -.034-.001, favouring boys), with confidence intervals at these waves approaching but not excluding zero. These patterns are consistent with a trend toward boys showing somewhat stronger effects for parental wellbeing and grandiose-deceitful traits during early and middle childhood and early adolescence, but this trend does not reach statistical reliability at the current sample size.

Taken together, both analyses converge on the same conclusion: the gender differences observed in variable importance rankings across the wave-specific models should be interpreted descriptively and with caution rather than as evidence of fundamentally distinct risk processes for boys and girls. The observed ranking differences may reflect natural sampling variability rather than genuine gender-specific predictor effects.

Several important limitations of these analyses should be acknowledged. First, both the GRF and the bootstrapped importance difference approach are relatively low-powered in samples of this size, particularly when applied to high-dimensional predictor spaces with 27 to 28 variables per wave. The sample sizes available for gender-stratified models (girls n = 988, boys n = 1,133) may be insufficient to reliably detect genuine but modest gender differences in predictor effects, and null results should therefore be interpreted as absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence. Second, the GRF framework operationalises gender as a binary treatment variable and estimates heterogeneity in its aggregate effect on delinquency, which is a somewhat indirect test of whether individual predictors operate differently across gender. The bootstrapped importance difference approach addresses this limitation more directly but is itself subject to the instability of permutation importance estimates in small gender-stratified subsets. Third, the high dimensionality of the predictor space, combined with the intercorrelations among predictors documented in Supplementary File A, may reduce the sensitivity of both tests to detect heterogeneity that is present but distributed across multiple correlated predictors. Fourth, the instability of the mean forest prediction estimates at later waves, as reflected in the very large standard errors at early adolescence (SE = 7.454) and mid-adolescence (SE = 25.067), suggests numerical instability in the GRF estimates at these developmental periods, which further limits the interpretability of the GRF calibration test results at these waves.

Given these limitations, both sets of analyses are treated as exploratory and are not used to draw firm conclusions about the presence or absence of gender heterogeneity. The wave-specific gender-stratified models presented in the primary analysis remain theoretically motivated and descriptively informative, and the patterns observed in importance rankings across genders are discussed as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than as confirmed evidence of genderspecific risk processes.

The 80/20 model produced an AUC of .795, virtually identical to the standard model (.787), with comparable accuracy (.721 vs .735), sensitivity (.896 vs .889), and a somewhat lower specificity (.442 vs .490). Variable importance rankings were highly consistent across the two models. Parent-rated conduct problems in mid-adolescence remained the top predictor, with ADHD inattentive in midadolescence, parent social competence in mid-adolescence, and impulsivity-need for stimulation in mid-adolescence all remaining in the top five across both models. Parental wellbeing in midadolescence remained a top-10 predictor (rank 7), and parental wellbeing in late childhood remained in the top 20 (rank 19), confirming that the contribution of parental wellbeing to the model is robust to the choice of train/test split. These findings confirm that the primary results are not an artefact of a specific data partition.

F4. Threshold Sensitivity Analysis

To assess whether model performance was sensitive to the choice of classification threshold, predictions from the full combined model were evaluated at three thresholds (0.3, 0.4, and 0.5) on the held-out test set. The default threshold of 0.5 was used in the primary analysis for consistency with standard practice in the machine learning literature, but given the class imbalance in the outcome variable this choice warranted examination. AUC is unaffected by threshold choice as it evaluates discrimination across all possible thresholds, and remains the primary performance metric throughout.

As expected, lowering the threshold from 0.5 to 0.4 and 0.3 produced a systematic trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. At a threshold of 0.3, specificity increased substantially (.912 vs .490) while sensitivity decreased sharply (.366 vs .889). At a threshold of 0.4, a more balanced profile emerged, with sensitivity of .662 and specificity of .750, producing the highest balanced accuracy across all three thresholds (.706 vs .690 at 0.5 and .639 at 0.3). The standard threshold of 0.5 produced the highest sensitivity (.889) but lowest specificity (.490), with the high sensitivity at this threshold arguably reflecting the model's tendency to classify most individuals into the majority non-delinquent class rather than genuine discriminative ability.

These findings suggest that a threshold of 0.4 may have been a more appropriate choice for this dataset, producing a more balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity and the highest balanced accuracy. However, two limitations of adopting a non-standard threshold should be acknowledged. First, selecting a threshold based on observed test set performance introduces a form of optimization bias, the chosen threshold performs well on this specific test set but may not generalize to new samples, a concern that would require validation in an independent dataset. Second, the optimal threshold depends on the relative costs of false positives and false negatives in the specific applied context, which were not formally specified in the present study. In the absence of a clearly defined cost function, the standard threshold of 0.5 was retained in the primary analysis to facilitate comparability with other published studies, and AUC is reported as the primary performance metric given its insensitivity to threshold choice.

Supplementary File F SMOTE. Sensitivity Analyses F1. Complete Case Analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the missForest imputation approach and assess whether the imputation procedure introduced bias into the results, the full combined model was re-estimated using only individuals with complete data across all predictor variables. If imputation had introduced substantial bias, the complete case model would be expected to produce broadly similar variable importance rankings and model performance to the imputed model despite the smaller sample. Conversely, large discrepancies in performance between the two models would suggest that sample size rather than imputation bias is the primary driver of any differences.

Listwise deletion reduced the analytic sample from 2,121 to only 90 individuals, representing a loss of 96% of the sample. This dramatic reduction reflects the cumulative effect of incorporating 111 predictor variables, each contributing some degree of missingness arising from item-level non-response and the substantial longitudinal attrition characteristic of cohort studies spanning multiple developmental periods. As the number of predictors increases, the probability that any given individual has fully observed data across the entire predictor set declines multiplicatively, since each additional variable introduces an independent opportunity for missingness. With 111 variables drawn from four separate data collection waves spanning thirteen years, even modest item-level missingness at each wave compounds substantially across the full predictor set.

A sample of 90 individuals was judged insufficient to support a meaningful train/test partition or stable random forest estimation, particularly given the moderate class imbalance in the outcome variable, which would leave an impractically small number of delinquent cases in any held-out test set. Attempting model estimation under these conditions would produce highly unstable and uninterpretable performance estimates rather than a meaningful robustness check. This finding itself constitutes informative evidence: it demonstrates that a complete case approach is not analytically viable once the predictor set is expanded to capture the full range of individual, family, and ecological risk factors relevant to delinquency, and underscores that missForest imputation was not merely beneficial but a methodological necessity for enabling model estimation with this predictor set.

F2. SMOTE Rebalancing Check

To assess whether the moderate class imbalance in the outcome variable affected model performance or variable importance rankings, the full combined model was re-estimated using a training set rebalanced through synthetic minority oversampling (SMOTE), which generates synthetic observations for the minority class until both classes are equally represented. SMOTE was applied to the training set only, producing a balanced training sample of 978 delinquent and 978 non-delinquent individuals, while the held-out test set was left unchanged to ensure an unbiased evaluation of out-of-sample performance on the original class distribution. The SMOTE model produced an AUC of .763 on the held-out test set, marginally lower than the conventionally trained model (.787). Accuracy was slightly lower (.720 vs .735), while specificity was notably higher (.564 vs .490) and sensitivity was slightly lower (.819 vs .889).

These differences reflect the expected trade-off when rebalancing classes, with the model becoming less forceful in predicting the majority class, resulting in improved specificity at the cost of some sensitivity. Variable importance rankings were broadly consistent across the two models. Teacher-and parent-rated conduct problems at mid-adolescence remained among the most important predictors in both models. The overall pattern of mid-adolescence and early adolescence predictors dominating the upper rankings was preserved, with parent-rated social competence, hyperactive symptoms, impulsivity-need for stimulation, inattentive symptoms, and grandiose-deceitful traits at mid-adolescence, and teacher-rated conduct problems at early adolescence, all remaining in the top 20 across both models. Parental wellbeing at midadolescence and late childhood remained meaningful predictors under both approaches, appearing in the top 20 of each model. Minor differences were observed in the relative rankings of some predictors, most notably teacher-rated conduct problems at mid-adolescence rising from rank 6 in the conventionally trained model to rank 1 in the SMOTE model, and grandiosedeceitful traits at early adolescence rising from rank 7 to rank 3. These shifts are modest and do not alter the substantive interpretation of the findings. The overall consistency in variable importance rankings between the two models confirms that the class imbalance did not meaningfully bias the importance rankings in the primary analysis, and that the pattern of results is robust to the choice of class balancing approach.
