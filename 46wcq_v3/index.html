Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Whether bilinguals share the same neural mechanisms for processing their first language (L1) and second language (L2) is a question of broad interest to researchers. Existing studies have accumulated substantial evidence, yet findings remain divergent. The primary sources of inconsistency lie in the language distance between bilinguals' L1 and L2, as well as differences in experimental tasks. Leveraging the advantages of meta-analysis, the present study examined brain activation patterns during L1 and L2 lexical processing in two groups of bilinguals: Chinese-English bilinguals with distant language distance (logographically-alphabetic) and alphabeticalphabetic bilinguals with close language distance-across different tasks (phonological and semantic). Results showed that in phonological tasks, Chinese-English bilinguals recruited the left inferior parietal lobule/supramarginal gyrus more for L2, a region implicated in orthography-tophonology conversion; in contrast, alphabetic bilinguals with close language distance shared overlapping neural mechanisms for L1 and L2. In semantic tasks, Chinese-English bilinguals engaged the left middle frontal gyrus and left precuneus more for L1 to support orthography-tosemantics conversion. In contrast, alphabetic bilinguals recruited the left middle temporal gyrus more for L1 for phonology-to-semantics conversion, and engaged language-control regions including the left middle frontal gyrus/inferior frontal gyrus, insula, and inferior parietal lobule more for L2. Further comparisons of brain activation during language-switching tasks between the two bilingual groups also revealed that alphabetic bilinguals with close language distance required greater involvement of executive control regions. Collectively, these findings indicate that the differential brain activation patterns observed when bilinguals process L1 and L2 arise from differences in underlying cognitive processes, jointly driven by language distance and task demands.

1 Introduction

Whether the bilingual brain reads words in two languages via shared neural mechanisms is a question that has garnered extensive attention and debate among researchers. Some investigators argue that bilinguals share the same neural network for processing their first language (L1) and second language (L2) (Brignoni-Perez et al., 2020; Hernandez et al., 2015), whereas others contend that bilinguals develop a second-language-specific neural network (Kim et al., 2016; Nichols & Joanisse, 2016). The long-standing divergence in findings stems, on the one hand, from the fact that the language distance (i.e., linguistic similarity) between L1 and L2 modulates whether bilinguals share overlapping neural substrates (Cargnelutti et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2016; Li et al., 2021; Shen & Del Tufo, 2022). On the other hand, existing results have been obtained under specific processing tasks, and bilinguals require distinct neural networks when performing phonological, semantic, and executive-control tasks (Comstock & Oliver, 2021; Sulpizio et al., 2020). Therefore, it is necessary to examine, across different task conditions, whether bilinguals with varying language distances share a common neural network for lexical processing in both languages. This complex issue is difficult to resolve through individual experimental studies alone; the present study leverages the advantages of meta-analysis to address it in depth.

Language distance refers to the difference in orthographic depth between two languages, reflecting the degree of similarity between them. Differing orthographic depths between bilinguals' languages may entail distinct underlying processing mechanisms (Oliver et al., 2017). Chinese characters are morphograms (logographs) whose orthography-to-phonology correspondences are irregular; they have deep orthographic depth and are considered opaque scripts. In contrast, alphabetic scripts such as Spanish and English are phonograms (alphabetic scripts) with relatively shallow orthographic depth; among them, Spanish can be decoded directly from orthography to phonology and is thus a transparent orthography. Previous studies have shown that orthographic depth influences phonological and semantic access during lexical processing (Mei et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2013). For deep-orthography scripts like Chinese, phonological processing relies more on the lexical route, tending to access semantics via orthography (Han & Bi, 2009; Tan et al., 2005); for shallow-orthography scripts like Spanish and English, phonological processing relies more on the sublexical route, tending to access semantics via phonology (Braun et al., 2009; Mei et al., 2015).

suggesting that these group differences reflect the influence of language distance on language-control regions. However, few existing studies have distinguished task demands, leaving the underlying mechanisms of these effects unclear.

Findings indicate that task demands can modulate bilingual activation networks; bilinguals recruit distinct neural networks when performing phonological, semantic, and executive-control tasks (Comstock & Oliver, 2021; Sulpizio et al., 2020). Even the same bilinguals exhibit different activation patterns under varying task demands (Ding et al., 2003; Vingerhoets et al., 2003). For example, Chinese-English bilinguals showed L1-L2 differences in bilateral inferior parietal lobule and bilateral postcentral gyrus during an orthographic task, whereas in a semantic task, differences emerged in the left middle frontal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, and right inferior frontal gyrus (Ding et al., 2003). The specific computational demands hypothesis posits that brain regions activated by L2 are the same as those activated by L1, and that activation differences between L2 and L1 arise solely from different computational demands (Perani & Abutalebi, 2005). Bilingual activation differences observed under specific tasks are accordingly linked to the underlying computational requirements, which are shaped by both bottom-up linguistic properties and top-down task demands (Dang et al., 2024). Therefore, it is necessary to examine in detail the mechanisms by which language distance influences bilingual processing in conjunction with task demands.

Some researchers have attempted to explore the effect of language distance on the neural mechanisms of lexical processing under phonological tasks, and have found that the closer the language distance between L1 and L2, the smaller the activation differences between the two languages. For instance, when Korean-Chinese-English trilinguals performed a visual lexical rhyming judgment task, Korean and English (with close language distance) showed differential activation only in the left inferior frontal gyrus, whereas Korean and Chinese (with distant language distance) engaged differential activation across multiple regions including frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes, leading the authors to conclude that language distance modulates bilingual activation differences in phonological processing (Kim et al., 2016). Languages with small language distance tend to recruit similar phonological processing pathways, and the activated phonological regions are consequently more similar. This interpretation is supported by representational similarity analysis: in a visual word naming task with Uyghur-Chinese-English trilinguals, representational similarity in phonological regions between Uyghur and English (close distance) was significantly greater than that between Uyghur and Chinese (distant distance) (Dong et al., 2021). These studies suggest that under phonological tasks, bilinguals with close language distance employ similar phonological processing pathways and show greater neural overlap.

To date, no study has systematically investigated the impact of language distance on brain activation patterns in bilinguals under semantic tasks. Existing evidence has shown that under semantic task demands, L1 activates more semantic-processing regions, whereas L2 recruits more executive-control regions (Comstock & Oliver, 2021; Sulpizio et al., 2020). This difference may be related to the orthographic depth and language distance between bilinguals' L1 and L2. According to the orthographic depth hypothesis, shallow-orthography scripts (e.g., English, Spanish) have regular orthography-to-phonology correspondences, leading readers to access semantics preferentially via phonological mediation (Braun et al., 2009; Tan & Perfetti, 1999); in contrast, deep-orthography scripts like Chinese have more direct orthography-to-semantics mappings, so readers may rely primarily on orthographic information for semantic access (Han & Bi, 2009; Law et al., 2006). Hence, the language distance between bilinguals' languages may influence the semantic neural pathways engaged during semantic processing, making language distance a potential key factor modulating L1-L2 activation differences in bilinguals.

Language control processing is a topic of broad interest in bilingual research. How language distance affects bilingual language control remains debated, giving rise to two opposing hypotheses.

One is the cross-language interference hypothesis (Coderre & van Heuven, 2014; Radman et al., 2021), which proposes that the smaller the language distance between L1 and L2, the stronger the cross-linguistic interference, and thus the more language-control resources are required.

Researchers comparing behavioural and electrophysiological responses of bilinguals with different language distances during executive-control tasks found that bilinguals with close language distance showed shorter reaction times and larger ERP amplitudes, indicating greater executive-control engagement (Coderre & van Heuven, 2014; Radman et al., 2021). An opposing view is the cross-language facilitation hypothesis (Costa et al., 2008; Finkbeiner et al., 2006), which argues that the smaller the language distance, the more shared linguistic representations, thereby facilitating language selection and requiring fewer language-control resources. For example, researchers have found that bilinguals with distant language distance require more attentional control and language-switching resources (Linck et al., 2008).

However, it is difficult to examine the joint effects of language distance and task demands within a single experimental design through empirical studies. Meta-analysis, by contrast, can integrate data from multiple studies and simultaneously assess the influence of multiple variables, thereby effectively disentangling complex scientific issues involving multi-factor interactions (Eickhoff et al., 2009). Moreover, meta-analysis performs quantitative statistical analyses on activation coordinates reported in a large number of studies, identifies consistency in spatial patterns, reduces inter-study heterogeneity, and enhances the robustness and reproducibility of conclusions (Fox et al., 1998; Wager et al., 2005). Several previous studies have attempted to use meta-analysis to investigate the effect of language distance on bilingual processing. For instance, Li et al. (2021) examined the modulatory role of language distance on brain activation during reading in bilinguals.

Another meta-analysis examined the influence of language distance on activation in language-control regions (Cargnelutti et al., 2022). However, these studies did not distinguish task demands, leaving the mechanisms underlying the effect of language distance on bilingual processing unclear. Research has shown that task demands can modulate the neural networks engaged in bilingual processing (Sulpizio et al., 2020). Recent multivariate pattern analyses have also found that task demands induce changes in bilingual neural activation patterns, indicating that task demands are a key variable that warrants attention when investigating the neural mechanisms of bilingual processing (Li et al., 2023).

Therefore, the present study employed meta-analysis to concurrently examine the effects of language distance and task demands on brain activation during lexical processing in bilinguals.

Specifically, by comparing brain activations between Chinese-English bilinguals (distant language distance) and alphabetic-alphabetic bilinguals (close language distance) across phonological, semantic, and language-switching tasks, we aimed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which language distance and task demands modulate bilingual processing. Considering the processing requirements of different tasks and the differences in linguistic properties between L1 and L2, we formulated the following predictions: (1) In phonological tasks, because both L1 and L2 in alphabetic bilinguals with close language distance rely on orthography-to-phonology conversion, activation differences between the two languages in phonological regions should be relatively small, whereas Chinese-English bilinguals with distant language distance should exhibit larger activation differences. (2) In semantic tasks, bilinguals with distant language distance engage different processing pathways for L1 and L2, and thus should show greater differences in semantic-processing regions. (3) In language-switching tasks, by comparing activation differences between the two bilingual groups in language-control-related regions, particularly in executive-control regions such as frontal and parietal cortices, we can examine whether L1-L2 linguistic similarity exerts a facilitating or interfering effect, thereby further testing the cross-language interference hypothesis versus the cross-language facilitation hypothesis.

2 Methods

2.1 Literature Search

A literature search was conducted in databases (Web of Science, PubMed, and CNKI) using the keywords "bilingual" and "neuroimaging" to retrieve studies published before May 2023.

The retrieved records were screened through a two-step process of inclusion and exclusion.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) studies published between 1990 and 2023; (2) experimental tasks focusing on phonological, semantic, or language-control processing; (3) experimental materials restricted to lexical stimuli; (4) participants were healthy bilinguals without reading disorders. After reviewing the brain-imaging results, further exclusion criteria were applied: (1) participants were bimodal bilinguals; (2) activation coordinates were incompletely reported (e.g., region-of-interest analyses) or not reported at all; (3) contrast conditions did not match our research interests.

Ultimately, 74 studies met the inclusion criteria.

2.2 Quality Assessment

To evaluate the methodological quality of the included studies, we established a quality assessment checklist comprising 4 dimensions and 12 items, based on the best-practice reporting standards in neuroimaging (the COBIDAS guidelines). The dimensions covered: (1) experimental design (sample-size reporting, task control, between-group matching); (2) data processing and preprocessing (procedural transparency, spatial normalization, motion-control measures); (3) statistical analysis (multiple-comparison correction, software reporting, model adequacy); and (4) result reporting and transparency (coordinate completeness, spatial consistency, data-sharing statements). Compliance with each item was recorded as "yes" or "no". The results showed that the vast majority of studies (81.1%, 60/74) were of high quality (score ≥ 10), while the remaining (18.9%, 14/74) were of moderate quality (scores7~9). These findings provide methodological support for the robustness of the conclusions drawn in this meta-analysis.

2.3 Literature Classification

We focused primarily on the neural mechanisms underlying three types of experimental tasks in bilinguals: phonological processing, semantic processing, and language switching. Phonological processing tasks primarily involve the extraction and analysis of lexical phonological information, including rhyming judgment, overt reading, and homophone matching tasks (Wu et al., 2012).

Semantic processing tasks mainly concern the retrieval and analysis of lexical meaning, such as semantic judgment, lexical decision, and semantic generation tasks (Vigneau et al., 2006).

Language-switching tasks focus on the executive-control components involved in the bilingual switching process; accordingly, these tasks are relatively standardised, typically taking the form of language-switching paradigms (Tao et al., 2021).

Based on bilinguals' L1 and L2 profiles, we primarily compared two groups: Chinese-English bilinguals with distant language distance, and alphabetic-alphabetic bilinguals with close language distance (Cargnelutti et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021). The alphabetic-script bilingual group was not restricted to any specific pair of languages, as long as both L1 and L2 belonged to alphabetic writing systems. Although English has a deeper orthography compared to transparent scripts such as Spanish, both share systematic orthography-to-phonology correspondence rules; thus, bilinguals mastering such language pairs were also classified as having close language distance.

According to the classification by experimental task and participant type, the 74 included studies were further divided into six groups: 18 studies on phonological processing in Chinese-English bilinguals, 12 studies on phonological processing in alphabetic-script bilinguals; 12 studies on semantic processing in Chinese-English bilinguals, 13 studies on semantic processing in alphabetic-script bilinguals; 9 studies on language-switching in Chinese-English bilinguals, and 14 studies on language-switching in alphabetic-script bilinguals (Supplementary Table 1 online).

Among these, four studies were assigned to two relevant but distinct groups due to the multi-faceted nature of their experimental designs: Kim et al. (2017) was included in both the phonological-processing group for Chinese-English bilinguals and the phonological-processing group for alphabetic-script bilinguals; Wang, Lin et al. (2007) was included in both the phonological-processing group for Chinese-English bilinguals and the semantic-processing group for Chinese-English bilinguals; and Klein et al. (1995) and Jung et al. (2018) were included in both the phonological-processing group for alphabetic-script bilinguals and the semantic-processing group for alphabetic-script bilinguals.

2.4 Data Analysis

The present study employed a coordinate-based meta-analytic approach. All analyses were performed using GingerALE 3.0.2 software (available at http://www.brainmap.org/ale). This software implements the activation likelihood estimation (ALE) algorithm to detect spatial convergence of functional activation coordinates across multiple independent studies, thereby identifying brain regions that are consistently activated across experiments (Eickhoff et al., 2009; Eickhoff et al., 2012). We extracted sample sizes and activation coordinates from the included studies. In the ALE algorithm, sample sizes of individual studies are used for weighting to adjust their contribution to the final probability distribution maps, while activation coordinates are mapped to specific voxels in standard stereotaxic space. To ensure that all coordinates were in a unified space, for studies originally reporting MNI coordinates, we converted them to the Talairach coordinate system using the "MNI to Talairach" transformation tool built into GingerALE 3.0.2.

Following the ALE software manual (http://www.brainmap.org/ale/manual.pdf) and previous literature (Cargnelutti et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021), the meta-analysis comprised the following two parts: First, we conducted activation analyses for specific conditions. Such analyses compute the functional activation associated with a particular condition (e.g., L1 activation and L2 activation during phonological processing in Chinese-English bilinguals). In this study, we separately calculated activation clusters for L1 and L2 under phonological and semantic tasks in both bilingual groups, as well as activation clusters for language-switching tasks in both groups. The threshold for the activation analyses was set at cluster-level FWE-corrected p < 0.05 (with 1,000 permutations, voxel-level p < 0.005).

Second, we performed contrast analyses. Contrast analyses aim to compare activation maps between two conditions, yielding regions of common activation and differential activation between the two conditions (e.g., regions commonly activated by L1 and L2 during phonological tasks in Chinese-English bilinguals, regions more activated for L1, and regions more activated for L2). The threshold for contrast analyses was set at voxel-level p < 0.05, with 10,000 permutations, and a cluster extent threshold of > 100 mm³.

The meta-analytic results were visualized using BrainNet Viewer (available at http://www.nitrc.org/projects/bnv) and xjView (available at https://www.alivelearn.net/xjview). The template used in BrainNet Viewer was the BrainMesh_ICBM152_smoothed_tal.nv (Xia et al., 2013).

4 Discussion

The present study employed a meta-analytic approach to investigate how language distance between bilinguals' L1 and L2 affects the degree of shared neural mechanisms during lexical processing under different experimental tasks. The results showed that the closer the language distance between L2 and L1, the greater the overlap in brain activation, and that experimental tasks modulate this degree of shared activation. Specifically, in phonological tasks, Chinese-English bilinguals (with distant language distance) exhibited greater activation in the left inferior parietal lobule/supramarginal gyrus for L2 than for L1, whereas alphabetic-script bilinguals (with close language distance) recruited the same neural network for L1 and L2. In semantic tasks, Chinese-English bilinguals showed greater L1-related activation (relative to L2) in the left middle frontal gyrus and left precuneus; for alphabetic-script bilinguals, L1 recruited the middle portion of the left superior temporal gyrus more, while L2 recruited language-control regions including the left inferior frontal gyrus/middle frontal gyrus, insula, and inferior parietal lobule to a greater extent. The language-switching task also revealed that alphabetic-script bilinguals required greater recruitment of executive-control regions. These findings indicate that task demands modulate neural network engagement in a top-down manner, whereas language distance influences language processing and language control in a bottom-up manner. Further analysis suggests that task demands and language distance jointly modulate the underlying processing demands; consequently, it is essentially these underlying processing demands that dynamically shape the neural mechanisms of bilingual lexical processing. A detailed discussion is presented below.

4.1 Brain activation in phonological tasks

In the phonological tasks, the present study found that Chinese-English bilinguals with distant language distance showed greater activation in the left inferior parietal lobule/supramarginal gyrus for L2 than for L1, whereas alphabetic-script bilinguals with close language distance activated the same neural network for both L1 and L2. This is consistent with previous findings. Kim et al. (2016) compared brain activation between Korean and English (close language distance) and between Korean and Chinese (distant language distance) during a visual lexical rhyming judgment task, and found that activation differences were smaller for the close-distance language pair. Our finding that alphabetic-script bilinguals with close language distance activated overlapping neural networks for L1 and L2 further supports the notion that, in phonological tasks, the closer the language distance, the more similar the phonological processing pathways used by bilinguals, and thus the smaller the activation differences.

Mei and colleagues have shown that the left inferior parietal lobule/supramarginal gyrus is a key region in the dorsal phonological pathway, primarily subserving sublexical orthography-to-phonology conversion, which is critical for phonological processing of alphabetic scripts such as English (Mei et al., 2014; Mei et al., 2015). When Chinese-English bilinguals process their L1 phonology, the logographic nature of Chinese characters leads them to preferentially use the lexical route for direct whole-word phonological retrieval (Tan et al., 2005); however, when processing alphabetic L2 like English, they rely more on the sublexical route for orthography-to-phonology conversion (Tham et al., 2005). Thus, Chinese-English bilinguals recruit the left inferior parietal lobule/supramarginal gyrus-a region responsible for sublexical orthography-to-phonology mapping-more extensively during L2 phonological processing (Xue et al., 2017; You et al., 2011).

4.2 Brain activation in semantic tasks

In semantic tasks, Chinese-English bilinguals showed greater activation in the left middle frontal gyrus and left precuneus for L1 than for L2; for alphabetic-script bilinguals, L1 processing recruited the middle portion of the left superior temporal gyrus more, while L2 processing recruited language-control regions including the left inferior frontal gyrus/middle frontal gyrus, insula, and inferior parietal lobule more extensively. The meta-analysis by Sulpizio et al. (2020) also found that bilingual activation differences were relatively small in phonological tasks but larger in semantic tasks. Specifically, in semantic tasks, L1 tended to engage semantic-processing regions more, whereas L2 recruited language-control regions more; the present findings are consistent with this observation.

Regarding the function of the left middle frontal gyrus, studies have identified it as subserving semantic processing (Tan et al., 2000) or orthography-to-semantics mapping (Tan et al., 2001; Wu et al., 2012), while the left precuneus has been implicated in orthographic processing (Liu & Cao, 2016; Sun et al., 2015). In the present study, the co-activation of the left precuneus and left middle frontal gyrus during L1 processing in Chinese-English bilinguals supports the view that this circuit is involved in orthography-to-semantics conversion. Because Chinese characters are morphograms with more direct orthography-to-semantics mappings, readers rely preferentially on the orthographic route for semantic access (Han & Bi, 2009); thus, L1 processing in Chinese-English bilinguals recruits the left middle frontal gyrus (associated with orthography-to-semantics mapping) and the left precuneus (associated with orthographic processing) to a greater extent.

The middle portion of the left superior temporal gyrus connects the posterior superior temporal gyrus (involved in primary phonological analysis) with the anterior temporal lobe (a semantic hub), leading some researchers to propose that this region may participate in phonology-to-semantics conversion (Hickok, 2022). Alphabetic scripts have more regular orthography-to-phonology mappings and are processed more via phonological mediation to access semantics (Braun et al., 2009); accordingly, L1 processing in alphabetic-script bilinguals recruited the left superior temporal gyrus more, a region implicated in phonology-to-semantics conversion.

It is noteworthy that in semantic tasks, alphabetic-script bilinguals also showed greater L2-related activation in executive-control regions including the left inferior frontal gyrus/middle frontal gyrus, insula, and inferior parietal lobule. The left inferior frontal gyrus is sensitive to task difficulty (Abutalebi et al., 2008), the left middle frontal gyrus is involved in conflict resolution and attentional modulation (Badre & Wagner, 2004), the left insula participates in interference suppression (Wager et al., 2005), and the left inferior parietal lobule has been linked to stimulus-based language conflict (van Heuven et al., 2008). Related studies have also found that the non-dominant L2 requires greater executive-control resources to ensure successful language comprehension and production (Abutalebi et al., 2008; Hou et al., 2024). The present study found that, compared to phonological tasks, semantic tasks elicited stronger engagement of the executive-control network during L2 processing, which may be related to task difficulty (Dräger et al., 2004). The phonological tasks in this study primarily involved overt reading and rhyming judgment, which are relatively low in difficulty and require fewer executive-control resources; in contrast, semantic tasks mainly consisted of lexical decision and semantic judgment, which require participants to recruit greater executive-control resources to complete the decision-making process, thereby activating language-control regions (e.g., the frontoparietal control network) more extensively.

4.3 Comparison of brain activation between the two groups of bilinguals in language-switching tasks

More importantly, the present study also revealed the influence of linguistic properties on bilingual executive control in the language-switching task, manifested as greater activation in the frontoparietal control network-including the left inferior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, and inferior parietal lobule-in alphabetic-script bilinguals with close language distance. This finding further corroborates the observation in semantic tasks that alphabetic-script bilinguals recruited more language-control regions during L2 processing, indicating that bilinguals with close language distance require greater executive-control resources.

Previous studies comparing behavioural and electrophysiological responses of bilinguals with different language distances during executive-control tasks found that bilinguals with close language distance exhibited shorter reaction times and larger ERP amplitudes, suggesting stronger executive-control engagement (Coderre & van Heuven, 2014; Radman et al., 2021). The present neuroimaging finding of greater frontoparietal control activation in alphabetic-script bilinguals, together with these earlier results, supports the cross-language interference hypothesis, which posits that bilinguals with closer language distance experience stronger cross-linguistic interference and thus require more executive-control resources.

In addition, the present study found that Chinese-English bilinguals showed greater activation in the left lingual gyrus and right hippocampus/caudate nucleus during language-switching tasks.

The left lingual gyrus has been implicated in visual orthographic analysis (Sun et al., 2015); its stronger activation in Chinese-English bilinguals may reflect the larger orthographic differences between their two languages. The hippocampus is associated with declarative memory (Ullman, 2001), and the caudate nucleus is involved in language selection and monitoring (Crinion et al., 2006). Previous research has shown that when L2 proficiency is low, the left caudate shows stronger activation, reflecting greater effort in selecting and monitoring between the two languages (Abutalebi et al., 2013; Mouthon et al., 2020). The present finding of right caudate activation in Chinese-English bilinguals with distant language distance similarly suggests increased switching and monitoring demands between the two languages (Luk et al., 2011).

It should be noted, however, that the language-distance effects observed in this study cannot entirely rule out the potential influence of proficiency. Although we grouped bilinguals by language distance (Cargnelutti et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021) and observed differential brain activation between the two groups, the lack of a unified proficiency assessment standard for direct comparison between groups means that the potential influence of proficiency cannot be completely excluded. Proficiency reflects the learner's knowledge and ability to use the target language, whereas language distance refers to the degree of similarity between two languages. Currently, there is relative consensus regarding the effect of proficiency on bilingual processing: studies have shown that, compared with highly proficient bilinguals, low-proficiency bilinguals require more attentional and selection resources (Cargnelutti et al., 2019; Grant et al., 2015; Sebastian et al., 2011). In the present study, both semantic and language-switching tasks revealed greater activation in the left inferior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, and inferior parietal lobule in alphabetic-script bilinguals. Previous studies have found that the left inferior and middle frontal gyri are sensitive to phonological competition (Hsieh et al., 2017; Matsuo et al., 2010), and the left inferior parietal lobule is associated with stimulus-based language conflict (van Heuven et al., 2008). Thus, our findings may more likely reflect stronger cross-linguistic interference arising from closer language distance in alphabetic-script bilinguals, rather than increased attentional and selection resources due to lower proficiency. Future studies should disentangle proficiency levels to further clarify the independent and interactive effects of these two variables on bilingual neural mechanisms.

4.4 Underlying processing demands dynamically modulate the neural mechanisms of bilingual lexical processing

The present study found that differences in brain activation between bilinguals' two languages during lexical processing are related to differences in the underlying processing demands specific to each task. For instance, the left inferior parietal lobule/supramarginal gyrus, which is responsible for sublexical orthography-to-phonology conversion (Mei et al., 2015), is critically important for phonological processing of alphabetic scripts such as English; accordingly, Chinese-English bilinguals showed greater activation in this region when processing L2 in phonological tasks.

Similarly, the left middle frontal gyrus is associated with orthography-to-semantics mapping for Chinese characters (Wu et al., 2012) and is crucial for semantic access in Chinese; thus, Chinese-English bilinguals recruited this region more during L1 processing in semantic tasks. These findings indicate that it is essentially the underlying processing demands that dynamically modulate the neural mechanisms of bilingual lexical processing.

At the same time, these underlying processing demands are jointly shaped by task demands and stimulus properties, as reflected in the fact that activation in reading-related regions is influenced by the interaction between task demands and stimulus attributes (Pattamadilok et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2012). For example, activation in language-control regions was influenced by both task demands and language distance, such that stronger engagement of the frontoparietal control network in alphabetic-script bilinguals with close language distance was observed only in semantic and language-switching tasks. This suggests that the neural mechanisms of bilingual lexical processing are the result of dynamic adaptation to task demands and stimulus properties, jointly modulated by both factors.

In summary, the present study investigated the neural mechanisms of bilingual lexical processing from the perspective of the interaction between linguistic properties and task demands, revealing that bilingual lexical processing exhibits both cross-linguistic commonality and language-specificity. The core of this dynamic activation lies in the interaction between task demands and linguistic properties, which together shape the underlying processing demands and give rise to dynamic activation differences between the two languages. Recent studies employing multivariate analysis approaches (e.g., MVPA, RSA) have also found that bilinguals activate overlapping brain regions, yet show independent representational patterns at the finer voxel-wise level (Brignoni-Perez et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2017), and that these representational patterns are dynamic, modulated by linguistic properties (Dong et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024), task demands (Li et al., 2023), and other factors. The present study, at the level of brain activation, reveals that bilinguals share a common neural substrate, and that activation differences between the two languages are dynamically modulated by the underlying processing demands.

4.5 Theoretical contributions of the present study

By employing a meta-analytic approach, the present study examined the effects of language distance and task demands on the neural mechanisms of bilingual lexical processing. Our findings provide empirical support for the specific computational demands hypothesis (Perani & Abutalebi, 2005), which posits that brain regions recruited by L2 are identical to those recruited by L1, and that activation differences between L2 and L1 arise solely from differential computational demands.

Specifically, in phonological tasks, alphabetic-script bilinguals activated the same brain regions for both languages, indicating that when underlying processing demands are identical, the neural substrates for L1 and L2 are also shared. Furthermore, Chinese-English bilinguals showed greater L2-related activation in the left supramarginal gyrus (associated with orthography-to-phonology conversion; Mei et al., 2015) during phonological tasks, and greater L1-related activation in the left middle frontal gyrus (associated with orthography-to-semantics mapping; Wu et al., 2012) during semantic tasks. These findings indicate that activation differences between the two languages are tied to the specific computational demands required by each language in a given task.

Our results also provide insights into the ongoing debate between the language-universal view (Feng et al., 2020; Nakamura et al., 2012; Rueckl et al., 2015) and the language-specific view (Brennan et al., 2013; Zhan et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2014) from a bilingual perspective. The language-universal view holds that there exists a common reading mechanism across writing systems. In line with this view, our study found that both languages in bilinguals activated typical language-processing regions, suggesting that the neural substrates involved are shared across different languages. The language-specific view, on the other hand, maintains that different writing systems recruit specialised reading mechanisms. Supporting this view, our findings showed that Chinese-English bilinguals recruited specific brain regions to accommodate the unique properties of each script. A recent study also pointed out that although there are common processing principles across all writing systems, readers of different orthographies naturally adapt to the specific properties of their script for efficient comprehension of written text (Li et al., 2022). The present study provides neural-level evidence for this perspective and enriches our understanding of language processing.

4.6 Limitations and future directions

The following issues warrant further discussion and investigation:

First, although we observed an interaction between language distance and task demands, the ALE meta-analysis is essentially a method for estimating and contrasting activation under specific conditions; it cannot directly estimate the interaction effect of "language distance × task demands" within a unified statistical model as experimental studies can. Our inference regarding the interaction was primarily derived from contrast analyses under different conditions, followed by indirect logical integration and interpretation. Future studies could employ more refined experimental designs combined with multifactorial ANOVA to directly test this interaction empirically, thereby advancing the field toward a deeper stage of inquiry.

Second, the ALE meta-analysis provides only information at the level of regional brain activation and cannot further explore functional collaboration between brain regions. In recent years, brain network analyses have revealed that bilingual experience may be associated with more efficient and flexible reorganization and reconfiguration of brain networks (Feng et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2019). For instance, although bilinguals and monolinguals activate overlapping brain regions, bilinguals show stronger functional connectivity between the left superior temporal gyrus and left lingual gyrus, as well as between the left middle frontal gyrus and left anterior cingulate cortex (Brignoni-Pérez et al., 2022). Other studies have also found that bilingual experience modulates functional connectivity both within language regions (Oliver et al., 2017) and between language and executive-control regions (Gurunandan et al., 2019). Future research could take the brain regions identified in this study-those modulated by underlying processing demands-as regions of interest, and further investigate the impact of these processing demands on functional interactions across brain networks.

Third, the present study focused primarily on lexical-level processing; whether the conclusions can be generalized to sentence-and discourse-level processing remains to be verified. Previous studies have shown that as the reading unit expands from words to more natural sentences and passages, readers place greater emphasis on semantic and syntactic integration at larger units, while phonological and semantic access for smaller units such as words becomes more automatic, thereby reducing cross-linguistic differences (Wang et al., 2015). Future studies could compare bilingual activation across different reading units to further explore the neural mechanisms of bilingual processing at a more detailed level.

Finally, bilingual executive control is a topic of broad interest among researchers (Abutalebi et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2020; Tao et al., 2021). Although the present study provides preliminary evidence for the influence of language distance on bilingual executive control, the generalizability of our conclusions is limited by the relative homogeneity of the experimental paradigms included.

Future research could combine proficiency measures with diverse experimental paradigms (e.g., attentional selection, interference suppression) to conduct a more comprehensive and in-depth analysis of this issue. In addition, language-switching tasks are relatively less correlated with language properties such as phonology and semantics; it is worth reflecting on what kind of processing demands the cross-linguistic differences observed in this task actually reflect.

The present study simultaneously focused on language distance and task demands, and employed a meta-analytic approach to systematically examine brain activation in Chinese-English bilinguals and alphabetic-script bilinguals during phonological, semantic, and language-switching tasks. By comparing bilingual activation patterns between the two groups under different task demands, we found that the closer the language distance, the smaller the activation differences between L1 and L2 in language-processing regions (e.g., phonological and semantic areas), yet the higher the executive-control demands. These findings provide novel empirical evidence that language distance and task demands jointly modulate the neural mechanisms underlying bilingual lexical processing.
