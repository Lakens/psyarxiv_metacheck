To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Abstract

Objective: The purpose of this study was to investigate the reliability of change indices in exposure therapy.

We analyzed data from 119 adults with elevated social anxiety who participated in studies of public speaking exposures. Participants reported their distress using the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDS) at regular intervals. The reliability of within-session habituation, between-session habituation, and expectancy change measures were estimated.

Results: Commonly used measures of between-session habituation (BSH) typically demonstrated only moderate reliability, and commonly used measures of within-session habituation (WSH) typically demonstrated poor reliability. Exploratory tests in expectancy change indices suggests these findings apply to other measures of change during exposure.

Our results indicate that measures underlying the study of habituation may be inadequate because of low reliability. This threatens the foundation of clinical and translational research, such that no substantive conclusions can be drawn from them.

Poor reliability in measuring habituation: Implications for clinical and translational research on exposure and extinction research

Anxiety disorders are among the most prevalent mental health disorders and can cause significant impairment (Bandelow & Michaelis, 2015). Evidence-based therapies are therefore in high demand to meet the needs of those with an anxiety disorder. Among these therapies is exposure, one of the most effective techniques used to treat anxiety disorders (Abramowitz et al., 2019). The aim of exposure therapy is for clients to systematically confront their fears, typically leading to a gradual reduction of fear (Wolpe et al., 1958). Despite the efficacy of exposure, some clients do not respond to the treatment or see a return in symptoms after improving shortterm (Arch & Craske, 2009). This indicates the need for further empirical work to optimize exposure as an intervention (Benito et al., 2024).

Two influential mechanistic theories of exposure therapy have dominated the literature in recent years, emotional processing theory and inhibitory learning theory. Foa and Kozak's (1986) emotional processing theory posits that exposure therapy is most effective when fear is activated in session, then reduced within and between sessions. Within-session habituation (WSH) refers to fear response reduction during an exposure session, and between-session habituation (BSH) refers to fear response reduction across multiple exposure sessions. For example, for a client with a public speaking phobia, WSH would refer to the change in anxiety experienced over a single speech, and BSH would refer to the change of peak anxiety over two successive speeches. According to emotional processing theory as originally formulated, both WSH and BSH should predict treatment outcomes (Foa & Kozak, 1986). Measures of WSH and BSH approximate the change in one's fear structure during exposure. When exposure is successful, the patient's fear structure is changed such that the patient better estimates the true danger of the stimulus, which leads to a decreased emotional response when faced with the stimuli. However, the evidence for this is currently mixed. There is significant empirical evidence connecting habituation to treatment outcomes across diverse presenting problems and populations, although typically through BSH only (Elsner et al., 2022; Essayli et al., 2023; Foa et al., 1983; Jaycox et al., 1998; Kozak et al., 1988; Smith et al., 2021; Sripada & Rauch, 2015; van Minnen & Hagenaars, 2002; Zoellner et al., 2023). However, other studies indicate both BSH and WSH fail to predict the expected outcome (Bluett et al., 2014; Miegel et al., 2022; Pitman et al., 1996; Rowe & Craske, 1998). In response to such findings, some researchers have proposed alterative mechanistic theories, such as inhibitory learning theory (Bouton, 1993) Craske and colleagues (2008), building on the work of Bouton (1993) as well as other research focused on animals, proposed that exposure therapy is better understood via an inhibitory learning approach. Craske and colleagues posit that an alternative association developed through exposure therapy (e.g., safety) competes with (i.e., as opposed to replacing) the original excitatory associations of the fear (Craske et al., 2008). Guided by inhibitory learning theory, clinicians conduct exposures with an emphasis on developing new associations and enhancing the retrieval of those associations above the old associations, rather than the reduction of fear (Craske et al., 2008; Craske et al., 2014). Inhibitory learning theory has garnered enthusiasm from the field; however, there are few methodologically strong studies testing the efficacy of an inhibitory learning theory approach to exposure, and those that exist generally demonstrate modest effects (Authors, 2018). Researchers focused on this approach have proposed this may be due to the lack of measures developed to capture the complex change processes during exposure guided by this theory (Stewart & Craske, 2020). Such concerns lead us to examine how change during exposure has been measured.

Historical and Current Measurement Practices

Change in distress during exposure informed by emotional processing theory is often quantified by the Subjective Units of Distress Scale (SUDSfoot_0 ; Wolpe, 1988; Wolpe & Lazarus, 1966). The SUDS is a self-reported measure of anxiety on a 0 (no anxiety) to 100 (maximum possible anxiety) scale. Procedures for using the SUDS scale presently vary among researchers (Benito et al., 2024). In their early work, Kozak, Foa, and Steketee (1988) conceptualized the peak SUDS rating as a participant's highest self-reported fear during the first twenty minutes of a forty-five-minute exposure session (Kozak et al., 1988). They also measured end fear response, by averaging the SUDS scores at minutes forty and forty-five of the session. WSH was then defined as the difference between peak and end response during a session. In the same paper, researchers conceptualized BSH (across-session habituation) as the difference between the peak self-reported distress score between two sessions of interest (Kozak et al., 1988).

Since that early study, many researchers have assessed SUDS ratings at regular intervals, varying in range from ten seconds to ten minutes (but most commonly every three to five minutes), throughout the exposure session (Kobayashi et al., 2022; Miegel et al., 2022; Park et al., 2023; Sripada et al., 2015; van Minnen & Hagenaars, 2002; Zoellner et al., 2023).

Alternatively, some studies rely on fixed time points (i.e., beginning, middle, end of session; beginning and end of session) (Bryant et al., 2023; Essayli et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2021). Further, it is common for habituation researchers to employ aggregation techniques to calculate WSH and BSH from a series of SUDS ratings, and these procedures have also varied (Essayli et al., 2023; Jaycox et al., 1998; Kozak et al., 1988; van Minnen & Hagenaars, 2002).

For researchers conducting an exposure through an inhibitory learning theory lens, there is little guidance about how to measure change in fear expectancy. Stewart and Craske (2020) suggest a procedure in which clients rate their expectancy (i.e., likelihood) that what they are worried about will occur (on scale of 0-100) prior to the exposure, and, following the exposure, their expectancy that what they are worried about would occur in an additional exposure (on a scale from 0-100). A change score (i.e., expectancy violation; pre-exposure expectancy ratingspost-exposure expectancy ratings) would then be calculated as one indication of the exposure success (Stewart & Craske, 2020). This procedure was applied to a public speaking study in which participants rated their threat expectancy on each item of two measures of public speaking outcomes, and the mean threat expectancy was used to assess overall change in threat expectancy (Johnco et al., 2025). Another example is calculating the rate of prediction error in learning to consider how the prior exposure has shaped expectations for the next one (e.g., regarding how much anxiety the client will experience); however, this procedure still relies on calculating a simple difference score (Winkler et al., 2025).

Concerns with Exposure Index Reliability

Importantly, researchers have generally proceeded from the assumption that change indices used in their studies are reliable. It is unclear, however, if this assumption is true for these studies. In our review of the literature, we were unable to find a clear report of reliability of WSH and BSH indices or inhibitory learning indices. Due to the limitations of the measurement practices reported above, we have reason to suspect that these indices are not reliable. For example, Author and colleagues (2024) reported that good reliability for measuring the change in average SUDS during an exposure required eight SUDS ratings per session; measurement with six SUDS ratings per session was barely adequate.

If the results of Authors et al., (2024) extend to exposure change indices, it would imply very poor measurement in the field, because the indices reviewed above have typically been calculated using the subtraction of single ratings. For example, two single-item fear ratings might be subtracted to measure WSH, or the difference between single-item expected and experienced anxiety ratings might be used to measure inhibitory learning. Subtracting two ratings provides a face-valid measurement of anxiety reduction (or expectancy change). However, face validity does not guarantee reliability. Without a confident estimate of reliability, we have no reason to expect we can measure individual differences effectively (see, e.g., Author, et al., 2016). Without indication of reliability of measurement of these change processes relevant to both theories, we cannot expect our evaluation of the theories' predictions to be reliable either.

One essential element of reliability of change scores is the correlation between the elements that are subtracted, because, as reviewed by Nunnally and Bernstein (1994), the direction of the correlation determines the impact of the difference score on overall reliability.

More specifically, subtracting positively correlated items in a difference score decreases reliability, whereas subtracting negatively correlated items produces better reliability. Thus, SUDS scale difference scores may not yield reliable estimates of habituation if the items subtracted have positive correlations. This problem is magnified if only a single measurement is taken at each timepoint, as a single item measure is more prone to measurement error. We would therefore expect a study that relies on single timepoints that are positively correlated to produce an index with poor reliability.

Many researchers have a general sense that difference scores are problematic and may try to overcome these limitations by combining several scores. Importantly, however, the reliability of a difference score is estimated in the same way as the reliability of adding two measures together; that is, as Nunnally and Bernstein describe, the reliability formula is that for a linear composite, where either subtraction or addition is a linear operation. Thus, adding additional measures to the calculation will only improve reliability if the measures are correlated positively (and may be hurt if the correlation is negative). Without report of reliability for each measure to be combined, as well as their correlations, the impact on reliability cannot be estimated.

Current Study

Investigating the reliability of typically used exposure change indices and considering factors that might improve reliability will allow us to better understand the existing literature and could allow us to precisely test the extent to which habituation, or an alternative mechanism, such as inhibitory learning, is the primary mechanism of exposure therapy (cf. Kuckertz et al., 2023). In the current study, we examined three existing datasets, two of which had frequent administration of the SUDS, to determine the average reliability of common measurement practices of WSH and BSH and use standard reliability formulas to estimate what different practices could yield. In exploratory tests, we looked beyond these datasets to determine whether we might expect similar results in other contexts, as well as for inhibitory learning tests. Our specific numbered hypotheses are below.

1)

We expect that SUDS ratings collected during an exposure would have at least moderate correlations, with notably higher correlations for ratings taken closer together. If this is the case, to maximize the reliability of WSH and BSH, ratings closer together should be aggregated, unlike the procedure used by Authors et al., (2024). 2) We also hypothesized that even when using best case scenario estimates from our data, commonly used WSH and BSH procedures would produce indices with poor reliability. Here, the commonly used methods in question involve subtraction of single SUDS ratings. We expected these estimates to improve with multiple WSH or BSH estimates combined, but not to approach strong reliability (reliability approaching .90) under realistic conditions (e.g., fewer than six BSH or WSH combined into an index). 3) By including multiple SUDS ratings per occasion into the indices, we expected reliability could approach strong criteria but only under conditions that would be challenging for pragmatic assessment of WSH and BSH. For example, we expected that if more than five peak SUDS ratings could be obtained across two exposures, this would probably produce strong reliability. However, in practice, peak anxiety during an exposure session may not last long enough to support five measurements, so this is likely not a practical solution. 4) Finally, we expected that using worst-case scenario estimates from our data would produce poor reliability for BSH and WSH indices even when multiple SUDS ratings were included in each component of the indices.

Method

Transparency and Openness

The current study was pre-registered at (https://osf.io/j2xyq/?view_only=6fe9149e3b6a4f03b0892a931fc5d9c2). Data from one of the archival studies is available at the link above. However, participants in the other two studies did not consent to their data being made publicly available.

Participants

Data from three archival datasets were analyzed in this study (N = 119). In all studies, participants were community members recruited from a mid-size Midwestern metropolitan area.

All participants provided informed consent to the study procedures. All studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the university where the studies were conducted. Participant demographics are available in Table 1. In Study 1, participants completed ecological momentary assessment (EMA) prompts about their anxiety and engaged in several exposure sessions (Authors et al., 2025). This dataset generated a report of limited reliability for average SUDS that led us to examine these data further alongside additional data sets. Participants for the current analysis (n = 27)foot_1 were eligible if they had a current diagnosis of social anxiety disorder as determined by administration of the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview Version 7.0.0 (MINI; Sheehan et al., 1998) and the Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale (Liebowitz, 1987). The second study was a randomized controlled trial testing the effect of d-cycloserine on anxiety in adults engaging in brief exposure therapy (Authors et al., 2013). Participants (n = 32) were eligible if they self-reported a fear of public speaking and had a current social anxiety disorder as determined by administration of the MINI Version 7.0.0 (Sheehan et al., 1998) and the LSAS (Liebowitz, 1987). The third study tested the effect of adding a positive mood induction to exposure therapy (Authors, 2018).

Participants (n = 60) were eligible if they self-reported fear of public speaking.

Procedure

In all studies, participants engaged in tasks before and after speech tasks, but we focus only on the speaking tasks here. All studies included in-vivo social anxiety exposures, with Studies 1 and 2 including two and Study 3 including three. All exposures involved giving a speech to the experimenter and a camera, and all participants were told that their speeches would be rated later for quality and content. The SUDS was administered multiple times during each speech. In both Studies 1 and 2, various aspects of the speech, including speech length, were determined by discussion with the participant as to what speech features would lead to a peak SUDS rating of 75. Speeches could be between 4 and 12 minutes long to accomplish this outcome, and thus overall number of SUDS ratings differed across individuals. In Study 3, speeches were 4 minutes long.

Study 1 participants completed two study sessions that involved giving a speech. SUDS ratings were obtained two minutes prior to, immediately before, every two minutes during, and at the end of the speaking task. As part of Study 2, all participants gave two speeches over the course of two study sessions. Some participants were randomized to receive 250mg Dcycloserine prior to the first exposure only whereas others received a placebo. During each exposure, SUDS ratings were obtained two minutes before, at the beginning, during the speech every two minutes, and at the end of the speech. Participants also retroactively reported their peak SUDS rating. Last, in Study 3, participants completed three in-vivo speech exposures over two sessions (two speeches in the first session and one in the second). Participants were randomized to two conditions: focusing on an approach goal during the second speech exposure or only receiving psychoeducation about exposure therapy. No significant differences in SUDS ratings were observed between groups that could be attributed to the intervention. SUDS were obtained four times through each speech: following a preparation period, prior to giving the speech, two minutes into the speech, and at the end of the speech.

Measures

In each study, the Subjective Units of Distress Scale was administered to assess participant fear throughout each exposure session (SUDS; discussed as the Subjective Anxiety Scale by Wolpe, 1988; Wolpe & Lazarus, 1966). The SUDS is a single-item self-report measure that requires participants to rate their distress on a scale from 0 (i.e., no anxiety) to 100 (i.e., maximum possible anxiety). The SUDS measure can be administered at multiple timepoints during an exposure session to capture the trajectory of habituation. Although the SUDS is a frequently used measure, significantly less work has been dedicated to its psychometric properties. The SUDS demonstrates face validity in that it seems to measure the target construct (i.e., subjective distress) and has the benefit of doing so with a single item ("How would you rate your distress right now?"). There is some evidence of the convergent validity of the SUDS in adult treatment-seeking populations, evidenced by moderate, significant correlations with both the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory A-Scale of generalized distress and negative emotionality (r = .35; Tanner, 2012) and the State Anxiety Inventory (Speilberger et al., 1983), which indexes state negative affect (rho = 0.5; Kim et al., 2008). The internal consistency and reliability of the SUDS for each sample is reported and discussed later in this paper.

Statistics and Data Visualization

We obtained correlation and internal consistency data using IBM SPSS Version 28. We then calculated reliability of WSH and BSH elements and indices using the reliability formulas for linear composites of standard scores provided by Nunnally and Bernstein (1994) and the well-known Brown-Spearman prophecy formula (often referred to as the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula). The supplemental materials include these formulas. To aid in visualization of the reliability of indices, we created a Shiny app using the Shiny app package (v1.10.0.9001; Chang et al., 2025) for R; we used R 4.4.2 (R Core Team 2024). The app can be found at https://habapp.shinyapps.io/ExposureHabituationReliabilityShinyApp/. More detail, including the code and underlying formulas, is available in supplemental material. The reader can use the app to verify our conclusions based on our data and investigate other possibilities.

Factors Affecting BSH Reliability

Because BSH is typically calculated as the difference between two estimates of peak SUDS, the formula for the reliability of the composite tells us that we should be concerned about (a) the internal consistency of each peak rating and (b) the correlation between peak ratings.

The first factor affecting BSH reliability is the internal consistency of the peak SUDS rating(s). For Study 1 and Study 2, we identified the two highest SUDS ratings for each participant for each speech. For Study 2, one of these ratings was always the peak SUDS value the participant reported at the end of the speech (i.e., as would be hoped if the participants followed directions). The internal consistency of these ratings was very high in each study and at each occasion (range: a = .94-.98). As noted in the introduction, most researchers have used a single SUDS rating to estimate peak anxiety. The internal consistency obtained for the two highest SUDS ratings allows us to estimate, using the Brown-Spearman prophecy formula, that a single rating would also have good reliability in these data (range: .89-.96). Study 3 did not have two ratings that were viable as peak ratings, because only beginning, middle, and end ratings were available, so it does not offer an estimate for peak SUDS reliability.

The second factor affecting reliability of BSH indices is correlations between the two peak estimates. We averaged the two peak SUDS ratings in each case and then transformed it into a z-score. These indices were moderately to highly correlated across speeches in each dataset when two ratings were used to estimate the peak (Study 2 r = .83, p < .001, Study 1 r = .49, p = .014). The correlations had a similar range when only a single (highest) rating was used (range: .37-.85).

Finally, if multiple BSH estimates are to be combined (e.g., across multiple pairs of exposures), a third factor affecting reliability is the correlation between successive standardized indices. Only the Study 3 data provide insight here because participants completed three exposures, and it must be remembered that the first two speeches occurred in a single session in these data, which is not typical of exposure studies. In any case, BSH indices correlated negatively in these data (r = -.25, p = .046), which would detract from reliability for averaged BSH ratings.

Factors Affecting WSH Reliability

As a reminder, WSH indices are typically estimated by subtracting the end SUDS from peak SUDS, which means we are concerned with the internal consistency of end and peak estimates as well as the correlation between them.

The first factor affecting WSH reliability is the internal consistency of peak ratings, which have already been estimated above, and were generally moderate to high. The second factor is the internal consistency of end ratings. For the datasets with multiple end ratings, the internal consistency of end ratings was moderate to high at each occasion (range: .71-.94), leading to modest to high estimates for the single ratings as well (range: .55-89). The third factor affecting WSH reliability is the correlation between peak and end ratings. The correlation between standardized peak and end ratings was also moderate to high on each occasion whether two ratings (range: .52-.88) or single ratings (range: .36-.98) were used, which will impact reliability of WSH estimates negatively.

Finally, if multiple WSH estimates are to be combined (e.g., across multiple pairs of exposures), whether the resulting index has greater reliability will depend on the correlation between successive standardized indices. Computing WSH using single items for peak and end SUDS resulted in relatively uncorrelated to moderately correlated estimates in our data (range: .10-.50).

As might be expected, these correlations were more uniformly strong when two items were used to estimate peak and end SUDS (range: .43-.50).

Post Hoc Examination of Peak-End Correlations

Our results revealed high correlations between peak and end SUDS ratings, which makes it difficult for WSH indices to achieve high reliability even when combining multiple indices across several exposures. If our estimates are unusually high, however, then our results may not apply to other data sets. We reached out to authors of other studies to determine whether the peak-end SUDS correlations we observed, which ranged from .36 to .98, were typical or aberrant. We received a response from Zoellner and colleagues (2023), who indicated that their peak-end correlations increased over the course of sessions, starting in Session 3 with a moderate correlation (r = .54) and ending in their Session 6 with a high correlation (r = .86). The middle of this range (.70) is higher than the middle of our range (.67), suggesting that our estimates might be too optimistic. Taking the middle of the range for our alphas (.93, .72) and combining it with the mid-range correlation estimate from Zoellner and colleagues leads to an estimate of poor reliability for a resulting WSH index (.42).

Post Hoc Examination of Correlations Relevant to Inhibitory Learning

As mentioned in the introduction, some investigations of inhibitory learning have focused on changes in expected anxiety (Winkler et al., 2025) and threat expectancy (Johnco et al., 2025) as examples of threat disconfirmation. Such a measure of threat disconfirmation depends on a difference score and thus may be vulnerable to the same problems we noted above, especially if correlations between successive measurement occasions are high. We obtained correlations between measurements of expected anxiety from data reported by Rodebaugh (2006) (provided by Rodebaugh here: https://osf.io/rqgsb/) and for threat expectancy from Johnco and colleagues (2025) from the authors. Correlations between successive ratings to be subtracted ranged from .45 to .72 in Rodebaugh's data and from .22 to .78 in Johnco and colleague's data. Most plausible scenarios for reliability at each occasion for single-item measures would lead to poor reliability if the correlation between occasions was as high as .72 or .78. However, in the case of Johnco et al.'s data, the .78 correlation was for post to follow-up ratings of a multi-item measure that had an internal consistency of .95 at each occasion, which results in good reliability (.77).

Discussion

To test the reliability of commonly used exposure measurement practices, we calculated WSH and BSH index reliability estimates in archival data and then used these to calculate likely reliability in the available literature. We found that commonly used BSH indices showed moderate reliability in many cases, which was higher than we had expected a priori. However, WSH indices rarely showed better than poor reliability. Using multiple SUDS ratings at each occasion to calculate estimates resulted in higher reliability that was generally good to excellent for BSH indices and sometimes good for WSH indices. However, the WSH indices we examined were limited by the high peak-to-end correlations we observed, suggesting that good reliability may be impossible in some studies, particularly when only one SUDS rating is used for peak and end estimates. In our post-hoc review of expectancy change, correlations of these indices showed a similar range, suggesting they are vulnerable to the same problems. Our tentative conclusion is that the existing literature on exposure mechanisms is entirely dubious: If indices are not estimated reliably, whether they predict outcome is not a relevant test of the theory that they are mechanisms of change.

We urge researchers to calculate the reliability of the exposure change indices in their studies and report reliability data in their publications. Without such evidence, we have no reason to expect tests of their predictive value to be valid. Fortunately, our results suggest that, with careful consideration of data collection and aggregation procedures researchers can achieve good reliability for these indices, particularly BSH. Our results suggest that one interpretation of the present findings in the habituation literature (as remarked upon by Essayli et al., 2023; Sripada & Rauch, 2015) may simply reflect BSH indices being more likely to obtain reasonable reliability under common practices.

It is important to be clear that researchers have taken many different approaches to measuring habituation, such as how they collect SUDS ratings and calculate relevant change indices. They have also examined hypotheses regarding BSH and WSH under many conditions.

We cannot claim to know for sure that all such practices and all such conditions necessarily are bound by the constraints we identified here. However, in the absence of routine reporting of reliability of BSH and WSH indices, or at a minimum the correlations between the ratings used to calculate them, we have every reason to think they are.

In our review of measurement practices for WSH and BSH indices, our overarching impression is that data have either not been collected with enough frequency to reliably capture habituation processes or, when such data are sufficient, not been used in such a way to maximize reliability. We found some researchers who collected SUDS ratings at most three times (i.e., before exposure, mid-exposure, post-exposure) during each session (Bryant et al., 2023; Essayli et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2021). Capturing an ongoing process of change such as habituation requires more data. For example, most clinicians and researchers will be familiar with the variable but generally curvilinear shape of SUDS ratings across an exposure. Modeling such a process in terms of a latent trajectory would require at an absolute minimum five time points, with more time points preferred (Bollen & Curran, 2005).

From this perspective, equating some relatively arbitrary point (e.g., the midpoint of the session) with the peak SUDS rating of the entire experience is perhaps particularly problematic.

We are unlikely to capture the peak of SUDS ratings through data collection at a small number of time points. An issue as seemingly innocuous as differences in the frequency of SUDS administration has implications for WSH and BSH indices, in that both are dependent on how the peak anxiety rating is defined. For example, with infrequent, rigid time intervals, a participant's true peak SUDS rating, or highest anxiety rating of the session, may not be assessed because it fell between two intervals (e.g., perhaps peak anxiety was at minute 27 but SUDS ratings were assessed only at five-minute intervals).

There are also reasonable concerns about retrospective reporting of the peak as used in one of our data sets. A latent trajectory approach would encourage us to, with sufficient time points, instead estimate the peak based on the individual's trajectory. We did not use that approach here and would recommend both a larger number of time points and a larger number of participants to any researchers interested in pursuing this possibility. More generally, by capturing only a few time points per exposure, we lose potentially important information about an individual's change in fear ratings through the exposure. This observation is not novel. Foa & Kozak (1986) expressed concerns for too limited collection of data and noted that limited data impaired a researcher's ability to parse apart habituation and a lack of fear evocation.

An additional concern is how the data are aggregated once collected to calculate WSH or BSH for an individual. There is significant variability in the literature in how researchers have combined multiple WSH or BSH indices. Although some may have collected data over many sessions, they may not include each of the data points in their final statistical model because of these aggregation techniques (Essayli et al., 2023; Jaycox et al., 1998; Kozak et al., 1988; van Minnen & Hagenaars, 2022). Here we show preliminary evidence that aggregating WSH indices may in some situations improve reliability substantially for this index. Averaging WSH or BSH indices across multiple sessions will not be useful to reliability, however, when the measures are negatively correlated. Before proceeding with analyses, researchers could examine the reliability of the indices they propose to use and form data aggregates based in part on whether such aggregation improves, or at least does not impair, reliability.

Further, even if reliability of habituation indices is improved, the validity of habituation indices requires careful attention. Multiple authors have noted that the theoretical importance of BSH and WSH indices is dependent on fear reduction due to emotional processing, as opposed to fear reduction resulting from distraction, withdrawal of the stimulus, or other factors (e.g., Foa et al., 2006). In our own data, for example, the final SUDS rating was obtained at a point at which participants were aware (or at least plausibly should have been aware) that the speech had ended.

Thus, one could question whether our resulting indices are valid measures of emotional processing, even if they were reliable, which they often were not. We presently have no systematic way of verifying why fear reduction has occurred, although some promising work has been conducted using trained observers of youth engaging in exposure (Benito et al., 2018).

We want to make a note of the possibility of measures of habituation that do not use selfreport at all. Indeed, Foa and Kozak (1986) imply that use of physiological measures may be important. As mentioned above, other researchers have used observer ratings to measure habituation in samples of youth diagnosed with obsessive compulsive disorder (Benito et al., 2018). We did not examine either of these possibilities here but offer two observations. First, it remains true that any measure has some level of reliability and no matter what is being measured, the reliability of the measurement is important. Researchers often behave as if measures that seem more face valid do not require evidence of reliability, yet the history of reliability of measurement tells us otherwise (Authors et al., 2016). Secondly, both physiological and objective measures (i.e., skin conductance, heart rate monitoring) often do not correspond with subjective ratings of distress (Lang, 1970; Miers et al., 2011). Whether this is a problem or not depends on our theory, but if we believe that the habituation of individual distress is what drives treatment outcome, then the failure of these methods to correlate with such distress represents a fatal validity problem.

Our findings are relevant to clinicians in two primary ways. First, several authors have proposed that that habituation is irrelevant to treatment outcomes because of the existing empirical literature (Craske et al., 2008; Craske et al., 2012; Jacoby & Abramowitz, 2016). We have not shown whether habituation is relevant or not, but we have shown that there are strong reasons to think that the existing literature cannot be evaluated without a better understanding of the reliability of measurement. Second, when clinicians are interested in assessing habituation, they should take multiple peak and end SUDS ratings when doing so. Broadly, taking frequent measurements throughout the exposure is good practice to capture more information about how a participant's fear may be changing throughout the exposure. However, there is no guarantee that a participant's true peak will be captured even with highly frequent assessments, because the true peak may fall between measurement occasions. One potential solution to this may be to increase the frequency of SUDS measurements around the most difficult part of the exposure and the end of the exposure and combine these values to estimate the peak and end SUDs ratings. Such a procedure may provide a more feasible alternative then checking SUDS measurements at a high frequency throughout the entire exposure, though this may be appropriate when it is not clear when participants' SUDS may peak in the exposure. Though we ultimately recommend multiple items be used at each occasion to assess fear during exposures, the use of a single item will minimize disruptions to the exposure. This practice will increase the chances of good reliability, although it must be said that BSH indices should still be expected to be more reliable than WSH indices.

We urge researchers to reconsider whether our traditional ways of measuring exposure change indices are well-suited to the prediction of treatment outcome. One way to understand our results is that WSH is hamstrung by the need to subtract SUDS ratings that are relatively close in time. A method that assesses the full SUDS curve and focuses on rapidity of SUDS descent or overall area under the curve (perhaps over several exposures) might prove to be more reliable, although whether such measurements really comport with theory requires careful consideration. Regarding BSH, if what we want to know is whether fear changes between sessions, it seems to us that the obvious answer is to assess change in fear literally between sessions. For example, participants could be asked how much fear they would have if asked to engage in a specific exposure right now, and then this rating could be made repeatedly over time.

With sufficient intensive longitudinal data, we could then estimate individually precise trajectories of truly between-session habituation. Although this method appears face-valid to us, we must take our own advice: Such a method requires demonstration of reliability and predictive validity regardless of how face valid it seems.

Limitations

The results of this study should be considered with several limitations. Participants were taken from three public speaking study samples that were recruited from a mid-size Midwestern city and required a clinical presentation of social anxiety disorder or related symptoms. The majority of participants included in analyses were white women. We can only say for certain that these reliability estimates are representative of these samples, and our findings should be replicated in other treatment studies, clinical presentations, and minority groups to increase our confidence in these conclusions. Further, the design of Study 3 included two exposures given in a single exposure, unlike Studies 1 and 2. Given that the two exposures in Study 3 occurred closer in time, this may have inflated estimates of BSH.

Conclusions

Reliable measurement is essential to scientific progress and is a key future direction in mechanistic research for exposure therapy (Benito et al., 2024). Capturing change over time in dynamic processes is a challenge across content areas but is of particular relevance to the treatment of anxiety. Currently, researchers often use variable, limited measurement practices in exposure therapy. Further, they may not document how they measured these constructs with enough detail that their measurement practices can be evaluated. Our current results suggest that many findings in the literature, particularly regarding WSH, may be unreliable and therefore uninterpretable. Further standardization and documentation of habituation measurement practices would improve our ability to evaluate the results of exposure studies and may help us to better understand the relation between habituation and treatment outcomes.

Formulas for Reliability Calculation

WSH and BSH estimates from a dataset can be entered into the equation for a linear composite of the subtraction of two z-scores that we derive from the equations provided by (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994).

This equation consists of an initial value of 1, from which is subtracted a fraction. The fraction consists of a numerator of 2 minus the sum of the internal consistencies of the two scores and a denominator of 2 minus two times the correlation between the two scores. If we seek high reliability, then we want the value in the parentheses to be very small. Accordingly, we want the numerator to be the smallest possible, which is achieved with alphas that are as high as possible (because these will be subtracted from 2). We also want the denominator to be as large as possible, because, for example, any value over a denominator of 1 will be larger than if the denominator is 2. The denominator is maximized if the correlation between the scores is negative, with an upper limit of 4, and the denominator becomes increasingly small to the extent that the correlation is positive, with a limit of zero. Notably, this equation is a simple transformation of the reliability for a composite in which two scores are added together and can be turned back into the formula for adding two z-scores simply by reversing the minus sign ahead of the expression involving the correlation. The broader formula for the reliability of a linear composite (also provided by Nunally and Bernstein) includes the variances of each score, and is thus more complicated-using z-scores makes the formula simpler because they have a variance of 1.
