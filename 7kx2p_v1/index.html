Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The investigational product, midomafetamine (commonly referred to as MDMA), was supplied by Resilient Pharmaceuticals, Inc; it is an investigational product and not approved by the FDA. This study was funded by revenue generated as part of the social enterprise model at the Portland Psychotherapy Clinic, Research, & Training Center and Portland Institute for Psychedelic Science.

Introduction

Social anxiety disorder involves intense distress or disruption due to fear of social or performance situations that involve the potential for scrutiny, judgment, embarrassment, or humiliation (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Treatment guidelines for social anxiety disorder generally recommend pharmacological intervention, typically selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and cognitive behavioral therapy (Andreoli et al., 2026). While these treatments show effectiveness compared to control or placebo conditions (Sun et al., 2025; Williams et al., 2020), treatment response (Springer et al., 2018) and care needs (Stein et al., 2021) are heterogenous. Novel treatments are warranted to expand treatment options, enhance outcomes and increase access, especially given the barriers people with social anxiety face to engaging with mental health treatment (Goetter et al., 2020).

Research on the effectiveness of psychedelic-assisted treatments for a range of mental health conditions, such as depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and substance use disorders is growing (Dominiak et al., 2025). 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine (MDMA) administered in the context of psychotherapy, termed MDMA-Assisted Therapy (MDMA-AT), has shown promising outcomes and preliminary evidence of safety for PTSD (Mitchell et al., 2021 (Mitchell et al., , 2023)), social anxiety in autistic adults (Danforth et al., 2018), and social anxiety in non-autistic adults (Luoma et al., 2026). The effects of MDMA-which has a phenomenological profile characterized by positive mood, social connectedness, compassion, openness, sociability and emotional empathy (Bershad et al., 2016; Kamboj et al., 2018; Peled-Avron et al., 2025)-are thought to enhance the effects of psychotherapy. This may especially be the case for people who face barriers to engaging in psychotherapy, due to their social anxiety, harsh self-criticism, or fearfulness expressing themselves openly or vulnerably in a therapeutic context (Luoma & Lear, 2021).

A key gap in the psychedelic and MDMA-AT literature is understanding the psychological processes through which treatment may exert its effects. To date, most work has examined the role of mystical experiences, dose, and intensity of acute subjective experiences in predicting outcomes (Viljoen et al., 2026). However, research on predictors and moderators of treatment outcomes indicates that other psychological processes of change appear to be at play, including increases in openness (Wagner et al., 2017), connectedness (Kałużna et al., 2022), ego dissolution (Kałużna et al., 2022), therapeutic alliance (Zeifman et al., 2024), and self-compassion (Fauvel et al., 2023; Zeifman et al., 2025), emotional breakthrough (Roseman et al., 2019) and decreases in experiential avoidance (Zeifman et al., 2023).

Understanding how people who undergo psychedelic-assisted therapy understand and interpret therapeutic benefits is key to advancing an understanding of how these treatments work. In this way, qualitative work can help refine theory on psychedelic-assisted therapy processes of change and ensure their measurement aligns with and captures the spectrum of patient experiences. Based on a qualitative thematic synthesis of 15 studies of patient experiences in psychedelic treatments, patients often reported that newfound insights, compassionate self-perspectives, feelings of interconnectedness, transcendental experiences, and accessing previously inaccessible emotional states contributed to the treatment's therapeutic benefits (Breeksema et al., 2020).

This study represents the first qualitative analysis to explore experiences of MDMA-AT for people with social anxiety disorder. We aimed to understand what changes participants noticed following treatment, as well as what aspects of treatment, if any, they attributed to these changes. Our analysis aimed to advance theory on how MDMA-AT is experienced by and may function for people with social anxiety disorder.

Methods

The parent study was a randomized waitlist, delayed-treatment control trial of MDMA-AT for 20 adults with social anxiety disorder (NCT05138068; Lear et al., 2023; Luoma et al., 2026). Participants were 45% women, 40% men, 15% transgender, predominantly white (85%), 38 years of age (SD = 9.5) and partnered or married (75%).

Regarding sexual orientation, participants were 60% heterosexual, 20% bisexual, 10% gay, and 10% pansexual. Further demographic information is available in the primary outcome paper (see Luoma et al., 2026). The intervention entailed three 90-minute preparation sessions, and two MDMA sessions followed each by three 90-minute integration sessions.

Resilient Pharmaceuticals Inc supplied midomafetamine (commonly referred to as MDMA), an investigational product not approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.

For the first six participants, the therapeutic approach was inner-directed, guided by the Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelics Studies (Mithoefer, 2017). For the remaining 14 participants, the team iteratively refined the manual to incorporate more active treatment elements pertaining to theorized mechanisms of action for MDMA and social anxiety disorder (Luoma et al., 2022; Luoma & Lear, 2021). These active treatment elements included exposure activities (e.g., "authenticity experiments", actively practicing desired social behaviors outside sessions) and self-compassion exercises (e.g., revisiting shame-laden memories from a compassionate vantage point, reframing experiences of shame and criticism through a compassionate lens).

The morning after each MDMA session, prior to their integration session, participants completed a 15-minute one-on-one semi-structured interview via Zoom with a research team member (AS, AS, CC). All participants elected to participate in both interviews. The interview guide (see Supplementary Online Materials Appendix A) consisted of open-ended questions inquiring about any potential beneficial, challenging, and harmful MDMA-AT experiences. The domains assessed in the post-dosing interview remained the same across all participants, though additional optional follow-up prompts assessing unique aspects of the intervention were added as the treatment was iteratively developed. For instance, after participants discussed beneficial MDMA-AT experiences, they may be prompted by the follow-up question: "Did you try any new social behaviors the session?" Interviews were video recorded then transcribed verbatim.

Analysis

We used reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019; Terry et al., 2017) to identify and interpret patterns of meaning across the dataset (the two post-dosing sessions).

Coding was abductive, as we aimed to allow participant narratives to guide theme generation without preconceived interpretations or theory, though analysis was also shaped by our interview guide (Morgan, 2020). We adopted a phenomenological orientation, seeking to capture how participants subjectively made sense of their treatment experiences. This was situated within a critical realist framework, which assumes that participants' accounts offer insight into their lived realities, while also recognizing that these accounts are shaped by social and cultural context, including, in the present study, the therapeutic context. Consistent with this stance, analysis attended to both explicit (semantic) and underlying (latent) meanings in the data.

Three researchers (AS, MD, and CC) first familiarised themselves with all transcripts and shared initial reflections on patterns across the dataset in team meetings. Feedback on preliminary themes was then garnered from another team member (JL). Next, a team member (CC) coded all transcripts in ATLAS.ti (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, 2023). Team members (AS, MD, CC) reviewed and discussed themes iteratively through team meetings, refining interpretations and ensuring that themes were well grounded in the data while incorporating multiple analytic perspectives. Because the analysis team collectively observed no key differences in themes between the two MDMA sessions, results are presented across the sessions. Team members agreed that codes stabilized by participant 15, while nuance within themes continued to be refined through the remaining interviews.

Additional methodological details are presented in the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al., 2007) in Supplementary Online Materials Appendix B.

Engaging in reflexivity, we acknowledge that our analysis was shaped by our interest in novel mental health treatments and a pre-existing view that MDMA-AT was generally beneficial alongside a wariness of psychedelic exceptionalism. Additionally, one researcher's perspective (CC) was likely shaped by her clinical psychology perspective and prior reading and conversation on potential mechanisms of change within MDMA-AT and another's (AS) was likely shaped by a background in contextual behavioral science. Participant reports of change (and thus the resulting themes) were likely shaped by the specific therapeutic approach, which was developed to capitalize on MDMA's pharmacological effects, and target psychological processes underlying social anxiety disorder (Luoma & Lear, 2021; Luoma et al., 2022). While qualitative interview questions were relatively open-ended, participants' understanding of their MDMA experiences may have been influenced by theories and frames of reference provided by the therapists, a topic we return to in the Discussion.

Discussion

This study drew on qualitative interviews of 20 participants who underwent MDMA-AT for social anxiety disorder, adding depth to the quantitative findings reported in the parent study (Luoma et al., 2026) by exploring participants' descriptions of their treatment experiences, the changes they experienced after MDMA sessions and the aspects of MDMA-AT they felt were most instrumental to beneficial changes. Overall, participants described how facing difficult experiences and emotions with curiosity, mindfulness and compassion catalysed healing and new understandings. Fully experiencing painful emotions allowed participants to move from stagnation towards greater flexibility. Participants described positive states (e.g., interconnectedness, social safety) as more of an embodied or felt-sense experience, versus an intellectualized understanding. Moments of vulnerable self-expression with therapists were important to participants, creating opportunities for relational learning to unfold during sessions. Together, MDMA session experiences appeared to provide a salient "anchor" that participants could return to after sessions in their ongoing growth process.

All participants described reduced barriers to examining difficult internal experiences during at least one of the MDMA sessions. In contending with these difficult internal experiences, participants were able to perceive their memories, behaviors, and thought patterns as a curious, compassionate observer. The mindful distance enabled shifts from shame and self-judgement toward greater self-compassion and understanding, helping participants cultivate a more holistic self-awareness, greater self-confidence and greater selfefficacy. Mindfulness and compassion practices have shown efficacy for social anxiety (Liu et al., 2021). Yet, many participants described difficulty applying such practices prior to the MDMA sessions, suggesting that MDMA may enhance people's ability to access and thus practice adopting mindful, curious and compassionate perspectives. The observed enhanced capacity to tolerate and move through challenging internal experiences is consistent with prior MDMA-AT for PTSD research, in which participants similarly described enhanced willingness to experience and explore, rather than suppress, uncomfortable emotions, which facilitated shifts in self-perception, including greater self-love and self-forgiveness (Barone et al., 2019; Godes et al., 2023).

Participants all also described a general broadening in their perspectives of themselves and others. Many described how reduced anxiety during MDMA sessions helped them understand their true desires, which had previously been masked by social anxiety. This parallels prior MDMA-AT for PTSD work, in which participants disentangled past traumatic events from their self-concept, and connected with "lost parts" of themselves (Godes et al., 2023). Our findings lend support to conceptualizations of authenticity as multidimensional, entailing self-awareness, unbiased processing of self-relevant information, behavior that is congruent with one's values, preferences and needs, and a relational dimension (Kernis & Goldman, 2006). Participants described each of these dimensions of authenticity as interconnected: more objective, mindful observation enabled greater self-awareness, and deepened connection with the authentic self was led to greater clarity around social needs (e.g., to be seen) and greater desires to practice new social behaviors to meet those needs (e.g., vulnerable self-expression). Clinically, our findings suggest that authenticity may serve as a valuable social anxiety treatment target. MDMA-AT may be particularly well-suited to target authenticity processes; MDMA appears to facilitate mindful, unbiased observation of the self, while the therapeutic context may promote and reinforce social insights.

In prior MDMA-AT studies, participants generally describe a newfound ability to reason through past experiences, leading to therapeutically beneficial insight that can be readily articulated (Barone et al., 2019; Godes et al., 2023). In the present study, participants not only described newfound clarity and insights, but also a mode of knowing that transcended the intellectual. Beliefs they had previously only been able to access intellectually (e.g., that they were lovable) were felt directly under the acute effects of MDMA and subsequently. Embodied experiences of self-worthiness (e.g., "I was just this beautiful, happy person") countered participants' typical shame-based ways of viewing themselves and may have served as corrective experiences in relation to a shame-bound sense of self. Participants also often described a distinct sense of calm and safety within the body, which facilitated greater comfort and feelings of safeness in interpersonal interactions, mirroring feelings of social safety and comfort described in prior MDMA-AT research (Barone et al., 2019; Godes et al., 2023).

Experiences of interconnectedness and oneness also stood out as impactful embodied states for participants. With classic psychedelics, self-transcendent experiences often are characterized by both feelings of unity and a sense of ego/self-dissolution (Nour et al., 2016).

In contrast, MDMA-occasioned self-transcendent experiences in this study were only characterized by broadened interconnection, suggesting a distinct experiential quality. Future work would benefit from assessing distinct dimensions of self-transcendent experiences to aid in advancing the phenomenology of distinct psychedelics, and predictors of distinct selftranscendental experiences. In our quantitative study with the same sample, self-transcendent experiences related to heightened connection, but not ego dissolution, predicted wellbeingrelated outcomes (belonging, gratitude, purpose in life) (Chwyl et al., 2026). This finding adds to a growing body of work finding this same pattern: self-transcendent experiences characterized by heightened connection are specifically associated with beneficial outcomes (Chwyl, Angelica, et al., 2025; Cova & Abatista, 2025; Kałużna et al., 2022; Tyson et al., 2022; Yaden, 2020). Our findings also provide insights into how transcendental experiences may give rise to benefits: participants described how experiences of love, compassion, and unity gave rise to greater empathy, understanding, and nuance in their perspectives, motivating them to deepen connections with themselves, others and their community.

In addition to describing embodied positive states, participants also often reported greater ability to experience typically inaccessible challenging states (e.g., suppressed anger).

This led to what participants felt was an "emotional release" or "catharsis." Often, participants described emotional relief experiences as intertwined with a felt physical release (e.g., MDMA as having a "muscle relaxing" quality). For two participants, physical pain relief endured beyond the acute effects of MDMA, contrasting most prior qualitative work instead focusing exclusively on emotional pain (for an exception, see : Godes et al., 2023; Watts et al., 2017). Our work adds to a small but growing literature on the potential for psychedelics (Robinson et al., 2024) and MDMA (Christie et al., 2022; Gasser et al., 2025) to treat certain types of pain. While the mechanisms through which psychedelics may influence pain remain largely theoretical (Castellanos et al., 2020), our results suggest that broadened emotional access may play a role. This is consistent with work suggesting that emotional processing treatments, which engage both the somatic and mental experiencing of an emotion, may be particularly effective in reducing chronic pain in individuals with trauma and psychosocial conflicts (Lumley et al., 2021).

Participants described the importance of not only approaching internal experiences but also practicing new behaviors in-session, such as disclosing material they habitually concealed, expressing emotions and needs directly, or asking for physical contact. For instance, one participant, attending closely to how the therapists were perceiving them, arrived at the realization that "it actually doesn't matter if they like me or not" while feeling calm and at peace in their body. MDMA's acute effects, such as reduced anxiety, and heightened trust, emotional openness and sociability (Bershad et al., 2016; Kamboj et al., 2018), may have made these behaviors possible for participants who could not otherwise attempt them. Setting likely amplified pharmacological effects, as participants reported that the therapeutic setting felt safe, attuned and flexible, which likely facilitated them facing difficulties, fully experiencing emotions, and more openly discussing experiences.

MDMA experiences functioned not only as a catalyst for healing, but also as an anchor for participants to return to thereafter. Some participants were able to carry forward useful symbols, metaphors, or new frameworks for self-understanding. Others, building upon progress and momentum established from the MDMA sessions, described greater selfefficacy or desire to try new social activities, cultivate community, or express themselves authentically. These findings build on prior psychedelic-assisted therapy literature, in which participants described bringing forward new modes of thinking, symbolic frameworks and renewed motivation into new actions in life following psychedelic use (Barone et al., 2019; Camlin et al., 2018; Watts et al., 2017). However, our analysis also yielded a dimension not commonly described in previous reports: participants expressed a desire to not only revisit cognitive insights but also reconnect with embodied states deeply felt during the sessions.

While participants expressed a strong desire to remember and revisit these feelings, some expressed uncertainty around how to "preserve that state" or "recapture that image and that feeling", such as a positive self-image, ease or less guardedness.

Overall, our work extends the literature on how participants experience change within psychedelic-assisted therapy to the context of MDMA-AT for social anxiety disorder. Prior qualitative studies have found many similarities in how participants describe their process of change across different psychedelics (e.g., classic psychedelics, MDMA, ketamine) and disorders (Breeksema et al., 2020). Similarly, many processes of change that the current findings point to align with prior themes identified in psychedelic-assisted therapy experiences (Agin-Liebes et al., 2024; Barone et al., 2019; Belser et al., 2017; Godes et al., 2023; Nielson et al., 2018; Pilecki et al., 2024; Renelli et al., 2020; Watts et al., 2017).

Together, these results suggest that certain dimensions of how participants experience therapeutic change may transcend psychopharmacology and diagnostic boundaries.

At the same time, other findings, such as how heavily interpersonal dimensions featured in themes, may be especially pertinent to a social anxiety population. For a condition characterized by social struggles and disconnection, interpersonal experiences within treatment (e.g., social insights, embodied feelings of social safety and interconnection, identifying and practicing social behaviors) may be key facilitators of change. In Theme 4 (Learning New Relational Patterns through Vulnerable Self-Expression), interpersonal experiences not only appeared to facilitate change but the therapeutic relationship itself also served as the arena in which change occurred. For people with social anxiety disorder, therapists themselves are instances of the feared situation: people who are watching, listening, and potentially judging. The MDMA sessions appeared to function as a form of in vivo exposure, or a live behavioral experiment, in which participants could notice their anxiety and anticipatory thoughts as they arose, act in new ways, and observe what actually happened. Feared predictions of rejection, judgment or humiliation could be tested and disconfirmed in real-time by the therapists' actual responses (e.g., warmth, acceptance). Such real-time interpersonal learning aligns with inhibitory learning and memory reconsolidation accounts of exposure, in which "expectancy violations" are conceptualized as key mechanisms of change (Craske et al., 2014; Luoma et al., 2022). New relational learning may be especially central to psychedelic-assisted therapy with social anxiety disorder, or struggles involving interpersonal or betrayal dimensions.

Our results also highlighted the centrality of emotional embodiment, which allowed for transformative experiences with positive emotions (e.g., feeling free, safe, loving), and for wisdom to be felt ("implanted in a physical way"). To continue their therapeutic progress, participants expressed a desire to reconnect with embodied states deeply felt during MDMA sessions. Our results emphasize the importance of therapeutic approaches that go beyond intellectual discussion and instead center in-the-moment felt emotional experience, such as emotion-focused (Greenberg, 2004), Gestalt (Yontef & Jacobs, 2005), and mindfulnessfocused traditions (Kabat-Zinn, 2003). For instance, descriptions of embodied wisdom align with Gestalt concepts of "feeling wisdom" (Yontef & Jacobs, 2005).

Our findings also spotlight the importance of positive affect: participants not only found it impactful to confront painful content but also found tremendous benefit in experientially feeling states that they often found difficult to access (e.g., feeling free, safe, loved). Such experiences were described as contributing to benefits well beyond social anxiety reduction, including greater self-understanding, connection with self and others, and wellbeing. Clinically, our results support therapeutic approaches that incorporate positive psychology principles (e.g., savoring, gratitude, positive imagery, leveraging strengths, engaging in pleasurable and rewarding activities, strengthening relationships) and target not just symptom reduction, but also wellbeing (Carr et al., 2021; Craske et al., 2023).

The fact that interviews were conducted the morning after MDMA sessions (versus post-treatment) may have allowed for visceral, difficult to describe emotional experiences and insights to be more readily captured in our analysis, and explain why such dimensions featured more heavily in our analysis than in prior work (Breeksema et al., 2020). At the same time, a psychedelic "afterglow" effect may have impaired memory, or skewed participants' recollection of their session to be more positive (Basedow et al., 2024). Given the nonlinearity of integration and healing (Chwyl, Elvenes, et al., 2025), how participants' understanding of their experiences changes over time, and after treatment concludes, remains an interesting open question.

It's important to acknowledge that the present themes unfolded within, and were shaped by, the particular context of the current treatment. Participants referenced how elements of set and setting-such as music, eye shades, preparation, caffeine/food intake, ceremony/ritual, and broader life circumstances-facilitated or complicated their experience.

Participants frequently discussed synergy between MDMA and the therapeutic relationship, aligning with prior qualitative (Breeksema et al., 2020) and quantitative work (Zeifman et al., 2024) that has highlighted the centrality of the therapeutic relationship and nonpharmacological factors in facilitating beneficial psychedelic outcomes. Findings from this study cannot isolate the contribution of MDMA's pharmacological properties from that of the therapeutic protocol itself.

A key aspect of the study that likely influenced the observed themes was the therapeutic approach, which may have influenced what experiences felt salient and important to participants, and the language they used to describe their experiences within interviews.

For instance, therapists provided psychoeducation around shame-based conceptualizations of social anxiety, the importance of creating opportunities for expectancy violations, and memory reconsolidation (Luoma et al., 2025). The therapeutic approach also featured several positive psychology principles, such as savoring exercises, noticing past/present moments of social safety, and encouraging engagement in more value-aligned actions, which may have shaped the thematic emphasis on positive experiences, alongside MDMA's pharmacological effects.

Finally, compared to most prior psychedelic and MDMA-AT studies, therapists adopted a more active role through in-session experiential exercises, such as guided imagery and "authenticity experiments." Moreover, therapists encouraged participants to capitalize on the heightened post-MDMA window for learning through homework, social-based goals, and revisiting important moments/feelings from the sessions. This active therapeutic stance may have influenced a range of themes, though especially themes involving interpersonal learning. Supporting this hypothesis, Themes 4 (Learning New Relational Patterns through Vulnerable Self-Expression) appeared more unique to the current study, surfacing less in prior work (Breeksema et al., 2020). Given the likely influence of this trial's more directive protocol elements, future MDMA-AT approaches for social anxiety disorder may benefit from deliberately incorporating structured components such as exposure and compassionbased exercises, and from training therapists to work directly with the in-session relationship, such as in functional analytical psychotherapy, where the in-the-moment therapeutic relationship is considered the primary vehicle for promoting lasting change (Kohlenberg & Tsai, 2007).

Limitations

Limitations of this study include a sample that is homogenous in certain dimensions (85% white), and highly screened (exclusions included psychotic disorder, bipolar 1, autism spectrum disorder, active eating disorders, substance use disorders, personality disorders (except avoidant), current PTSD, serious suicide risk, and recent ketamine or antidepressant use). It's also important to note that while the current paper does not present adverse or challenging experiences, these will be reported in a separate study (participants commonly shared challenging yet beneficial experiences and infrequently reported adverse experiences).

The MDMA-AT experiences presented in this paper may therefore not generalize to certain groups, and rates of challenging or adverse experiences may be greater with a more representative sample.

Conclusions

MDMA-AT appeared to serve as a catalyst for gently facing, deeply feeling, and experiencing emotions, which enabled both new perspectives and an embodied form of knowing that transcended an intellectual understanding. MDMA's pharmacological effects and the therapeutic protocol, designed specifically to capitalize on those effects in service of a particular conceptualization of social anxiety disorder, appeared to work synergistically to enable salient moments of connection, compassion and authentic self-expression. In this way, MDMA-AT may facilitate key experiences that are important for therapeutic change, yet hard to access for people with social anxiety: a curious and compassionate stance towards difficult internal experiences, and experiences of genuine acceptance and social safety that counter expectations of rejection or shame. These findings connect MDMA-AT to established literatures on exposure and inhibitory learning, relational and corrective learning in psychotherapy, and experiential and emotion-focused psychotherapy, suggesting that the therapeutic significance of MDMA-AT may lie in part in making processes already central to effective treatment accessible to those who struggle most to engage them.
