research_questions If the difficulty of establishing attentional control at the latent-variable level results from the diversity of the measures, we hypothesize that a factor representing shared variance across the different versions of the AX-CPT paradigm should be observed. The reason is that in the present study, the same AX-CPT paradigm is used; only the materials used in the versions differ.
Moreover, if the difficulty of establishing attentional control at the latent-variable level results from the fact that in previous measures, proactive and reactive control could not be differentiated, a coherent factor would be expected in the present study because both control processes are disentangled. In particular, because the proactive-control measures are assumed to assess the maintenance of goal-relevant information and the maintenance of relevant information has been established at the latent-variable level using, for example, WM tasks (e.g., Engle et al., 1999; Kane et al., 2004; Oberauer et al., 2000, 2008), we expect to establish a factor using the proactive-control measures. However, if the proactive-control measures assess the same maintenance process as the WM tasks, we would expect a single factor across both types of tasks. In this case, the divergent validity of the proactive-control measures would be challenged, thus undermining proactive control as a unique and specific construct. For the reactive-control measures, two contradictory hypotheses are theoretically possible. That is, if the reactive-control measures assess the goal reactivation caused by the distraction of irrelevant information (Braver et al., 2007; see Braver, 2012), a coherent factor should be established. In contrast, if the reactive-control measures assess the task-specific distraction induced by irrelevant information (see Rey-Mermet et al., 2018, 2019, 2020), no factor should be observed. Please note that in case neither a factor using reactive-control measures is established nor a factor using proactive-control measures is found or distinguished from WM, this would further support the view that attentional control does not exist as a psychometric construct (see Rey-Mermet et al., 2018, 2019, 2020).

study_design_overview In the present study, all participants will be asked to perform all trial types (AX, AY, BX, and BY) from all five versions of the AX-CPT paradigm (i.e., with letters, words, dot patterns, matrices, and pictures as materials) as well as all three complex spans (i.e., with verbal, numerical, and spatial material). We thus have a within-subject 5 x 4 design with an independent variable “AX-CPT version” including five levels (letter, word, dot pattern, matrix, picture), and an independent variable “Trial type in AX-CPT” including four levels (AX, AY, BX, BY). Moreover, we assess working memory with the independent variable “complex span” including three levels (verbal, numerical, spatial). The primary dependent variables are the error rates and the reaction times for the AX-CPT paradigm, and the proportion of items not recalled at the correct position for the complex spans (partial-credit unit score; see Conway et al., 2005). In addition, indices typically used in previous research will be computed for the AX-CPT paradigm (e.g., Gonthier et al., 2016; Richmond et al., 2015; Snijder, 2021): (1) the “d’ context” computed as a d’ between AX and BX trials, (2) the “d’ reactive” computed as a d’ between AX and AY trials, (3) the “BX probe interference” computed as a difference between BX and BY trials, (4) the “proactive behavioral index” (PBI) computed as (AY – BX)/(AY + BX) and (5) the “A-cue bias” computed as a C criterion between AX and AY trials.
As the present study is a correlational study, each AX-CPT version and each span include one pseudorandom trial sequence, which will be administered for all participants. The order of the tasks will be, however, counterbalanced to control for practice effects. That is, for half of the participants, the tasks are presented in the following order: verbal complex span, spatial complex span, numerical complex span, AX-CPT with words, AX-CPT with dot patterns, AX-CPT with letters, AX-CPT with matrices, and AX-CPT with pictures. To control for practice, this order will be reversed for the other half of participants, that is: AX-CPT with pictures, AX-CPT with matrices, AX-CPT with letters, AX-CPT with dot patterns, AX-CPT with words, numerical complex span, spatial complex span, and verbal complex span.

Different versions of the AX-CPT paradigm
The AX-CPT paradigm with letters as material (e.g., Gonthier et al., 2016): The stimuli are all letters in uppercase, except the letters K and Y because they are perceptually similar to X. The letters are presented in series of two. In this trial, the first letter is displayed in blue, whereas the second letter is displayed in black. There are four different trial types: (a) AX trials in which the first letter is A and the second letter is X (see Figure 1); (b) AY trials in which the first letter is A and the second letter is any other letter than X; (c) BX trials in which the first letter is any other letter than A and the second letter is X; and (d) BY trials in which the first letter is any other letter than A and the second letter is any other letter than X. The AX trials require a target key press (i.e., the key 1 on the upper row of the keyboard) with the left index finger; the other trial types require a non-target key press (i.e., the key 9 on the upper row of the keyboard) with the right index finger.
Except for the letters A and X, each letter is presented approximately equally often. Following previous research (e.g., Gonthier et al., 2016), the letter A is never presented as a second letter, and the letter X is never presented as a first letter. The letters repeat across the trials, but not within the trials. The trial types did not repeat across two consecutive trials. The same pseudorandom trial sequence will be administered for all participants
The AX-CPT paradigm with words as material (Braver & Bongiolatti, 2002; Paxton et al., 2008): This AX-CPT version is similar to the AX-CPT paradigm with letters as stimuli, except that the stimuli are 24 words consisting of four letters displayed in uppercase. All words have a frequency of 12 or 13 (M = 12.5, SD = 0.51; see Leipzig Corpora Collection, 2020, for the frequency computation).
For the sake of simplicity, the trial types AX, AY, BX, and BY will be referred with the letter notation for all AX-CPT paradimgs, including the AX-CPT paradigm with words as material. Accordingly, the word succession requiring a target key press – that is, the AX-trial – consists of the word “Witz” (in English: joke) followed by the word “Bart” (in English: beard; see Figure 1).
The AX-CPT paradigm with dot patterns as material (e.g., MacDonald et al., 2005): This version is similar to the AX-CPT paradigm with letters as stimuli, except that the stimuli are 24 dot patterns. The width of the dot pattern ranges from 0.67° visual angle to 1.43° visual angle. The height ranges from 0.67° visual angle to 2.20° visual angle. The AX-trial is presented in Figure 1.
The AX-CPT paradigm with matrices as material: This AX-CPT version is similar to the AX-CPT paradigm with letters as stimuli, except that the stimuli are squares in a 5x5 matrix. Each square has a length of 1.24° visual angle. In order to have the same number of stimulus exemplars in each AX-CPT paradigm (i.e., 24), we display no square in the middle of the matrix. The AX-trial is presented in Figure 1.
The AX-CPT paradigm with pictures as material (Chatham et al., 2009): This AX-CPT version is similar to the AX-CPT paradigm with letters as stimuli, except that the stimuli are 24 pictures. These were selected from Cycowicz et al. (1997). Each pictures comprises a width of 4.96° visual angle and a height of 4.77° visual angle. In every trial, the first picture has a blue frame, whereas the second picture has a black frame. The AX trial consists of the picture representing a kite followed by a picture representing a French horn (see Figure 1).

Different versions of the complex spans
Verbal complex span task (e.g., Lewandowsky et al., 2010): In the encoding phase, a prompt “Bereit” (in English: Ready) with the instruction to press the space key to start will be first presented centrally in black color and in 36-point monospace font. Then, three to five words will be presented as memoranda. These words consist of 5 or 6 uppercase letters with a frequency ranging from 10 to 13 (M = 11.8, SD = 1.07; see Leipzig Corpora Collection, 2020, for the frequency computation). Between the presentation of the memoranda, a distractor task will be performed. This consists of judging the validity of sentences by pressing the left- and right-pointing arrow key, respectively. Each sentence will be presented centrally in black color and in 36-point monospace font. The stimulus-response mapping will be presented in grey and in 24-point monospace font with a width of 18.92° visual angle on the lower part of the screen (3.82° visual angle). In the recall phase, memoranda will be asked to be recalled in correct serial order. To this end, a text field in which participants can only type letters is presented centrally, and the instruction to confirm their response by pressing the enter key is presented in 24-point monospace font on the lower part of the screen (2.39° visual angle). To keep track of the serial order, a counter (e.g., “word 1” for the first word to be recalled) will be presented on the upper part of the screen (4.30° visual angle).
Numerical complex span task (e.g., Rey-Mermet et al., 2019; von Bastian et al., 2016): The numerical complex span is similar to the verbal complex span, except for the following modifications. First, the memoranda are two-digit numbers. Second, the distractor task consists of judging the validity of equations (e.g., “1 + 3 = 5”). Third, the stimulus-response mapping have a width of 12.37° visual angle. Fourth, the counter will include the word “number” (e.g., “number 1” for the first number to be recalled).
Spatial complex span task (e.g., Rey-Mermet et al., 2019; von Bastian & Eschen, 2016): The spatial complex span is similar to the verbal complex span, except for the following modifications. First, the memoranda are the position of four to six red squares. The squares will be presented sequentially in a 5 x 5 matrix. Each square has a length of 1.24° visual angle. Second, the distractor task consists of judging whether the pattern emerging from four squares presented concurrently and arranged in an L-shape is vertical or horizontal. Third, the stimulus-response have a width of 12.37° visual angle. Fourth, the counter will include the word “position” (e.g., “position 1” for the first position to be recalled).

Block and trial structure
Versions of the AX-CPT paradigm: Each version of the AX-CPT paradigm consists of a practice block, followed by four experimental blocks. The practice block includes 20 trials, und each experimental block includes 52 trials (including two warm-up BY trials at the beginning). Following previous research (Gonthier et al., 2016; Richmond et al., 2015), 40% of the experimental trials in each block are AX trials, 10% are AY trials, 10% are BX trials, and 40% are BY trials. This proportion of trials across the different trial types has three advantages. First, A and B cues are presented with equal frequency (i.e., 50%) so that the cue for a target and non-target response does not differ in frequency. Second, the cue validity is equated. That is, A cues occur before one type of probe (X probe) on 40% of the trials, and B cues occur before one type of probe (Y probe) on 40% of the trials. Third, X and Y probes are presented with equal frequency (i.e., 50%) so that it is advantageous to process the cue to perform the task.
In each block, a trial starts with the presentation of a fixation cross for 500 ms, which is followed by the presentation of the first stimulus for 1000 ms. Then, a blank screen is displayed for 4000 ms. After this long delay period, the second stimulus is presented for 500 ms, followed by the prompt “???” until a response is given or 1000 ms elapses. In the practice block, the feedback about the accuracy of the given response is displayed for 500 ms. Finally, a blank screen is presented during an intertrial interval of 500 ms.
Complex Spans: Each complex span consists of a practice block, followed by two experimental blocks. The practice block includes two trials, und each experimental block includes six trials.
For each complex span, the encoding phase will start with the prompt “Ready”. This will be displayed until the participant presses the space key. Then, each memoranda (i.e., a word in the verbal complex span, a number in the numerical complex span, and a position in the matrix in the spatial complex span) will be presented for 1000 ms. Between the presentation of the memoranda, each distractor task will be presented for 5000 ms in the verbal complex span and 3000 ms in the numerical and spatial complex spans. In the practice block, the feedback about the accuracy of the given response is then displayed for 500 ms. After the encoding phase, the recall phase will start with the presentation of the counter with the text field for the verbal and numerical complex spans or with the empty matrix for the spatial complex span. This display remains on screen until a response is given. In the practice block, the feedback about the accuracy of the given response is then presented for 500 ms. measured_variables For each version of the AX-CPT paradigm, the primary dependent measures are the error rates and the reaction times (RTs). For their computation, performance on the practice block and warm-up trials will be excluded. In addition, for RTs, RTs from errors and anticipatory responses (i.e., RTs smaller than 250 ms) will be removed. No upper limit when removing outlying RTs is necessary because each AX-CPT version includes a deadline. Further, RTs falling 3 SD beyond an individual’s mean per trial type (AX, AY, BX, and BY) and version (letter, word, dot pattern, matrix, and picture) will be excluded. Then, each dependent measure – that is, error rates and RTs – will be averaged for each trial type, each version, and each participant separately.
Following previous research (Gonthier et al., 2016; Richmond et al., 2015; Snijder, 2021), we will also compute five indices: (a) the d’ context (i.e., the z(hit rate to AX trials) minus z(false alarm rate to BX trials)); (b) the d’ reactive (i.e., the z(hit rate to AX trials) minus z(false alarm rate to AY trials)); (c) the BX probe interference (i.e., BX – BY) for both error rates and RTs; (d) the PBI (i.e., [AY – BX]/[AY + BX]) for both error rates and RTs; and (e) the A-cue bias (i.e., (1/2) * [the z(hit rate to AX trials) plus z(false alarm rate to AY trials)]).
Following previous research (Gonthier et al., 2016; Snijder, 2021), we will take into account hit and false alarm rates equal to 0 or 1 by applying the following formula to all error rates: error rate = (number of errors + 0.5)/(number of trials + 1).
For each complex span, the dependent measures are the proportion of items not recalled at the correct position (partial-credit unit score; see Conway et al., 2005). These error rates will be averaged for each complex span and each participant separately.

To have some descriptive information about our sample, we will ask participants to respond to a questionnaire assessing sociodemographic variables, such as age, gender, handedness, color blindness, nationality, native language, foreign language(s), number of education years, socio-economic status, synesthetic experience, and leisure activities (e.g., music, sport, video game) as well as about general health status and preferences for dorsal/ventral processing types. In each session, there will be questions about medication use and sleep in the last 24 hours as well as nicotine, alcohol and drug consumption in the last 2 hours as this may have an impact on attention. Participants will be also asked to confirm that they perform the experiment in a laboratory-like setting (e.g. close all computer programs, but the browser window with the tasks and are alone in a quiet room). Finally, in the last session, because of the known effects of depression on cognition (e.g., McDermott & Ebmeier, 2009; Rock et al., 2014), participants will perform the Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI-II; Hautzinger et al., 2006) and the German version of the Perceived Stress Questionnaire (Fliege et al., 2005). statistical_tests Results will be computed in two steps. First, we will examine the skew, kurtosis, reliability and the correlational patterns of all measures (i.e., the measures from the five versions of the AX-CPT paradigm and the three complex spans). Second, we will investigate the relations between the tasks at the latent-variable level using structural equation modeling (SEM). All analyses will be implemented in R (R Core Team, 2021).

Skew, kurtosis, reliability and correlations
For each task, we will consider the measures as normally distributed if skew and kurtosis are smaller than 3 but larger than -3. Furthermore, reliability estimates will be calculated by adjusting split-half correlations with the Spearman–Brown prophecy formula. Split-half correlations will be computed using the splithalf package (Parsons, 2021).
Pearson correlation coefficients will be computed between all AX-CPT versions and between the AX-CPT versions and the complex spans. The correlations will be computed separately for each type of AX-CPT measure (i.e., error rates, RTs, and performance indices). In addition to presenting the significance of each correlation, we will report the upper and lower confidence intervals. Moreover, to assess the weight of evidence for each correlation, we will compute a Bayes factor (BF) in favor of the alternative hypothesis (BF10, i.e., in favor of a correlation) and a BF in favor of the null hypothesis (BF01, i.e., in favor of the absence of the correlation). BF will be estimated using the BayesFactor package (Morey & Rouder, 2015) with default prior scales.

Structural equation modeling
Latent-variable models will be estimated using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012). SEM analyses will be first performed with the performance indices. Then, we will conduct SEM analyses with the primary dependent measures (i.e., error rates and RTs) using a bifactor modeling approach.
SEM using performance indices: The first model – Model 1 – will include the following five performance indices: (a) d’ context, (b) d’ reactive, (c) BX probe interference as a RTs score; (d) PBI as a RT score; and (e) A-cue bias. It will be so fitted that the same performance indices of the different AX-CPT versions will be forced to load on one factor, and the three complex spans will be forced to load on another factor. In this model, all factors will be allowed to correlate, and error variances from the same AX-CPT versions will be allowed to correlate. This model is presented in Figure 2. Then, a model reduction strategy will be applied. According to this strategy, Model 1 will be gradually simplified (i.e., by merging factors with high latent correlations, by constraining low correlations to be orthogonal, and/or by removing the correlations between error variances) until further simplification substantially decreases the model fit.
A second model – Model 2 – will be fitted using the same performance indices, except that the BX probe interference and the PBI will be error-rates scores. The same reduction strategy will be applied.
SEM using the primary dependent measures: For the RTs and error rates of the AX-CPT versions separately, we will use a bifactor-model approach. That is, in addition to fitting a WM factor, we will model a general factor and two specific factors with the AX-CPT measures. This model – that is, Model 3 for RTs and Model 4 for error rates – will be fitted in the following way. First, the three complex spans will be forced to load on a WM factor. Second, all trial types from all AX-CPT versions will be forced to load on a general factor to account for general baseline performance in this paradigm. Third, AY trials – in which there is interference due to the increased expectancy for the probe X – will be forced to load on a factor “expectancy interference”. Finally, BX trials – in which there is interference due to the prepotent response tendency to the target response – will be forced to load on a factor “response interference”. The first specific factor “expectancy interference” is rather a proxy of proactive control, where the second specific factor “response interference” is rather a proxy of reactive control. transformations If data are not normally distributed (i.e., skew and kurtosis +/- 3), we will apply an arcsine square root transformation for error rates, and a logarithm transformation or a Yeo-Johnson transformation (Yeo & Johnson, 2000).
inference_criteria An alpha level of 0.05 will be used for all tests. Moreover, Raftery’s (1995) classification scheme will be used to interpret the BFs. According to this classification, a BF between 1-3 is considered as weak evidence, a BF between 3-20 is considered as positive evidence, a BF between 20-150 is considered as strong evidence, and a BF larger than 150 is considered as very strong evidence.

Structural equation modeling
Model fit will be evaluated via multiple fit indices (Hu & Bentler, 1998, 1999): the χ2 goodness-of-fit statistic, the Bentler’s comparative fit index (CFI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), the standardized root-mean-square residual (SRMR), the Akaike information criterion (AIC), and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC). For the χ2 statistic, a small, non-significant value indicates good fit. For the CFI, values larger than .95 indicate good fit, and values between .90 and .95 indicate acceptable fit. RMSEA values smaller than .06 and SRMR values smaller than .08 indicate good fit. For the AIC and the BIC, smaller indices indicate better fit.
We will test if one model fit the data better than another by conducting two analyses. The first analysis will consist of χ2 difference (Δχ2) tests on nested models. If the more complex model (i.e., the model with more free parameters) yields a reduction in χ2 that is significant given the loss of degrees of freedom, it is accepted as having better fit. The second analysis will be a Bayesian hypothesis test in order to assess the strength of evidence not only for the alternative but also for the null hypothesis. To this end, we will compute a BF in favor of the null hypothesis (BF01) by using the BIC approximation (Wagenmakers, 2007). The BF in favor of the alternative hypothesis (BF10) will be computed as 1/BF01. Raftery’s (1995) classification scheme will be used to interpret the BFs (see above).
In addition, in order for a model to be considered as a “good” fitting model, the following criteria have to be met: (1) The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index – a measure of whether the correlation matrix is factorable – should be larger than .60 (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019). (2) Most of the error variances should be lower than .90. (3) Most of the factor loadings have to be significant and larger than .30. (4) No factor should be dominated by a large loading from one task. (5) The amount of shared variance across the measures – that is, “factor reliability” – has to be high. For the Models 1 and 2, this will be assessed by the coefficient omega (Raykov, 2001), and it will be considered as high when it is about .70. For the specific factors in the bifactor modeling approach (i.e., Models 3 and 4), the factor reliability will be assessed by the omega hierarchical subscale, and it will be considered as high (or typical) when it is about .20 or larger (Gignac & Kretzschmar, 2017). (6) The quality of how well the factor is represented by a set of measures – a measure sometimes called construct reliability or replicability – has to be good. This will be assessed by the index H and it needs to meet the standard criterion of .70 (Rodriguez et al., 2016).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Abstract

Attentional control refers to the ability to maintain a goal and goal-relevant information in the face of distraction. Previous research has highlighted the lack of reliability and shared variance in attentional-control measures. This may result from the heterogeneity of the control processes induced by the tasks. The aim of the present study was to reduce or control for this heterogeneity in two ways. First, we used one task -the AX-CPT -with different stimulus materials (letters, words, dot patterns, matrices, and pictures). In this task, a target response is required in trials where two specific stimuli occur in sequence (e.g., the letter "A" followed by the letter "X"). In all other trials, a non-target response is required. Second, we distinguished between proactive control (goal maintenance) and reactive control (goal reactivation) by computing separate indices for each process. Using data from 667 young adults, we obtained good reliability for most indices. Confirmatory factor analysis identified models with factors representing shared variance across stimulus materials. In a subsequent analysis, we bypassed the indices by modeling proactive and reactive control directly from error rates and response times.

Using a bifactor modeling approach, we found shared variance across stimulus materials for error rates only. However, in both analyses, the pattern of latent correlations contradicted the expectations. For example, a positive and very high correlation was observed between indices representing proactive and reactive control. Overall, these findings raise concerns about the validity of AX-CPT measures.

AX-CPT Measures Show Good Reliability and Shared Variance, but Raise Concerns About Their Validity

Attentional control -also referred to as attention control, executive functions, inhibition or cognitive control -has been defined as the ability to maintain a goal and goal-relevant information in the face of distraction (von Bastian et al., 2020). Although this ability has been assumed to underlie performance in different tasks, recent research has reported a difficulty to establish shared variance across the tasks (e.g., Gignac & Kretzschmar, 2017; Rey-Mermet et al., 2018 , 2019). So far, this difficulty has been assumed to result from methodological challenges, such as high measurement error (e.g., Hedge et al., 2018; Rouder, Kumar, et al., 2023) or the neglect of individual differences in speed-accuracy trade-offs (e.g., Draheim et al., 2019).

However, the difficulty remained even when the methodological challenges were addressed (see Rey-Mermet et al., 2026, for an overview). Another reason for the difficulty may be the heterogeneity of the attentional-control tasks. Typically, attentional-control tasks differ in their instructions, decisions to be performed, or stimulus materials. Because of this heterogeneity, these tasks may assess different control processes, which could account for the lack of shared variance observed across tasks. The purpose of the present study was therefore to reduce or control for this heterogeneity to establish shared variance across attentional-control measures.

Methodological Challenges in Establishing Shared Variance Across Attentional-Control Measures

At first glance, previous research seemed successful in establishing shared variance across attentional-control measures, with several studies reporting structural equation models with attentional-control factors (Engle, 2002; Friedman & Miyake, 2004; Kane et al., 2016; Miyake et al., 2000; Miyake & Friedman, 2012; Redick et al., 2016; Stahl et al., 2014; see Unsworth et al., 2024, for a review). However, this view has been challenged by a series of findings (see Rey-Mermet et al., 2026, for an overview). First, several studies showed that most measures from typical attentional-control tasks are only weakly correlated (rs < .30; e.g., Erb et al., 2023; Paap & Greenberg, 2013; Whitehead et al., 2020). Second, when a factor of attentional control was established, this factor often did not represent much shared variance across the tasks. This was evidenced by generally high error variances, and by one or two task loadings that were much higher than the other loadings (see, e.g., Gärtner & Strobel, 2021; Kane et al., 2016; Pettigrew & Martin, 2014; Rey-Mermet et al., 2018 , 2019; Shipstead et al., 2014). Third, a reanalysis of previous datasets revealed a bias towards publishing well-fitting but non-replicable structural models that included factor(s) of attentional control (Karr et al., 2018). These models, which fit the data well but failed to replicate, were interpreted as the result of overfitting in underpowered samples.

So far, the difficulty of establishing shared variance across attentional-control measures has been explained by two methodological challenges (see Rey-Mermet et al., 2026, for an overview). First, attentional-control measures have been shown to have high measurement error (see, e.g., Rouder, Kumar, et al., 2023), as evidenced, for example, by low reliability estimates (e.g., Enkavi et al., 2019; Hedge et al., 2018). As measurement error is inherently task-specific and thus unrelated across tasks, this high measurement error may account for the difficulty of observing shared variance across attentional-control measures. Second, individual differences in speed-accuracy trade-offs are typically neglected (e.g., Draheim et al., 2019; Rey-Mermet et al., 2019). Most attentional-control measures are computed using either response times or accuracy.

However, some participants may focus on speed over accuracy, while others may focus on accuracy over speed. Thus, by focusing either on response times or accuracy, meaningful variance may be neglected. Moreover, these speed-accuracy tradeoffs may differ across tasks, explaining the difficulty of observing shared variance across attentional-control measures.

Recently, researchers have addressed these methodological challenges by pursuing two different lines of research. In a first line of research, new tasks have been developed (e.g., Burgoyne et al., 2023; Draheim et al., 2021 Draheim et al., , 2024;; Kucina et al., 2023). For example, to address the measurement error in these new tasks, researchers increased attentional-control variance by increasing attentional-control demands (e.g., Burgoyne et al., 2023 Burgoyne et al., , 2024;; Kucina et al., 2023).

The neglect of individual differences in speed-accuracy tradeoffs was bypassed by pushing all variance into accuracy (e.g., Draheim et al., 2021 Draheim et al., , 2024;; Tsukahara et al., 2025). The results showed good reliability and shared variance. Furthermore, the measures in these tasks were also observed to uniquely predict other abilities which have been proposed to rely on attentional control (e.g., working memory capacity; Draheim et al., 2021 Draheim et al., , 2024;; Tsukahara et al., 2025). At the same time, they were reported to be distinct from abilities typically assumed to be distinct from attentional control (e.g., latent correlations with processing speed ranging from .60 to .76 in Burgoyne et al., 2023; Draheim et al., 2021 Draheim et al., , 2024;; Tsukahara et al., 2025; but see Robison et al., 2026, who reported high latent correlations ranging from .83 to .90). Nevertheless, despite the promises of these new tasks, there are some open questions. For example, it is so far not clear to what extent the shared variance between the measures specifically reflects attentional control (Oberauer, 2024). The reason is that the measures are computed on average performance without controlling for non-attentional-control processes. In a second line of research, modeling approaches have been used. For example, the impact of measurement error was reduced by using hierarchical Bayesian models, in which individual participants' estimates are informed by group-level distributions estimated from all participants (e.g., Kucina et al., 2023; Rey-Mermet et al., 2025; Rouder, Kumar, et al., 2023; Rouder & Haaf, 2019). The individual differences in speed-accuracy tradeoffs were estimated using the diffusion model (Hedge et al., 2022; Löffler et al., 2024; Rey-Mermet et al., 2025; Weigard et al., 2021). In this model, speed-accuracy trade-offs are considered because the model accounts for both error rates and response times, and it estimates each person's speed-accuracy setting with a separate parameter. Despite the advantages of these modeling approaches, the results did not show shared attentional-control variance.

Heterogeneity in Attentional-Control Tasks

Beyond these methodological challenges, one reason why it is difficult to establish shared variance across the measures may be the heterogeneity of the control processes induced by the attentional-control tasks. Attentional-control tasks differ in the decisions participants must make and, consequently, in the instructions and in the stimulus materials (see Table A1 in Appendix A for a description of the attentional-control tasks typically used in previous research). For example, the color Stroop task requires naming the print color of color words while ignoring the word meaning. In contrast, the arrow flanker task requires identifying a central arrow while ignoring the flanking arrows. This heterogeneity in the decisions, instructions and stimulus materials may result in different processes of attentional control. This heterogeneity of processes has been acknowledged by experimental research using different labels, such as selective, sustained, or divided attention (e.g., Eriksen & Hoffman, 1972; Manly et al., 1999; Miller, 1982; but see Tsukahara et al., 2025; Zivony et al., 2026, for some recent applications of these labels in individual-differences research). Furthermore, the heterogeneity of processes has been acknowledged by the different taxonomies of tasks (e.g., Friedman & Miyake, 2004; Kornblum et al., 1990; Nigg, 2000; Stahl et al., 2014; see Table A2 in Appendix A, for an overview).

According to these taxonomies, one task or group of tasks is assumed to assess one subconstruct of attentional control, whereas another task or another group of tasks is assumed to assess another subconstruct of attentional control. However, there is little consensus across the taxonomies. Some tasks are assumed to assess the same subconstruct in some taxonomies, but different subconstructs in other taxonomies. For example, the Stroop and flanker tasks are assumed to assess the same subconstruct in Nigg (2000) and Stahl et al. (2014), but different subconstructs in Kornblum et al. (1990) and Friedman and Miyake (2004). Together, this lack of consensus suggests that the attentional-control tasks can be classified using such a broad variety of criteria that each task may assess a very specific attentional-control process. Consequently, this heterogeneity may explain why it has been so difficult to establish shared variance between the attentional-control measures.

Besides the heterogeneity of attentional-control processes between the different tasks, some researchers have suggested that responding to an attentional-control task may engage more than one control process. For example, Braver and colleagues have proposed a theory of two distinct control processes: proactive and reactive control (Braver, 2012; Braver et al., 2007).

According to their theory, proactive control involves the maintenance of goal-relevant information in the anticipation of distraction. In contrast, reactive control involves the reactivation of this information after the distraction has occurred. Many attentional-control tasks used so far may assess both proactive and reactive control to varying degrees (see Braver et al., 2007). This heterogeneity of control processes within tasks may explain the difficulty of observing shared variance across attentional-control measures.

Together, the heterogeneity of control processes between and within tasks may explain the difficulty of observing shared variance across attentional-control tasks. In the next section, we describe how these two forms of heterogeneity in attentional-control tasks may be reduced to observe shared variance across attentional-control measures.

How Can the Heterogeneity of Control Processes Between Tasks Be Reduced?

A way to reduce or control for the heterogeneity of control processes between attentionalcontrol tasks is to follow the example provided by research on working memory capacity. In this research, there exist different versions of the same task which differ only in the stimulus material. An example for such a task is the complex-span task (Conway et al., 2005). In this task, participants are asked to memorize items while intermittently performing a distractor task (e.g., judging the correctness of an equation). Critically, the items to be memorized can be words, digits or matrices. Using such versions of the complex span, it was possible to observe shared variance in working memory capacity (e.g., Conway et al., 2002; Engle et al., 1999; Kane et al., 2004).

So far, in attentional-control research, only a few studies have reported correlations between different versions of the same attentional-control task (e.g., versions of the Stroop, flanker or antisaccade task; see Table A1 in Appendix A for a description). In most cases, the results showed low correlations (Kane et al., 2016; Rey-Mermet et al., 2018 , 2019). The reason is that the different versions of the tasks differed not only in the stimulus materials but also in the instructions and the decisions to perform. There is, however, one exception. High correlations and a factor of attentional control were observed when the antisaccade task was performed with arrows, digits, and letters as stimulus materials (Chuderski, 2014). However, the measures in the different versions of the antisaccade task did not isolate attentional control from other abilities or processes contributing to task performance. This is problematic because recent research has highlighted the substantial impact of non-attentional-control processes in this task (Frischkorn & Oberauer, 2025; Rouder, Chávez De la Peña, et al., 2023). Therefore, it is possible that the shared variance observed across the three versions of the antisaccade task reflects these nonattentional-control processes rather than attentional control.

Overall, this suggests that the tasks used so far, and their implementation across different versions, were suboptimal to detect shared variance in attentional-control measures. Thus, alternative attentional-control tasks may be required for this purpose.

A way to reduce or control for the heterogeneity of control processes within attentionalcontrol tasks is to distinguish between proactive and reactive control. A task typically used to distinguish between these control processes is the AX-Continuous Performance Test (AX-CPT; e.g., Braver et al., 2007 Braver et al., , 2009;; Braver, 2012; Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016). In the version of this task with letters as stimuli, sequences of two letters are presented with a delay.

In the experimental approach, proactive and reactive control have been distinguished using experimental manipulations. Overall, the results showed the expected pattern: proactivecontrol manipulations increased the proactive-control indices, and reactive-control manipulations increased the reactive-control indices. Nevertheless, the results also raised some questions about how selective these manipulations are. In many cases, the proactive-control manipulations also reduced the reactive-control indices, and the reactive-control manipulations reduced the proactive-control indices (e.g., Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016; Rosales et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023). More critically, these effects were reported to be unsystematic. In Rosales et al. (2022), the reactive-control manipulation affected some proactive-control indices (the d′ context and the response-time based PBI) but not others (the A-cue bias and the error-rate based PBI).

Besides the neuroscientific and experimental approaches, an increasing number of studies have used the AX-CPT in an individual-differences approach. The focus was primarily on the relationship between AX-CPT performance and working memory capacity. Most of this research has reported evidence for such a relationship, in particular for the proactive-control measures (Ball, 2015; Boudewyn et al., 2015; Hao et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2024; MacDonald et al., 2005; Robison et al., 2023; Stawarczyk et al., 2014; but see Belletier et al., 2019; Rosales et al., 2022, for findings questioning this relationship) 1 . Recently, Snijder et al. (2024) have shifted the 1 For completeness, it should be noted that a relationship between AX-CPT and working memory capacity has also been reported when participants were categorized into high and low working memory capacity groups, rather than using a correlational analysis (Redick & Engle, 2011; Wiemers & Redick, 2018).

research focus by examining the reliability of proactive-and reactive-control measures and their correlations with other attentional-control tasks. To this end, they used the AX-CPT and other attentional-control tasks, such as the Stroop task. Moreover, they asked their participants to perform these tasks not only under baseline conditions but also under conditions in which proactive or reactive control was manipulated experimentally. In all conditions, the results showed good split-half reliability (range: .53 to .80, median: .72), but low correlations with other attentional-control tasks (range: -.37 to .34, median: -.02). However, as the tasks used by Snijder et al. (2024) differed in stimulus materials, instructions, and decisions, these low correlations may result from the heterogeneity of the tasks and their implementations.

In sum, the present overview suggests that minimizing heterogeneity of control processes between and within tasks requires a task with three properties : (1) the task should have versions that only differ in stimulus materials and not in instructions or decisions; (2) the task should allow distinguishing between proactive and reactive control; and (3) the measures computed from the task should isolate attentional-control variance from the other abilities or processes.

A good candidate for such a task is the AX-CPT. First, the AX-CPT can be implemented in different versions by simply manipulating the stimulus materials. Critically, this would not change the instructions or the decisions. Moreover, previous research has already used versions of the AX-CPT other than the version with letters, such as versions with dot patterns (see, e.g., Lopez-Garcia et al., 2016; MacDonald et al., 2005), pictures (see Chatham et al., 2009) or words (see Braver & Bongiolatti, 2002; Paxton et al., 2008). Second, the AX-CPT is designed to distinguish between proactive and reactive control (see, e.g., Braver et al., 2007; Braver, 2012; Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016). Third, the AX-CPT allows for the computation of different indices assumed to control for baseline performance (see, e.g., Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016; Tang et al., 2023).

The Present Study

The goal of the present study was to reduce or control for the heterogeneity of control processes in one task to determine whether shared variance can be established. If we established shared variance in this task, this would provide a starting point for addressing the broader measurement problem in attentional control. To pursue our goal, we used the AX-CPT, and we asked participants to perform five different versions. Critically, these versions only differed in stimulus materials (i.e., letters, words, dot patterns, matrices, and pictures).

To distinguish between proactive and reactive control as well as to control for baseline performance, we computed the indices most typically used in previous research (see Table 2; e.g., Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016; Tang et al., 2023). In addition to the indices, we also fitted bifactor models directly on error rates and response times. Moreover, as working memory capacity has been related to AX-CPT performance, in particular to the proactive-control measures (see, e.g., MacDonald et al., 2005), we asked our participants to perform three complex spans. This allowed us to investigate to what extent the AX-CPT measures capture a construct different from working memory capacity.

We hypothesized that if the difficulty of establishing shared variance across attentionalcontrol measures results from the heterogeneity of control processes (see, e.g., Braver et al., 2007; Friedman & Miyake, 2004; Kornblum et al., 1990; Nigg, 2000; Stahl et al., 2014), shared variance across the different AX-CPT measures should be observed separately for proactive and reactive control. The reason is that in the present study, we reduced or controlled for the heterogeneity of control processes in two ways. First, we reduced the heterogeneity across tasks by asking participants to perform the same task in five different versions which differed only in the stimulus materials. Second, we controlled for the heterogeneity within tasks by distinguishing between proactive and reactive control.

Moreover, if the AX-CPT measures, in particular the proactive control measures, assess to some degree working memory capacity (Ball, 2015; Boudewyn et al., 2015; Hao et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2024; MacDonald et al., 2005; Robison et al., 2023; Stawarczyk et al., 2014), we expected a substantial correlation between the proactive-control measures and working memory capacity. In contrast, if the proactive-control measures do not assess working memory capacity (Rosales et al., 2022), we expected no or only a weak correlation between the proactive control measures and working memory capacity. Such a finding would support the interpretation of proactive control as a unique and specific construct.

AX-CPT with Letters as Materials

In the AX-CPT with letters as materials (e.g., Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016), we used as stimuli all English uppercase letters, except "K" and "Y" which were perceptually too similar to "X". The letters were presented in sequences of two. The first letter was displayed in blue, whereas the second letter was displayed in black. Except for the letters "A" and "X", each letter was presented approximately equally often. Following previous research (e.g., Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016), the letter "A" was never presented as the first letter, and the letter "X" was never presented as the second letter. The letters were repeated across the trials, but not within the trials. That is, the same letter could not appear as both the first and second stimulus in the same trial. The sequences of two letters were categorized in four trial types (see Table 1).

The trial types did not repeat across two consecutive trials. The same pseudorandom trial sequence was administered for all participants.

Following previous research (Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016; Richmond et al., 2015; Rosales et al., 2022), 40% of the trials were AX trials, 10% were AY trials, 10% were BX trials, and 40% were BY trials. These proportions served to induce a strong prepotent response tendency, while controlling possible learning confounds associated with the frequencies of the trial types. Such confounds were controlled because the letter "A" was presented as often as the first stimulus (AX and AY trials) as all other letters than "A" (BX and BY trials). In addition, the letter "X" was presented as often as the second stimulus (AX and BX trials) as all other letters than "X" (AY and BY trials).

AX-CPT with Words as Materials

This version of the AX-CPT was similar to the AX-CPT with letters, except that the stimuli were 24 words (see Braver & Bongiolatti, 2002; Paxton et al., 2008, for such a version of the AX-CPT). The words consisted of four letters displayed in uppercase and were taken from a German news corpus (Leipzig Corpora Collection, 2020). All words had a frequency of 12 or 13 (M = 12.5, SD = 0.51).

AX-CPT with Dot Patterns as Materials

This version of the AX-CPT was identical to the AX-CPT with letters as stimuli, except that the stimuli were 24 dot patterns (see, e.g., MacDonald et al., 2005, for such a version of the AX-CPT). The width of the dot patterns ranged from 0.67° visual angle to 1.43° visual angle.

The height ranged from 0.67° visual angle to 2.20° visual angle.

AX-CPT with Matrices as Materials

This version of the AX-CPT was identical to the AX-CPT with letters, except that the stimuli were 24 squares presented in a 5x5 matrix. In order to have the same number of 24 stimulus exemplars in each AX-CPT version, we displayed no square in the middle of the matrix.

Each square had a width and height of 1.24° visual angle.

AX-CPT with Pictures as Materials

This version of the AX-CPT was similar to the AX-CPT with letters, except for two modifications. First, the stimuli were 24 pictures (see Chatham et al., 2009, for such a version of the AX-CPT). The pictures were line drawings of common objects (e.g., a kite and a French horn), based on normed stimulus sets reported by Cycowicz et al. (1997). Each picture comprised a width of 4.96° visual angle and a height of 4.77° visual angle. The pictures were presented in black and white. The second modification was that the first picture in the trial had a blue frame, whereas the second picture had a black frame.

Verbal Complex Span

In this task, the memoranda were three to five words consisting of five or six uppercase letters. The words were taken from a German news corpus (Leipzig Corpora Collection, 2020).

The words had a frequency ranging from 10 to 13 (M = 11.8, SD = 1.07). For the distractor task, valid and invalid sentences in 36-point monospace font were used. The stimulus-response mapping was shown in grey and in 24-point monospace font with a width of 18.92° visual angle on the lower part of the screen (3.82° visual angle from the screen center). At recall, for each memorandum, a text field in which participants could only type letters was presented centrally.

The instruction to confirm the response by pressing the enter key was presented in 24-point monospace font on the lower part of the screen (2.39° visual angle from the screen center). In addition, a counter (e.g., "word 1" for the first word to be recalled) was displayed on the upper part of the screen (4.30° visual angle from the screen center) to keep track of the serial order of the memoranda.

Numerical Complex Span

The numerical complex span was programmed following Rey-Mermet et al. (2019; see also von Bastian & Eschen, 2016). This span was similar to the verbal complex span, except for the following modifications. First, the memoranda were two-digit numbers. Second, simple valid or invalid equations (e.g., "1 +3 = 5") were used for the distractor task. Third, the stimulusresponse mapping had a width of 12.37° visual angle. Fourth, the counter included the word "number" (e.g., "number 1" for the first number to be recalled).

Spatial Complex Span

The spatial complex span was programmed following Rey-Mermet et al. (2019; see also von Bastian & Eschen, 2016). This span was similar to the verbal complex span, except for the following modifications. First, the memoranda were the position of four to six red squares. The squares were presented sequentially in a 5x5 matrix. Each square had a length of 1.24° visual angle. Second, for the distractor task, patterns of four squares were used. Third, the stimulusresponse mapping had a width of 12.37° visual angle. Fourth, the counter included the word "position" (e.g., "position 1" for the first position to be recalled).

Procedure

Participants were tested remotely using a browser-based online experiment. The experiment consisted of three sessions. Participants were instructed to perform each session between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. The three sessions were so organized that two sessions were separated by at least one night and maximally 1 week. The maximal duration was extended if the participant could not conduct the session as planned (e.g., due to illness). Each session lasted approximately 70 min. During testing, participants were alone but had the possibility to phone the student experimenter who recruited them in case of questions or problems. At the beginning of the first session, participants received details about the study and confirmed their consent to participate.

Following Rey-Mermet and Rothen (2023), we asked participants at the start of each session to confirm that they did the experiment in a laboratory-like setting, that is, in a room without external distractions and no other programs or browser tabs open. Then, participants performed a scaling task. We implemented this task to ensure that all stimuli were displayed in the same size. This was necessary because the experiment was run on the participants' own hardware with different screen sizes and resolutions. In the scaling task, participants were asked to adapt the size of a rectangle to the size of a credit card. Based on the results of this task, we calculated the pixel density. This allowed us to scale each stimulus down or up to match the presentation size on the screen that had been used for programming the experiment.

Across the three sessions, participants performed five different AX-CPT versions and three complex-span tasks. The order of presentation of these tasks was counterbalanced across participants to control for practice effects. Half of the participants performed the verbal, spatial, numerical complex spans, followed by the word version of the AX-CPT in the first session, the dot pattern and letter versions in the second session, and the matrix and picture versions in the third session. The order of these tasks was reversed for the other half of the participants. That is, participants performed the picture and matrix versions of the AX-CPT in the first session, the letter and dot pattern versions in the second session, and the word version of the AX-CPT, followed by the numerical, spatial and verbal complex-span tasks in the third session. Each version of the AX-CPT and of the complex spans started with instructions explaining how to carry out the task. These instructions were followed by a practice block that participants could repeat once. In the middle of each session, participants had the option to take a long break of up to 10 min. At the end of each session, participants were asked about their current health status. Furthermore, they were asked to perform the sociodemographic questionnaire at the end of the first session as well as the BDI-II and PSQ at the end of the third session.

Block and Trial Structure of the Complex Spans

Each complex span consisted of a practice block, followed by two experimental blocks.

The practice block included two trials, and each experimental block included six trials.

Participants were allowed a break of up to 3 min between two blocks.

Each trial included an encoding phase and a recall phase (e.g., Lewandowsky et al., 2010). In the encoding phase, sequences of stimuli had to be memorized. This phase started with a prompt "Bereit" (in English: "Ready") with the instruction to press the space key to initiate the presentation of the memoranda. Then, each memorandum (i.e., a word in the verbal complex span, a number in the numerical complex span, and a position in the matrix in the spatial complex span) was presented for 1000 ms. Between the presentations of the memoranda, each distractor task was presented for 5000 ms in the verbal complex span and 3000 ms in the numerical and spatial complex spans. For the verbal and numerical complex spans, the distractor task consisted in judging the validity of sentences and equations, respectively. In the spatial complex span, the distractor task consisted in judging whether the pattern emerging from four squares presented concurrently and arranged in an L-shape was vertical or horizontal. In the practice block, the feedback about the accuracy of the given response (i.e., a smiling or frowning face) was then displayed for 500 ms. After the encoding phase, the recall phase started with the presentation of a counter with a text field for the verbal and numerical complex spans or with an empty matrix for the spatial complex span. This display remained on screen until a response was given. For the verbal and numerical complex spans, participants were asked to type in the words or numbers from the encoding phase, and to confirm their response by pressing the enter key. For the spatial complex span, they were asked to click on the positions in a 5x5 matrix to reproduce the squares from the encoding phase. In the practice block, the feedback about the accuracy of the given response (a smiling or frowning face) was then presented for 500 ms. Finally, a blank screen was displayed for 500 ms.

Measures

For each task, performance on the practice block and warm-up trials was excluded. For the AX-CPT versions, the error rates and the response times were computed for each trial type.

For the computation of the response times, errors and anticipatory responses (i.e., response times smaller than 250 ms) were removed. No upper limit when removing outlying response times was necessary because each AX-CPT version included a deadline. Furthermore, response times falling 3 SD beyond an individual's mean per AX-CPT version and trial type were excluded. Then, error rates and response times were averaged for each participant, AX-CPT version and trial type separately. For both error rates and response times, higher scores indicated worse performance.

Analyses were performed using two different types of AX-CPT measures. First, we calculated performance indices in each AX-CPT version. The computation of these indices is described in Table 2. For the calculation of the d′ context, d′ reactive, and the error-rate based PBI, we avoided hit and false alarm rates equal to 0 or 1 by applying a correction to all error rates (Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016; Snijder et al., 2024). We used the following correction: error rate = (number of errors + 0.5) / (number of trials + 1). Second, we performed the analyses using response times and error rates. For these analyses, we applied a logarithm transformation to response times before averaging and an arcsine square root transformation to error rates to achieve approximate univariate normality.

For the complex-span tasks, the dependent measure was based on the partial-credit load score (see Conway et al., 2005). This scoring method was chosen to ensure comparability with previous studies using the AX-CPT and complex-span tasks (Richmond et al., 2015; Rosales et al., 2022). 2 For consistency with the error rates in the AX-CPT versions, we expressed the partial-credit load scores as error rates. That is, we used the proportion of items not recalled at the correct position, such that higher scores indicated worse performance. To achieve approximate univariate normality, we applied an arcsine square root transformation.

Data Analysis

An alpha level of .05 was used for all tests from the null hypothesis significance testing framework. For the Bayesian hypothesis testing, we used default prior scales. Moreover, Raftery's (1995) classification scheme was used to interpret the Bayes factors. According to this classification, a Bayes factor between 1 and 3 was considered as weak evidence, a Bayes factor between 3 and 20 was considered as positive evidence, a Bayes factor between 20 and 150 was considered as strong evidence, and a Bayes factor larger than 150 was considered as very strong evidence.

For each type of measures (performance indices vs. response times and error rates), we performed the analyses in three steps. In the first step, we examined descriptive statistics and split-half reliability for each measure. If the measure was not approximately normally distributed and did not have acceptable to good split-half reliability estimates, we removed it from the subsequent analyses. In the second step, we computed the product-moment correlations between the measures. In the third step, we used confirmatory factor analysis to investigate the latent structure of the measures. Next, we describe the first and third steps in more detail next.

Descriptive Statistics and Split-Half Reliability

In this step, we computed as descriptive statistics the means, standard deviations, skew, and kurtosis. A measure was considered to approximately follow a univariate normal distribution when the absolute values of skew and kurtosis were lower than 3. Split-half reliability was computed using a permutation-based approach (Parsons et al., 2019). In this approach, the data were randomly split into halves 5000 times, and correlations between halves were calculated. These correlations were then adjusted using the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula and averaged.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

For the confirmatory factor analysis, we estimated different models for each type of measures (performance indices vs. response times and error rates). For the models with performance indices, the same performance indices of the different AX-CPT versions were forced to load on one factor. As PBI and BX-probe interference include response-time and errorrate based measures, we estimated the models separately for each type of PBI and BX probe interference. For the models with response times or error rates, a bifactor-model approach was used. That is, all trial types from all AX-CPT versions were forced to load on a general factor to account for general performance. AY trials were additionally forced to load on a specific factor, which may be regarded as a proxy of proactive control. BX trials were additionally forced to load on a specific factor, which may be taken as a proxy for reactive control. The bifactor models were estimated separately for response times and error rates. In all models, we added a factor of working memory capacity, on which all complex spans were forced to load.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices (Hu & Bentler, 1998 , 1999) In addition, for a model to be considered as a well-fitting model, the following criteria had to be met. (1) The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin index -a measure of whether the correlation matrix is factorable -had to be larger than .60 (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019). (2) Most of the error variances had to be lower than .90. (3) most of the factor loadings had to be significant and larger than .30. (4) no factor should be dominated by a large loading from one task. (5) the amount of shared variance across the measures -that is, "factor reliability" -had to be high. For the models with the performance indices, this was assessed by the coefficient ω (Raykov, 2001). Values of about .70 were considered high. For the specific factors in the bifactor models with response times and error rates, the factor reliability was assessed by the ωHS (hierarchical subscale).

Values of about .20 or larger were considered as high or typical (Gignac & Kretzschmar, 2017).

(6) The quality of how well the factor is represented by a set of measures -a measure sometimes called construct reliability or replicability -had to be good. This was assessed by the index H and it needed to meet the standard criterion of .70 (Rodriguez et al., 2016).

To find the most parsimonious model that still fit the data well, we conducted two analyses. The first analysis consisted of χ 2 difference (Δχ 2 ) tests on nested models. If the more complex model (i.e., the model with more free parameters) yielded a significant reduction in χ 2 given the loss of degrees of freedom, it was considered to have better fit. The second analysis was a Bayesian hypothesis test to assess the strength of evidence not only for the alternative but also for the null hypothesis. To this end, we computed a Bayes factor in favor of the null hypothesis (BF01), that is, in favor of the more parsimonious model, by using the BIC approximation (Wagenmakers, 2007). The Bayes factor in favor of the alternative hypothesis (BF10), that is, in favor of the more complex model, was computed as 1/BF01. As this second analysis is based on the BIC approximation, it considers model complexity, thus providing a better indication of the most parsimonious model.

We first present the results from the analysis of the performance indices. Then, we report the results from the analysis of response times and error rates. For each type of analysis, we describe the descriptive statistics, reliability estimates, correlations and results from the confirmatory factor analysis.

AX-CPT Performance Indices and Complex Spans

Descriptive Statistics and Split-Half Reliability

Descriptive statistics and split-half reliabilities are presented in Table 5 for the AX-CPT performance indices and in Table 6 for the error rates in the complex spans. Nearly all measures were approximately normally distributed as indicated by values of skew and kurtosis between -1.50 and 2.21. One exception, however, concerns the BX probe interference. The error-rate based BX probe interference was non-normally distributed for all materials. Furthermore, the response-time based BX probe interference was non-normally distributed when letters were used as material.

Discussion

Previous research has shown a difficulty of establishing shared variance across attentional-control measures (e.g., Erb et al., 2023; Gärtner & Strobel, 2021; Gignac & Kretzschmar, 2017; Rey-Mermet et al., 2018 , 2019; Whitehead et al., 2020). One possible reason for this difficulty is the heterogeneity of the control processes induced in the attentional-control tasks. In the present study, we reduced or controlled for this heterogeneity in two ways. First, we asked our participants to perform five versions of the AX-CPT that only differed in stimulus materials. Second, we distinguished between proactive and reactive control. At first glance, the results appeared promising. We observed good reliability for most AX-CPT measures (see the BX probe interference for one exception). Moreover, we found shared variance across the different versions of the AX-CPT. However, the promise of these results is contradicted by the pattern of latent correlations. The reason is that this pattern of results substantially deviated from the expectations. In a next step, we bypassed the performance indices by applying a bifactor modeling approach directly to response times and error rates. Using this approach, we identified a model for error rates only. However, again, the pattern of latent correlations substantially deviated from the expectations. Together, these results raise concerns about the validity of the measures typically used in the AX-CPT.

AX-CPT Performance Indices and Their Validity Concerns

Proactive and reactive control have been conceptualized as distinct constructs (e.g., Braver et al., 2007; Braver, 2012). This distinction should be reflected in the pattern of correlations among the measures. That is, correlations among proactive measures should be high.

Similarly, correlations among reactive measures should be high. Moreover, because reactive control is assumed to be primarily engaged when proactive control is no longer effective (e.g., Braver et al., 2001 Braver et al., , 2005 Braver et al., , 2007;; Paxton et al., 2008), correlations between proactive and reactive measures should be small or negative. However, the results for the performance indices, particularly the latent correlations, did not match this expected pattern. First, most indices used to assess proactive control were only weakly correlated even at the latent-variable level (latent correlations < .30). At the same time, one of the proactive-control indices (i.e., d′ context) was positively and very strongly correlated with one index used to assess reactive control (i.e., d′ reactive; latent correlation = .86).

How can this pattern of results be accounted for? One explanation lies in how the performance indices are computed. Per design, AY and BX trials are the least frequent in the AX-CPT. However, per theory, they are critical because they are used to distinguish proactive and reactive control (see, e.g., Braver et al., 2007). The infrequency of AY and BX trials but their theoretical relevance has several consequences (see Cooper et al., 2017, for a detailed discussion). First, because AY and BX trials are the least frequent trial types, performance in these trial types may be measured less reliably. This may explain, for example, the low reliability of the BX probe interference that we observed in the present study. Second, if performance is close to ceiling in one of these two trial types, the performance indices may fail to distinguish between proactive and reactive control (see Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016). As a consequence, the performance indices become largely determined by the variance from the complementary trial type used in their computation (e.g., AX trials for d′ context; AY trials for the error-rate based PBI). Depending on which complementary trial type is used to compute the other performance index, this could explain the weak or strong correlation. For example, for the correlation between d′ context and the error-rate based PBI, variance is mainly driven by AX trials for d′ context but by AY trials for the error-rate based PBI. Because the complementary trials differ across both indices, this could explain the weak latent correlation observed between both indices assumed to assess proactive control. A similar explanation can be used to account for the strong latent correlation observed between d′ context and d′ reactive. Namely, both indices are computed from the same trial type (i.e., AX trials), and their variance is mainly driven by this trial type (see Tang et al., 2023, for highlighting the issue of mathematical dependence). Overall, these results raise concerns about the validity of the performance indices typically used in the AX-CPT.

One may argue that the validity concerns about the AX-CPT performance indices may be overcome by applying experimental manipulations used to induce proactive and reactive control (e.g., Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016; Rosales et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023). While these manipulations have been increasingly used in recent research, they raise their own questions.

Proactive control was induced by training participants to prepare their response upon presentation of the first stimulus (e.g., Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016; Rosales et al., 2022; Tang et al., 2023). Thus, in contrast to a version of the task without any manipulation, it may be argued that the measure no longer reflects proactive control. Rather, it may assess the ability to implement the trained strategy. While this may be intended (see Lin et al., 2024), training participants to use a specific strategy goes against the view that attentional control is most needed when a task is novel (see, e.g., Burgess, 1997; Shallice & Burgess, 1996).

For reactive control, two manipulations have been used. First, no-go trials were added. In these trials, for example, a digit was presented instead of a letter as the second stimulus (e.g., Rosales et al., 2022). However, no-go trials may introduce dual-task processes (e.g., responding after the second stimulus while monitoring for the occurrence of the no-go trials). In the other manipulation, no-go trials were presented as well. In addition, the second stimulus in AY, BX, and no-go trials was presented in a distinct position and with a distinct border color (e.g., Tang et al., 2023). For example, the second stimulus in these trial types was presented in the lower half of the screen with a red border. The second stimulus in the other trial types (i.e., AX and BY) was presented in the upper half with a white border (see Table 13 for an overview). Braver et al. (2021) proposed that the location and border color act as a signal to reactivate goal-relevant information. However, it may be argued that the reactivation of goal-relevant information is not necessary. The identity, position, and border color of the second stimulus are in principle sufficient to provide the correct response. For example, when an "X" is presented in the lower half and with a red border, this implies only one possibility: the trial is a BX trial, and the correct response is the non-target key. In contrast, when an "X" is presented in the upper half and with a white border, this implies only one possibility: the trial is an AX trial, and the correct response is the target key. If participants learn these regularities, the first stimulus is no longer necessary to provide the correct response. Thus, it is possible that participants perform the task without reactivating goal-relevant information, questioning whether they use reactive control. Taken together, the experimental manipulations used to induce proactive and reactive control may change the processes to be measured, thus raising new questions regarding the validity of the AX-CPT measures.

Furthermore, irrespective of the conditions, no-go trials were presented to reduce the overall use of proactive control typically observed in young adults (see Gonthier, Macnamara, et al., 2016). The results showed good split-half reliabilities for A-cue bias, d′ context, PBI, and BX probe interference for all three conditions.

However, mixed evidence was observed for the zero-order correlations. Some indices behaved as expected. For example, the proactive-control indices A-cue bias and d′ context were positively correlated (r = .53 to .57). In contrast, some proactive-control indices were negatively correlated.

For example, the response-time based PBI was negatively correlated with the error-rate based PBI (r = -.72 to -.34), and the A-cue bias (r = -.34 to -.15). Moreover, a proactive-control index (d′ context) was strongly and positively correlated with one reactive-control index (errorrate based BX probe interference; r = .79 to .87). Therefore, even with experimental manipulations used to induce proactive and reactive control, the results highlighted that the AX-CPT performance indices did not produce the theoretically expected pattern of correlations. This suggests that these experimental manipulations did not solve the questions regarding the validity of the AX-CPT measures.

In sum, the results observed with the performance indices in the present study did not refute the distinction between proactive and reactive control. Rather, these results raise concerns about the validity of the performance indices typically used in the AX-CPT.

Is the Bifactor Modeling Approach a Solution?

In the present study, we bypassed the validity concerns with the performance indices by applying a bifactor modeling approach. Using this approach, we identified a bifactor model for the error rates only. In this bifactor model, we estimated one general factor and two specific factors representing proactive and reactive control, respectively. In line with the assumption that proactive and reactive control are distinct constructs (e.g., Braver et al., 2001 Braver et al., , 2005 Braver et al., , 2007;; Paxton et al., 2008), the latent correlation between the two specific factors was small and negative (latent correlation = -.19). More surprisingly, working memory capacity was not correlated with any of the specific factors. It was only correlated with the general factor, and this correlation was relatively small (latent correlation = .31). This latent correlation is, however, consistent with a recent study by Rosales et al. (2022). In that study, the authors reported that working memory capacity predicted overall performance in the AX-CPT, rather than specific performance in the trial types assumed to reflect proactive control. Thus, the AX-CPT and complex-span tasks seem to share some, but not all, cognitive processes.

But which of these processes can be shared between both tasks? One candidate may be the temporary maintenance of mental representations. This process is measured for example in complex-span tasks (e.g., Conway et al., 2002). It seems also to be involved in the AX-CPT because participants are asked to retain access to the first stimulus. Because this temporary maintenance is also required in trials used as baseline (e.g., AX trials), this may explain the finding that working memory capacity was correlated with general task performance in the bifactor modeling approach. However, there is one key difference between the AX-CPT and complex-span tasks. In the AX-CPT, interference occurs through expectations or prepotent response tendencies that should be ignored. This interference occurs only once the second stimulus is presented. Thus, it cannot affect the temporary maintenance of the first stimulus before the second stimulus is presented. In contrast, in a complex-span task, a distracting task is performed between the presentation of the memoranda. Thus, the mental representation of the memoranda is affected by interference. As interference has been used to explain the limit of working memory capacity (Oberauer et al., 2012; Oberauer & Lin, 2017), this difference in how and when interference is triggered may explain the weak correlation with working memory capacity. Overall, this emphasizes the necessity to consider more thoroughly which processes or abilities AX-CPT and complex-span tasks may share.

Are the Assumptions of the Present Study Reasonable?

In the present study, we made two critical assumptions. We next discuss each of these assumptions.

Our first assumption was that attentional control is not affected by varying stimulus materials. In the present study, we varied stimulus materials in both the complex-span tasks and the AX-CPT. Such a manipulation is common practice in research on working memory capacity, where different versions of the complex-span task are used to assess the same ability (e.g., Engle et al., 1999; Kane et al., 2004; Wilhelm et al., 2013). In contrast, only a few studies have varied stimulus materials in the AX-CPT (e.g., Lopez-Garcia et al., 2016; MacDonald et al., 2005). In this research, varying stimulus materials has been suggested to engage similar attentional-control processes, but different non-attentional-control processes (in particular, memory processes; -Garcia et al., 2016). These findings are in line with the typical conceptualization according to which attentional control is not material-specific (see, e.g., Burgoyne & Engle, 2020; Kane et al., 2004). Thus, based on the typical conceptualization and these findings, we assumed that varying stimulus materials would not affect the control processes. However, one may argue that varying the stimulus materials may result in a different relative engagement of proactive vs. reactive control. This argument is, however, not supported by our data. Our second assumption was that shared variance serves as a key criterion for convergent validity. This is reasonable from a traditional latent-variable perspective. However, the results may be interpreted using an alternative account, such as process overlap theory (Kovacs & Conway, 2016). According to this account, positive correlations among measures are not necessarily produced by a common construct, but by partially overlapping sets of domaingeneral and task-specific processes. Thus, even within different versions of AX-CPT, performance may reflect overlapping processes that vary by trial type, stimulus material, or type of control (proactive vs. reactive). From this perspective, the high correlation between proactiveand reactive-control indices may indicate overlapping attentional-control, memory, perceptualmotor, or response-selection processes. This could be implemented and tested in a network model (see Rosales et al., 2023; Troche et al., 2021, for examples). The reason is that network models allow relations among measures to be represented without requiring all shared variance to be explained by a common construct. Using such an approach in future research would allow researchers to investigate how AX-CPT measures are clustered (e.g., by trial type, stimulus material, proactive-vs. reactive-control demands).

Are There Alternative Tasks or Measures to Pursue the Goal of the Present Study?

Overall, our results raised concerns about the validity of the AX-CPT measures.

Therefore, we could not draw conclusions about whether reducing heterogeneity in control processes would allow us to establish shared variance across attentional-control measures. This raises the question of whether there is a task or measure that allows us to pursue this goal.

A first candidate may be the flanker task. In this task, participants are asked, for example, to identify the central letter of a row, while ignoring the flanking letters. Proactive and reactive control could be distinguished by implementing experimental manipulations used in previous research (e.g., Bugg, 2015; Bugg & Gonthier, 2020; see also Gonthier, Braver, et al., 2016). For example, proactive control may be manipulated by presenting more incongruent trials (e.g., "SSSCSSS") relative to congruent trials (e.g., "SSSSSSS"). As a result, participants should strategically bias their attention away from the irrelevant information (i.e., the flanking letters; see Gonthier, Braver, et al., 2016). Reactive control may be manipulated by presenting specific flanking letters more often in incongruent trials (e.g., the flanking letters "S" are presented more often in incongruent trials than in congruent trials). These specific flanking letters should act as a signal for high control demands and thus trigger a reactivation of the task goals (see Gonthier, Braver, et al., 2016). Moreover, different versions of the flanker task can be created by using different stimulus materials, such as colors, shapes, letters, and arrows. However, previous research has shown low correlations between different versions of the flanker task (Kane et al., 2016; Rey-Mermet et al., 2018; Salthouse, 2010). This raises some questions about using the flanker task to observe shared variance after reducing heterogeneity in control processes.

A second candidate may still be the AX-CPT, but with model parameters as measures instead of the performance indices. So far, there are a few computational models of the AX-CPT that have yielded parameters that could be used to distinguish between proactive and reactive control (e.g., Braver et al., 1999; Lieder & Iwama, 2021; Shen et al., 2024; Shvartsman et al., 2016). These models have been reported to adequately reproduce behavioral results at the group level, but so far, not at the individual level. Therefore, their applicability in an individualdifferences approach remains to be established.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to test whether reducing task heterogeneity and distinguishing between control processes is sufficient to establish shared variance across attentional-control measures. To this end, we implemented five versions of a single task -the AX-CPT -with different materials, and we computed different measures used to distinguish between proactiveand reactive-control processes. We observed good reliability for most AX-CPT measures and shared variance across the five AX-CPT versions. However, despite these promising results, the latent correlations did not show the theoretically expected pattern. Thus, the results of the present study raise concerns about the validity of the AX-CPT measures.
