Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

1. Introduction

In the mid-1600s, Blaise Pascal introduced the first mathematical framework for understanding human decision-making (Pascal, 1670). He emphasized the notion that humans ought to be acting in a way that rationally and consistently maximized some objective -a mathematical representation of the goal an individual seeks to achieve through their choices. Reasoning that more of a good thing was always better than less, he concluded that the only rational objective was to maximize long-term average outcomes or returns. Humans, he argued, should simply be behaving consistently to get more of what they liked and less of what they disliked. That strategy, now called expected value theory, was used to define rational decision-making in domains ranging from religious beliefs (Pascal, 1670), to fear of accidental death, to wealth maximization (Arnauld & Nicole, 1996). Overall, Pascal's hypothesis rested on two key insights: First, that the objective value of an option is simply the product of gain (or loss) and the probability of that outcome (Pascal & Fermat, 1654). Second, that humans should rationally structure their choices to consistently pursue their goals (Pascal, 1670).

Since Pascal, choice theory has, perhaps unfortunately, undergone a series of revolutions that have shifted it away from this relatively simple and intuitive framework. The first shift was driven by the observation that humans often exhibit what is called risk-averse behavior, apparently preferring lower long-run earnings in order to minimize variance across their possible outcomes. Daniel Bernoulli recognized that this riskaversion was incompatible with most scholars' understanding of Pascal's ideas. He argued that humans act to maximize a different objective, the mathematical product of a logarithmic transformation of gain (the utility function) and the probability of receiving that gain (Bernoulli, 1738).

By the mid-20th century, the notion of what humans were trying to maximize had been relaxed even further based on new mathematical (Pareto, 1906) and theoretical-empirical (Samuelson, 1947) insights. In the expected utility theory, which emerged from these insights, the focus shifted away from the specification of a particular utility function to basic tests of whether observed choice behavior could be accounted for by any utility function at all (Samuelson, 1952; Savage, 1954; Von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944). Scholars stopped defining what a given individual should maximize, they simply tested whether observed behavior was consistently aimed at maximizing any goal at all. By the 1970s, however, it was clear that human choices were not reliably pursuing even the most byzantine of fixed utility functions (Allais, 1953; Ellsberg, 1961). These observations led many economists and psychologists to abandon the notion that behavior was logically consistent: that behavior reflected the rational pursuit of any goal at all. Accordingly, psychologists shifted away from making statements about why humans made the choices they did, or even from making statements about what humans should choose. Instead, they shifted towards simply predicting what a given human chooser would do in a given situation. Kahneman and Tversky (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1981), in their seminal work defining prospect theory, laid the foundation for these purely predictive theories, abandoning logical consistency (or more formally: transitivity) in favor of descriptive power. Their model adopted a referencedependent representation of utility (formally a value function) with multiple empirically derived features and a subjective notion of probability -all of which varied significantly from individual-to-individual and context-to-context. With these components, prospect theory and its progeny did a better job of predicting what humans do choose. What the theory lacked, however, was an explanation of why people made such apparently inconsistent and undirected decisions.

Here, we employ a new approach in an effort to explain the why of many of the classes of choice behavior that Kahneman and Tversky accurately described. To accomplish this goal, we first return to the simplest possible objective: Pascal's assertion that people want to go home with as much good-stuff as possiblefoot_0 . In the spirit of Herb Simon (Simon, 1955), we acknowledge that humans have limited cognitive capacity, and that adding capacity always comes at a cost (a requirement we have shown is imposed by the laws of physics (Steverson et al., 2019)). We define cognitive capacity in the domain of human decision-making as a limit on representational precision (the entropy of the representation), capturing the notion that a given finite level of precision acts as a kind of filter which conditions our internal representation.

How does a simple expected value-like objective combine with limited precision to define optimal choice behavior? We argue that the human nervous system must, in essence, balance the costs of precision against the gains achieved by a more accurate maximization of expected value (Steverson et al., 2019). Drawing inspiration from Bayesian Theory (Bayes, 1763) and Signal Detection Theory (Green & Swets, 2000), we acknowledge that computing the tradeoff between cost and benefit when the world is imprecisely represented requires some knowledge about the structure of the choice environment (essentially a distributional Bayesian prior). We conclude that if the chooser takes as inputs a knowledge (perhaps imperfect) of the structure of the choice environment and knowledge of their own level of precision, they can use that information to select an optimal task-appropriate utility function. This kind of utility function, one selected implicitly by the brain, can be conceptualized as a tool selected by the decision-maker to sculpt their internal representation of value in a way that maximizes average earnings (or minimizes errors) given the limits of their precision. In our construction, the objective-of-choice (maximizing reward or minimizing errors) and the utility function are thus formally separated. The utility function (and the risk attitude it implies) becomes a tool used by the nervous system to achieve the simplest possible goal. Once we employ this approach, we find that many features of human choice previously thought of as irrational resolve as simple strategies for consistent maximization given the constraints imposed by physics on the human brain.

Our approach thus differs from many classical approaches in a number of ways: • We assume that the detailed probabilistic structure of the current reward environment matters to choosers.

This represents a significant departure from most mainstream theories in economics and psychology that define a choice problem as the (sometimes probabilistic) options faced by a decision-maker but leave out the likelihood with which each option is encountered. We consider the likelihood that any given option becomes available for choice to be fundamental for the meaningful specification of a choice problem. In this, we deviate from classical and neoclassical economics, requiring a different and more complete specification of the choice environment as a fundamental part of the choice problem.

• Following Pascal, we assume that a decision-maker's goal is either to 1) maximize long-run average earnings, or 2) minimize their error rates (where an error is choosing an option other than the one that maximizes earnings). Thus, we abandon the notion that humans want to minimize risk in the situations we are modeling. Risk attitudes emerge, in our approach, as a byproduct of the utility functions that choosers select to maximize their objective goals.

• Like most previous theorists, we assume that humans transform objective value inputs to subjective values using a utility-like function. Where we differ from traditional approaches is that we place that representational function under the (non-conscious) control of the chooser (either by their brain or by evolution). In the language of economics, we endogenize the utility function and separate it from their objective.

• We initially leave as a free parameter the precise level of cognitive capacity available to the chooser, examining exhaustively the optimal utility functions that emerge for choosers at any possible level of precision. In our approach, different levels of cognitive capacity are a primary source of individual variation. (A secondary source of variation arises from the subject's individually held prior about the reward distribution of the environment.) Perhaps surprisingly, we find that once the utility function is under the control of the chooser, the advantage incurred by increasing cognitive capacity (which we define formally below) is often surprisingly small. Losses produced by having low cognitive capacity can often be well-compensated for with simple changes to the utility function. In the language of economics: the returns-to-precision are less than might be expected.

• In an allied paper (Glimcher et al., 2026), we extend this set of observations and explore the notion that allocated cognitive capacity itself may also be under the control of the chooser (at least to some extent). In that extension of this work, we define the precise cost of precision and allow the chooser to optimally select how much precision to purchase before selecting a utility function. This final phase of our approach allows us to fully define human choice as an optimization problem where the only currently unmeasured parameter is the cost of precision to the decision-maker. One unique feature of this approach is that it defines the entire choice problem and specifies the utility function and the chooser's stochasticity without resorting to any free parameters in the model of theory. This approach is, in essence, a one parameter model of decision-making that can account for all of behaviors observed in prospect theory.

The work presented here, it should be noted, stops short of exploring decisions over risky lotteries. While in this paper, we view the world as probabilistic, we explore choices which arise in that probabilistic world that are always made between two certain outcomes. In the discussion section, we describe how our approach can be extended to the domain of risky choice. We wish to be explicit about this scope restriction: the theoretical framework, analytical derivations, and simulations presented in this paper apply exclusively to this class of riskless choice -although we find that many classes of puzzling behavior classically associated with risky-choice may not involve risk at all. We adopt this restriction deliberately to isolate the role of representational capacity in shaping value functions, without introducing the additional complexity of probability representation. Readers seeking a general theory of decision under risk will find that our current results, while suggestive, do not yet constitute one. Our approach, however, is extensible to the domain of risky choice and a second presentation of that set of extensions is forthcoming.

What stands out for us in this theory of decision-making amongst certain outcomes is how many puzzling phenomena become easily explainable. The sigmoidal value function of Kahneman and Tversky (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), the concave utility function of Bernoulli (Bernoulli, 1738), and the linear utility function of Pascal (Pascal, 1670) all emerge as nested examples of rational strategies that are required of choosers who face different environments with varying degrees of precision. It is in this regard that we hope our approach has the most impact, effectively serving as a theory of why seemingly irrational behaviors and distortions from objective value-coding emerge. We note that our approach is closely related to a number of recent approaches that have inspired and shaped this work. Of particular note, our work descends from the original theory of stochastic choice pioneered by McFadden (McFadden, 1973), whose random utility framework models the probabilistic nature of observed choices by adding noise to an otherwise fixed utility function. The key difference between our framework and that of McFadden is that in his specification, the utility function and the noise distribution are both exogenous and separately specified, whereas in ours, the utility function is selected by the chooser in response to their noise level. However, like McFadden, we rely principally on an additive Gaussian noise term, although this is not required. In the Supplementary Materials we show that our findings are robust to other noise specifications, including those explored by Zhou et al., (2024) for efficient sensory encoding. In an extension of this work (Glimcher et al., 2026), we show that even the noise level, irrespective of the distributional structure of the noise, can be selected by the chooser in response to a specified cost-of-cognition -further reducing the number of free parameters required for this approach.

We focus on the two objectives of expected earnings maximization and error minimization as these represent the two most theoretically grounded and empirically motivated candidates in the literature, descending respectively from Pascal's normative benchmark and from the empirical works (Robson, 2001; Polanía et al., 2019; Woodford, 2014).

With regard to other closely related work, we note that our approach descends from efficient-coding and noisy-representation accounts of value and choice, which show that distortions in value or probability can arise as optimal adaptations to limited precision (Glimcher, 2011). In one prominent example derived from Glimcher (2011), Woodford (2014) derived stochastic choice behavior from Shannon capacity constraints under rational inattention. Polanía, Woodford & Ruff (2019) extended this approach by providing neural and behavioral evidence that the brain adapts its representational resolution to the prior distribution of experienced outcomes. Khaw, Li, & Woodford (2021) demonstrated that log-normally distributed noise in value representations produces apparent small-stakes risk aversion. Barretto-García et al. (2023) extended this to individual differences in risk attitudes. Frydman & Jin (2021) derived both utility curvature and probability weighting from efficient coding of risky lotteries. Juechems et al. (2021) derived optimal utility and probability functions for agents with finite computational precision. Heng, Woodford & Polanía (2020) showed that humans adapt their sampling behavior to environmental distributions to economize on cognitive resources.

We share with all of these papers, which descend from Glimcher (2011), the central premise that apparent distortions in value representation reflect rational adaptation to finite capacity, but innovate and consolidate to yield a single and highly tractable framework. First, we focus on riskless choice between certain outcomes before using our consolidated approach with regard to risky lotteries. This is not a trivial distinction and extending our framework to risky lotteries requires separately modelling the capacity-constrained representation of outcome probability using exactly the approach proposed here for utility. Second, and more fundamentally, these accounts derive the utility function as a fixed consequence of a specific coding problem, with a given prior and a given noise model as the universal inputs that drive our model. Our framework differs fundamentally from these earlier approaches in that for us, the utility function is the explicit variable over which the chooser optimizes. Third, rather than characterizing a single optimal function for a given prior and noise model, as in these other approaches, we exhaustively map optimal utility functions across the full space of precision levels, from near-zero to near-infinite precision, and across many reward distributions. This allows us to generate the full family of optimal functions as a continuous output and allows our approach to be used in almost any situation, rather than yielding a point solution for a particular problem of interest. We also find, for the first time, that the returns to increasing cognitive precision are surprisingly small once the utility function is optimally adjusted to the available capacity, with a doubling of encoding resources yielding only a two to three percent improvement in average earnings.

Our approach also incorporates important features of the Decision-by-Sampling literature (e.g., Stewart et al., 2006 Stewart et al., , 2015)). In that literature, Bhui & Gershman (2018) provided an important normative foundation for our general approach, showing that rank-based sampling is formally equivalent to efficient coding. Our framework also, of course, captures ideas from evolutionary and biological accounts of utility (Netzer, 2009; Netzer et al., 2025; Rayo & Becker, 2007; Robson, 2001; Robson et al., 2023). What unifies all these accounts, and what we adopt from them, is the principle that the utility function is an optimized response to the structure of the environment and the constraints under which choices are made. Our approach departs from these in that we treat the full probabilistic structure of the reward environment as a formal component of the choice problem and treat the function as both dynamic and fully endogenous to the chooser.

Finally, our work is also closely related to, and draws deep inspiration from, recent contributions that study the cognitive limits of valuation and choice, including work by Charness et al. (2013), Oprea (2024), and Webb et al. (2021). These papers collectively establish that there is substantial individual variation in the quality of utility maximization that cannot be captured by a single fixed utility function, and that cognitive limits are a primary driver of that variation. Our approach complements this literature by providing a normative account of how valuation itself should adapt to such constraints, rather than treating the resulting utility distortions as exogenous or descriptive.

2. Methods

We present our methods in two ways. In sections 2.1-2.3, we present a formal mathematical specification of the problem we set out to solve, which may not be appropriate for all readers. In section 2.4, we present a graphical overview of our methods that should be appropriate for all readers. Readers with a deep mathematical interest in our approach should read all four sections. Readers with intermediate interest are encouraged to skip sections 2.2 and 2.3. Readers interested in the general approach should proceed directly to section 2.4.

It is also important to note that in attempting to answer the larger question: What is the optimal utility function for a given environment when facing a riskless choice problem, we have developed three overlapping mathematical approaches. We have developed a proof-based approach, an analytic approach that relies on features of Gaussian noise to achieve computational simplicity, and a general-purpose Monte Carlo-based approach which can solve essentially any choice problem. The latter two approaches are described in this paper. Interested readers can examine the proof-based approach in an adjacent economicsstyle paper, which is partially overlapping in content but aimed at an economics audience (Glimcher et al., 2026). The analytic approach generalizes that purely mathematical solution to environments in which choosers face a larger number of possible reward values 2 . The bulk of the material presented here relies on this analytic approach. This analytic approach, however, cannot be used to identify an optimal utility function when the decision-maker must choose between more than two options (a change which has a significant impact on the shape of the optimal utility function). For that class of problems, we generalize our solution to a numerical Monte-Carlo-style strategy (Metropolis & Ulam, 1949). This allows us to solve for optimal utility functions under essentially any conditions.

2.1 General Mathematical Assumptions About the Choice Environment

We begin by stating explicitly the objective of the chooser is either to 1) maximize average earnings or 2) minimize the number of errors. Our goal is to recover an optimal utility function 𝑢(𝑥), which is dependent on the choice environment 3 and the cognitive capacity of the chooser, and which maximizes the stated objective function.

The choice environment is defined by a formal set containing a fixed number, 𝑚, of possible rewards (or punishments) which we call 𝑋 = {𝑥 ! , . . . , 𝑥 " }, where each 𝑥 # is a real number (a possible reward value that the decision-maker can encounter). Without loss of generality, but to make the problem more intuitive for this presentation, we assume that 𝑥 ! ≤ 𝑥 $ ≤. .. ≤ 𝑥 " (thus 𝑥 " is always used to label the largest possible reward and 𝑥 ! the smallest). Additionally, we assume that each next reward in 𝑋 increases in objective value by the same increment, for example, by one dollar.

Choice sets are constructed by drawing one or more possible rewards from the set 𝑋. A decision-maker's task is to select one option from a given choice set. The probability that any given reward will be drawn from the environment (𝑋) into a choice set with 𝑛 possible rewards is specified by a probability distribution 4.

We adopt weak rather than strict monotonicity to allow for the possibility that a decision-maker is indifferent between two reward levels, that is, that two objectively different rewards may be assigned the same subjective utility. Restricting the utility function so that it cannot have an infinite range goes against a feature of many standard economic models (e.g., Bernoulli, 1738) but anchors our theory to the physical world the human nervous system occupies and is a fundamental constraint on our theory. Together, these two assumptions define the candidate set of utility functions considered throughout this paper. No further parametric restrictions are imposed, and we do not assume any functional form, such as power utility or logarithmic utility. The optimal utility function 𝑢 * is identified by searching exhaustively over this entire candidate set, making our approach, uniquely, nonparametric with respect to the shape of the utility function.

This approach requires the following simplifications, two of which can be relaxed in the next section: 1) That the utility axis is discrete rather than continuous. 2) That choosers must select from only two options on a given trial. 3) That the choice options be drawn i.i.d. from the environment. 2.2.3 Finding the Optimal Utility Function

Knowing how to recover the probability of an error for any specific choice between two options for any utility function at any given capacity, we can proceed to calculate the overall earnings (gains or losses) and error rates associated with any particular utility function in any given environment. We accomplish this in two stages. First, for each choice set under a given utility function, we calculate the probability of an error as well as expected earnings. Then, after we calculate this for all possible choice pairs, we total up the aggregate errors and earnings while taking into account how often each choice set is encountered in the given environment. Below, we formally describe this process for our two optimality criteria: (1) maximizing earnings and (2) minimizing the number of errors.

2.3 A Monte-Carlo Approach: Choices between more than two options

The analytic approach described above can be used to identify optimal utility functions when the choice sets (the options being compared) contain only two options with rewards independently drawn from the environment (where the environment is defined by the set X and its attendant probabilities of occurrence).

The key limitation of this approach is that the exact probability of correctly identifying the best option relies on the closed-form properties of the difference of two independent Gaussian random variables (a difference which is described by a Gaussian). When decision-makers must choose between more than two options or choice sets have complex covariance structures that make the rewards non-independent, this specific closed-form expression is no longer available, and the error probability cannot be derived analytically in the same tractable way. For those choice conditions, we use a Monte-Carlo-style simulation to identify the optimal utility function.

As with our analytic solution, we begin by specifying the choice environment and the capacity of the chooser (the level of internal noise). As before, the utilities for the lowest reward value and the highest reward value are set to 0 and 𝑐, respectively. As before, we generate a list of all utility functions we wish to consider: {𝑢 ! , 𝑢 $ , . . . , 𝑢 ) }. Next, for each 𝑢, we simulate one million choices and calculate either the earnings (for the reward maximization objective) or the number of errors (for the number of error minimization objective) across these simulated choices.

Our procedure is as follows: We assume a candidate utility, 𝑢 # , and a capacity level, 𝑐. Next, we draw one million choice sets (choice problems) according to the environmental reward probability structure. Then, one choice set at a time, we assign a noisy utility to each reward in this choice set following 𝑈(𝑥 # ) = 𝑢(𝑥 # ) + 𝜀 # . Then, the algorithm selects from this choice set the reward with the highest noisy utility 𝑈.

After doing this for one million choice sets, we compute the average earnings and the total number of errors for the assumed 𝑢 # with capacity level 𝑐. Finally, to identify the optimal utility function, we select the one that maximizes expected reward or minimizes the total error.

We note that the original utility function proposed by Bernoulli (Bernoulli, 1738) took essentially this form and the choice problem he was trying to explain (the St Petersburg Paradox) had a positively skewed distribution of possible rewards, just as this example. This may suggest that Bernoulli's utility function would be optimal for a chooser trying to maximize reward in the St Petersburg game environment, with the caveat that the classic game explicitly involves risk which is not directly modeled here.

• When all options are equally probable, in a uniformly distributed environment, 𝑢 * is sigmoidal, suggesting an adaptive balance between what might be classically described as risk-aversion and risk-seeking behavior. The function exhibits greater sensitivity to mid-range values, ensuring efficient differentiation across the entire value spectrum which minimizes the most-costly mistakes, erroneously selecting 𝑥 ! or 𝑥 $ when 𝑥 ; is available. We note that the original sigmoidal utility function of Kahneman and Tversky was largely developed to account for behavioral data from experiments in which the probabilities of encountering a given reward were uniformly distributed. The prospect theory utility function developed for risky choice, although quite different from Bernoulli's function, might be seen as an optimal strategy for maximizing reward, this time in an experimental psychology laboratory.

• In a negatively skewed environment, 𝑢 * is convex, which would imply risk-seeking behavior where lotteries are employed, a strategy that prioritizes differentiation among common high-value options. Thus, a convex utility can, under some conditions, be optimal. This stands in stark contrast to the standard economic conclusion that such functions, at least in the domain of risky-choice, are considered irrational because they violate second-order stochastic dominance.

3.5 Result V: As Choice Set Size Increases, Optimal Utility Functions Steepen and Shift to the Right

A limit of the analytic approach employed up to this point is that it cannot identify optimal utility functions when there are more than two alternatives in the choice set. Here, we show that a strategy of exhaustive simulation, which does not have this limitation, can be used to identify optimal utility functions under essentially any conditions (details in the Methods section). We first validated our Monte Carlo solution by confirming that it replicates the results of the analytic method for binary choice problems across all three canonical environments; this validation is presented in Supplementary Materials Section B. Having established the accuracy of our solution we next investigate the effect of increasing choice-set size on the shape of the optimal utility function.

Figure 14 shows how capacity and choice set size interact in three different environments. The first row of optimal utility functions replicates our earlier findings. For a choice between two options, optimal utility functions show diminishing curvature as capacity increases. The third row of the figure shows how these optimal utility functions change when four options are drawn at random from the choice environment and the decision-maker must select a single option from this four-option set. The fourth and fifth rows present functions for choice set sizes of 6 and 10. Notice that as the choice set size grows, the functions noticeably steepen to the right. This is a key insight that has not yet been the subject of much inquiry by economists or psychologists. The goal of the utility function that maximizes the average rate of return is to discriminate effectively amongst the highest valued of the available options. As the number of options in the choice set grows, the likelihood that at least one option will be very highly valued also grows. This puts a premium on making an effective discrimination amongst the most highly valued options, causing the functions to steepen to the right as the size of the choice set grows. The novel implication of this finding is that for an optimal chooser, risk attitudes should depend on choice set size.

4.1 Dataset

To provide an initial empirical assessment of our framework and to define the procedures required for fitting empirical data to our theory, we reanalyzed data from a previously collected two-stage experiment (Kurtz-David et al., 2024). That experiment was designed to examine how value representations adapt when the statistical structure of the choice environment is changed, a core feature of our theory. These valuations were used to estimate an individual subjective-value function using a power-utility specification. For subjects proceeding to Stage 2, participants were selected whose risk aversion during the BDM bidding procedure (which is known to be distinct from their risk aversion level during choice) was narrowly bounded (𝑝𝑜𝑤𝑒𝑟 𝑢𝑡𝑖𝑙𝑖𝑡𝑦 𝑐𝑢𝑟𝑣𝑎𝑡𝑢𝑟𝑒 𝑎𝑡 0.9 < 𝜌 < 1.1). Because each participant experienced both environments, the dataset allows us to examine how estimates of representational capacity (and the shape of the optimal utility function) change within the same individual as the environmental statistics change. This permits us to examine two properties of the capacity estimates obtained from our framework: 1) whether individual differences in estimated capacity are preserved across environments, consistent with capacity reflecting a relatively stable individual constraint on reward representation; and 2) whether the level of estimated capacity also changes with the statistical structure of the environment, potentially reflecting differences in the benefits of allocating cognitive resources.

4.2 Analysis

4.2.1 Constructing the Individual Error-matrices

Recall that our theory was specifically designed to predict precisely when and how errors (failures to pick the higher valued option) occur, this differs from most standard models. We also treat the objective value axis as discrete in our theory, another important difference. For each participant, we therefore first discretized the range of lottery expected values into subject-specific value bins. To ensure that the two environments were evaluated on a common value scale, the bin boundaries were defined as evenly spaced quantiles of choice problems using the participant's lottery values in the Uniform environment and then held fixed for the Pareto environment. The expected values of both options from all Uniform trials were thus pooled and divided into 12 equally populated bins using a simple quantile-based binning procedure with the lower and upper boundaries fixed at the minimum ($0) and maximum ($60) values encountered. These same 12 bins were then also employed in the Pareto environment. The midpoint of each bin was used as its representative value.

For each environment, every binary-choice trial was thus assigned to a pair of value bins according to the expected values of its two options 7 . A choice was classified as an error if the participant selected the option with the lower expected value. For every choice-pair (or more precisely for every choice-pair bin), we calculated both the number of observed choices involving that pair and the proportion of those choices that were errors. This yielded a symmetric empirical error matrix for each participant for each environment. The trials in which both options fell within the same value bin, the diagonal cells, were excluded from model fitting as errors are not possible in those bins. To avoid fitting to error rates determined by a single observation, cells containing fewer than two trials were also excluded from our analysis. Only one triangular half of the symmetric matrix is presented, which captures all possible pairwise choices. An example of such a matrix for a subject can be seen in Figure 16D.

4.2.2 Computing Expected Error Rates as a Function of Capacity

Recall that our theory rests on the notion that choosers adapt their utility functions to the current environment in order to maximize overall returns. In order to most precisely specify the environment each chooser encountered, we used how often each value bin appeared in the participant's choices to describe the specific value environment that chooser experienced. For each bin (and hence choice problem), we counted how often an error was observed in that bin and how many total problems were encountered by that chooser in that bin. Dividing errors by the total number of encountered problems we derived an error rate and a total count for each bin. This gave us the probability of encountering each value bin in that environment and the error rate observed in that bin for that chooser.

Our goal is to derive, for the observed choice environment defined by the encounter rates, optimal utility functions for every possible capacity level. Once these optimal utility functions have been derived for this specific environment at each possible capacity level, we can convert these optimal utility functions into predicted error rates that should have been observed in each bin if an ideal chooser was employing that optimal utility function, and hence the capacity level associated with that utility function. Using the binomial likelihood to correct for the number of encounters, we can then ask which theoretical error matrix (each associated with a different capacity) best matches the observed error rates produced by a given subject.

5. Discussion

Biologists and engineers think of decision-making as a mechanism directed towards achieving an objective goal. These approaches descend from Pascal, who defined a decision-maker's objective goal as maximizing how much good-stuff they have in the long-run (Pascal, 1670). The notion that human decisions are guided by purely subjective goals, captured by an idiosyncratic utility function that does not maximize how much stuff you have at the end of the day, entered choice theory in the 1700s. In developing that approach, Bernoulli (Bernoulli, 1738) was motivated by the observation that humans showed an aversion to risks in the famous St. Petersburg game. Bernoulli interpreted this behavior as inconsistent with the goal of maximizing objective average rates of return. In the years that followed, subjective utility functions became the standard for psychologists as well as for economists. By the late 1900s, with the work of Kahneman and Tversky (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1981) and their development of an (intransitive) value function, the notion that human decision-making was subjective, could not be considered optimizing, and could not be construed as driven by any clear objective function had become widely accepted.

There were, however, elements of this approach that raised persistent challenging questions. Matthew Rabin (Rabin, 2000), for example, showed that if one uses the curvature of the human utility function measured in the laboratory to predict human behavior when making very large purchases of durable goods, it predicts a level of risk aversion that is never observed. What the Rabin Paradox revealed is that the risk aversion implied by the standard utility function approach derived in the laboratory and those derived from realworld analysis of major purchases are incompatible.

Here, we argue that this comes from a misunderstanding of just why the human utility function arises in nature. In our work (Glimcher et al., 2026) and other recent work like it (Barretto-García et al., 2023; Oprea, 2024), it is hypothesized that the curvature of the human utility function for small stakes decisions has nothing to do with a desire to avoid risk. For a decision maker with limited cognitive capacity, a curved utility function (which produces what can be interpreted as an aversion to risk) is required if one's goal is to maximize how much good-stuff one has on average. This leads us to suggest that, at least for small stakes, the utility function is not a goal, but rather a tool. It is not an objective in and of itself, but a reward encoding strategy used by the nervous system to achieve a different and well-specified objective function. We believe that this is our key contribution, and an extension of earlier work that has explored similar themes (Barretto-García et al., 2023; Frydman & Jin, 2021; Glimcher, 2011; Khaw et al., 2021; Netzer, 2009; Netzer et al., 2025; Polanía et al., 2019; Rayo & Becker, 2007; Robson, 2001; Robson et al., 2023; Woodford, 2014). A separation of the objective and utility functions, and a reinterpretation of the utility function as being an empirical tool dynamically configured by the human (and animal) decisional architecture in service of the objective function -at least for small stakes day-to-day decision-making -is, we believe, a necessary and fundamental reanalysis of the utility function that has been gaining traction in the last fifteen years. In addition to this primary contribution, we can also draw what we believe to be six significant conclusions about the shape of the optimal utility function.

5.1 Summary of Major Findings

5.1.1 Reward Distributions Determine Optimal Utility

The first of our observations is that the distribution of possible rewards, the context of the choice, determines the shape of the optimal utility function for choosers with limited cognitive capacity. For choosers facing a world in which small rewards are prevalent and larger rewards are rare (right-skewed distributions), optimal utility functions are concave and resemble the kinds of risk-averse utility functions Bernoulli favored. In fact, if we write out the specific distribution of possible rewards in the famous St Petersburg gamefoot_5 , we discover that the optimal utility function is almost exactly the function Bernoulli proposed (𝑙𝑜𝑔 (𝑥)). Bernoulli's utility function maximizes Pascal's objective for a noisy chooser facing a St Petersburg game-like distribution of possible outcomes.

In contrast, when choosers face a uniformly distributed set of rewards, the optimal utility function is sigmoidal. The sigmoidal utility function was first widely popularized by Kahneman and Tversky (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), but see also Savage and Friedman (Friedman & Savage, 1948) who argued that Bernoulli's function could not account for the behavior of their subjects. One cannot help but note that Kahneman and Tversky performed most of their experiments with environments in which rewards were distributed uniformly. Environments in which rewards are uniformly distributed, even in the absence of true risk, create a new shape for the utility function that mirrors the one Kahneman and Tversky encountered.

Finally, we found that if choosers face a left-skewed distribution, the optimal utility function is in fact convex. Choosers in these environments should show a utility structure classically associated with riskseeking behavior. In traditional economics, this kind of risk-seeking behavior is often referred to as a violation of second-order stochastic dominance. What this finding suggests is that violations of secondorder stochastic dominance in a left-skewed environment, long treated as irrational, may be much more reasonable than has been generally supposed.

The environments considered here are intentionally simplified. A natural question is how this environmental sensitivity operates in the real world, where decisions from many different contexts are randomly interleaved and choosers face the hard problem of selecting an estimate of the environment in which they are operating. While our framework does not include a specific description of how this selection is made, we infer from existing work on this problem that adaptation to the environment is best conceptualized as a slow, implicit process, likely analogous to gain control in sensory systems (Carandini & Heeger, 2012). In such a specification the utility function reflects a gradually updated approximation to the aggregate reward distribution experienced over the relevant timescale. This interpretation is consistent with empirical work showing that value representations shift in response to recently encountered reward statistics through mechanisms of range adaptation and divisive normalization (Barretto-García et al., 2023; Khaw et al., 2021; Polanía et al., 2019; Webb, Glimcher & Louie, 2021). How precisely the brain performs this implicit averaging, and over what timescales, remains an important direction for future empirical work and likely is a source of significant inter-individual variation in the selected utility function expressed by different choosers.

5.1.2 Capacity Determines Both Utility and Earnings

Our second class of findings relates to the relationship between cognitive capacity, operationalized as the magnitude of the internal noise in the brain's representation of reward magnitude, and 1) the shape of the utility function and 2) the impact of noise on earnings. We find that as cognitive capacity increases (or equivalently as internal noise decreases), utility functions in all three straighten. This is an almost trivial observation. Pascal demonstrated four centuries ago that a straight utility function was return maximizing for any environment in a chooser with infinite capacity. This is what we observe. As noise goes to zero, optimal utility functions shift towards the straight lines Pascal defined as optimal for his noiseless choosers. What is not trivial is that the nonlinear utility functions that emerge at low to moderate capacity levels are not imperfect approximations to the straight-line ideal but genuinely optimal strategies in their own right.

Much more interesting than this is to examine how reductions in noise change the actual earnings of subjects. Many hold the intuition that if choosers are very noisy, then they behave randomly and earn very little. Our findings run against this intuition. Increases in capacity from 2 to 3, and perhaps even to 4 standard deviations do improve earnings, but much more slowly than one might expect -as long as the chooser is allowed to adapt their utility function to their specific capacity level. Even more interesting is the observation that above about 4 standard deviations, increases in capacity have very small impacts on overall earnings. This has, we believe, huge implications for biological choosers. If one is creating an internal representation of value with a finite biological system that effectively employs just 2 standard deviations in a left-skewed environment, one outperforms a random chooser by 7%. Doubling the cognitive capacity (roughly doubling the biological cost of the encoding system (Glimcher, 2022)) yields only a 2% additional increment in earnings. What has often been treated as a limitation of biological decision-making systems may instead reflect a rational equilibrium, in which the brain operates at the minimum precision level needed to achieve near-optimal earnings, reserving metabolic resources for other demands. In Glimcher et al. (2026) we formalize this insight, arguing that choosers or evolution may in fact select a precision level that exactly balances cognitive costs against the benefits of higher precision to maximize their net return optimally.

These results also raise the question of the extent to which human decision makers might intentionally reduce their cognitive costs, reduce their cognitive capacity when in low-stakes environments. If we think of cognitive costs as being roughly fixed and monotonic, we can assess the degree to which capacity is adjustable by simply repeating the same experiment at different payoff levels. As we show in some detail in (Glimcher et al., 2026), such experiments could even allow us to determine the cost of capacity and the exact structure of our hidden cognitive cost function. Using this approach in the companion paper, we show how an optimal chooser facing a fixed noise cost can select both a capacity and a utility function optimally. This approach allows us, essentially, to eliminate all free parameters from our model and to treat the selection of an optimal utility function as a fully closed problem.

5.1.3 Maximizing Rewards vs Minimizing Errors

Recently, significant effort has been devoted to assessing whether human choice behavior can be better thought of as aimed at minimizing errors or maximizing rewards. Woodford and colleagues (Polanía et al., 2019; Woodford, 2014), amongst others, have tentatively concluded that the two objectives yield such similar optimal choice strategies that it is difficult to empirically resolve this issue.

Our data confirm these conclusions in many, but not all, environments. In uniformly distributed environments, for example, optimal utility functions for maximizing gain and for minimizing errors are almost identical, which might explain why existing experiments have struggled to discriminate between these two objectives. However, they can be discriminated in bimodally distributed environments, particularly at low capacity levels. Our data suggest that an appropriately designed environment can be used to experimentally discriminate between these two objectives.

5.1.4 Generalization to Bigger Environments: Steeper Sigmoids

The bulk of the data we present was computed for environments in which there are only 4 possible prizes that lie equally spaced on the objective value axis. Our fourth general result was developed by examining equally spaced sets of rewards containing more than 4 elements. While there is no conceptual barrier to selecting sets of any size (or employing any spacing on the value axis), larger sets take more computational time. For this reason, we limited this presentation to a maximum of 6 possible evenly spaced rewards (See Glimcher et al., 2026, for 7 possible rewards). As the size of the set of possible rewards grows, the utility functions we encounter become smoother and more classical-looking. For uniformly distributed rewards, we found that the sigmoidal functions also become steeper and generally look more like the classical value function of Kahneman and Tversky. Perhaps surprisingly, we found that even left-skewed distributions can yield sigmoidal optimal utility functions, at very low capacities, suggesting that the sigmoid might not be diagnostic of a uniform environment but rather emerges as a universal feature of low-capacity choosers facing sufficiently rich reward sets.

5.1.5 Larger Choice Sets Shift Optimal Utility Functions to the Right

The final result we highlight concerns a variable that has received surprisingly little attention in the utility function literature: the size of the choice set itself. We found that the optimal utility functions steepen and shift to the right as the size of the choice set grows. This makes intuitive sense. As the number of possible draws from the environment increases, the likelihood of encountering at least one option that lies far to the right increases, and the utility function thus shifts to the right to improve accuracy in detecting this best option. This sensitivity of the optimal utility function to choice set size has not, to our knowledge, been previously presented. (Although many studies have examined choice set size (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Schwartz, 2004; Webb et al., 2021).)

It should also be noted that these Monte-Carlo-based estimates are not quite as well-behaved as our analytical results. One will notice immediately that estimates of utility function curvature around reward values that are very rarely encountered in the environment can be somewhat noisy. This can be seen both in the color plots of all possible utility functions and in the 'best utility function' plots. This imprecision arises because the Monte-Carlo approach which yields poor estimates of events which are very infrequent. This can be overcome, of course, by using a much larger number of iterations in the simulations.

5.1.6 Empirical Evaluation

Our final general result comes from applying our new framework to an existing dataset in which the same individuals made repeated choices in Uniform and Pareto reward environments. Unlike standard models in which a utility (or value) function and a noise term are fit by some form of a least squares procedure, to test our model we examine the error matrices produced by our subjects directly and compare them to the error matrices predicted by our theory. Testing our theory thus resolves empirically to employing a single free parameter and a binomial theorem-based likelihood analysis to assess goodness of fit. We fit capacity to a subject's observed choice behavior in a three-step process. First, we explicitly define as an error any choice in which the chooser selects the lower valued option. Next, we aggregate the pattern of observed errors into an error matrix. Finally, we compare the empirically observed error matrix to the set of theoretically predicted error matrices which differ in the capacity allocated by the chooser to the choice problem. We perform this comparison using a simple binomial likelihood procedure. The result is a best-fit capacity parameter and an overall likelihood score.

The empirical analysis provides two insights into the role of representational capacity in human choice. First, estimated capacities were strongly correlated across the Uniform and Pareto environments, suggesting that the capacity parameter captures relatively stable individual differences in decision-making precision.

Participants with relatively high capacity in one environment tended to have relatively high capacity in the other. Second, despite this stability in relative capacity, the estimated levels differed across environments, with participants generally exhibiting lower capacity in the Pareto environment. Interestingly, this environment also offered lower potential earnings. One possible interpretation is that choosers adjust the capacity allocated to decision-making in response to the benefits of greater precision, a possibility developed formally in Glimcher et al. (2026). The current results are consistent with this interpretation but do not establish the mechanism underlying capacity allocation.

It is worth noting that models like EU and prospect theory struggle to fit highly stochastic subjects. For these subjects the utility or value function cannot be well defined because the models rely on their noise terms to account for these choices of these choosers rather than relying on the utility function -thus leading to unspecified utility functions for these choosers. In our theory, these low-capacity choosers are a key feature of the model. They are easily and precisely accounted for by our approach. In contrast, our model struggles to define the precise capacity level of choosers who are nearly perfect expected value maximizers. This is because these choosers have, in our model, very high-capacity levels. Whether a chooser has a capacity of 20 or 25 can be hard to distinguish for our model. Said another way, for these choosers of very high capacity the underlying utility functions are all virtually identical making model fitting difficult. In contrast, models like expected utility theory and prospect theory can take advantage of this homogeneity because it is the utility function, rather than the capacity, that they are trying to fit.

Finally, we note that all of these differences can make the two classes of models, standard utility models and our capacity model, hard to compare empirically. The very different fitting procedures, the fact that they can account for different kinds of choosers in different ways, and the different numbers of parameters, all make head-to-head comparisons difficult. One future direction for our research program will be to develop experiments specifically designed to stress test these models against one another.

5.2 Implications for What are Traditionally Considered Risky-Choice Behaviors

The utility analysis presented here has been focused on riskless choice, choices between two certain options. All theoretical derivations and simulations apply exclusively to this domain. While many of the utility curves we recover bear striking similarities in shape to those employed by Bernoulli, the Utilitarians of the 19th Century, and Kahneman and Tversky, nearly all of that research was focused on risky-choice and rested on the assumption that the curvature of the utility functions they observed was specifically about a response to risk. Our finding of essentially identical curvature for the utility function in the absence of risk raises the question of whether a riskless framework can explain phenomena that have traditionally been characterized as specifically related to the domain of risk, a suggestion which builds on recent work by Barretto-García et al. (2023) and Oprea (2024), amongst others.

5.2.1 The Reflection Effect

The reflection effect is, at its core, a claim about the shape of the utility function around a central reference value. In 1981, Kahneman and Tversky (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981) described what they referred to as the Reflection Effect, the observation that gambles of equal magnitude above and below the reference point show roughly equal and opposite risk preferences. More broadly, this is often taken to imply that for a given chooser, risk attitudes are symmetric but inverted about the reference point. If the risk attitude above the reference point is modeled as 𝑢(𝑥) = (𝑥 -𝑟𝑝) F , the risk preferences of the same subject can be modeled below the reference point as 𝑢(𝑥) = -(𝑥 -𝑟𝑝) 1F.

While Kahneman and Tversky made this characterization using risky lotteries as the objects of choice, it is of some note that our framework recovers this structure precisely as optimal under specific conditions without any appeal to risk. As Figure 10F shows, a perfectly symmetrical function can be optimal for a perfectly uniform environment with the inflection point of that sigmoid naturally defining the reference point, but non-uniform environments can require sigmoids that are non-symmetrical. In our framework, optimality depends critically on both the capacity of the chooser and the distributional structure of the environment and can yield curved cardinal utility functions derived from the patterns of errors a chooser makes, rather than from indifference points amongst the risky lotteries employed by Kahneman and Tversky.

Given this, it seems relevant to note that the actual patterns of observed utility function curvature are highly variable from subject to subject and environment to environment (Bucher & Brandenburger, 2022; Glimcher & Tymula, 2023). In Chung et al. (2019), we systematically examined utility function curvature in the gain and loss domains and found very little evidence for a reliable reflection of risk attitudes at the single-subject level. Only at the population level did we see clear symmetry. This observed heterogeneity has often led to challenges to the reflection effect. Under our assumptions, optimality depends on the specific environment encountered by the decision-maker, the decision-maker's beliefs about that environment, and the capacity of the decision-maker.

5.2.2 Loss Aversion (Gain-Loss Asymmetry)

Another fundamental element of behavioral decision science is Loss Aversion (Kahneman et al., 1991; Kahneman & Tversky, 1979), also known as a Gain-Loss Asymmetry (Plott & Zeiler, 2007). Where the reflection effect concerns symmetric curvature, loss aversion concerns asymmetric curvature. In 1979, Kahneman and Tversky noted that human decision-makers were not indifferent between a sure gain of $0 and a lottery which had equal likelihoods of yielding a gain of $10 and a loss of $10. This was puzzling. If the human utility function is symmetrical about $0, the equal losses and gains should cancel each other out and choosers should be indifferent between these two options. Instead, humans show an asymmetry in the assessment of gains and losses under a wide range of conditions. To understand this phenomena, early prospect theorists made two strong assumptions: First, that the reference point (although unmeasured) was fixed in this experiment at $0. Second, that the first derivative of the value function above and below the reference point had an identical absolute value (an assumption known as the reflection effect). Given those two assumptions, the only way to account for these observed phenomena was to hypothesize that "losses loom larger than gains" independent of the curvature of the gain and loss utility functions. Very important to this analysis, however, are these two assumptions: that the reference point is fixed at a fully-known location and that the utility function has fixed and identical degrees of curvature above and below this reference point.

This immediately raises an important caveat when considering empirical tests of loss aversion. These tests almost all rely on assumptions about the exact location of the reference point held by the chooser. To understand this, consider the utility function presented in Figure 10F for an environment with uniformly distributed rewards. By most definitions, the reference point for this function lies midway between 3 and 4. If an experimentalist were to define 4 as the expected reference point when subjects were using this value function, they would observe a gain-loss asymmetry, a kind of loss aversion: If subjects were given a choice between $4 for sure or a 50/50 chance of either $3 or $5, they would prefer the $4 for sure. The asymmetry arises because the value function rises more slowly above 4 than it falls below 4. Were one to repeat the experiment around $3.5, however, the human choice behavior would be perfectly symmetrical and there would be no evidence of loss aversion. This highlights the importance of an experimentalist's definition of the reference point.

Our data does, however, support the notion that the value function can, under some conditions, be quite asymmetric even in the absence of risky lotteries. To see this, compare Figures 13F and 13G. While 13F shows a symmetrical function driven by a uniformly distributed environment, 13G shows a highly asymmetrical function driven by a left-skewed environment. In this construction, however, the loss-gain asymmetry is in no way irrational or puzzling, it is simply a strategy for dealing efficiently with some classes of input distributions and emerges as a feature of decision-makers in the absence of risky lotteries.

Both of these observations suggest that what we call loss aversion can emerge from either a misspecification (by the experimenter) of the reference point, or from an asymmetrical value function driven by the chooser's efficient behavior in a non-uniform environment. More importantly, perhaps, our approach reveals that loss aversion-like behavior can be observed in the absence of lotteries.

5.3 Extensions of Our Framework to Decision Under Risk -Implications for Future Work

Next, we turn to important extensions to our framework into the domain of truly risky choice. It is important to note that what follows are speculative extensions of our findings that are not formally derived within the current framework but which we consider to be highly relevant to this work.

While we do adopt a representation of the environment as probabilistic in this paper, it is important to be aware that we do not here include the notion that the object of an actual choice can be, in essence, a lottery ticket. Extrapolating our approach to prospects that are themselves risky or uncertain requires two additional steps that we have not taken here. First, we must incorporate the notion of a representation of probability (not just utility) inside the chooser, which is itself cognitively costly and capacity-limited. If we extend our approach to include the subjective representation of probabilities, we must ask the question: What is the optimal probability weighting function while at the same time asking: what is the optimal utility function? In a forthcoming presentation we extend our approach to probability weighting functions in this manner.

To address risk in its traditional sense we need to define how choosers should (in a normative sense) represent the environment when it includes probabilistic outcomes. The critical idea here is that for choosers with limited capacity facing lotteries, there are really two kinds of probability: The probability of encountering a lottery (the environment) and the probability of winning a lottery (the outcome probability).

These two probabilities have different impacts on choice and must be handled separately, which may not be immediately obvious.

One is tempted to bundle these two kinds of probability together: To assume that choosers encode a lottery ticket with a 50% chance of winning $10 as an environment that independently includes both a possible reward of $0 with a probability of 50% and possible reward of $10 with a probability of 50%. The drawback of such a simple representation is that it entirely neglects the non-independence of these two outcomesthe fact that one cannot choose either the $0 outcome or the $10 outcome but rather that one must choose them together. To capture the complex non-independence of lottery-like objects one must employ a richer representation of the environment which independently represents these two kinds of probabilities: The probability of encountering a lottery in the environment and the probability of winning that lottery if one selects it.

To study this class of problem using our approach requires an enriched definition of the environment we present in the Methods section. This enriched definition must capture both kinds of probabilities and any arbitrary covariant structure between offers and lotteries.

Mellers' and Tetlock's classic work (Mellers et al., 2015) on this very problem which examined how different individuals show different degrees of consistency and accuracy in how they use probabilities is a key insight for understanding capacity limits in probability representation. Their work reveals that different individuals possess different cognitive capacities for representing probability. This is a finding very closely aligned with our normative observation that individuals would be expected to employ different cognitive capacities for representing subjective value, or utility. Our ongoing work in the domain of probability representation rests heavily on these insights.

Both Vieider and colleagues and Maloney and colleagues have completed beautiful work which is also central to the extension of our approach to the domain of risky lotteries (Vieider, 2024; Zhang et al., 2020).

Their work examines how decisional accuracy depends on the probabilistic structure of the environment. This mirrors much of the work we present here, but with a focus more directly on probabilistic representation. They have devoted significant effort to exploring how distortions of probability might arise as the environmental probability structure is manipulated. This work also serves as a deep foundation for understanding the optimal representation of probability.

5.3.1 The Four-Fold Pattern of Risk Attitudes

One other key feature of human risk attitudes captured by prospect theory is the so-called four-fold pattern. Risk attitudes over lotteries depend not just on whether the prospect lies within the gain or loss domain (relative to the reference point) but also depend on the probability of the outcomes under consideration.

Classically, this is presented as four classes of risk attitudes: 1) Risk aversion for high probability gains, 2) Risk seeking for high probability losses, but also 3) Risk seeking for very low probability gains, and 4) Risk aversion for very low probability losses.

The probability weighting function accounts for this phenomenon by overweighting low probability events and underweighting high probability events. If the four-fold pattern is indeed a robust feature of human choice behavior (a point which is in debate), it is important to note that prospect theory requires the probability weighting function to achieve this pattern specifically because the value function is considered stationary. In prospect theory, the value function cannot change shape in response to changes in the choice environment and in most experiments, the value of the reference point is treated as fixed for the duration of the experiment.

In the approach we present here, both of these restrictions on the value function have been relaxed. The shape of the utility function is dependent on both the environment and on cognitive capacity. The numerical value of the reference point, defined as an inflection in the utility function, also depends on the environment. While it is not clear from the work we present here whether the four-fold pattern can emerge from these changes, there is some reason to suspect that it might. When we have previously examined divisive normalization-based models as alternatives to prospect theory (Glimcher & Tymula, 2023), we have found some indication that relaxing these restrictions on the value function may allow a choice system to account for the four-fold pattern without relying on a specific s-shaped distortion of the underlying representation of probability.

5.4 Summary

Traditional theories of decision-making assume that humans possess a fixed utility function that maps objective rewards to subjective value. From Pascal's expected value to Bernoulli's expected utility theory, they sought to describe how rational agents ought to behave but struggled to reconcile the inconsistencies observed in real human choices. Modern predictive models such as prospect theory improved the descriptive power of choice theory but largely abandoned normative explanation, leaving unanswered the question of why these irrationalities occur. In this paper, we propose a normative framework that reconciles these apparent inconsistencies by suggesting that the utility function itself is an adaptive, rational construct shaped by a number of underlying constraints, including environmental statistics and representational precision.

Our framework proposes that the decision-maker, faced with finite representational precision, optimally selects a task-appropriate utility function to maximize long-run average earnings or minimize decision errors for the current environment. Through analytical derivations and Monte Carlo simulations, we show that the classical utility functions with which all scholars are familiar emerge naturally as optimal encoding strategies under varying environmental and capacity constraints. Concave utilities optimize performance in environments dominated by frequent small rewards; sigmoidal utilities in uniformly distributed contexts; and convex utilities in environments where large rewards are most common. As precision increases, these functions converge toward linearity, reflecting a diminishing need for a nonlinear transformation of value in order to optimize the underlying representation.

Further, we quantify the incremental gains from expanding cognitive capacity and find them to be modest once the utility function is optimally adjusted to available capacity. This finding suggests that bounded rationality may instead reflect a rational equilibrium between computational cost and behavioral efficiency. The brain, in essence, might well employ the minimal precision necessary to achieve near-optimal earnings given its energetic and informational constraints.

Our findings suggest that when utility is treated as a flexible encoding tool rather than a fixed psychological quantity, bounded rationality becomes not a limitation but an efficient design principle. The diversity of observed risk attitudes and utility functions across individuals and species can be seen as reflecting an optimal adaptation. By reframing the utility function as an endogenous, rationally selected encoding mechanism, our framework offers a bridge between prescriptive and descriptive theories of choice.
