Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Considerable research in recent decades has focused on developing measures assessing transdiagnostic dimensions, including measures that target dimensional symptoms rather than traditional diagnostic categories. Furthermore, a sizeable literature has focused on assessing social-cognitive vulnerabilities (e.g., experiential avoidance) thought to be linked to an array of symptoms and functioning difficulties, and a range of measures of transdiagnostic, socialcognitive vulnerabilities have been developed. However, few of them include item content directly assessing conflict resolution difficulties and ineffective interpersonal relational strategies, which also are linked to a range of psychopathology and are targeted in cognitivebehavioral and third-wave treatments. We address this gap in the transdiagnostic assessment literature by presenting initial validation data for a measure of ineffective interpersonal strategies across three studies and four samples of participants (N = 301 adults; N = 808 adults selfidentifying a mental health diagnosis; N = 368 undergraduate students; N = 226 community adults). Analyses based on these samples spanned evaluation of participant perspectives on item face validity, quantitative examinations of internal structure, test-retest stability, and evaluating validity associations with a range of self-report and interview measures. Results collectively support the initial validation of an efficient interpersonal measure, the 6-item Transdiagnostic Ineffective Relational Strategies Scale (TIRSS), which is provided in appendix materials for free use for research purposes. We discuss future validation steps to build from this work, as well as ideas for sharpening interpersonal and transdiagnostic assessment research more broadly.

Development and Initial Validation of a Brief, Transdiagnostic

Measure of Conflict Resolution Difficulties and Ineffective Relational Strategies

Transdiagnostic frameworks have been widely studied in recent decades, and measures targeting transdiagnostic dimensions are increasingly used in research and practice. For example, the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP; Conway et al., 2019) has been proposed as an alternative classification system intended to address limitations of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (currently 5th ed., text rev. ; DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2022), and the HiTOP consortium has devoted considerable effort into developing symptom measures consistent with this hierarchical, dimensional framework (e.g., measures of HiTOP internalizing; Watson et al., 2022).

Whereas the HiTOP focuses on symptom assessment, other transdiagnostic research avenues focus on studying social-cognitive vulnerabilities (SCVs; for reviews of different transdiagnostic models and assessment, see Conway et al., 2019; Wendt et al., 2023). SCVs often are conceptualized as mechanisms and transdiagnostic risk factors related to the development and maintenance of a range of symptoms (Hong et al., 2025; Naragon-Gainey & Watson, 2018), and they are common treatment targets in cognitive-behavioral and third-wave interventions such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and Dialectical Behavioral Therapy (DBT; Harris, 2019; McKay et al., 2019; Sauer-Zavala et al., 2023). Examples of SCVs include emotion regulation difficulties, perfectionism, and intolerance of uncertainty, some of which could be conceptualized as facets of a broader experiential avoidance domain (Baldour-Wang et al., 2024; Naragon-Gainey & Watson, 2018). A range of measures are now available for assessing specific SCV (e.g., the Brief Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire [BEAQ]; Gámez et al., 2014) or multiple SCVs concurrently (e.g., Hong et al., 2025). However, although some measures include items assessing general patterns of avoidance or anxiousness that may apply to social situations, most measures of SCVs do not directly assess interpersonal communication difficulties or ineffective relational strategies.

Interpersonal communication difficulties and ineffective relational strategies are often identified as core to personality disorders (PDs; Hopwood et al., 2026), and they are commonly targeted in treatment research on cognitive-behavioral processes (e.g., McKay et al, 2019). For example, material from various ACT and DBT protocols (Harris, 2019) and novel interventions such as the Borderline PD Compass (Sauer-Zavala et al., 2023) describe how passive-aggressive or aggressive communication often are ineffective strategies contributing to the development or exacerbation of relationship difficulties and various mental health symptoms. These treatment protocols also include material focused on supporting individuals to develop effective and values-aligned communication strategies, self-advocacy, and conflict resolution skills.

Furthermore, ineffective conflict resolution and relational strategies are described as transdiagnostic factors relevant to a range of psychopathology outside of treatment and/or PDfocused research, as they are linked with internalizing (e.g., social anxiety) and other psychopathology as well (e.g., Manuel & Levin-Aspenson, 2026).

However, there are few psychometrically-sound and efficient tools available for assessing ineffective conflict resolution and relational strategies in a manner aligning with cognitivebehavioral and related third-wave interventions despite these factors being described as key SCVs showing broad, transdiagnostic psychopathology associations (for review of other interpersonally-oriented factors contributing to functioning difficulties or symptoms, see Choudhary & Thapa, 2012; Janovsky et al., 2020). Indeed, Wendt et al.'s (2023) review of the assessment literature indicates that a far greater number of measures used in psychopathology research align with emotional distress compared to the number of measures available for assessing interpersonal behavior in a manner consistent with transdiagnostic frameworks. These measurement-related issues may also connect with broader issues that have been identified concerning priorities and training efforts in the field, where for example, clinical psychology PhD students, particularly those in adult-focused research areas, report receiving less direct training in interpersonal treatment and assessment compared to many other areas (Hyatt et al., 2025).

Rationale for Current Measure Development Efforts and Construct Description

To address measurement gaps for interpersonal behavior related to SCV and third-wave research, we aimed to develop a freely available, efficient self-report measure of ineffective relational strategies. Specifically, we aimed to conduct the initial development and validation of a measure consisting of items targeting conflict resolution difficulties and ineffective communication strategies described in SCV research and third-wave treatment manuals. Various treatment and research descriptions vary somewhat in the exact set of interpersonal strategies they describe (e.g., some manuals highlight identification of interpersonal values to guide strategies more than others). However, common elements spanning many ACT, DBT, and related protocols (e.g., the Borderline PD Compass) include description of passive aggressive or indirect, avoidant (e.g., avoiding apologizing or having difficult discussions), and aggressive (e.g., disregarding others' viewpoints in conversations) interpersonal strategies (Harris, 2019 , McKay et al., 2019; Sauer-Zavala et al., 2023). Therefore, we sought to develop a brief general index assessing each of these various strategies that are often described as factors leading to the maintenance and development of interpersonal and other difficulties, recognizing that some items may reflect the use of multiple ineffective strategies or be interpreted as such (e.g., an item assessing a refusal to apologize or acknowledge errors could assess either avoidance or aggressive behavior depending on context).

Theoretically, items assessing these interpersonal strategies should overlap with externalizing psychopathology characterized by interpersonal difficulties. Furthermore, our proposed scale would likely have theoretical overlap with internalizing symptoms and psychopathology more generally as reviewed (e.g., individuals may have more difficulty using effective communication strategies when experiencing a range of symptoms, at least to some degree). Although a broad range of interpersonal behaviors, cognitive styles, or attitudes could be targeted when developing a new measure such as this, focusing on conflict resolution and the related interpersonal strategies reviewed enabled us to develop an efficient measure that (a) covers content linked broadly to various psychopathology and functioning difficulties, and (b) can be easily integrated into studies varying in complexity of design. Creating measures that are efficient may be particularly beneficial in dimensional, transdiagnostic research, as it enables researchers to evaluate associations amongst a range of SCV, personality dimensions, and symptoms concurrently, versus examining associations in a piecemeal fashion.

Related Measures of Personality and Interpersonal Patterns

We briefly review existing measures of interpersonal constructs towards describing how our new measure increments extant literature. First, measures of five-factor and sixfactor/HEXACO personality overlap with our proposed measure through their assessment of traits such as agreeableness and honesty-humility. Commonly used "normal range" personality measures include the Big Five Inventory-2 (BFI-2; Soto & John, 2017), and various measures developed for providing dimensional PD assessment (e.g., the Personality Inventory for DSM-5-Brief Form; PID-5-BF; APA, 2013) also include items assessing interpersonal antagonism. Additionally, interpersonal circumplex measures are frequently administered in clinical research (Markey & Markey, 2009), and related inventories of interpersonal problems also have been widely used (e.g., the 64-item Inventory of Interpersonal Problems; Alden et al., 1990; 28-item inventories of PD problems; Stern et al., 2000). Although various personality and circumplex measures align with our proposed measure to some degree, they focus on assessing a much broader range of content often including assessment of general attitudes and cognition (e.g., general mistrust; Soto & John, 2017). Many of these inventories also are relatively long, which may make it challenging to administer them in some study contexts. Furthermore, although we disagree, some researchers and clinicians may view personality trait measures such as the BFI-2 or PID-5-BF as having limited clinical utility (Scott et al., 2026; cf. Levin-Aspenson & Stanton, 2026), whereas a measure developed specifically to assess ineffective relational strategies described in SCV research may be perceived as having more direct clinical relevance.

Other potentially relevant measures include lengthier measures of related but distinct constructs such as problem-solving (e.g., 50+ items for assessing social problem-solving in Dixon-Gordon et al., 2015). Researchers also sometimes use checklists to assess the degree to which clinician and/or participant behaviors align with specific theoretical or treatment approaches (e.g., the DBT Ways of Coping Checklist; Neacsiu et al., 2010). As noted previously in the case of personality measures, these measures may assess a broader range of content focused on problem-solving, coping, and self-regulation versus directly assessing specific conflict resolution or relational strategies per se. The 21-item Passive Aggression Scale (Lim & Suh, 2022) is another measure overlapping with our proposed measure, but this measure provides in-depth assessment of one specific communication approach and is framed as a trait measure; in contrast, our brief, proposed measure of interpersonal strategies spans passiveaggressive and other communication strategies (e.g., aggressive communication).

Lastly, measures of attachment, rejection sensitivity, and related constructs are used to assess interpersonally-oriented schemas, attitudes, and behavior in clinical and other research areas (Janovsky et al., 2022; Scott et al., 2026). However, their scope also differs somewhat from our targeted measure. For example, attachment measures such as the Experiences in Close Relationships Scale-Relationship Structures Questionnaire often are administered to assess specific relationship domains (e.g., romantic partner relationships; Fraley et al., 2011), although more global instructions can be used. Other related measures such as the Revised Conflict Tactics Scale (Straus et al., 1996) assess physical and psychological aggression, but again, this focuses on specific relationship contexts versus interpersonal behavior more broadly.

Overview of the Current Study

Our proposed scale is intended to efficiently assess between-person differences in ineffective conflict resolution and relational strategies (e.g., passive-aggressive communication).

Such a measure could provide more targeted assessment than broader inventories of coping skills, trait, or interpersonal difficulties. Our initial measure development efforts here focus on validating our new measure for use in basic psychopathology research across studies varying in design and complexity, and downstream measure development efforts could focus specifically on validation in treatment contexts given the potential relevance of our proposed measure to various cognitive-behavioral treatment protocols as reviewed. We followed measure development guidelines to conduct an iterative process addressing phases often neglected in measure development (Boateng et al., 2018; Boness et al., 2025), as we examined participant perspectives on item validity, test-retest stability, and validity associations across assessment methods.

Specifically, Study 1 assesses researcher and participant perceptions of the face validity of proposed items. Second, Study 2 presents data from adults recruited online and examined initial item-level structure, validity associations with external measures, and test-retest stability in over 300 participants. Lastly, Study 3 presents data from both undergraduate and community adult samples, focusing on (a) additional tests of item-level structure as well as (b) examination of validity associations with other self-report and interview measures.

Our studies were not preregistered, but deidentified data are available on the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/bemp7/overview?view_only=259f21f80a1047d0b4ff5f7d5a86bdeb.

Regarding key study predictions, we aimed to develop a brief interpersonally-oriented scale consisting of at least 4-5 items for reliability and content purposes (e.g., to ensure this scale assesses more than one specific behavior). Our examinations of test-retest reliability focused on a 2-week assessment period wherein measure scores should not change drastically, so we predicted that 2-week retest coefficients would exceed .70. Next, we anticipated that scores on our newly developed measure would associate moderately with personality traits such as negative affectivity/emotionality, a domain showing broad, nonspecific symptom associations. We also expected that our measure would correlate moderately with traits such as agreeableness/antagonism and related externalizing psychopathology (e.g., narcissistic traits).

However, we wanted to demonstrate that interpersonal scale associations with these specific trait ratings were not so strong as to suggest redundancy or discriminant validity issues, as many widely-used SCV often show questionable discriminant validity in relation to measures of negative emotionality in particular (e.g., Gámez et al., 2014; Naragon-Gainey & Watson, 2018).

Additionally, we present analyses examining how our proposed measure was associated with a range of symptom dimensions, substance use, and specific criterion ratings (e.g., occurrence of recent arguments or interpersonal difficulties). We expected that our new scale would associate moderately with a range of symptoms, while ideally demonstrating that associations were not so strong as to suggest that our scale primarily captures general distress or other specific symptoms.

Study 1 Method Item Generation and Researcher Rating Results

Study 1 focused on assessing both researcher and participant perspectives of the face validity of items for our proposed measure. Initial item generation involved four study authors with PhDs in clinical psychology writing and reviewing item content assessing conflict resolution difficulties and/or passive-aggressive, avoidant, or aggressive communication strategies relevant to transdiagnostic research (e.g., McKay et al., 2019). This initial evaluation step was conducted to evaluate the degree to which researchers with expertise in transdiagnostic assessment and treatment generally agreed that items included in our initial pool were suitable for further testing. We began with an initial pool of 11 items, which Online Supplemental Table S1 presents. We recognize that this item pool is smaller than that for many other measure validation studies. However, we focused on a small pool of items to ensure that our final measure was as brief and efficient as possible given our study goals, versus starting with a large item pool and then potentially making somewhat arbitrary decisions on which items to drop versus retain.

For this initial measure validation step, the author group (of four authors noted) independently rated the degree to which each of these 11 items was a clear item marker of the target construct on a 1 (poor alignment with construct) to 6 (clear and strong alignment with construct) scale.

Participant Sample, Rating Approach, and Results

Following this initial face validity examination, we assessed participant perceptions of the general face validity of generated items. Boness et al. (2025) describe the value in obtaining participant feedback early in the measure development process to identify items that may be difficult for participants to understand or that may bring to mind experiences other than those targeted by items (also see Boness et al., 2025 for discussion and for review of other, more indepth strategies for evaluating item clarity and integrating lived experience). Towards evaluating participant perceptions of face validity, we recruited a sample of 301 US adults via Prolific, 1 a crowdsourcing platform with utility for clinical and measure development research (Stanton et al., 2022). The study was advertised to participants as focusing on providing their feedback on the clarity and usefulness of questions that are used in research on personality and mental health.

Prolific provides free filters to tailor recruitment, and only participants who endorsed having a current or past "diagnosed, ongoing mental health" history were eligible. This filter was used towards ensuring that individuals with a range of mental health histories were represented in our sample. The sample originally consisted of 321 participants, but 20 participants had their data removed due to invalid responding (e.g., based on use of attention checks; see Online Appendix S1). Mean age for the final sample of 301 adult participants was 37.2 years (SD = 12.0), and 56.5% of participants were women (40.2% men, 3.0% nonbinary; 5.0% were transgender). 2 Most participants were White or European American (85.4%; 7.0% Black or African American, 4.3% Multiracial, 4.0% Asian American, 2.0% Native American or Indigenous,); 8.3% were Hispanic or Latine. Participant Face Validity Measures and Other Measures Completed

Participants were asked to read a construct definition and then evaluate item face validity, and items and instructions as participants viewed them are provided in Online Appendix S2 (also see Online Appendix S1 for description of measures that were administered to different samples that could not be included in study analyses on theoretical or practical grounds). Participants were asked to indicate how well they thought each item described having or using ineffective relational and communication strategies on a 1 (Not at all) to 6 (Definitely or quite a bit) scale.

Participants also completed the 10-item Dysphoria scale (e.g., "worried all the time") from the Expanded Inventory of Depression and Anxiety Symptoms (IDAS-II; Watson et al., 2012) and the Inventory of Interpersonal Problems-3 (IIP-3; Pilkonis et al., 2019) to assess their own general distress levels and interpersonal issues, respectively. Inclusion of IDAS-II Dysphoria and the IIP-3 enabled us to evaluate how participants' own experiences associated with their perceptions of items; for example, an item could potentially be considered problematic if participants with more interpersonal difficulties identified that item as less face valid and less relevant to capturing ineffective interpersonal relational strategies.

Study 1 Results

Researcher Ratings of Face Validity

As reviewed, four study authors independently rated the degree to which each item generated by the research team was a clear item marker of the target construct on a 1 to 6 scale.

Independent ratings from these four authors were then averaged, and items with a mean rating of less than 4.5/6 were removed prior to subsequent study phases. This cutoff of 4.5/6 was chosen to allow for a reasonably broad item pool to be retained for later analysis while still evaluating whether items were generally deemed as aligning with the target construct. This resulted in three items being removed (see Online Supplemental Table S1; "…shouldn't have to keep explaining myself…"; "people get frustrated…"; "…act like nothing happened…"), reducing the remaining pool to eight items (all other items had average ratings ≥ 4.75). Consistent with recommendations to iteratively add items throughout the measure development process (Clark & Watson, 2019), we also added one item at this stage to ensure our item pool remained sufficiently large for further measure development stages. Specifically, study authors agreed to add the item, "I get defensive when other people don't agree with me," which then increased the total item pool to nine items for subsequent analyses.

Participant Ratings of Face Validity

Means and SDs for participants' face validity item ratings (rated on a 1-6 scale) are provided in Online Supplemental Table S1. All ratings had a mean ≥ 4.15, with six of nine items having a mean > 4.5. Over 50% of participants also provided a rating of a "5" or "6" on a 6-point scale for every item (Online Supplemental Table S2 provides full frequency ratings for all items). Thus, although item means were slightly lower than researcher-generated ratings for several items, (a) most means and participant ratings were still relatively high and (b) we expected more variation in item perceptions in a large sample of participants than in a small group of researchers. Thus, we retained all nine items for subsequent quantitative analyses towards erring on the side of over-inclusiveness for subsequent quantitative evaluation.

Next, both self-rated IDAS-II Dysphoria (sample M = 26.2, SD = 10.1; omega = .93) and IIP-3 (M = 4.1, SD = 2.3; omega = .53) scale scores correlated positively, though very weakly to weakly in magnitude, with all the nine interpersonal item face validity ratings (Pearson rs for IDAS-II Dysphoria ranged from .04 to 17 and average r = .11; Pearson rs for IIP-3 ranged from .12 to .22 and average r = .18). Thus, participants who reported higher levels of distress and possible interpersonal difficulties tended to rate candidate interpersonal items as slightly more valid, acknowledging that some correlations were quite weak.

Examination of Initial Factor Structure, Unidimensionality, and Measurement Invariance

We first conducted exploratory factor analyses (EFAs) in the Time 1 Prolific data, followed by confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) in the Time 2 Prolific data. All factor analyses were conducted using a maximum likelihood robust estimator (MLR). We also conducted Rasch analyses in R using the easyRasch package (Johansson, 2026; Robitzsch et al., 2025) towards further evaluating item functioning. Among other purposes, these Rasch-based analyses provided methods for thoroughly evaluating unidimensionality, thereby providing supplemental information beyond traditional structural analytic methods for identifying problematic multidimensionality in item sets (Johansson, 2026). When considering information from model fit indices in standard factor analytic models, we report widely-used metrics such as the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI); Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). For comparison with the broader literature, we interpret model fit based on widely-used guidelines such as CFI and TLI ≥ .95 indicating acceptable/good fit and SRMR and RMSEA values ≤ .08 indicating acceptable/good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999), recognizing that the degree to which such cutoffs apply varies based on model characteristics and study design. More relaxed benchmarks also are commonly used in clinical/personality research (e.g., CFI and TLI ≥ .90; RMSEA ≤ .10; Hopwood & Donnellan, 2010), and we viewed model fit as one source of information versus prioritizing the best fitting model in all cases (Greene et al., 2022; Stanton et al., 2023).

In the Time 1 Prolific results, we conducted a parallel analysis using principal components analysis (PCA) to inform how many factors to extract, which supported focusing on a singlefactor solution (first actual eigenvalue was 4.21 versus a first simulated eigenvalue of 1.17; second actual eigenvalue was .91 versus second simulated eigenvalue of 1.11). When extracting a single EFA factor, all item loadings > .50 as Table 1 shows. Additionally, Online Appendix S3 describes the range of Rasch analysis metrics considered, which included evaluating item fit, differential item functioning (DIF) and invariance, response category ordering, and residual components and residual correlations amongst items. We examined DIF by both gender and age, which are factors often evaluated in interpersonal clinical assessment (Hummelen et al., 2025), as well as measurement invariance research more broadly (Ock et al., 2020). See Online Supplemental Figure S1 for item distributions separated by gender, 4 and no statistically significant aspects of DIF were identified by gender (e.g., Andersen's likelihood-ratio [LR] test yielded non-significant results, indicating invariance; LR value = 52.03, chi-square df = 44, pvalue = .19). The easyRasch package also enables age to be treated as a continuous variable (Johansson, 2026), and no DIF-related issues were identified by age as a continuous variable.

However, other Rasch-based procedures indicated that the following items were potentially problematic: "give people the silent treatment"; "shut down in conversations"; "get defensive."

The item "shut down in conversations" was removed due to showing weaker observed than model expected associations with other items (observed-expected discrepancy of .10; see Online Supplemental Table S7). Some issues also were identified for the item "give people the silent treatment" (e.g., see threshold ordering issues in Online Supplemental Table S8), and we also decided to remove this item after deeming it somewhat less specific on content grounds than other similar but potentially more specific remaining items. The item "get defensive" was flagged as having significant residual correlations with other items in Rasch analyses, in addition to having bivariate correlations > .55 with other measure items (Online Supplemental Table S5).

Although correlations of this magnitude are not problematic per se, we also decided to remove the item "get defensive" towards improving the efficiency of our overall set.

Paralleling other examples (Johansson, 2026; Rozental et al., 2023), we re-ran Rasch analyses to evaluate this reduced set of six items (again, see Online Appendix S3). In this reanalysis, all items had infit values near 1.0, and the first eigenvalue from a PCA of Rasch model residuals = 1.45. This value falls within benchmarks suggesting that eigenvalues under 1.5-2.0 may be indicative of unidimensionality, keeping in mind that such benchmarks are only general rules of thumb that may not apply equivalently across study contexts (Johansson, 2026). Items sample size was slightly smaller due to being based on comparing two groups (men and women; ns = 317 and 435, respectively), as other gender identity groups did not have a sufficient number of participants for comparison. also were invariant again by gender and age in DIF-based analyses with this reduced set of six items (Andersen LR test with six items, LR-value = 39.08, chi-square df = 29, p-value = .10). We then conducted another EFA on these items, with parallel analysis again indicating extracting only a single factor (actual eigenvalues = 3.07, .74, respectively; simulated = 1.12, 1.06, respectively). All six items loaded strongly onto a common factor as Table 1 shows, and this model fit well overall (RMSEA = .064; CFI = .97; TLI = .95; SRMR = .028; model χ2 = 38.9, df = 9). 5 We also examined a solution with two factors for comprehensiveness, but this model was not viable due to only a single item loading strongly onto the second factor. Therefore, we summed all six items into a composite representing an initial version of a scale we labeled the Transdiagnostic Ineffective Relational Strategies Scale (TIRSS). The TIRSS had a mean of 10.98 (SD = 6.4) and coefficient omega = .81. Additionally, the average inter-item correlation (AIC) was .41 (range of .30-.49) amongst TIRSS items.

Examination of Structure at Time 2 and Measure Score Stability

A follow-up CFA in the Time 2 data (N = 364) indicated that all six items again loaded strongly (loadings > .50) onto a common factor (see Table 1), and this model showed good fit (RMSEA = .046; CFI = .986; TLI = .977; SRMR = .023; model χ2 = 15.97, df = 9). We thus scored the TIRSS in the Time 2 data using the same six items, and this scale had a mean of 10.00 (SD = 6.3) in the Time 2 data with coefficient omega = .82. The AIC was .44, with interitem correlations ranging from .33 to .58. Time 1 and Time 2 TIRSS scores were strongly correlated when quantifying stability over the 13.5-day mean retest period using a Pearson correlation (r = .82). Similarly, an intraclass correlation (ICC) for Time 1 and 2 scores was .81, reported as a two-way mixed effect, single measurement, absolute agreement value (Koo & Li, 2016). Time 1 (M = 10.7, SD = 6.3 for N = 364 who completed both Time 1 and 2), and Time 2 means (M = 10.00; SD = 6.3 at Time 2 as reviewed) were significantly different when compared using a paired samples t-test (t[363] = 3.26, p < .001). However, this difference was trivial in magnitude (d = .17), and other coefficients also indicated strong stability as reviewed.

In contrast, the TIRSS's weakest associations across methods were with substance use, select mania (e.g., IDAS-II Euphoria), and select "normal range" personality traits (e.g., r = -.07 with BFI-2-S Extraversion).

Online Supplemental Table S13 presents correlations for TIRSS scores in the undergraduate data with personality domains, AUDIT scores, and other symptom measures that also were completed by Study 3 community participants (i.e., IDAS-II Dysphoria, Euphoria, Mania). Correlational patterns for the TIRSS in the undergraduate sample were very similar to those from the community sample, as the TIRSS's strongest correlations were again with negative affectivity (r = .51, p < .001) and antagonism (r = .48, p < .001). The TIRSS also correlated -.44 with BFI-2 Agreeableness and .41 with disinhibition, respectively (both ps < .001), but otherwise, the TIRSS had no correlations ≥ .40 in Online Supplemental Table S13.

Thus, these associations in the undergraduate sample provide further evidence that the TIRSS displays hypothesized associations with domains (e.g., antagonism, negative emotionality), but does not associate so strongly with other domains or with indicators of general distress and symptoms as to suggest redundancy with these variables. Online Supplemental Table S14 presents correlations with other personality and symptom measures that undergraduate participants completed (e.g., other IDAS-II scales) but that the Study 3 community adults did not. These supplemental results indicated that TIRSS's strongest associations were with theoretically-expected CAT-PD facets (e.g., r = .48 with CAT-PD Anger). Lastly, Online Supplemental Table S15 provides the results of supplemental analyses focused on examining the TIRSS associations with key theoretically-relevant dependent variables (e.g., interview-assessed interpersonal psychopathology) when entered jointly as an independent variable into multiple regression models with measures of personality (e.g., PID-5-BF scales). These Table S15 results indicate that TIRSS scores associated significantly with dependent variables in many cases (e.g., number of past week arguments), even when accounting for overlap with personality measures.

Discussion

Our 6-item scale, the TIRSS, provides researchers with an efficient option for assessing interpersonal conflict resolution and communication difficulties, and Online Appendix S4 provides the full, freely available scale for use in research. Overall, structural results across sample types supported that the TIRSS items reflect a single common interpersonal dimension.

The degree of item intercorrelation varied somewhat across samples (i.e., slightly lower in the Study 3 community than in other samples), and consistent with viewing our studies as an initial validation effort, it would be useful to examine item properties in other samples in follow-up validation stages. For example, specific samples in our study were homogeneous in some ways or were relatively small (e.g., 226 participants in Study 3), such that it could be useful to examine item and overall TIRSS properties in larger, more representative samples. Additionally, the TIRSS scores had retest coefficients > .80 over a 2-week period, and as a comparison, metaanalytic research indicates that five-factor model trait scores show stability of roughly .80 over periods of roughly a month (Gnambs, 2014). It is possible then that the TIRSS's retest stability may have been slightly lower than .80 had we used a longer retest period of a month, which may be consistent with viewing conflict resolution and interpersonal strategies as relatively stable, though perhaps not quite to the degree of trait ratings. Future investigations of the TIRSS's psychometric properties could directly examine temporal stability over longer periods and in comparison to other measure types.

Validity associations for the TIRSS also were promising overall. The TIRSS generally showed weak to moderate positive correlations with a range of symptoms) that were not to a magnitude typically deemed indicative of discriminant validity issues. The TIRSS associated most strongly with self-rated negative affectivity and antagonism across studies and with related interview ratings of psychopathology linked with interpersonal difficulties (e.g., borderline PD).

However, the TIRSS also correlated moderately with other traits in some instances as well (e.g., disinhibition), indicating that individuals with elevated levels of a range of PD traits were more likely to endorse TIRSS items. Evaluating associations and the differentiability of TIRSS item content in relation to personality in other samples will be especially important in light of research indicating possible discriminant validity issues for many interpersonal functioning and SCV measures as reviewed (e.g., Naragon-Gainey & Watson, 2018).

Regarding use of our measure, it is important to underscore that our measure was developed to identify general, between-person levels of interpersonal strategies, similar to how measures of other SCVs often are used to assess general standing on target dimensions (e.g., obtaining general perfectionism scores). We advise researchers to keep this assessment scope in mind, as scores from measures developed for between-person assessment sometimes are mistakenly described or interpreted as capturing within-person processes (Levin-Aspenson & Stanton, 2026). In other words, our measure development approach did not specifically target within-person variation in conflict resolution or strategies across contexts, even if it provides a general index for assessing broader interpersonal strategies that potentially then could be used to guide hypothesis testing (e.g., future research could link scores on our measure to daily behaviors or processes assessed via daily diary or ambulatory methods). Furthermore, our general, brief measure does not account for variations in the degree to which specific communication behaviors and styles may be effective or functional across specific cultural or social contexts, which are other key points to consider when administering and interpreting scores from interpersonal measures (Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2023). Keeping these points in mind, we hope that (a) our measure will provide an efficient tool for general interpersonal assessment and (b) future complementary research will focus on refining measurement of withinperson interpersonal processes and behavior across contexts (Hopwood et al., 2026).

Connections with Other Broader Issues in Transdiagnostic Assessment Research

Other key future directions involve developing interview-and informant-based measures for assessing SCVs, traits, and symptoms in a manner consistent with transdiagnostic frameworks. We find it encouraging that an increasing number of self-report measures are available for assessing both symptoms (e.g., Blais et al., 2021) and factors related to symptom onset and maintenance (e.g., Hong et al., 2025; Rosellini & Brown, 2019), but efforts to date, including our own here, have focused primarily on developing self-report measures. Self-report has significant value and is necessary in many cases, such as when targeting emotional experiences and individuals' self-perceptions and understanding. However, it would also be beneficial for clinicians to have efficient, transdiagnostic interview-based measures available when assessing interpersonal constructs to evaluate the degree to which self-other perceptions align, as well as clinician perceptions of change throughout treatment (for examples of interview measures of related interpersonally-oriented constructs, see Heissler et al., 2021).

As a second key area for future transdiagnostic research, many researchers from different training and theoretical backgrounds recognize that assessment and treatment approaches based on traditional categorical models are flawed in many ways, but efforts to address these issues remain fragmented to some degree. Studies integrating information from research on SCVs, models such as the HiTOP, and other frameworks such as the Research Domain Criteria concurrently are becoming increasingly common. At the same time, research on these different models often remains separate and is even framed as competing. For example, research on SCVs is sometimes viewed as incompatible with research guided by the HiTOP or personality-based frameworks, which may contribute to jingle-jangle and discriminant validity issues that have been identified across the SCV and personality literatures (i.e., if measures with different labels are created to assess very similar constructs across literatures; Stanton et al., 2026).

Identification of issues concerning fragmentation across transdiagnostic literatures is not new, and providing a full set of recommendations for more effectively integrating these various transdiagnostic literatures is beyond our scope here (Conway et al., 2019; Sauer-Zavala et al., 2023; Wendt et al., 2023). Acknowledging that, key points relevant to our measure development efforts here involve more fully mapping SCV-HiTOP dimension associations towards better understanding how treatment mechanisms align with empirically-based symptom dimensions.

Significant research has evaluated how specific SCVs such as experiential avoidance are related to internalizing (e.g., sadness, worry) and detachment (e.g., anhedonia; Gámez et al., 2014).

However, studies directly assessing a range of SCVs and symptoms concurrently would permit more informative evaluation of SCV-HiTOP dimension associations.

Examples relevant to our aims here could involve explicating how interpersonally-oriented relational strategies are implicated in the development and maintenance of a range of symptoms spanning HiTOP spectra concurrently within studies rather than on a piecemeal basis. For example, some measures of interpersonal strategies and related constructs (e.g., agreeableness) align closely with HiTOP antagonistic externalizing on content and theoretical grounds.

However, measures of interpersonal relational strategies may also play a direct or exacerbating role in the development of other spectra such as internalizing or detachment, if for example, use of ineffective relational strategies leads to feelings of disconnect and isolation from others over time that is characteristic of detachment. More fully connecting the SCV and HiTOP literatures may also help to identify previously mentioned jingle-jangle issues early in the measure development process (e.g., if researchers with different expertise areas can share knowledge of item content for measures in their specific expertise areas; Stanton et al., 2026).

We recognize challenges associated with assessing a breadth of SCVs and symptom dimensions in many contexts (e.g., in repeated measures studies when time is limited, in multimethod studies), which is we why focused on developing a very brief, efficient measure here. Recognizing that comprehensiveness and efficiency need to be balanced on a case-by-case basis, one opportunity for transdiagnostic measure development involves creating measures that are more efficient than traditional DSM-based measures, in addition to potential empirical benefits of transdiagnostic assessment. For example, current DSM-based assessment approaches often require repeatedly assessing many of the same symptoms to evaluate standing on purportedly distinct diagnoses (e.g., assessing insomnia across many diagnostic modules), whereas such symptoms could be assessed within a module or measure for a single HiTOP spectrum potentially in some cases (e.g., assessing insomnia once as an internalizing indicator).

Because our study cannot directly answer these broader questions regarding etiology and clinical implementation, these questions will be important to address in follow-up research.

Regarding other related, future directions, we hope that future research will integrate the TIRSS into treatment contexts. Although our measure was created for use across a broad range of study designs, it could be beneficial to evaluate the degree to which change in TIRSS scores is associated with symptom and functioning improvement while providing helpful information guiding treatment planning. Specific, downstream clinical validation efforts could include targeting conflict resolution and relational strategy assessment in relationship, couples, or group therapy settings where interpersonal skill development often is emphasized, in addition to examining validation and change in standard individual therapy contexts.

Other Future Directions, Limitations, and Conclusion

Several other limitations and future directions are worth discussing, in addition to those reviewed. First, although we included a range of self-and interview-rated measures of interpersonal and other dimensions, resource and time limitations prevented us from including other measures that also would have been helpful to incorporate. For example, measures such as the Pathological Narcissism Inventory (Pincus et al., 2009) may assess vulnerable narcissistic features related to passive aggressive communication and conflict resolution difficulties overlapping with our measure. We also did not include measures developed specifically to assess the interpersonal circumplex or related interpersonal problems, measures such as the Passive Aggression Scale (Lim & Suh, 2022), or measures of constructs such as rejection sensitivity.

Regarding related issues centered on the breadth of interpersonal assessment, various ACT and DBT treatment manuals highlight avoidant, passive-aggressive, and aggressive communication styles as core facets of ineffective interpersonal communication (e.g., McKay et al., 2019). However, our brief 6-item scale does not provide assessment of potentially distinct interpersonal facets. Our initial item pool also consisted of only 11 items, which limited the range and breadth of interpersonal relational strategies we were able to assess. We also added an item as part of Study 1, but still, this item pool was relatively small and limited overall. Thus, future work could focus on validating measures that provide multifaceted assessment in a manner directly aligning with third-wave frameworks to promote clinical uptake, while also building on literature examining the convergence and incremental utility of various interpersonal measures such as those reviewed here more generally (e.g., Du et al., 2021) For Studies 1 and 2 based on recruitment via Prolific, we sought to compensate participants at a rate matching or exceeding $15/hour towards increasing the likelihood of quality data collection and ensuring that participants were fairly compensated. Prolific provides compensation estimates, and we adjusted compensation rates as needed based on these estimates to ensure compensation of approximately $15/hour. For Study 3 community participants, total participation time including all surveys and interviews was estimated to require 1.5-2 hours on average. These participants were compensated $35 using e-giftcard cards to again ensure that compensation matched or exceeded $15/hour. Finally, Study 3 undergraduate participants were compensated with SONA credit as reviewed in the main manuscript.

Analysis of residual correlations indicated that the items "try to get my point across rather than listening" and "get defensive when others don't agree with me" had a potentially problematic residual correlation with one another (.12; relative cutoff was .016; see Christensen et al. 2017 for further discussion and interpretation). These items also had Pearson correlations of > .55 with one another across timepoints (Online Supplemental Table S5). These Pearson correlations are not overly problematic per se, though they were consistent with Rasch-based analyses in indicating these variables were strongly correlated. Based on content and wording considerations, we decided to remove the item "get defensive" and to retain the item "try to get my point across" (i.e., people may be less likely to openly endorse being "defensive"; "try to get my point across" may also assess more specific aspects of behavior); additionally, the "get defensive" item also generally had slightly higher, stronger correlations with the item "hard time admitting when I'm wrong" (e.g., .54 in the Time 1 data; Online Supplemental Table S5). Upon removing these three items ("give people the silent treatment"; "shut down in conversations"; "get defensive"), we re-ran various Rasch-based procedures. These revised analyses provided somewhat stronger overall evidence for unidimensionality, and the item set again was invariant by age and gender (for Andersen LR test with six items, LR-value = 39.08, chi-square df = 29, p-value = .10). In this revised analysis, the item "try to get my point across" now had an observed (.56) versus model expected (.49) correlation difference value of .07 (statistically significant).

However, we did not deem this isolated piece of information as sufficient evidence to remove this item, and this was the only correlation difference flagged as significant in our revised analysis (whereas several significant differences were identified previously). One item also had slightly disordered thresholds (i.e., "Other people should know how I'm feeling…"; threshold1 = -.23, threshold2 = .00, threshold3 = -.01, threshold4 = .92, threshold5 = 1.06). Online Appendix S1 Additional Details Regarding Assessment of Response Validity

Surveys administered to participants across samples and studies included a series of attention and validity checks for assessing participants' response quality. Specifically, across all three studies, participants had their response quality assessed using attention check items, review of completion patterns, and evaluation of overall completion time (e.g., participants in Study 1 completing all items in under 5 minutes, which was deemed indicative of a reasonably high likelihood of failing to attend carefully to item content based on median completion times and study piloting). Attention check items instructed participants to select a specific response (e.g., "Select 'strongly agree' to show you are paying attention") or type a word (type "start" before beginning the survey). In total, 20 Study 1 participants had their data removed for failing one or more of these attention checks, which reduced our overall sample from 321 to 301 as stated in our primary manuscript. Similarly, based on a similar set of validity checks, data from 40 participants in Study 2 also were removed prior to analysis at Time 1, which reduced our final analytic sample size from 848 to 808. At Time 2 for Study 2, the original sample size was 387 participants; however, data for 23 participants were removed due to validity concerns, reducing the final Time 2 sample size to 364 participants. For the Study 3 community sample, 10 participants either failed to complete the core set of interpersonal items or failed attention check items, which reduced our initial sample size from 236 to 226 for final analyses. Lastly, for the Study 3 undergraduate sample, 15 original participants had their data excluded due to failing to complete any or the vast majority of survey items. Some participants also had duplicate submissions, such that we retained only the submission with the highest portion of the survey completed; this resulted in exclusion of 63 submissions.

As described in the main text, we used a series of Rasch-based modeling procedures from the easyRasch package (Johansson, 2026; Rozental et al., 2023) to evaluate the item functioning and unidimensionality of the interpersonal item set. Johansson (2026) describes a range of procedures available for these purposes, and we began by examining differential item functioning (DIF) across both gender (categorical grouping variable) and age (continuous variable; see Johansson, 2026 for more description on how specific Rasch functions can be used to evaluate DIF by continuous variables such as age). Notably, these analyses indicated no significant differences by gender or age for our item sets via use of the RIdifTable function.
