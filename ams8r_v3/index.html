Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. A Ternary Framework of Basic Goal Types: Improving, Protecting, and Maintaining

What We Have

Think of a teenager desperately begging her parents for a dog. She would clean her room immaculately, do her homework immediately after school, offer help with cooking and groceries-she would simply do whatever it takes. Her parents are nevertheless concerned that once the new member joins the family, their daughter would not care for it with the same rigor she recruited to get it. In other words, they suspect that her efforts to attain something will differ from her subsequent effort to maintain it. The daughter's maintenance efforts may also differ from her efforts to avoid losing the dog. She may ferociously protect her dog in the face of immediate danger, while still slacking in routine care for it.

The difference between attaining, protecting, and maintaining may seem negligible on the scale of a single household, one teenager's house chores, but on a larger scale it becomes critical. Nothing persists by inertia: Roads, power grids, and water systems, like families, organizations, and communities, are held in place by continuous, skilled work (Denis & Pontille, 2022 /2025; Graham & Thrift, 2007; Jackson, 2014). More than 70% of engineers work on maintaining or overseeing existing systems rather than designing new ones (Russell & Vinsel, 2019). Yet this type of work, of sustaining existing states, rarely receives the credit it deserves in the public eye. Infrastructure that functions goes unnoticed and becomes visible only when it breaks down (Graham & Thrift, 2007). Voters reward governments for spending on disaster relief but not for the ongoing preparedness that keeps communities resilient (Healy & Malhotra, 2009), and officials invest less in the upkeep of public infrastructure as a result (Mullin & Hansen, 2023).

People's neglect to recognize the role of maintenance in society is paralleled by the relative neglect of maintenance goals in psychological research (Ecker & Gilead, 2018; Lappi & Wilkowski, 2020). Psychology has historically focused on "one-shot" goals such as obtaining a degree, reaching a sales target, or completing a project (Heckhausen et al., 2010; Milyavskaya & Werner, 2018). Yet many of life's most important pursuits are about maintaining what one has through ongoing cultivation and care: health, relationships, households, and skills (de Ridder & de Wit, 2006). This ongoing care cannot be fully understood through one-shot frameworks, as it involves continuous effort.

The idea that behavior serves to restore and hold a steady state is, of course, not new in psychology. Theories from astonishingly different traditions, from Hull's (1943) drive theory and Lewin's (1935) field theory to Powers's (1973) control theory and Atkinson and Birch's (1970) dynamics of action, converge on systems whose main purpose is to sustain an equilibrium: Something has disturbed it, and behavior aims to restore it. In all of these accounts, however, keeping the equilibrium is an overarching aim that is acted upon only after it has been upset. Once the equilibrium is in place again, no further goal is pursued. As a result, the focus of these traditions is to understand behavior directed towards change (a return to the equilibrium) and the motivational apparatus required for change. From this theoretical landscape, which contains maintenance and hides it at once, grew a myopic focus on approach and avoidance in research on goal-directed behavior. Biology does offer a principle that fills this gap: Allostasis (Sterling & Eyer, 1988) describes a system that adjusts to future demands, and so acts without a discrepancy. This principle, however, has rarely been carried into psychological theories (for an exception, see Ecker & Gilead, 2018).

A growing number of research programs have nonetheless begun to address this gap.

Maintenance has been studied most extensively within the lifespan-developmental tradition, which treats orientations towards maintenance and loss-prevention as distinct from growth and documents a shift towards them across the lifespan (Ebner et al., 2006; Freund, 2006).

Maintenance has also been studied as a distinct stage in behavior change (Kwasnicka et al., 2016; Rothman, 2000) and as a motivational process with antecedents distinct from those of attainment goals (Stamatogiannakis et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2015). More recently, research has documented a unique phenomenology and motivational signature of maintenance goal pursuit compared to approach and avoidance goals (Ecker et al., 2022 (Ecker et al., , 2023;; Ecker, Lange, & Cook, 2026; Ecker, Ohlig, et al., 2026). Yet despite these advances, what is still missing is an overarching theoretical framework that integrates the disparate lines of work and clarifies how maintenance goals relate to the dominant categories of approach and avoidance goals (Elliot, 2006), as well as to promotion and prevention regulatory focus (Higgins, 1997).

Without such an account, findings across disparate research programs remain difficult to connect, and the construct of maintenance goals risks staying underspecified.

Meeting the broader call for theoretical integration in psychology (Borsboom et al., 2021; Borsboom, 2013; see also Kruglanski, 2001; Vallacher & Nowak, 1997), we develop a ternary framework that positions maintenance goals as a distinct goal type alongside approach and avoidance goals. This conceptualization grants maintenance goals their appropriate theoretical prominence, reflecting their prevalence and significance in daily life -from health maintenance to relationships to environmental stewardship. By specifying the mechanistic underpinnings of each goal type, the framework sets the stage for researchers to move beyond studying maintenance goals in isolation and to investigate how approach, avoidance, and maintenance goals differ in their antecedents, characteristics, and consequences across diverse life domains.

We proceed in five sections. Section 1 revisits the existing predominant dichotomy between approach and avoidance goals (Cloninger, 1987; Elliot & Thrash, 2002; Gray, 1990), proposing revised definitions that we term progress and protection goals. Section 2 demonstrates how the revised definitions resolve ambiguities at the intersection of goals and regulatory focus. Section 3 introduces the ternary framework in which maintenance goals are put forward as a third basic goal type next to progress and protection goals. Section 4 further illuminates the distinction between the three goal types by embedding them within Moors's (2022; Moors et al., 2017) goal-directed theory (GDT) of behavior causation, resulting in a ternary framework. Finally, Section 5 discusses implications of the ternary framework, including testable predictions about effort investment and prioritization, downstream consequences for well-being, new research directions at the intersection of the framework and regulatory focus theory, a reconceptualization of the motivational underpinnings of political ideology, and applications to behavioral interventions for global challenges such as climate change.

Revisiting Approach and Avoidance Goals

The distinction between approach and avoidance dates far back in psychological thought (Elliot, 2006; Elliot & Covington, 2001; Lewin, 1935). Elliot (2006) defined approach motivation as the energization of behavior towards positive stimuli (i.e., rewards, e.g., monetary gain), and avoidance motivation as the energization of behavior away from negative stimuli (i.e., punishments, e.g., monetary loss). We argue, however, that the standard conceptualization of avoidance requires revision to create the theoretical clarity necessary for introducing maintenance goals as a distinct category.

Attempts to conceptualize the mechanistic differences between approach and avoidance motivation have been predominantly guided by control systems theory (e.g., Carver & Scheier, 2000; Miller et al., 1960; Powers, 1973; Vancouver et al., 2010). Carver and Scheier (2000) described the dynamics of approach via a discrepancy-reducing feedback loop, wherein the system (i.e., an individual) detects a discrepancy between a current state or status quo and a certain standard or desired state, which can be considered as an initial goal.

After this, the system activates an approach goal that operates to reduce or eliminate this discrepancy. For instance, an individual may detect a discrepancy between their current level of physical fitness and the level of fitness they desire, prompting behavior aimed at increasing fitness until it matches the desired level. Conversely, these authors described the dynamics of avoidance via a discrepancy-enlarging feedback loop, wherein the system detects an undesired state or "anti-goal", leading to the activation of an avoidance goal that operates to increase the distance from this anti-goal. The authors illustrated this dynamic with the example of a teenager who wants to avoid being like her parents. The teenager has no plan for how she would be and nothing that she wishes to resemble; the motivation to avoid pushes her only away from being like her parents but not in any particular direction.

We argue, however, that the description of avoidance as a movement away from an anti-goal is essentially not goal-directed. Based on the widespread definition of goals as internal representations of valued outcomes or desired states (Austin & Vancouver, 1996), anti-goals-which are not valued nor desired-do not qualify as goals. We therefore propose to re-conceptualize avoidance goal pursuit in terms of a discrepancy-reducing feedback loop, similar to approach goal pursuit. Returning to the teenager example, the teenager's attempt to move away from the anti-goal of resembling her parents can be reframed as the attempt to move towards the goal of being dissimilar to her parents. Thus, we may model the teenager's avoidance striving in terms of a discrepancy-reducing feedback loop in which she attempts to close a gap between a desired state of dissimilarity to her parents and the looming possibility of becoming similar to them.

While we propose that approach and avoidance goals are similar in that they both work towards a desired state (as all goals do by definition), we do not suggest that psychology should discard this core distinction, which we believe remains central to motivated behavior. Instead, we suggest that the critical difference between these two goal types is the state that is compared (and found to be discrepant) with the desired state.

Approach goals arise when the desired state is discrepant with a current state whereas avoidance goals arise when the desired state is discrepant with an anticipated state. In the example of the teenager, the desired dissimilarity to her parents is not discrepant with her current state. In fact, she is still too young to resemble her parents much. The similarity that she wishes to avoid is a future possibility, not a current reality. Therefore, the gap that the teenager attempts to close is not between a desired state of dissimilarity to her parents and a current state of similarity; it is rather between a desired state of dissimilarity to them and an anticipated state of similarity.

Empirical support for conceptualizing avoidance, like approach, as a discrepancyreducing feedback loop comes from studies showing that avoidance motivation decreases as the individual moves away from the negative reference point (Ballard et al., 2016 (Ballard et al., , 2017a)).

We argue that such findings are better explained with a discrepancy-reducing feedback loop than with a discrepancy-enlarging feedback loop. If one wished only to increase the distance from a negative reference point, it would be difficult to determine when to relax efforts. As a result, goal-directed behavior would continue indefinitely. Indeed, a simulation study by Ballard and colleagues (2017b) demonstrated that modelling avoidance as a negative feedback loop, in which motivation decreases with increasing distance from the undesired state, led to decreases in motivation over time, in line with the empirical findings, whereas modelling it as a positive feedback loop, in which motivation increases with increasing distance from the undesired state, resulted in an implausible pattern of continuous increase in motivation. 1 In sum, we reject the standing characterization of approach and avoidance goals in terms of movements towards desired or positive states and away from undesired or negative states and propose instead to redefine them in terms of attempts to reduce current-desired and anticipated-desired discrepancies. From a Binary to a Ternary Goal Distinction

We propose that while progress goals operate to reduce a current-desired gap and protection goals operate to reduce an anticipated-desired gap, maintenance goals hold the gaps stable-they do not operate to reduce them, whatever their magnitude. In the paradigmatic case the magnitude of discrepancy is zero: The desired state is discrepant with neither the current nor the anticipated state. But, as we show below, the target of maintenance can be a discrepancy of any magnitude. The defining feature is thus the absence of an attempt to reduce a discrepancy, not the presence or absence of a discrepancy as such.

To illustrate the paradigmatic case, consider Sarah maintaining a close friendship with her college roommate. She regularly calls, sends messages, and makes plans to visit. Their friendship is strong and fulfilling (there is no current-desired gap), and she has no doubt that their friendship will remain strong (no anticipated-desired gap). Sarah's efforts do not aim to make the friendship closer or better, nor do they reduce a threat of drifting apart. The reason we consider such a case "paradigmatic" is that it showcases the independence of maintenance from discrepancies: While discrepancies can exist in maintenance, maintenance-in contrast with progress and protection-does not require them.

One might object that maintenance is never truly discrepancy-free, since it always operates against a counterfactual threat: Were Sarah to stop calling and visiting, the friendship would deteriorate. While we grant that the outcome of not acting may be on Sarah's mind, it may also be absent. Moreover, even if it is on her mind, this counterfactual outcome is hypothetical rather than anticipated. An anticipated state is not a representation of what would happen under just any course of action. It is the agent's prediction of how things will be, and that prediction takes the agent's own plans into account. As long as Sarah plans to keep calling and visiting and expects this to remain feasible, she anticipates no deterioration of the friendship. As we discuss in Section 4, this counterfactual may encourage Sarah to invest active effort, but it is not a discrepancy that she acts to reduce. Protection striving should therefore vary with the size of the anticipated-desired discrepancy, whereas maintenance striving should vary with the value of the state being maintained and be insensitive to counterfactual decline. Section 5 presents a paradigm that separates the two, along with initial evidence.

To illustrate the non-paradigmatic case, consider Jane maintaining her aging car: The car shows signs of wear and tear (current-desired discrepancy), and despite regular maintenance, breakdowns remain likely as parts age (anticipated-desired discrepancy). Jane continues routine maintenance that keeps both types of discrepancy stable. She cannot expect outcomes beyond her car continuing to run at its current levels of functioning and of risk.

Here, a current-desired and an anticipated-desired discrepancy both persist, yet Jane operates on neither-she holds them stable. Her goal is a maintenance goal for exactly the same reason as Sarah's: not because the discrepancies are zero (they are not), but because she does not attempt to reduce them.

To bring out the differences between the three goal types even better, consider three training programs an athlete might follow. The first aims to rehabilitate an existing injurythe current state (a damaged knee) falls short of the desired state (a healthy knee), and training works to close that gap and is terminated once the knee heals. This is a progress goal.

The second program aims to reduce the risk of a future injury. Here the knee is currently healthy; what falls short of the desired state is an anticipated state-the elevated chance of injury down the road-and training works to avert it. At the end of this program, one should come out with the same healthy knee and a reduced chance of future injury. This is a protection goal. The third program aims at neither closing a present gap nor an anticipated one, but simply at sustaining the knee's current condition and its current, acceptable level of risk-continuously holding things as they are. Unlike the first two, this last program reaches no endpoint at which it is complete: Rather than terminating, it becomes part and parcel of the athlete's life. This is a maintenance goal.

It is worth noting that maintenance goals have a distinctive temporal character.

Whereas close-ended goals specify a desired state to be reached and then checked off, a maintenance goal is concerned with the continuation of that state across many future time points. In wanting to maintain our health, we do not want to be healthy merely now, tomorrow, or in five years, but as long as possible, continuously. The gap between occupying a desired state at one moment and sustaining it indefinitely is neither bridgeable nor operable, and no discrepancy can be computed against continuity as a standard. We therefore do not treat the gap with continuity as a discrepancy of the kind that drives progress and protection.

It nonetheless lends maintenance an inherently open-ended character, a feature that sets maintenance apart from the close-ended, one-shot goals on which goal research has traditionally focused.

In advocating for a distinct category for maintenance goals, we underscore their significant role and frequency in daily life. Consider, for example, the crucial efforts to sustain physical health through balanced diets and regular exercise, or the work involved in preserving intimacy in a long-term relationship amid the demands of full-time careers and child-rearing. Empirical research indicates that maintenance goals represent approximately 40% of individuals' daily goals across various life spheres, significantly outnumbering the 10% represented by protection goals (Ebner et al., 2006; Ecker et al., 2022). These figures may even underestimate the prevalence of maintenance, as some of the most common examples of maintenance are conventionally labeled in terms of protection. For instance, regularly caring for one's health is often referred to as "preventive care". Thus, someone may say that they brush their teeth to "prevent" cavities. If the person brushes to reduce an anticipated level of cavities, the goal is indeed a protection goal. Yet often people brush to keep the current state of their teeth, including the current level of cavities, stable. Even if people expect their teeth to deteriorate somewhat despite their efforts, they may try to keep a small anticipated-desired discrepancy stable rather than reducing it. This is maintenance, not protection.

Despite the prevalence of maintenance goals in daily life relative to protection goals, the latter have historically overshadowed the former in psychological science. We therefore think there is a need to recalibrate the emphasis towards the distinct nature of maintenance goals. A first step is to spell out the mechanism of maintenance striving, which we do next by integrating the ternary goal distinction within the GDT (Moors, 2022 (Moors, , 2026a;; Moors et al., 2017).

Integration of the Ternary Goal Distinction in the GDT

The original version of the GDT combines elements from various theoretical traditions, including cybernetic models (Miller et al., 1960; Powers, 1973), expected utility and operant learning theories (e.g., Heyes & Dickinson, 1990; Tolman, 1955), and goalsetting theory (Locke & Latham, 1990). So far, however, the theory has limited its focus to progress and protection goals. After articulating how the binary GDT makes sense of these two goal types, we extend the theory by including maintenance goals, thus working towards a ternary framework. We conclude this section by sharpening our proposal through a closer examination of different forms of maintenance and the interplay between the three goal types.

Original, Binary GDT: Progress and Protection Goals

The original, binary GDT proposes a feedback cycle, which is put in motion by the detection of a discrepancy between a desired state or goal (i.e., the representation of a valued outcome; O v ), and the representation of a current state (cS). When such a discrepancy is perceived, a second goal arises-the goal to reduce the discrepancy. To achieve this second goal, individuals may choose between three broad strategies: (a) assimilation-taking action to change the current state (cS) in the direction of the desired state (O v ); (b) accommodation -changing the desired state (O v ) in the direction of the current state (cS); and (c) immunization-reinterpreting the current state (cS) so that it no longer appears discrepant with the desired state (O v ). Once a strategy is selected, an intention to execute it arises. This intention reflects a third goal in the cycle -the goal to execute the selected strategy, which (in the case of assimilation) may lead to overt behavior and bring about an actual outcome. The outcome is fed back to the cycle as the novel input to the next run of the cycle, which is repeated until the discrepancy is reduced (i.e., exit) or is interrupted by a discrepancy with a more valued Goal 1.

To illustrate, imagine that your refrigerator is empty (cS). This state is discrepant with your desired state that the fridge will be full, which is your Goal 1 (O v ). In response to this discrepancy, you form Goal 2 to eliminate the discrepancy. This Goal 2 can be accomplished by replenishing your food supply (assimilation), by deciding that you actually prefer the fridge empty at the moment (accommodation), or by convincing yourself that the two stale slices of cheese and a half-rotten tomato you still have constitute a full fridge (immunization).

Within the strategy of assimilation, individuals typically have more than one action option in their action repertoire to choose from. For instance, there is likely more than one way to replenish a fridge. Action selection is based on the comparison of expected utilities of the accessible action options, that is, their effectiveness for reaching Goal 1. The expected utility of an action option is determined by the value of the outcome of the action and the expectancy that the action will indeed lead to the outcome (e.g., Heyes & Dickinson, 1990; Tolman, 1955). How likely are you to succeed in replenishing your fridge when going to the shop? If you know the shops are currently out of stock, your chances of success might be better when knocking on your neighbor's door. Given that people strive to fulfil multiple goals, the total expected utility of a single action option equals the sum of the expected utilities for each of these goals. If you were anyway looking for a reason to chat with your neighbor, then knocking on their door-an action serving multiple goals-will more likely be chosen. Alternatively, if your additional goal is to avoid contact with your neighbor, then going to the shop might gain priority.

Even if going to the shop has an extremely high expected utility (they are never out of stock), this action option might still not be feasible. For instance, it might be that your bike broke down and the shop is not within walking distance. In that case, despite its high expected utility, going to the shop is not a feasible option. Hence, an optimal action choice should maximize both expected utility and feasibility (see also Bargh et al., 2010; Liberman & Trope, 1998; Moors, 2022).

Once a behavior is selected and executed, its outcome both serves as the current state (cS) in a new cycle and updates behavioral expectancies within the individual's repertoire.

For instance, if you opted for knocking on a neighbor's door to ask for groceries and the neighbor was reluctant to help, then the next cycle will still feature an empty fridge and no discrepancy reduction. This may lead to reduced expectancy that asking the neighbor for help will help replenish your fridge.

The above illustration pertains to the case in which Goal 2 is a progress goal, expressing a wish to close a discrepancy between a current (cS) and a desired state (O v ). In other cases, the desired state (O v ) is identical to the current state (Cs) but discrepant with an anticipated state (aS). When this happens, Goal 2 is a protection goal, and a strategy is chosen for how to achieve it. Put differently, when an anticipated state falls short of how one wishes things to be, this can generate protective action. Imagine that your fridge is rather full, but you know that you are hosting friends this weekend and therefore expect to have a completely empty fridge by Monday. If your anticipated state of an empty fridge on Monday (aS) is discrepant with your desired state to have a full Fridge on Monday (O v ), you may choose to avoid this discrepancy, for instance, by making time to go shopping on Monday morning. 3 The pursuit of multiple goals corresponds to a horizontal dimension of goal complexity, with some goals aligning and others competing. Goal hierarchies introduce a second, vertical dimension of goal complexity, in which the goal-directed cycle detailed above is embedded within a broader structure of superordinate and subordinate goals (Moors, 2026a, Figure 1). Each goal in the hierarchy is selected because it has the highest expected utility for achieving the goal above it. For instance, the Goal 1 to have a full fridge may itself have been selected because having a well-stocked fridge is estimated to contribute most effectively to the superordinate goal of maintaining an organized household. The hierarchy also extends downward: Once the behavioral goal to go to the shop is selected, a new discrepancy opens up between the desired state of being at the shop and the current state of still being at home. This subordinate discrepancy is then resolved by selecting among concrete action options-taking the bicycle or taking the bus.

In fact, it is probably the typical case that a current-desired gap is initially accompanied by a similarly-sized anticipated-desired gap. When recognizing that one's fridge is empty, one likely also anticipates the fridge to remain empty as long as no action is taken. Integrating Maintenance Goals in the GDT

The ternary framework that we propose in this paper expands on the GDT by specifying a third type of goal-directed striving. Unlike progress and protection goals, which aim to reduce discrepancies, maintenance goals operate by keeping a discrepancy absent or stable. Below, we describe how maintenance enters the goal-directed cycle both when a discrepancy is absent and when it is present.

Absence of Discrepancy

In the original, binary GDT, when the agent evaluates that a discrepancy with some goal is effectively reduced or absent, the cycle ends (i.e., exit), so that the agent can turn to discrepancies with other goals. In the ternary version of the GDT proposed here, the absence of a discrepancy gives rise to two options: ending the cycle or maintaining the zero discrepancy. The latter reflects the paradigmatic case of maintenance, where the desired state (Ov) is neither discrepant with the current state (cS) nor with the anticipated state (aS). Returning to our earlier example: Imagine that your fridge is adequately stocked and you have established a weekly food delivery routine. Given this routine, you do not anticipate supplies dwindling-the anticipated state matches both your current and desired states. There is no discrepancy to close; the goal is simply to keep things as they are.

As the example suggests, paradigmatic maintenance goals typically rest on a routine that is already in place: It is the routine that keeps the anticipated state from falling short of the desired state. Often the routine is the outcome of a protection goal achieved through assimilation. Suppose your fridge is currently full, but you anticipate that supplies will dwindle over the weekend. Assuming that your environment is stable-you expect similar levels of food consumption every week-you set up a weekly delivery routine to counteract this anticipated shortfall. Once the routine is in place and functioning, the anticipated-desired discrepancy is eliminated: You no longer expect your supplies to run out. The protection goal has been achieved. At this point, however, the job is not yet done. The routine must still be sustained, and sustaining it requires a maintenance goal. A routine can also be the outcome of a progress goal, as when a fitness regimen that served to improve one's condition is toned down to sustain what was achieved. Alternatively, it may be learned from others, as when one starts a fitness routine to keep up with friends who do so and later on sets a goal to maintain fitness. The routine then initially served a goal concerning a different desired state (e.g., fitting in), and so no progress or protection fitness goal preceded it.

Whether the absence of a discrepancy ends the cycle or gives rise to a maintenance goal depends centrally, we propose, on the estimated decayability of the valued state and hence the degree to which the state requires regular investment-a routine-to remain stable (Ecker, Lange, & Imhoff, 2026). Many valued states are not expected to decay, and we typically form no maintenance goal regarding them: A book on the shelf, a ring in a drawer, and a painting on the wall seem to stay as they are without our help. The clearest cases are one-shot achievements, which count forever once attained: Having earned a PhD or having visited Australia cannot decay. Decayability is a quality of a state that the agent learns. The lesson may be supplied by a preceding protection goal (having anticipated that the fridge would run empty), by experience with an ongoing routine (the fridge is full because the deliveries are kept up), or by verbal instruction (marriage advice for newlyweds). It is also easily missed: Observing a stable state with no further information about its upkeep invites the expectation that it will hold on its own (the common-sense law of inertia; McCarthy, 1986; Shanahan, 1997), which may be one reason why maintenance goals are often overlooked in the social sphere (Graham & Thrift, 2007).

Within the ternary GDT, paradigmatic maintenance goal striving unfolds as follows.

The discrepancy-free state gives rise to a Goal 2 to keep things that way. To pursue this goal, the individual turns to their action repertoire. The set of action options considered is not fixed and may differ from one occasion to the next. Continuing the routine is a likely option, which may then be compared against the option of discontinuing it. Here enters the counterfactual outcome we discuss earlier: the state of the world if one did not continue the routine. This continue-versus-discontinue comparison is considered in the same way that the outcome of knocking on the neighbor's door was weighed against that of going to the shop. It is not an anticipated state (aS) that is discrepant with the desired state. As long as continuing has the highest expected utility among the options considered, the individual is likely to persist.

It is also possible that a new action option (e.g., a new routine) will emerge with greater expected utility and replace the existing routine, or that the individual will turn to the covert strategies of accommodation or immunization rather than the overt strategy of assimilation. Consider Michael, who has established a routine of following a healthy diet. If his health gradually deteriorates because he has stopped eating vegetables regularly, he may preserve the sense that nothing is amiss by lowering his health standards (accommodation) or by minimizing the significance of the deterioration (immunization). Assuming instead that Michael continues his healthy diet (assimilation), the discrepancy-free state remains contingent on the routine's continued feasibility.

Presence of Discrepancy

In the binary version of the GDT, the presence of a discrepancy gives rise to Goal 2 to reduce the discrepancy, which can be either a progress or protection goal. In the ternary version proposed here, the presence of a discrepancy gives rise to two options: reducing the discrepancy (through progress or protection) or keeping the (size of the) discrepancy (with a current or anticipated state) stable. The latter reflects the non-paradigmatic case of maintenance, as in the example of Jane's car.

Whether a discrepancy with some goal is reduced or held stable depends, we propose, on three factors. The first is how tolerable the discrepancy is: A discrepancy which is small enough to fall below a certain threshold may not call for reduction. The second is whether the discrepancy is outcompeted by discrepancies with other, more pressing goals. The third factor is whether the agent has a strategy or action option that would reduce the discrepancy with an above-zero expected utility and feasibility. Unlike the first two, this factor comes into play only after a progress or protection cycle could not be successfully completed. Suppose a Goal 2 to reduce the discrepancy was set, but the agent discovers that no fitting strategy or action option is available. In this case, the agent may replace the initial Goal 2 to reduce the discrepancy with a Goal 2 to keep the discrepancy stable (see Figure 1). This would count as accommodation at the level of Goal 2. Jane's situation illustrates this route. She would still prefer a car without wear and tear, but restoring it is not worth the cost, and letting her routine lapse would make things worse. She therefore holds the car at its current level of functioning and of risk. On both routes, non-paradigmatic maintenance inherits its discrepancy from the progress or protection goal that was not pursued or was given up, which is why its target can be a discrepancy of any magnitude.

To further sharpen the ternary framework, we examine the interplay between the three goal types in the dynamics of daily life. A key insight is that the boundaries between progress, protection, and maintenance striving are not static: The three goal types can co-exist when situated at different levels of the goal hierarchy, and individuals may switch between them within the same hierarchical level as circumstances change. Maintenance Goals situated at Different Levels in the Goal Hierarchy

Although maintenance striving is the attempt to keep a discrepancy absent or constant, it may in fact involve change at subordinate levels in the goal hierarchy.

Maintenance goals govern dynamic systems in which opportunities and threats arise and are then dealt with, until they arise again. Put differently, a maintenance goal may be considered as a superordinate goal that governs subordinate progress and protection goals, resulting from short-term discrepancies. Crucially, these subordinate discrepancies represent expected fluctuations within the system's normal operating range rather than threats to the superordinate level itself. This is also where the ternary framework meets Powers's (1973) hierarchy of control systems: The continuous error correction that his loops describe is, in our terms, the subordinate progress and protection striving through which a superordinate maintenance goal is implemented. Maintenance efforts that fit this description are ubiquitous in daily life, both in mundane tasks (e.g., household chores) and in fundamental life tasks (e.g., caring for family and friends).

Consider Camila maintaining her vibrant front lawn. At the superordinate level, her maintenance goal is to keep the lawn as vibrant as it is. While Camila wants to keep the overall condition of her lawn constant, she continuously monitors subordinate goals such as soil moisture. Every now and then, Camila may discover that the soil is dryer or moister than it should be (e.g., due to weather changes) and she may respond by setting a subordinate progress goal to close the discrepancy between the current state of the soil and its desired state. Likewise, Camila may also anticipate that there will be variations in dryness or moisture in the future and respond by setting up a subordinate protection goal to reduce these potential discrepancies by watering a bit more or a bit less. While we acknowledge that this form of maintenance involves discrepancy reduction at the level of subordinate goals, it may not involve discrepancy reduction at the level of the overarching maintenance goal.

We also acknowledge that maintenance goals may themselves be governed by superordinate progress or protection goals. For instance, Camila may want to maintain her lawn in good shape with the further aim of impressing visitors when she wants to sell her house for a good price (i.e., superordinate progress goal) or she may maintain her lawn in order to reduce the anticipation of making a bad impression during an upcoming dinner party (i.e., superordinate protection goal).

Goal Switching Within the Same Level of the Goal Hierarchy

A key aspect of successful goal pursuit is monitoring whether there are any deviations from the intended path towards the desired state. Within the GDT, monitoring is not an additional process layered onto goal pursuit but constitutes the discrepancy detection phase that initiates (and concludes) each cycle. This means that goal monitoring may take at least two forms: evaluating the magnitude of a current-desired discrepancy or of an anticipateddesired discrepancy. Both forms of monitoring may be available when pursuing progress and protection, as well as when pursuing maintenance. For instance, when maintaining an optimal state of health, one may periodically test whether one's current and anticipated states still correspond to one's desired state or instead deviate from it.

In research on maintenance in behavior change programs, such deviations are often referred to as "slips" or "lapses" into old behavior patterns (Rothman, 2000). Once the individual concludes that a deviation from the desired state has indeed occurred, they will likely set a progress goal to return to the desired state. For instance, after successfully quitting smoking, the person still needs to maintain this achievement indefinitely. During this period, more often than not, people experience lapses into smoking (Shiffman et al., 1996). Once a lapse has occurred, they may either fully relapse or turn back to the routine by setting a shortterm progress goal to do so. However, even after the desired state (smoking cessation) is reattained, the recent experience of having relapsed may lead to the anticipation that this will happen again, which may prompt a protection goal. In the case of smoking cessation, the individual may strive to improve their routine to better resist the temptations that led to a relapse before. Once the new and improved routine is in effect and the threat of temptation is removed or optimally reduced, maintenance striving begins again.

Maintenance as a Sui Generis Goal Type

Our proposal that maintenance goals constitute a sui generis goal type, in that they do not involve an attempt to reduce discrepancies, has met with several objections. We discuss four. The first is based on affinities between maintenance and protection, and the second on affinities between maintenance and inaction. The third challenges whether maintenance is goal-directed. Finally, the fourth objection calls on the presumed lack of an evolutionary ground for maintenance goals. For each of these objections, we articulate why maintenance nonetheless stands as a goal type in its own right.

Objection 1: Maintenance can be Reduced to Protection.

Research in lifespan developmental theory found that self-reports of maintenance orientation and loss-prevention orientation could not be empirically separated, loading on a single factor, which led researchers to conclude that maintenance is not a distinct goal type (Ebner et al., 2006; Freund & Ebner, 2005). This research, however, measured stable goal orientations that vary across persons and shift with age-dispositional traits rather than the mechanisms of individual goals. Characterizing how strongly a person leans towards maintenance says little about what, mechanistically speaking, separates holding a state from changing it. And without a criterion for distinguishing the stabilizing of a discrepancy from its reduction, it is unsurprising that a measure of maintenance would slide into loss-prevention.

Mechanistically speaking, however, maintenance is not tied to protection. As described earlier, maintenance rests on a routine that removes the anticipated-desired discrepancy. That routine is often the outcome of a protection goal, but it can also follow a progress goal or a goal concerning a different desired state. And even when protection comes first, maintenance need not follow. Suppose a person responds to a health threat by purchasing a fitness watch. Once that task is done, and assuming that the threat is removed (the fitness watch reveals reassuring results), one may take the successful achievement of the protection goal as the beginning of a routine to check the fitness watch daily in order to maintain one's health. Alternatively, one may simply be reassured, put the watch away in a drawer, and think no more about one's health. Thus, while many maintenance goals may begin with (a relatively brief) act of protection, not all do, and not all protection goals lead to maintenance.

Objection 2: Maintenance can be reduced to Inaction. Our discussion thus far may give the impression that maintenance goals necessarily involve active intervention to preserve a desired state. However, maintenance striving can also take a passive form, where the goaldirected behavior involves intentionally refraining from action rather than engaging in it. This passive maintenance occurs when the current environment naturally sustains the desired state, requiring only that the individual avoid disrupting it. Consider a person who has a savings account. To maintain their current financial status, they may simply refrain from withdrawing money. Similarly, someone who has a well-organized digital archive might maintain its order by deliberately choosing not to modify or reorganize it. In these cases, maintenance goals manifest as decisions not to act in ways that would disrupt the current state.

It is crucial to distinguish such passive maintenance from mere inaction. Within the ternary framework, passive maintenance still represents a goal-directed process. In the paradigmatic case, for instance, a discrepancy-free situation (where current, anticipated, and desired states align) gives rise to Goal 2 (to maintain this alignment), which gives rise to Goal 3 (the intention to refrain from disruptive actions). Refraining is here one action option among others, selected because its expected utility exceeds that of acting. The goal-directed nature of this restraint differentiates it from simple neglect or indifference.

Passive maintenance striving may be particularly common in stable environments where forces of change are minimal. However, even passive maintenance requires monitoring to ensure that the environment continues to support the desired state naturally. Should environmental conditions shift, rendering the current state unstable, passive maintenance may no longer suffice, requiring a transition to more active maintenance strategies or to progress or protection goals if discrepancies emerge. This distinction between active and passive forms of maintenance striving highlights the flexibility of maintenance goals in responding to different environmental conditions while preserving the same underlying goal structuremaintaining alignment between current, anticipated, and desired states.

Objection 3: Maintenance Is Mere Habit. Maintenance goals rely on routines, such as a weekly food delivery or a daily check of a fitness watch. The habit literature offers a simpler account of such routines: Habits are stimulus-response (S-R) associations, learned through repetition, that are triggered by the stimulus and therefore not goal-directed (Wood & Neal, 2007; Wood & Rünger, 2016). In a habit, the outcome of the routine is not represented when the response is selected, and no comparison between states precedes it. In maintenance as we conceive it, by contrast, both are present: The routine follows the detection that current, anticipated, and desired states align, and it is selected because its expected outcome (e.g., a stocked fridge) has a higher expected utility than the alternatives. Habits are therefore a rival account of routine behavior.

Where do the two accounts diverge empirically? Standard tests change the outcome of the behavior through outcome devaluation (making the outcome less desirable), contingency degradation (making the outcome equally likely without the behavior), or contingency reversal (switching the behaviors required for desired outcomes): Behavior that remains unchanged is taken to be habitual, and behavior that adapts is taken to be goal-directed (Wood & Rünger, 2016). We expect most daily maintenance routines to adapt: If the deliveries stopped keeping the fridge stocked (e.g., items arrive damaged or bags go missing), a person with a maintenance goal should start looking for a different service. Rigid behavior, however, would not settle the matter, because it may also stem from a goal-directed process that relies on outdated expectancies. An old, overtrained response-outcome expectancy may compete with a newer, weaker one and win (you may mistakenly order from the old service, fully expecting all items to arrive intact; Buabang et al., 2023; Van Dessel et al., 2024).

Whereas rigidity alone cannot distinguish a habit from a maintenance goal, adaptation to a change in outcome marks a routine as goal-directed. We therefore argue that the balance tilts towards a goal-directed (maintenance) account.

Objection 4: Maintenance Motivation Lacks an Evolutionary Ground.

Critics may question whether maintenance constitutes a sui generis goal type because it lacks an obvious evolutionary basis. While progress and protection can be said to have developed for responding to opportunities (food, mates) and threats (predators, heights), respectively, maintenance does not seem to have an obvious domain of action. If maintenance goals lack the elements of opportunity (desired-current gap) and threat (desired-anticipated gap), then such goals might not be effectively energized by the commonly distinguished systems of progress (or approach) and protection (or avoidance) such as the Behavioral Activation System (BAS) and the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) or the Fight-Flight-Freeze System (FFFS; Gray, 1990).

A solution proposed by Ecker and Gilead (2018) is that maintenance goals rely instead on the parental caregiving system (PCS; Schaller, 2018). Contemporary evolutionary theory identifies parental care as a distinct biologically-rooted motivational system (Aunger & Curtis, 2013; Kenrick et al., 2010), characterized by an experience of tenderness towards a dependent other who is not currently in danger (Beall & Tracy, 2017; Kalawski, 2010). This mirrors the relationship between a person and any object of maintenance: Like a child in a safe environment, the object of maintenance is an already-attained positive state under conditions of low threat. Importantly, the PCS has been shown to motivate a wide range of behaviors beyond parenting itself, including altruistic behavior (Batson, 2011; Goetz et al., 2010; Preston, 2013) and romantic affiliative behavior (Bell, 2009; Schaller, 2018), suggesting that it is well positioned to serve as a general motivational foundation for maintenance striving across domains.

Research has distinguished between two aspects of the PCS: protection and nurturance (Hofer et al., 2018; Buckels et al., 2015). The protection aspect reflects a biologically-rooted motivation to prevent threats, one that extends beyond parenting to other domains (Eibach & Mock, 2011; Fessler et al., 2014; Sherman et al., 2009). The nurturance aspect, by contrast, concerns the ongoing care and cultivation of a valued dependent other, and it is this aspect that may underpin a general motivation to maintain. This potentially promising link between nurturance and maintenance has yet to be investigated empirically.

Implications of the Ternary Framework

The ternary framework bears several key implications. It generates testable predictions about effort investment and prioritization of maintenance striving and about the downstream consequences of maintenance striving for well-being. It also opens up new theoretical territory at the intersection of maintenance and regulatory focus, it offers a fresh perspective on the motivational underpinnings of political ideology, and it points towards novel behavioral interventions for global challenges such as climate change. We discuss each of these implications in turn, highlighting directions for future research. As before, we focus mainly on the paradigmatic case of maintenance.

Effort and Prioritization

The ternary framework generates testable predictions about when and how much people invest in maintenance relative to progress and protection. Progress striving should increase as dissatisfaction stemming from the gap between the current and desired state grows larger, while protection striving should increase as threat stemming from the anticipated-desired gap becomes more pronounced (Oettingen & Reininger, 2016).

Maintenance striving, by contrast, cannot draw on a discrepancy. In our account, a (paradigmatic) maintenance goal is formed when current, anticipated, and desired states align and the valued state is estimated to be decayable. We therefore predict that greater satisfaction with one's current situation, lower threat from an anticipated situation, and higher estimated decayability of the valued state should each increase maintenance striving. Goal value is relevant to all three goal types, but we expect it to weigh more heavily in maintenance, where no discrepancy adds motivating force of its own. Initial evidence supports these predictions. Ecker et al. (2023) found that participants reported greater willingness to invest in maintenance (and lesser willingness to invest in progress or protection) the closer their current state was to an ideal across domains, including weight and income. Ecker et al. (2022) further showed that participants reported less threat and greater satisfaction in relation to their personal maintenance goals compared to their progress and protection goals. Extending the measures from self-reported intentions to behavior in natural settings, a preregistered experience-sampling study (Ecker, Ohlig, et al., 2026) assessed momentary current-desired discrepancy (satisfaction), anticipated-desired discrepancy (threat), goal value, and investment across all three goal types six times daily over two weeks. In lagged within-person analyses, time invested in each goal type was elicited by distinct antecedents: The current-desired gap uniquely predicted progress striving, the anticipated-desired gap uniquely predicted protection striving, and goal value drove striving across all three goal types, but most strongly for maintenance. Finally, evidence for the role of decayability comes from work on individual differences (Ecker, Lange, & Imhoff, 2026): The belief that keeping what one has requires investment, a notion closely related to decayability, predicted costly effort in a house-maintenance game and stronger effort invested in maintenance than in progress or protection across work, health, and relationships over two months of weekly reports.

The ternary framework also speaks to why maintenance may be underprioritized relative to progress and protection. Consider Jill, who has a loving intimate relationship with her partner-a state she currently has (absence of current-desired discrepancy) and anticipates to continue having (absence of an anticipated-desired discrepancy). Our framework predicts that Jill will put effort into maintaining her relationship when the action options at her disposal have a higher expected utility than inaction. The absence of a discrepancy makes inaction especially attractive: It is easier to conclude that no effort is needed when all one wishes for is already there. Moreover, inaction carries a high expectancy for the additional goal of minimizing effort. Jill may therefore reinterpret her relationship as one that requires no investment-an immunization strategy within the GDT-thereby perceiving any potential maintenance action as having a low expected utility. Thus, the observation or intuition that people tend to invest less in maintenance than in progress or protection (Ecker & Gilead, 2018) may be attributed to the fact that the total expected utility of maintenance actions is lower than that of inaction.

Maintenance Striving and Well-being

The claim that (paradigmatic) maintenance goals lack anticipated-desired and currentdesired discrepancies may have downstream implications for affect and well-being. To spell these out, we distinguish four sources of affect in goal pursuit. The first is the presence or absence of a discrepancy. A discrepancy yields negative affect, whose intensity grows with the magnitude of the discrepancy, whereas the absence of a discrepancy yields positive affect (see Hilpert et al., 2026; Moors, 2026a Moors, , 2026b;; Moors et al., 2021). The discrepancies that define progress and protection thus yield negative affect, whereas in the paradigmatic form of maintenance, this source of negative affect is absent. 4 The second source is the valence of Goal 1 (the desired state), which is positive in all goal types. The third is the valence of the current and anticipated states that are compared with Goal 1. In paradigmatic maintenance, these states equal the desired state and should therefore also be positive. This may not be true in progress and protection. The fourth source is the valence of the event contained in Goal 1, which is positive in a promotion focus but negative in a prevention focus. This last source varies orthogonally to goal type (see the discussions of regulatory focus in Section 2 and in the next section). Taking all these sources together, and all else being equal, maintenance goals should have a more positive influence on well-being than protection and progress goals.

Beyond the affective tone of the goal type itself, we suggest that maintenance goals also encourage greater well-being through the overt behavior they bring about. Research shows that routine behavior, which is central to maintenance striving, predicts a greater sense of meaning in life (Heintzelman & King, 2019; Mohideen & Heintzelman, 2023). As these authors argue, routines encourage a sense of meaning by enabling a continuous and coherent sense of self. Maintenance routines may be especially powerful in this regard, as they are specifically directed at supporting and stabilizing a valued current state, making them particularly likely to foster the kind of continuity that bestows a sense of meaning.

Regulatory Focus and Maintenance Goal Pursuit

As we have argued in this paper, the core distinction between promotion and prevention focus (Higgins, 1997 (Higgins, , 2018) ) rests on the valence of the event contained in the desired state. So understood, the promotion-prevention dichotomy is orthogonal to the ternary distinction between progress, protection, and maintenance. Let us illustrate this now for maintenance goals: Consider a student maintaining her grade level throughout her studies.

Knowing that she has a few years of studying ahead of her, she sets up a study routine (e.g., regular study groups alternated with regular breaks) that has a high expected utility and is feasible. Importantly, the student's desired state may be either a gain-the presence of high grades, which is a positive event (e.g., presence of high grades) or it may be a non-loss-the absence of low grades, which is a negative event, reflecting either a promotion or a prevention focus.

The proposed ternary framework suggests novel predictions that can be tested in future empirical research. One prediction might be that (paradigmatic) maintenance is sustained more readily under a promotion than a prevention focus (i.e., state level), and that prevention-focused individuals show a preference for progress and protection goals compared to maintenance goals (i.e., trait level). Recall that a promotion focus refers to a sensitivity to positively valenced events (gains and non-gains), whereas a prevention focus refers to a sensitivity to negatively valenced events (losses and non-losses). Under a prevention focus, the desired state in all goal types is a non-loss: While overall positive, it contains a negative event. In maintenance goals, the current and anticipated states are also non-losses, which are positive on the overall level but negative on the component level. In contrast, the current state in progress goals and the anticipated state in protection goals are losses, which are negative on both the overall and the component level. Because a prevention focus guides attention towards negative valence, and maintenance contains less of it than progress and protection do, we expect maintenance to be sustained more readily under a promotion focus. These predictions should hold not only at the trait level but also at the state level.

States whose value lies chiefly in the absence of a negative event (a non-loss, marking a prevention focus) should be the hardest to maintain. The daily struggle to maintain sobriety is a case in point. If a maintained state of sobriety is framed as the absence of the negative event of drinking, it cannot draw motivational power from such a positive event nor from the prospect of change or of the threat of relapse being removed, which progress and protection would hold out (McLellan et al., 2000).

These predictions run directly counter to Higgins's (2018) account, which goes as far as defining prevention itself as the motivation to maintain: For prevention, "0" is a non-loss and therefore a state worth maintaining, whereas for promotion it is a non-gain and therefore a state to leave behind. We argue instead that a "0" maintained under a promotion focus is not a non-gain but a gain to be held, and once the status quo can be a gain, the fit between maintenance and prevention loses its basis. The evidence Higgins (2018) marshals for prevention's "strong motivational force for maintaining the status quo" (p. 46) comes from participants who had just suffered a loss: the stronger their prevention motivation was, the more they chose whichever option could return them to "0" (Scholer et al., 2010). By our definitions, restoring a lost state (a non-loss) is a prevention-focused progress goal, not maintenance. Conversely, in the same paradigm, promotion-focused participants who had just made a large gain prioritized keeping it over winning further gains, while prevention-focused participants were largely unaffected by the gain (Zou et al., 2014). In our terms, keeping the gain may be promotion-focused maintenance. 5Maintenance under a prevention vs. promotion focus should differ not only in how readily it is sustained but also in the affect it generates. While the stability that comes with maintenance can provide psychological comfort (Landau et al., 2015), the comfort of a maintained positive event (promotion focus) and the comfort of a maintained absence of a negative event (prevention focus) may not be the same. Holding on to a positive event offers something to remain engaged with, so that its upkeep can be experienced as meaningful (Heintzelman & King, 2019). Holding on to the absence of a negative situation offers no such object of engagement. It may register as relief and then monotony rather than being meaningful (see Higgins et al., 1997; van Tilburg & Igou, 2012; Westgate & Wilson, 2018).

Investigating these questions requires renewed attention to methodology. The ternary framework can guide future experimental work on how goal type and regulatory focus interact. Consider Brodscholl et al.'s (2007) study of regulatory fit, which crossed goal type with regulatory focus and reported that maintenance fits a prevention focus. In their paradigm, participants who already held 100 tokens earned the right to retain five tokens for each word they solved. What distinguishes this from approach, on their account, is that the current state already equals the desired state. But this criterion does not, on its own, distinguish maintenance from avoidance: A person who already holds a valued state and acts to keep it may be maintaining that state, or may be avoiding its loss. This is even apparent in the authors' own description, stating that participants in the maintenance condition "worked to keep the tokens that they had won in the first task from being taken away" (p. 632). By our definitions, a participant working to reduce the tokens that would otherwise be taken away is reducing an anticipated-desired discrepancy; this is protection, not maintenance.

Our framework can help inform careful goal inductions and measurement in future research. For instance, one possibility we are currently considering is a spatial task in which participants occupy a position in a small environment, each neighboring position being better than, the same as, or worse than the current one. 6 Goal type then follows from the environment: A better position within reach calls for progress, a safer position within reach (that is, one from which later moves are less likely to end in a worse position) calls for protection, and an environment in which no position is better or safer, and some are worse, calls for maintenance. Crucially, the same environments can be presented as gains versus non-gains or as losses versus non-losses, so that regulatory focus varies independently of goal type. Moving to a better position (progress) may mean securing a gain or leaving a loss position for a non-loss one. Moving to a safer position (protection) may mean reducing the chance of landing in a loss position or reducing the chance of falling from a gain position.

Holding the current position (maintenance) may mean keeping a non-loss position or keeping a gain position. A task of this kind would allow a direct test of the prediction that maintenance is sustained more readily under a promotion focus.

Reconceptualizing Political Ideology

For decades, political psychology has characterized liberalism as rooted in progress motivation and conservatism as rooted in protection motivation and threat sensitivity (Janoff-Bulman, 2009; Jost et al., 2003). On the most influential account, the appeal of conservatism lies in its capacity to satisfy a cluster of epistemic and existential needs. The epistemic needs reflect a wish to minimize and manage uncertainty, while the existential needs reflect a wish 6 The first author is developing this methodology in collaboration with David Melnikoff.

to manage the threat of one's own mortality (Jost et al., 2003). Preservation of the status quo and resistance to change yield certainty and security and thereby serve these needs, whereas change introduces uncertainty and threat. The existing social arrangement is defended and viewed as legitimate, natural, and good, because the alternative is feared (Jost et al., 2004; Jost & Hunyady, 2005). This framework faces mounting empirical challenges. Systematic replication failures have undermined physiological findings linking conservatism to threat responses (Bakker et al., 2020; Johnston & Madson, 2022), and meta-analyses reveal associations between personality and political preferences that are inconsistent and substantially smaller than initially assumed (Brandt et al., 2021; Costello et al., 2022). The ternary framework can help make sense of these inconsistencies because it distinguishes two ways of holding to the status quo that the dominant account conflates. In protection, it stems from the goal to reduce an anticipated discrepancy, whereas in maintenance, it stems from the goal to keep the valued state as it is. Jost et al. (2003) treat holding to the status quo as synonymous with "resistance to change", characterizing conservatives as uniquely sensitive to the possibility of loss. 7Resistance to change, as they conceive it, therefore reflects a goal to protect.

Zooming out again, we argue that the progress-protection dichotomy may inadequately capture the motivational architecture underlying political orientation. If maintenance is added into the mix, it could be examined whether, beyond liberals prioritizing future improvements (progress), conservatives are focused on the loss of valued conditions (protection) or, alternatively, on the preservation of these conditions (maintenance). The latter option would reconceptualize conservative psychology not as dispositionally fearful or avoidance-oriented, but as oriented towards active stewardship and preservation of valued institutions and traditions-a characterization closer to how conservative thinkers have described their own position (Burke, 1790 (Burke, /1987;; Rossiter, 1962). This possibility yields falsifiable predictions: Conservative political actors should express and support maintenance goals more than liberal actors do, and this difference should hold net of protection goals. Conversely, if conservatives and liberals differ in protection but not in maintenance, the maintenance-conservatism mapping fails and conservatism reduces to protection.

While most of the empirical work to date has concentrated on the protection pathway, some evidence already documents the maintenance pathway. Ecker and Lammers (2026b) scored every sentence of 892 party platforms from 11 countries in the Manifesto Corpus (Lehmann et al., 2024) with a classifier trained to predict human ratings of progress, maintenance, and protection orientation (held-out agreement with human ratings averaged r = .56). Right-wing parties were more maintenance-oriented than left-wing parties competing in the same country, whereas protection orientation did not differ. Protection orientation was instead higher among parties at the poles of the spectrum, on the left as well as on the right.

The same study provides evidence about voters' own goal structure from six samples in which citizens described goals they wanted their country to pursue: Overall, maintenance orientation increased with conservatism. Again, protection orientation was highest at both ends of the ideology scale, and maintenance orientation peaked among moderate conservatives and fell again at the far right.

Evidence about the effects of political messaging comes from pooled experiments in which participants rated their support for policy statements drawn from party platforms: Support for maintenance-framed statements increased with conservatism, support for progress-framed statements with liberalism, and protection framing showed no reliable interaction with ideology (Ecker & Lammers, 2026b). A replication of the same pattern along with causal evidence comes from the domain of environmental policy (Ecker & Lammers, 2026a). In a preregistered experiment, pro-environmental policies from party platforms were rewritten using maintenance, progress, and protection framings with their content held constant. Support declined with conservatism under every framing, but the liberalconservative gap was markedly smaller under maintenance framing than under the other two.

These findings converge across party texts, citizens' own words, and experiments, but they come from a limited set of democracies. Future research should map the patterns more broadly and follow their fluctuations and realignments within countries and across time as the political climate changes.

Behavioral Interventions for Global Issues

Understanding the underlying mechanism of maintenance striving offers new insights into real-life problems of behavior change. Current applied research already points to maintenance as an important stage in behavior change programs, noting that many attempts to alter maladaptive behavioral patterns fail precisely at this stage-after the desired behavior has been acquired but before it has been consolidated into a stable routine (Foxx, 2013; Kwasnicka et al., 2016; Nigg et al., 2008; Rothman, 2000; van Stralen et al., 2009). The ternary framework offers a mechanistic account of why this is so: Once a desired behavioral state has been achieved, the absence of discrepancy makes inaction attractive and reduces the perceived expected utility of continued effort. This tendency towards inaction may be mitigated by interventions that increase the perceived value of the current state.

An additional insight from our mechanistic analysis is that interventions to boost maintenance striving may be uniquely effective in situations where threat is not salient enough to give rise to protection striving, for instance, when negative anticipated outcomes are delayed, as in climate change. The environmental messaging findings reviewed above fit this expectation: Maintenance-oriented messages raised conservatives' support for climate policy, whereas protection-oriented messages lowered it (Ecker & Lammers, 2026a)

Conclusion

This paper set out to develop a theoretically grounded account of maintenance goals and their place within the broader architecture of goal-directed behavior. To this end, we proposed a ternary goal framework that distinguishes maintenance goals from progress and protection goals on the basis of underlying mechanistic differences. By redefining approach and avoidance goals in terms of current-desired and anticipated-desired discrepancies respectively, the framework resolves a longstanding ambiguity in the goal literature and creates the conceptual space necessary for recognizing maintenance as a third basic goal type. This novel framework generates testable empirical predictions, offers a fresh perspective on the motivational underpinnings of political ideology, and points towards new tools for designing behavioral interventions. Maintenance goals are ubiquitous in daily life, yet they have remained theoretically underspecified and empirically understudied. We hope that the ternary goal framework offered here will help to redress this imbalance and encourage the sustained scholarly attention that this important motivational construct deserves.
