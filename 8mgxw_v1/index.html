Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. This module retrieves information from repositories.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Food addiction is increasingly invoked to explain compulsive overconsumption of highly processed foods, and its evidence base rests largely on the Yale Food Addiction Scale 2.0 (YFAS 2.0). It scores 11 criteria adapted from DSM-5 substance use disorder, yet studies commonly report only a food addiction classification, symptom count or severity band. We examined whether the criteria underlying these summaries, including tolerance and withdrawal, are visible in the published record, and whether mixed results are interpreted in a balanced way. Seventy-eight empirical studies administering the full 35-item YFAS 2.0, identified through PubMed, were double-coded according to a preregistered protocol. Sixtyseven studies (85.9%, 95% CI [76.5, 91.9]) did not report all 11 criteria, and 64 (82.1%) reported none; these same 64 omitted both tolerance and withdrawal. Of 69 studies with mixed results, 29 (42.0%, [31.1, 53.8]) drew conclusions that did not reflect that mix, including 6 of the 10 complete reporters with mixed results, and all 29 emphasised significant findings. These results do not show that food addiction is invalid or that unreported symptoms were absent; however, they indicate that readers can rarely see which symptoms produce a classification, and that interpretation of mixed findings emphasises statistically significant results. Criterion-level reporting and balanced interpretation are separate problems requiring separate remedies. We propose a minimum reporting standard for YFAS 2.0 research.

1.0 Introduction 1.1 The Clinical and Public Health Context

Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) account for approximately 57% of energy intake in the UK diet (Rauber et al., 2019) and 66% among UK adolescents (Chavez-Ugalde et al., 2024). Higher UPF exposure is associated with an elevated risk of common mental disorders and all-cause mortality (Lane et al., 2024).

For appetite research, the central behavioural question is why intake of these foods is so difficult to regulate. In a controlled inpatient trial, an ultra-processed diet produced excess energy intake of approximately 500 kcal per day over two weeks, compared with a minimally processed diet matched for presented calories and macronutrients (Hall et al., 2019). One influential explanation is that, in vulnerable individuals, highly processed foods trigger an addictive-like eating pattern characterised by intense craving, impaired control over intake, continued consumption despite negative consequences, and distress when attempting to cut down (Gearhardt et al., 2009; Schulte et al., 2015).

Whether compulsive UPF consumption is best conceptualised as a substance use disorder (SUD) or within established eating-disorder models has direct implications for dietary care (Gearhardt et al., 2023). In a 12-month international Delphi process, 37 of 40 experts from 10 countries endorsed a consensus statement that UPF addiction shows the biochemical, neurological and behavioural features of an SUD (Unwin et al., 2025), and proponents argue that highly processed foods have addictive properties (Gearhardt & DiFeliceantonio, 2023). This addiction framework has been used to support structured abstinence from trigger foods (Thompson, Briones, et al., 2025). Eating-disorder treatment models often emphasise flexible, non-restrictive eating, reflecting concerns that rigid dietary control may exacerbate disordered eating in some individuals (Meule, 2019). These concerns have prompted harm-reduction alternatives within the food addiction framework, such as the Food Addiction Clinical Treatment (FACT) manual (O'Hea et al., 2024). Evaluating these clinical models requires knowing which specific eating-behaviour and physiological symptoms produce positive food addiction classifications in the empirical literature.

1.2 Food Addiction as a Candidate Diagnosis

Food addiction (FA) refers to compulsive, habitual overconsumption meeting criteria analogous to those for SUD (Gearhardt et al., 2009) but lacks formal recognition in the DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) or ICD-11 (World Health Organization, 2019). Despite this, prevalence data are often cited in support of recognition. A meta-analysis of 272 studies reported a corrected pooled prevalence of 24%, with 14% in non-clinical samples and 63% in binge eating samples (Praxedes et al., 2022; "Correction to 'Prevalence of Food Addiction'," 2024), and a recent study reported 17.4% FA prevalence among Chilean young adults, with most classified as having a severe FA (Díaz-Torrente et al., 2026).

These figures are comparable to US 12-month prevalence estimates for DSM-5 alcohol use disorder (AUD) of 13.9% (Grant et al., 2015) and tobacco (nicotine) use disorder of 20.0% (Chou et al., 2016).

Comparisons of this kind feature prominently in advocacy for diagnostic recognition (Unwin et al., 2025).

The comparison is consequential but also potentially misleading. Diagnostic criteria for AUD and tobacco use disorder have been refined over decades against convergent evidence drawn from structured clinical interviews, epidemiological surveillance, pharmacological challenge trials, multiple competing measurement instruments, and biological markers, under the validity standards that have guided psychiatric nosology since Robins and Guze (1970) and their successors (Hasin et al., 2013; Kendler, 1990). FA has no comparable evidential base. Its prevalence estimates rely heavily on a single operationalisation of the construct, so consistent figures across studies cannot, on their own, demonstrate independent construct validity (Fletcher & Kenny, 2018; Oliveira et al., 2025). It remains unclear to what extent these prevalence estimates, including the widely cited pooled figure (Praxedes et al., 2022), reflect a distinct addictive disorder, features shared with established eating disorders, or the breadth of the criteria used to classify food addiction. Current reporting conventions, particularly aggregate-only reporting, make these possibilities difficult to separate, and this is the measurement problem the present study addresses.

1.3 The YFAS 2.0 and Single-Instrument Dependence

Measurement in the FA field is dominated by one family of instruments (Meule & Gearhardt, 2019): the Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS) and its DSM-5-aligned revision, the YFAS 2.0. This 35-item selfreport questionnaire assesses eating-related experiences over the past 12 months against the 11 DSM-5 SUD criteria (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Gearhardt et al., 2016). The YFAS 2.0 shows good reliability within a meta-analysis examining the whole YFAS family (Jahrami et al., 2025), and its symptom counts are associated with overeating and loss-of-control eating in daily life (Kalan et al., 2024).

Neither reliability nor association with eating behaviour, however, establishes whether the scale measures addiction specifically.

A single YFAS 2.0 completion produces four analytically distinct outputs. Three are aggregate: a binary FA-positive or FA-negative (FA+/FA-) classification, which requires at least two criteria and clinically significant impairment or distress; a symptom count from 0 to 11; and, where impairment or distress is present, a severity grade of mild (two or three criteria), moderate (four or five) or severe (six or more).

The fourth is endorsement of each individual criterion (Gearhardt et al., 2016). These outputs answer different questions: aggregate outputs describe prevalence and overall symptom burden, whereas criterion-level outputs identify the SUD features endorsed and their frequency.

An FA+ classification therefore summarises a symptom profile without identifying it. Among individuals meeting the impairment or distress requirement, 2,036 combinations of criteria reach the threshold: the 2,048 possible subsets of 11 criteria, minus the empty set and 11 single-criterion subsets (our calculation from the scoring rule in Gearhardt et al., 2016). Two people can thus receive the same classification without sharing a single criterion, although this count says nothing about how frequently each profile occurs. Because every criterion is scored to derive the aggregate outputs, criterion-level results are generated in every fully scored YFAS 2.0 dataset, whether reported or not. These data are necessary to establish whether aggregate findings involve tolerance and withdrawal, the criteria most directly tied to the addiction model's account of neuroadaptation (Koob & Volkow, 2016).

Alternative instruments, such as the Addiction-like Eating Behaviour Scale (Ruddock et al., 2017), have generated far less research, so FA+ classifications are rarely tested against an independent measure. Consistent findings may therefore reflect a reproducible phenomenon, repeated use of the same measurement architecture, or both. Flake and Fried (2020) identify undisclosed measurement and reporting decisions as consequential but often invisible sources of researcher degrees of freedom. The selection of YFAS 2.0 outputs for publication is a specific instance of this problem. Without a reporting standard specifying which outputs should be published, readers cannot determine whether conclusions reflect the instrument's full architecture or only a selected portion. To our knowledge, consistency with which the literature reports these outputs has not been systematically examined.

Recent measurement developments sharpen the distinction between aggregate and criterion-level outputs.

Scores on a new Ultra-Processed Food Consumption Scale were positively associated with food addiction symptoms, but not with body mass index or Mediterranean-diet adherence, in an exploratory moderation analysis, the interaction between consumption and Mediterranean-diet adherence did not reach statistical significance (p = .0999), although the authors interpreted it as a possible attenuation of the association between consumption and food addiction symptoms (Mantzios et al., 2026). Exposure, dietary context and addiction-like symptoms therefore appear related but non-equivalent. In 415 adults, severe food addiction and obesity were associated with greater selection of unhealthy relative to healthy food images in a probabilistic image-choice task, and this bias remained stable over one year in a follow-up subsample (Solinas et al., 2026), linking YFAS-defined food addiction to observable choice. Neither study's aggregate findings, however, establishes which criteria account for the associations: craving and impaired control, continued use despite negative consequences, or tolerance and withdrawal. Evidence concerning the external correlates of YFAS 2.0 classifications may therefore accumulate while their internal diagnostic meaning remains undisclosed.

1.4 Neurobiological, Preclinical, and Pharmacological Evidence

The theoretical case for treating compulsive eating as an addiction rests on parallels between food and drug reward in mesolimbic circuitry. Positron emission tomography (PET) has revealed lower striatal dopamine D2 receptor availability in individuals with obesity, mirroring findings in drug addiction (Wang et al., 2001), and Volkow et al. (2008) proposed that chronic overconsumption of energy-dense food disrupts the reward, motivation and inhibitory-control circuits that drugs of abuse dysregulate. Within the YFAS 2.0, this account maps most directly onto tolerance and withdrawal (Gearhardt et al., 2016; Koob & Volkow, 2016).

Recent evidence, however, complicates this picture. In the UK Biobank, high UPF intake was linked to adverse adiposity and metabolic profiles, and to cellularity changes in feeding-related subcortical brain regions, associations partially mediated by dyslipidaemia, systemic inflammation and body mass index (Morys et al., 2025). The supra-additive effect of combining fat and carbohydrate on food reward (DiFeliceantonio et al., 2018) and the metabolic effects of food (Ludwig, 2026) offer further explanations for overconsumption. Using [ 11 C]raclopride PET, Darcey et al. (2025) found no significant post-ingestive striatal dopamine response to an ultra-processed milkshake, and the highly variable individual responses were not significantly related to adiposity. Dopaminergic neuroadaptation and metabolic or inflammatory dysregulation therefore appear compatible with the same aggregate endpoints, and aggregate YFAS 2.0 outputs cannot adjudicate between them.

Preclinical and self-report evidence is open to similar alternative readings. In rodents, sugar-addictionlike behaviour emerges mainly under intermittent-access schedules (Avena et al., 2008; Westwater et al., 2016), and extended access to a cafeteria diet produces D2 receptor downregulation and compulsive-like intake (Johnson & Kenny, 2010), although translation to human eating remains debated (Westwater et al., 2016). Many drugs of abuse increase extracellular dopamine concentrations in mesolimbic pathways (Di Chiara & Imperato, 1988). In associative learning, phasic dopamine responses to natural rewards can diminish as reward delivery becomes predictable, with responses emerging instead to reward-predicting cues (Keiflin & Janak, 2015; Schultz, 2016). Hedonic adaptation, the ordinary decline in pleasure with repeated exposure, could therefore account for some of the experience the tolerance item was designed to capture, and qualitative interviews indicate that respondents may misinterpret the YFAS 2.0 tolerance and withdrawal items (Schiestl et al., 2022). If so, scoring may count some non-physiological experiences as tolerance or withdrawal.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists illustrate the same interpretive problem. A systematic review of 11 functional neuroimaging studies, available as a preprint, found tentative and inconsistent evidence that acute administration reduces brain reactivity to food cues in appetite and reward regions, mostly in samples of 20 or fewer participants per group (Dang et al., 2026). Reduced cue reactivity is compatible with lower hunger, altered incentive salience, general reward dampening or modification of an addictionspecific process. If tolerance and withdrawal identify a physiologically distinct subgroup, their endorsement should predict neural or treatment responses beyond craving, impaired control, total symptom count and food exposure. Testing this requires criterion profiles, so reporting practice bears directly on treatment research.

1.5 Measurement, Validity, and Criterion-Level Invisibility

A measure is valid for an attribute if the attribute exists and variation in it produces variation in the measurement outcome (Borsboom et al., 2004). Evaluating that claim requires access to the measurement structure as well as to a summary score. Good internal consistency does not settle the matter: in an analysis of 15 widely used measures in social and personality psychology, many scales with acceptable reliability failed more demanding tests of structural validity and measurement invariance (Hussey & Hughes, 2020).

For the YFAS 2.0, the question is therefore whether readers can see the symptoms from which its classifications are built, as well as whether the scale is reliable.

The current study addresses two questions about how the YFAS 2.0 literature reports food addiction. The first concerns completeness. We use the term criterion-level invisibility to describe situations in which criterion-level results are generated during scoring but not reported in the published article, even though the YFAS 2.0 algorithm requires each criterion to be scored before a food addiction classification or symptom count can be derived (Gearhardt et al., 2016). When the omitted criteria are tolerance and withdrawal, readers cannot tell whether either self-reported symptom contributed to a classification. This is a reporting gap. It does not imply that unreported symptoms were absent or deliberately concealed, or that any data were fabricated. A study may have observed high rates of tolerance and withdrawal and simply not reported them.

The second question concerns interpretation. Spin, the presentation of results in a way that makes them appear more favourable than the data justify, is well documented in biomedical research (Boutron et al., 2010; Boutron & Ravaud, 2018; Chiu et al., 2017). In psychiatry and psychology journals, spin was identified in the abstracts of 56% of randomised trials with non-significant primary outcomes (Jellison et al., 2020). Selective outcome reporting and publication bias can likewise make a body of evidence appear more favourable than the complete results (Chan et al., 2004; Turner et al., 2008). We use the term interpretative imbalance for conclusions that emphasise one part of a mixed pattern of results without acknowledging the remainder. Completeness and balance are separate issues. A study can report every criterion and still draw an unbalanced conclusion, and a study that reports only aggregate outputs can interpret them cautiously. We therefore examined them separately and then tested whether they were related.

1.6 The Present Study

We audited published empirical studies that administered the full YFAS 2.0 to address three primary questions. First, to what extent do YFAS 2.0 empirical studies comprehensively report all DSM-5 diagnostic criteria across the scale, and what is the prevalence of incomplete or selective outcome reporting? Second, when results contain a mix of statistically significant and non-significant outcomes, are the conclusions nuanced or non-nuanced? And third, when conclusions based on mixed results are non-nuanced, are they more likely to be driven by significant or non-significant outcomes?

We proposed five confirmatory hypotheses to address these questions: • H1.

Most YFAS 2.0 studies do not report results for all 11 criteria (proportion > .50).

• H2a. Among studies that report all 11 criteria and obtain mixed results, non-nuanced conclusions occur (proportion > 0).

• H2b. Among all studies with mixed results, non-nuanced conclusions occur (proportion > 0).

• H3a. Among non-nuanced studies in the H2a subset, most conclusions emphasise statistically significant findings (proportion > .50).

• H3b. Among non-nuanced studies in the H2b subset, most conclusions emphasise statistically significant findings (proportion > .50).

Five exploratory analyses, which were not part of the protocol, characterised the extent of criterion reporting (EA1), the reporting of tolerance and withdrawal (EA2), the style in which YFAS 2.0 findings were communicated (EA3), the co-occurrence of these omissions (EA4) and the association between reporting completeness and interpretative balance (EA5). Importantly, our study did not test whether food addiction exists as a distinct disorder or whether the YFAS 2.0 is a valid measure of addiction.

Instead, we examined the extent to which published research provides information about the symptoms underlying food addiction classifications, and whether conclusions reflect the pattern of results obtained.

2.0 Methodology

2.1 Study Design

This study used a meta-scientific design to evaluate reporting completeness and interpretative practice in published research using the YFAS 2.0. Meta-scientific research applies empirical methods to evaluate the quality of scientific literature and detect potential bias (Ioannidis et al., 2015). A protocol detailing the search strategy, eligibility criteria, coding framework, and planned analyses was posted publicly on OSF on 13 February 2026, after screening but before the commencement of coding( https://osf.io/6wbyt/files/dnuhs).

2.2 Sample Selection

2.2.1 Search Strategy

Eligible studies were identified through a PubMed search conducted in November 2025 using the search string ("Yale Food Addiction Scale" [Title/Abstract] OR "YFAS" [Title/Abstract]), which returned 511 records; the exported record set is available on OSF (https://osf.io/6wbyt). Equivalent scoping searches for two alternative self-report FA measures ("Addiction-like Eating Behaviour/Behavior"; "Processed Food Withdrawal") returned 22 and 3 records, respectively, confirming the YFAS family as the dominant instrument in this literature.

PubMed was selected for its broad biomedical coverage and comprehensive indexing of the journals in which YFAS 2.0 research is published (Gusenbauer and Haddaway, 2020). Scoping searches in PsycINFO, Web of Science, and EMBASE showed near-total overlap with PubMed, which also returned the largest record set; PubMed was therefore used as the sole formal source.

2.3 Coding

2.3.1 Coding Team and Allocation

Five researchers (A.C., C.G., I.T., K.K. and T.Y.) coded the studies under the supervision of C.C. The 86 screened papers were divided into five overlapping article ranges, each assigned to a different pair of coders, so that every paper was independently coded by two team members. 2.3.2 Coder Calibration and Consensus

Before formal coding, all five coders independently applied the full protocol to a calibration paper and discussed every disagreement until consensus was reached. This aligned the interpretation of ambiguous definitions, particularly for the two variables requiring the greatest degree of subjective judgement conclusion: nuance and biological framing. During formal coding, the two coders assigned to each paper coded independently and resolved discrepancies through discussion, with the supervisor adjudicating any that remained. A brief written justification was recorded for every judgement of nuance and biological framing. Pre-consensus codes were not retained, so inter-rater agreement (e.g., Cohen's kappa) could not be calculated, and the final dataset should be treated as a consensus product.

2.4 Statistical Analysis

Analyses followed the protocol, except where noted below and in Supplementary Table S3. The five confirmatory hypotheses and their outcome variables and tests (Table 4) were specified in the protocol before coding began; however the adjusted benchmark for H2a and H2b (Section 2.4.1), the robustness analyses, and all exploratory analyses were specified afterwards and are reported as such. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS statistics (Version 29.0). Proportions are reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals.

2.4.1 Confirmatory Hypotheses

Each confirmatory hypothesis was tested with a one-sided binomial test, because each outcome was dichotomous and was compared with a benchmark proportion (Table 4).

The protocol specified a benchmark of 0 for H2a and H2b, reflecting the expectation that, under ideal transparency conditions, no mixed results study would draw a non-nuanced conclusion. However, because a binomial test cannot be run against a proportion of zero, the benchmark was adjusted to .05 as an appropriate workable alternative. This value was chosen over .01 or .02 because .05 is the smallest benchmark at which a single non-nuanced study cannot, on its own, produce a significant result at any sample size; against .01 or .02, one study would suffice in samples of up to five or two studies, respectively. This change was made after coding had been completed and is reported as a deviation from the protocol.

Three robustness analyses, not specified in the protocol, were added after the confirmatory tests. First, a fragility index was calculated for each test: the minimum number of studies whose classification would need to change for the result to lose significance at α = .05. This was particularly relevant to H2a and H3b, which drew on small subsamples. Second, we applied Holm and Bonferroni corrections across the five confirmatory tests. Third, H2a and H2b were re-tested against benchmarks from .10 to .50 to demonstrate how far their results depended on the choice of .05.

2.4.2 Exploratory Analyses

The exploratory analyses were not part of the protocol and were conducted after the confirmatory tests.

Their results are therefore interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

2.4.2.1 EA1: Individual Criterion Reporting

The binary coding of H1 established whether studies reported all 11 criteria but treated a study reporting no criteria the same as one reporting ten. EA1 classified each study as None, Some or All (Table 3), using the same criterion coding (C1-C11) as H1. We report frequencies with Wilson CIs and the mean, standard deviation, median and range of criteria reported. One-sided exact binomial tests compared the proportion of None studies with .50, both across all studies and within NOT_ALL studies, to establish whether incomplete reporting mainly reflected the absence of any criterion-level data rather than partial reporting.

2.4.2.2 EA2: Reporting of Tolerance and Withdrawal Criteria

EA2 examined reporting of tolerance and withdrawal, the criteria most closely linked to physiological adaptation in substance-addiction models. Their absence prevents readers from evaluating this part of the food-addiction construct. Absence of reporting was not interpreted as absence of endorsement.

Studies in the "Some" category were classified into four EA2 categories (Table 3). Because only three studies fell into this category, this classification was reported descriptively without inferential testing. To provide a field-level estimate, we applied the same categories to all 78 studies, and a one-sided exact binomial test compared the proportion of studies reporting neither criterion against a .50 benchmark.

Agreement between tolerance and withdrawal reporting was assessed using Cohen's kappa, and an exact

McNemar test examined whether one criterion was reported more often than the other.

2.4.2.3 EA3: Reporting Style

EA3 examined whether findings were communicated through aggregate outputs, individual criteria or both, using the definitions in Table 3. This differs from EA1, which established how many criteria each study reported. This distinction matters because the two levels answer different questions: aggregate outputs summarise food-addiction status or overall symptom burden, whereas criterion-level outputs show which symptoms produce that result.

Frequencies and percentages were calculated for each reporting style, with Wilson CIs, consistent with the confirmatory analyses. Among Aggregate-Only studies, we calculated the frequency of each aggregate output type, with Wilson CIs, and the number of studies using more than one. Because a study could use more than one output, these categories were not mutually exclusive, and the percentages do not sum to 100. Studies for which output type was not recorded were excluded from this analysis. To test whether aggregate-only reporting was the dominant reporting style, a one-sided exact binomial test compared the proportion of Aggregate-Only studies against a .50 benchmark, mirroring the benchmark used for H1. A proportion significantly above .50 would indicate that most studies communicated YFAS 2.0 findings without showing any individual criterion results. Uncertain classifications were checked against the full text; where the full text could not be accessed, the team's criterion-level coding was used.

2.4.2.4 EA4: Co-occurrence of Omissions

EA4 examined whether the omissions identified in EA1-EA3 occurred in the same studies. We crosstabulated criterion reporting category, tolerance and withdrawal reporting, and reporting style, and counted studies that reported no criteria, neither tolerance nor withdrawal, or only aggregate outputs, with a Wilson CI. We also counted studies that reported any other criterion while omitting both tolerance and withdrawal.

2.4.2.5 EA5: Reporting Completeness and Interpretative Balance

EA5 examined whether reporting completeness was associated with interpretative balance. Among mixedresults studies, the proportion of non-nuanced conclusions was compared between YES_ALL and NOT_ALL studies using a two-sided Fisher's exact test, with an odds ratio and 95% CI. Because few mixed-results studies were YES_ALL, the analysis was interpreted through its confidence interval rather than its p-value.

2.5 Ethical Considerations

The study involved secondary analysis of publicly available published research and did not involve data collection from human participants; formal ethical approval was therefore not required.

4.0 Discussion

4.1 Principal Findings

In this audit of 78 studies, most articles did not show which symptoms contributed to a food addiction classification. Sixty-four reported no individual criteria, and 3 of the 14 that did report criteria described their findings in the main text only through aggregate outputs. Tolerance and withdrawal, the two criteria most closely tied to the substance-based account, were unreported in the same 64 studies. A separate finding concerned interpretation: of the 69 studies with mixed results, 29 drew conclusions that did not reflect the mixed pattern, with all of them emphasising statistically significant findings. Studies that reported every criterion were no less likely to draw such conclusions. Neither finding supports or refutes food addiction as a construct but together they identify what readers can and cannot learn about food addiction from the published record.

4.2 A Reporting Default, Not an Isolated Omission

The distribution of non-reporting is more informative than its overall rate. Of the 67 incomplete reporters, 64 reported no individual criteria and only three reported a subset. If authors were deciding case-by-case which criteria were relevant to their research question, a graduated pattern of partial reporting would be expected. A near all-or-none distribution is more consistent with a shared convention in which the classification, symptom count or severity band is treated as the result and the criteria as intermediate scoring steps. Because the audit did not ask authors why they reported as they did, this remains an interpretation, but it is compatible with evidence that practices rewarded by publication can spread through a field without anyone intending the consequences (Smaldino & McElreath, 2016).

A reasonable objection is that aggregate reporting is psychometrically justified. The original YFAS 2.0 validation supported a one-factor structure (Gearhardt et al., 2016), and pooled evidence supports reliability (Jahrami et al., 2025). If the 11 criteria index a single latent dimension, a total score can offer an efficient summary. This argument is sound as far as it goes, but it answers a different question. Factor structure describes how criteria covary across a sample but does not show that people with the same total share the same symptoms, and reliable scales can still conceal structural validity problems (Hussey & Hughes, 2020). As Section 1.3 showed, two individuals can receive the same classification without sharing a criterion, so two samples with the same prevalence need not share a symptom profile. Criterion-level data show that the distinction has empirical consequences: after bariatric surgery, some criteria declined more slowly than others (Ban et al., 2025), a pattern that a falling prevalence would conceal. The psychometric case for aggregate scores is therefore incomplete, although not wrong. Evidence that the criteria cohere does not show that they are interchangeable, and reporting the underlying measurement decisions and results would allow readers to judge both (Flake & Fried, 2020).

The distinction between aggregate and criterion-level outputs matters most when food addiction is compared with binge eating. Graver et al. (2026) found that tolerance and withdrawal were represented on the YFAS 2.0 but not on the five binge-spectrum measures. This is a difference in instrument content: the YFAS 2.0 asks about tolerance and withdrawal and the binge-spectrum measures do not, but this does not show that people classified with food addiction experience these symptoms more often than people with binge eating.

A study reporting criterion endorsements could test whether these symptoms distinguish groups after accounting for shared features such as loss of control; a study reporting only prevalence cannot. Given the high co-occurrence of food addiction classifications with binge eating disorder (Gearhardt et al., 2012) and the mechanisms the two conditions share (Schulte et al., 2016), aggregate outputs alone leave open whether a classification reflects addiction-specific symptoms or the bingespectrum features the instruments have in common.

4.3 Unreported Tolerance and Withdrawal

Sixty-four studies reported neither tolerance nor withdrawal, 12 reported both, two reported tolerance alone and none reported withdrawal alone. This high concordance (κ = .908) refers to whether the two criteria were reported and says nothing about whether participants experienced both symptoms. It is nonetheless informative about the kind of omission involved. Selective omission of an unfavourable criterion might be expected to produce greater divergence between the two, such as studies reporting tolerance while omitting withdrawal. The near-uniform pattern is more consistent with both criteria disappearing as a by-product of the aggregate convention described in Section 4.2. That interpretation cannot be confirmed from published reports, because the audit did not establish which criteria participants met (i.e., the symptoms they reported) or why authors chose particular outputs. Comparing a protocol with a publication can reveal discrepancies for prespecified outcomes (Chan et al., 2004), but where a protocol does not specify individual YFAS criteria, that comparison cannot establish whether criterion-level reporting was planned.

One counterargument to the importance of reporting tolerance and withdrawal is that neither symptom is necessary or sufficient to establish the addictive potential of a substance (Gearhardt et al., 2026a). This is consistent with DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, which permit a diagnosis of substance use disorder in the absence of either symptom (Hasin et al., 2013). However, if greater diagnostic weight is placed on features such as craving, impaired control and continued consumption despite adverse consequences, it becomes particularly important to establish whether these features distinguish food addiction from established eating disorders. These are also the criteria that overlap most with binge-spectrum measures (Graver et al., 2026). The argument therefore shifts the question from whether physiological symptoms are present to whether the remaining behavioural criteria distinguish food addiction from binge eating disorder, and answering that question also requires criterion-level data.

The opposing case also depends on the same missing information. McCarthy et al. (2026) interpret the absence of reported withdrawal during dietary switching as evidence against an addiction account. A review found controlled evidence for withdrawal-like responses in animals but noted that human findings were largely retrospective (Parnarouskis et al., 2022), and some respondents interpreted the YFAS 2.0 tolerance and withdrawal items differently from their intended meanings (Schiestl et al., 2022). Reporting those responses would clarify which symptoms participants reported, although it could not by itself demonstrate neuroadaptation.

Sixteen of the 78 studies adopted an explicitly biological framing (Table 5), yet the biological evidence on which such framings draw, including proposed reward mechanisms (Hough et al., 2026), dopamine responses to an ultra-processed milkshake (Darcey et al., 2025) and excess energy intake on an ultraprocessed diet (Hall et al., 2019), concern outcomes other than the criteria underlying a YFAS classification. Whether tolerance and withdrawal are essential, peripheral or poorly measured, the question can be examined only in criterion-level data, and those data were absent from more than four-fifths of the studies we audited.

4.4 Interpretative Imbalance is a Separate Problem

Interpretative imbalance was common and consistently directional. All 29 non-nuanced conclusions emphasised statistically significant findings (Table 6), a pattern consistent with the spin documented in biomedical and psychological research (Boutron et al., 2010; Chiu et al., 2017; Jellison et al., 2020) and with a broader decline in the reporting of negative results (Fanelli, 2012). In food addiction research, such framing has a specific consequence. Consider a study that finds associations between food addiction status and some variables but not others. When conclusions report only the significant associations, the literature may appear more consistent in linking food addiction with psychopathology, weight or biological markers than the underlying results justify. Across many studies, this could strengthen the apparent convergence on which arguments for diagnostic recognition rely. Selective publication has produced a similar inflation in other fields (Turner et al., 2008), while the present findings concern a related pattern of emphasis within published articles.

Two qualifications are important. First, the pattern is consistent with selective emphasis but does not prove that it is unjustified. Nuance was a judgement about whether a conclusion reflected the full pattern of results, and some conclusions may have appropriately emphasised significant findings because they addressed the primary hypothesis. Second, mixed results were coded across all YFAS 2.0 outcomes rather than across criteria specifically, as the protocol had specified. This broader definition means that H2 and H3 concern the interpretation of YFAS 2.0 findings in general, not only the interpretation of criterionlevel results.

The comparison between complete and incomplete reporters (EA5) shows why completeness and balance must be addressed separately. Complete reporters drew non-nuanced conclusions in 60.0% of cases and incomplete reporters in 39.0%, but the confidence interval for the odds ratio was wide (0.60 to 9.23), so the data are compatible with anything from a modest protective association to a substantial increase. The findings do not provide evidence that complete criterion reporting alone is associated with more balanced interpretation. Three studies also reported criterion data without referring to them in their interpretation.

Reporting all 11 criteria makes the symptom profile available but it does not ensure that authors use it or interpret it in a balanced way. Editorial guidance alone has not reliably improved reporting in other journals (Diong et al., 2018), which suggests that reporting requirements need to be paired with checks on interpretation.

4.5 Implications for Food Addiction Research

Four implications follow from the current findings. First, YFAS prevalence has been compared with estimates for SUDs (Gearhardt et al., 2023). Because the same classification can arise from different combinations of criteria (Section 1.3), and because criterion profiles are rarely reported, similar prevalence should not be read as evidence of similar symptom profiles. Such comparisons would be more informative if the proportion of classified participants endorsing tolerance, withdrawal, craving and loss of control were reported alongside prevalence.

Second, how a patient's eating is framed may matter for treatment. Thompson et al. (2025) used illustrative clinical vignettes to discuss the possible benefits and risks of targeted abstinence, without testing treatment efficacy. In individual cognitive behavioural therapy for adults with binge eating disorder, NICE advises against dieting to lose weight during treatment because it may trigger binge eating (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, 2017, recommendation 1.4.7). These are different clinical questions, and a YFAS classification alone cannot determine which approach is appropriate. A patient classified mainly through craving and loss of control presents a different clinical picture from one who also reports tolerance and withdrawal, yet the two are indistinguishable in an aggregate report.

Third, pharmacological research faces the same constraint. Semaglutide reduced food cravings and improved control of eating in a laboratory trial (Blundell et al., 2017), and such findings are often cited as convergent evidence for reward-based accounts. Whether people classified mainly on tolerance and withdrawal respond differently from those classified mainly on behavioural criteria cannot be examined while treatment studies report only aggregate change.

Fourth, proposals for diagnostic recognition and regulation (Unwin et al., 2025; Gearhardt et al., 2026b) depend on what the symptom evidence shows.The Delphi consensus statement (Unwin et al., 2025), in which 37 of 40 experts agreed that UPF addiction shows the features of an SUD, records expert agreement; it is not, and does not claim to be, a synthesis of criterion-level data. Publishing the individual criteria would let proponents and critics examine the same observations and weigh the case for a food addiction diagnosis on its symptom content rather than on the frequency of a classification

4.6 A Minimum Reporting Standard for YFAS 2.0 Research

The current findings are unlikely to result from a lack of general guidance: reporting guidelines, preregistration and open data are already well established (Nosek et al., 2018). What is potentially missing is guidance specific to the YFAS 2.0, whose scoring produces several outputs that answer different questions. Because criterion-level results are generated during scoring (Gearhardt et al., 2016), following such guidance would require no additional data collection.

Table 8 proposes a minimum reporting standard. It retains the aggregate outputs that most studies already report, adds the criterion-level and interpretative information whose absence we documented, and can be checked by reviewers and editors from the manuscript alone. Two items need brief justification. Tolerance and withdrawal should be reported as separate criteria, because combining them into a single physiological index would conceal any divergence between them. Analyses should be identified as confirmatory or exploratory, because comparing 11 criteria across groups generates many possible tests. Because our results suggest that disclosure alone does not guarantee a balanced interpretation, reviewers could also be asked to check conclusions directly against the results table.

4.7 Strengths and Limitations

The audit has three main strengths. It separates two questions that require different evidence: whether a symptom profile was reported and whether the conclusions reflected the results. Every study was coded independently by two people following a documented procedure (Figure 2), with written justifications for the highest-inference judgements, and all deviations from the protocol are reported in Supplementary Table S3. Fragility indices, multiplicity corrections and benchmark sensitivity analyses show where the findings are robust and where they are most uncertain (Table 6; Supplementary Tables S3 and S5).

Perhaps the most important limitation is that our audit describes what was reported, rather than what participants experienced. In criterion-complete data, tolerance and withdrawal may be endorsed at rates comparable to other criteria, in which case the reporting convention would limit what readers can evaluate without changing the substantive picture. The absence of tolerance and withdrawal data therefore limits what the current literature allows readers to test; it is not evidence that these symptoms were rarely endorsed. Authors who judged criterion outputs unnecessary for their research question would also produce uniform omission, and distinguishing that position from an unexamined convention would require asking authors directly.

The audit therefore cannot establish that food addiction classifications are biologically invalid, that the YFAS 2.0 is circular, that participants were misclassified, or that any results were deliberately suppressed.

Our study also has several methodological limitations. First, the sample was drawn from a single database, and only titles and abstracts were examined as part of the initial search. Eligible studies not indexed or retrieved by the PubMed search may therefore have been missed, and excluding non-English studies, short forms and the children's version limits generalisation to the wider YFAS literature. Second, pre-consensus codes were not retained, so inter-rater reliability cannot be reported. The use of paired coders, written justifications, and supervisor adjudication reduces but does not remove the influence of individual judgement, particularly for conclusion nuance, the most subjective variable. Third, the protocol was posted after screening and before coding, and the confirmatory analyses included deviations and additions: the .05 benchmark for H2, the broader coding of mixed results, and the fragility and multiplicity analyses (Supplementary Table S3). Fourth, mixed results were coded as present or absent, so H2 does not distinguish a study with one non-significant finding among many significant ones from one with an even split. Fifth, the H2a, H3a and EA5 comparisons rested on small subsets of studies. H3a was based on six non-nuanced conclusions and had a fragility index of 1, so reclassifying a single conclusion would have made the result non-significant (p = .109). The 95% confidence interval for H2a, based on 10 studies, was wide (31.3% to 83.2%). EA5 compared 10 complete reporters with 59 other studies with mixed results, and its odds ratio had a 95% confidence interval from 0.60 to 9.23, too wide to estimate the association between completeness and balance with any precision. Sixth, reporting style was coded from the main text, whereas criterion completeness included supplementary material, so the two variables answer slightly different questions. Finally, we did not stratify by clinical versus community samples or by publication year, and we specified all exploratory analyses after the confirmatory tests, so they should be treated as hypothesis-generating.

5.0 Conclusion

In this study, we asked whether the published YFAS 2.0 literature allows the symptoms underlying food addiction classifications to be fully evaluated. In the 78 studies we examined, criterion profiles were generated during scoring but seldom reported, and where results were mixed, conclusions often emphasised statistically significant findings. Routine reporting of all 11 criteria, with tolerance and withdrawal shown separately, together with conclusions that represent the full pattern of results, would allow the central claims of the food addiction construct to be evaluated directly.
