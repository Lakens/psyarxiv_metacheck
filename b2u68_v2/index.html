Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

The Macarthur-Bates Communicative Development Inventories (CDI) are a set of widely used parentreport instruments for measuring young children's language development. The most widely used measures for English-speaking children are the CDIs for 8-30 months. There is a strong need for both clinical and research purpose for comparable measures for three-year-olds. The previously published U.S. English CDI-III yielded norms for 30-37 months only, due to ceiling effects, and assessed limited aspects of language. Recent adaptations for Swedish and other Scandinavian languages, have used a different design and showed strong psychometric properties for children 2.5-4 years old. The current study utilizes insights from that work to revise the CDI-III for an expanded target age range, and to incorporate more aspects of language, including morphology and metalinguistic and print awareness. Results are presented for a sample of 202 children as reported on a pilot version of the new instrument, for both the full set of items and those items selected on the basis of IRT analysis for the final instrument, and also for a preliminary norming sample of 322 children utilizing the final instrument. The CDI-IIIR appears to have good reliability and developmental sensitivity across the target age range of 30 through 48 months. A larger norming sample, and validation evidence, are essential next steps.

Parent report instruments, such as the MacArthur-Bates Communicative Development Inventories (CDIs), have proven to be highly useful measures of young children's language and communicative development (Marchman & Dale, 2023). Parents tend to be keen observers of their children and can provide valid reports of their behaviors, especially in a recognition rather than recall format, that are based on extensive observation over many contexts, rather than the brief and limited observation possible in a research or clinic visit. The breadth of observation also reduces biases due to the variable frequency of use of words and specific structures, particularly omission of low-frequency items in brief samples.

Consequently, these instruments are well suited for capturing the range of children's language knowledge and use, which benefits from observation across diverse interactional contexts. In addition, parental (or other caregiver) questionnaires do not require a trained clinician or researcher, making them a costeffective option for assessment. For these reasons, parent report measures make possible research and norming studies with far larger samples than methods that require a trained researcher or clinician and child cooperation. Indeed, the two original versions of the CDIs -the Words and Gestures (CDI:WG), for children 8-18 months, and the Words and Sentences (CDI:WS), for children 16-30 months -have grown widely in use over the last twenty years, including adaptation into over 100 languages (Laudańska et al., 2026; Marchman & Dale, 2023;Weisleder et al., 2024). However, the need for a parent report instrument that captures the language development of English-speaking three-year-olds has largely been left unmet. Here, we describe the development of a revised version of the CDI-III, the CDI-IIIR, aimed at English-speaking children from 30 to 48 months.

The original CDIs focused on capturing children's language and communication in the first and second years, enabling users to track the emergence of language, the vocabulary explosion, and the beginnings of grammar (Fenson et al., 1994; Frank et al., 2021). The success of these CDIs naturally gave rise to a desire for a comparable instrument that worked beyond the age of 30 months, when children enter a more advanced stage of language development. During the third and fourth years, children continue to exhibit growth in vocabulary, with the noticeable addition of more abstract words, including a larger number of verbs and adjectives, and nouns that describe less observable concepts such as feelings and mental states (Clark, 1993; Dunn et al., 1987; Gleitman et al., 2005). There are also tremendous advances in morphology and syntax during this period, as children use more inflectional forms and start to produce sentences with multiple clauses (Bloom et al., 1980; Bowerman, 1979; Vasilyeva et al., 2008).

Children's pronunciation tends to become clearer and more intelligible after age 3, and some three-yearolds exhibit skills associated with early literacy, such as noticing words that rhyme or identifying their initial sound (Anthony & Francis, 2005; Hustad et al., 2020). In sum, there are many advances in children's language use at this age that are desirable to capture.

Assessment in this age range is also important for practical reasons. Measures of language at or after 3 years of age have been shown to be better predictors of later language and literacy abilities than measures taken at earlier ages (Flax et al., 2009; Hammer et al., 2017; Jin et al., 2020; Lee, 2010). Hence, they are especially appropriate for measuring the effects of early experiences, such as preterm birth or exposure to environmental toxins, and for recommending specialized speech and language services to those who are not meeting the expected benchmarks (Bishop et al., 2016). In the United States, the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) Part B, mandates that schools provide appropriate services for children between 3 and 5 years of age who have developmental delays, creating a need for effective tools to evaluate language delays in preschool children.

However, there are few parent report instruments to assess language and communication in English-speaking children 3-4 years of age, and those that do exist have significant limitations (see Table 1; see also (Feltner et al., 2024).

[div-01]

For example, the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ (Bricker et al., 1999) is a developmental screener for children 4 to 60 months that is commonly used in pediatric settings, yet it includes only a broad, 5-7 question assessment of children's communication, without detailed information about vocabulary, morphosyntax, or other areas of language. In contrast, the Developmental Vocabulary Assessment for Parents (DVAP, (Libertus et al., 2013)) focuses exclusively on vocabulary (and in fact is heavily based on nouns, verbs and adjectives that are easily picturable, as it is based on the PPVT word list), and the Intelligibility in Context Scale (ICS (McLeod et al., 2012)) focuses exclusively on pronunciation/intelligibility, each leaving out key areas of language development. Finally, the General Language Screen (GLS) targets multiple areas of language, including comprehension, expression, articulation, and pragmatics, yet is available only for 36 months. Thus, most existing parent report instruments are either too broad or too narrow, in scope and/or age range, to fulfill the need for a language assessment for English-speaking preschool children (see Figure 1).

The original U.S. English CDI-III and its limitations

A CDI for 3-year-old children, the CDI-III, was developed in the late 1990s in the context of a large, longitudinal twin study in the United Kingdom, with some minor modifications later made for US English (Dionne et al., 2003; Fenson et al., 2007). The CDI-III contains a 100-item vocabulary checklist, a 12-item syntax scale developed from the complexity measure in CDI-W&S, and a third section with 12 items reflecting language use in a broad sense. Studies using the CDI-III have shown it has high internal consistency and concurrent validity with the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test-Revised, the McCarthy Scales of Children's Abilities, and the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (Feldman et al., 2005; Mercure, 1999; Oliver et al., 2002; Perra et al., 2015). Other studies have demonstrated diagnostic validity of the CDI-III in clinical contexts, including its ability to discriminate between children with and without language impairment with strong specificity and sensitivity (98.0% and 88.9% respectively) (Skarakis-Doyle et al., 2009). Lastly, the CDI-III has been used successfully in large epidemiological studies, in which it has shown associations with both genetic and environmental variables hypothesized to relate to language ability (Dionne et al., 2003; Feldman et al., 2003).

Thus, the CDI-III addresses many of the needs identified above: it has all the advantages of parent-report instruments, including ease of use in large-scale projects, measures several areas of language relevant to this age range, and has strong internal and external validity. However, a major limitation of the CDI-III is a substantial ceiling effect, especially in the domain of vocabulary. Therefore, although originally intended for children up to age 4, norms were developed and published only for children between 30 and 37 months (Fenson et al., 2007), significantly limiting its potential use.

Over the last decade, the CDI-III has been adapted to several other languages with greater success in covering a broader age range. See Appendix A for an overview of current CDI-III adaptations. There have been two main paths taken to develop these adaptations (see Šmit Brleković & Kuvač Kraljević, 2023, for an overview). The first category, including Basque, Hungarian, and Persian, were created based on the original CDI-III, in that the vocabulary list has been very broad with respect to the semantic categories included. The emphasis has been on finding individual words appropriately difficult for the target age range. The Basque adaptation of the CDI-III, the KGNZ-3, for example, was extensively adapted and modified to reflect both the cultural context and structure of this non-Indo-European language (Ezeizabarrena et al., 2013). New sections were also added to assess pronunciation, pre-reading and school abilities, narrative questions and grammatical markers. Many of these changes were motivated by the goal of developing an instrument appropriate for children up to 50 months. Ezeizabarrena et al (2013) reported steady increments for vocabulary production through 42 months and for sentence complexity through 50 months of age. Thus, the ceiling effect for vocabulary was only partially resolved.

Similarly, the Hungarian adaptation showed a ceiling effect for vocabulary at around 39-42 months (Kas et al., 2022).

A different model was followed by the Swedish adaptation (Eriksson, 2017). Here, the vocabulary list focuses on four specific semantic categories: food-related words, body-related words, cognitive words and emotion words. These categories were selected based on evidence that they are developmentally appropriate, show substantial growth during the target age range, and relate to themes that are central in the lives of most children. To evaluate morphology and grammar, a section of 10 items asks about the child's use of complex phrases and another section of eight items queries the child's use of grammatical markers. A section of seven items on metalinguistic awareness asks caregivers to comment on their child's phonological and orthographic awareness as well as their awareness of the existence of other languages. Finally, one question asks about children's pronunciation/intelligibility. This version was normed on a nationally representative Swedish sample for children up to 48 months of age, very substantially reducing the ceiling effect observed in the original CDI III.

Several other adaptations have since followed the Swedish model, both with respect to the structure of the vocabulary checklist and the incorporation of scales for additional aspects of language.

They include those for Norwegian (Holm et al., 2023), Finnish (Stolt, 2023), European Portuguese (Cadime et al., 2021), Croatian (Šmit Brleković & Kuvač Kraljević, 2023), Estonian (Tulviste & Schults, 2020), and Slovenian (Penko et al., 2026). The Mexican (Jackson-Maldonado et al., 2024) and Arabic (Abdelwahab et al., 2025) adaptations combine elements from the Swedish adaptation and the original English CDI-III. Although the existing reports differ with respect to design, age range, and validation measures, overall the results are positive and similar to those for Swedish. Furthermore, five studies have reported concurrent validity of these adaptations, showing agreement between the CDI-III and the New Reynell Development Language Scales (Penko et al., 2026; Šmit Brleković & Kuvač Kraljević, 2023; Tulviste & Schults, 2020), the Griffiths Mental Development Scales (Cadime et al., 2021), and spontaneous language sampling (Holm et al., 2023). This research thus provides overall evidence that the Swedish CDI-III offers a useful model of a CDI for this age range.

The current project

The purpose of the current project was to take advantage of the successful work done in other languages to revise the CDI-III to achieve two aims: (1) expand the useable target age range, and (2) incorporate more aspects of language. The revision leaned most heavily on the model used for the Swedish CDI-III and other Scandinavian forms, given their success. The present paper describes the development of the revised CDI-III, the CDI-IIIR, and reports on a pilot study to evaluate its psychometric properties.

Method

Development of the Pilot CDI-IIIR

A working group was convened for the development of a revised CDI-III, to be titled the CDI- The largest and most central component, as usual for early language measures, was Vocabulary. In the Swedish and other recent versions, there were four subcategories: Food-related words; Body-related words; Words about thinking; and Words about feeling. The present working group wished to include a new category of time-, space-and quantity-related words, so Words about thinking and Words about feeling were combined into a single category, and the new category served as the fourth. However, these categories primarily served to help parents recall related words. Norms for the individual vocabulary categories were not developed in this project, or in most other CDI-III projects.

The working group then contacted five researchers (see Acknowledgements) with extensive experience in early language assessment to review this first version, and a number of changes were made.

The final pilot version is available upon request to the first author of this paper.

The pilot study

The pilot study, to determine the developmental appropriateness of the measures, as well as to enable final item selection, was conducted in the fall of 2025, using Prolific, an online research platform, to recruit, manage, and compensate participants. Potential participants were identified as adults with children 28 to 50 months of age who currently reside in primarily English-speaking homes in the US.

Eligible participants who agreed to participate were then directed to Web-CDI (https://webcdi.org/ (DeMayo et al., 2021). Participants were first asked to provide informed consent with a waiver of documentation and to complete basic demographic and health questions. Participants who did so were then asked to complete the CDI-IIIR form. Participation was completely voluntary and participants could discontinue their participation at any time. Upon completion of the CDI-IIIR, participants were redirected back to Prolific where they received a payment of $6. This study was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (IRB), Michael C. Frank, Principal Investigator.

Pilot study sample

A total of 227 caregivers provided information on the language of their children between 29 and 49 months. The additional month's data was included to improve curve-fitting estimates at the extremes of the age range. The sample included 106 males and 121 females. The following exclusionary criteria were applied: (1) born at least 3 weeks early and birthweight < 5 lbs, 8 oz (approx. 2500 grams), and (2) significant exposure to a language other than English (> 16 hours/week, based on hours/day x days of week). This resulted in a final sample of 202 children.

Pilot study results

We first report analyses of the "full pilot" dataset, including age trends, sex differences, correlations among subtests, and reliability. Where appropriate, these results are compared with those for the original CDI-III. Following final item selection described in the next section, we report the corresponding analyses for the "trimmed pilot" dataset, that is, only the items included in the final CDI-IIIR.

An initial analysis determined that the pronunciation items did not show a developmental trend (r with age = .07), and that interrelation among the items was so weak (coefficient alpha = .256) as to preclude construction of a scale. We conclude that these items, while of interest, are not primarily developmental measures. It should be noted that many, perhaps most, CDI-III adaptations do not include pronunciation, though a valid scale for this aspect of language would have considerable value. We have chosen to follow what some of the other forms have done, which is to include two questions, which clinicians and others often ask, about general intelligibility of the child, similar to those in the Intelligibility in Context Scale (ICS; McLeod et al., 2012). Specifically, the form includes just two of the questions: Can you understand the words your child says (except when your child is saying nonsense for fun)? And Can people outside your home/family understand the words your child says (except when your child is saying nonsense for fun)? In the remainder of this document analyses are reported for the remaining five subscales.

Figures 2 through 7 depict the age trend for each scale; the data in these figures is restricted to the target age range of 30-48 months. Given the small sample for this pilot study and the primary interest in age trends, the figures present empirical means for two-month age windows, There is considerable age-to-age variability, but there is generally a steady increase over the target age range, except for 46-48 month olds. This trend constitutes a substantial improvement over the original CDI-III.

The lack of an increase in the highest age range does not appear to be a ceiling effect, as the scores are well below the maximum, which is in fact attained by several children; instead, it appears to be sampling variability, which can be large with a relatively small sample such as this one.

Table 1 also includes Cronbach alpha as a measure of reliability for each measure except General level, which has only one question. The value of .99 for Vocabulary is comparable to the .98 value for the original CDI-III Vocabulary (Marchman et al., 2023). The value of .94 for Grammar is also comparable to that for Sentences on the CDI-III, which is the most similar scale on that measure. The lower value for Talking about print is likely due to a very short (4 item) list, and diversity of items which may not be fully unidimensional.

The effect size is reported as the percent of variance accounted for relative to the total variance after age was eliminated. In this relatively small pilot sample, only Vocabulary exhibited a significant sex difference, with higher vocabularies in females and an effect size of 2.2% of the variance. In the larger norming sample for the CDI-III (Marchman et al., 2023), all three components yielded small but significant prediction by sex of 2.0%, 2.2%, and 1.7%, respectively.

Development of the CDI-IIIR from the pilot study results

Final item selection was predominantly based on IRT analysis for each scale. A two-parameter (2PL) model was identified as the most appropriate. Item decisions were based primarily on the goals of maximizing the uniformity of distribution of item difficulty to ensure sensitivity at the extremes, and within that framework, maximizing item discrimination. We also tried to minimize potential ambiguity to parents as well as items with excessive cultural specificity. These additional issues beyond IRT parameters led to replacement for about 10 of the items initially selected based on the statistics alone, generally using the item with next highest item discrimination in that difficulty category. The overall framework of the final form, with example items for each subsection, is shown in Appendix B.

Following item selection, the "trimmed pilot" scales, which constitute the final CDI-IIIR, were analyzed with the same procedure reported for the "full pilot" above. The results are presented in Tables 3 and 4. Graphs for these results are nearly identical in pattern with Figures 2 3 4 5 6 7, though the scores are reduced in absolute level by the item selection for the trimmed version, and are not included here. A comparison of Tables 1 and 3 confirms the essential similarity of the trimmed pilot results to those of the full pilot with respect to correlation with age and sex differences, despite the notably shorter form.

Reliability is slightly lower for the two syntax measures, and Talking about Language, but generally satisfactory. Tables 2 and 4 report very similar levels of intercorrelation between the four primary language subscales, sometimes higher in one sample, sometimes the other. They are consistently modestly lower for the two "meta" scales, likely reflecting their brevity.

The norming study

On the basis of the preceding, largely positive results from the pilot study and selection of final items, we have begun the norming study for the CDI-IIIR. For measures in the fourth year of life, twomonth age windows are typical and will probably be used in final norming results. For the period 30-48 months, this would entail 9 such windows plus a one-month window. Assuming a target of 40 children of each sex for each age category, this would require a total of 800 children, although more would be better.

Also highly desirable would be a norming sample less skewed toward higher maternal education/SES than in the pilot study, and more ethnically diverse. This led us to inviting child language researchers to use the CDI-IIIR on Web-CDI, as well as using Prolific for recruitment. Announcements were made on info-childes and other email lists (e.g., the Cognitive Development Society), and at the Symposium on Research in Child Language Disorders (SRCLD), informing researchers of the availability of the CDI-IIIR, and offering the use of Web-CDI for administration and scoring at no charge in exchange for sharing of anonymized data.

Preliminary norming study sample

As of 5/3/2026, we have completed the initial phase of collecting a norming sample of language assessment of children between 30 and 48 months inclusive. (Note the restriction to the actual target range for the instrument in these analyses.) These participants were all recruited through Prolific. We are close to having exhausted recruitment through Prolific for our inclusion criteria, and are seeking to obtain additional data through ongoing research projects. For sample description in this preliminary report, actual age in months (rather than two-month age categories) will be used. 408 parents of children in this age range began the questionnaire, but 55 did not complete it, resulting in an initial sample of data for 370 children, of whom 194 are female, 174 are male, and 2 for whom parents chose not to report. Maternal education was relatively high in this sample, as expected; median education was 16 years, and only 14.9% had 12 years or less of education.

The following exclusionary criteria were then applied successively: (1) born < 37 weeks gestational age and birthweight < 5.5 lbs, approx. 2500 grams, resulting in exclusion of 13 children; (2) significant exposure to a language other than English (> 16 hours/week -derived by taking hours/day * days of week), resulting in exclusion of an additional 29 children; (3) reported hearing loss (reported ear infections or placement of tubes was not exclusionary), resulting in exclusion of an additional 2 children; (4) report of known developmental and/or genetic disorders and/or disease likely to result in language impairment (e.g., Down Syndrome, SD, DLS), resulting in an exclusion of an additional 4 children.

This resulted in a final sample of 322 children. Note that the method of successive elimination means that no information is presented here concerning exclusion for multiple reasons.

Discussion

The present paper is essentially a progress report on an assessment project that is far from complete.

Nonetheless, the results of the pilot study and the initial phase of norming for the CDI-IIIR are very encouraging. Of particular interest is the developmental sensitivity beyond that of the original U.S.

CDI-III, for which a ceiling effect in vocabulary limited published norms to 30-37 months (Fenson et al., 2007). Every CDI-IIIR scale increased steadily with age across most of the 30-to 48-month target range, and the four oral-language scales did so without approaching their maxima. We attribute this improvement primarily to adopting the Swedish model (Eriksson, 2017), in which the vocabulary checklist is restricted to a few semantic fields chosen for their developmental range rather than assembled for maximum breadth. Scores did level off between 46 and 48 months in both samples; because group means at those ages remained well below the maximum (which only a few children reached) this most likely reflects sampling variability in sparsely populated age cells rather than a true ceiling, but a larger sample will be needed to settle the question.

As is typical for CDI measures, reliability is a major strength. Internal consistency was excellent for Vocabulary (α = .98-.99) and good for Grammar and Sentence Complexity (α = .87-.95), comparable to the corresponding scales on the original CDI-III (Marchman et al., 2023). The two brief metalinguistic scales were less reliable (Talking about Language α = .70-.78; Talking about Print α = .64-.70) and are best regarded as short indicators of an emerging domain. These properties were essentially unchanged when IRT-based item selection reduced the pilot item set by roughly half, indicating that the final, shorter form retains the measurement characteristics of the pilot version. Sex and socioeconomic effects were small and in line with prior CDI work: a female advantage reached significance only for Vocabulary (about 2% of age-residualized variance), and maternal education was reliably related only to Sentence Complexity (about 2%), an association likely attenuated by the restricted range of maternal education in these samples. The pilot pronunciation items neither cohered nor tracked age and were dropped as a scale, though two global intelligibility questions were retained for their clinical utility.

There were also substantial intercorrelations among the subscales, with strong correlations between the three oral language scales (Vocabulary, Grammar, and Sentence Complexity) and weaker to non-significant correlations between the oral language and the metalinguistic scales. This pattern is consistent with what has been reported for other CDI-IIIs, which report strong intercorrelations between most of the subsections except for pronunciation and metalinguistics (Holm et al., 2023; Brieković & Kraljević, 2023; Tulviste & Shults, 2020). Notably, the single General level item correlated nearly as highly with the other oral-language scales as those scales correlated with one another, supporting its use as an efficient global screen.

A further perspective on the instrument comes from comparison with other CDI-IIIs, especially Eriksson's Swedish CDI-III, with respect to correlations with age. Table 8 presents comparable effect size results (percent variance) for comparable measures on the CDI-IIIR and the Swedish measure; note that comparison with the original U.S. CDI-III is not appropriate here, because it covers a much narrower age range. In the preliminary norming sample, age accounted for 13.2% of the variance in CDI-IIIR Vocabulary, compared with 24.1% for Swedish. The Swedish Syntax scale combines items that we keep separate as Grammar and Sentence Complexity; age accounted for 20.3% of the variance in that combined scale, compared with 14.2% and 11.4% for the two CDI-IIIR scales. Comparable figures for Finnish are about 23% for both Vocabulary and Language Structure (Stolt, 2023). Most other CDI-III publications do not report these values. The somewhat smaller age effects for the CDI-IIIR are not unexpected: U.S. samples typically show greater variability in health, environmental exposures, and access to services than the more homogeneous European samples on which these adaptations were normed (e.g., Bradbury et al., 2015).

Several steps remain essential before the value of the CDI-IIIR for research and clinical work can be confirmed. For norming, a larger and more representative sample is needed, particularly with respect to sex and ethnicity. Following the approach used for the CDI: Words & Gestures and CDI: Words & Sentences norms (Marchman et al., 2023), it is highly desirable to have enough representation across maternal education and race/ethnicity to permit weighting, so that population parameters can be estimated more accurately relative to the U.S. population. Validation studies are equally important: concurrent validity against other language measures for this age range -both structured assessments and language-sample measures -and longitudinal studies relating CDI-IIIR scores to earlier and later CDI scores. Finally, the sensitivity and specificity of the CDI-IIIR for identifying language delay relative to established clinical measures will matter greatly to many users; we have already received several expressions of interest in carrying out such research.
