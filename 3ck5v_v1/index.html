Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Introduction

There is growing recognition that societies today are facing a 'polycrisis': a complex web of intersecting social, economic, political, and environmental pressures [1] [2] [3] . From the legacy of the COVID-19 pandemic and geopolitical instability to increasingly frequent extreme climatic events, these pressures have exposed failings in leadership and begun to erode public trust and social cohesion [4] [5] [6] [7] . Yet their consequences may depend not only on the objective events and conditions they experience, but also on how they are perceived. Through direct and indirect experiences, people develop internal models [8] [9] [10] of the social worlds in which they liverepresentations of social groups and institutions around them and how they function. These Social World Models may be fundamental to understanding how people think, feel, act and interact and, in turn, how individuals and societies navigate periods of social change, uncertainty, and adversity 11.

The idea of Social World Models draws on both psychological and sociological theory.

Seminal psychological theories, including Bowlby's attachment theory 12 and schema-based accounts of social cognition 13 , propose that individuals develop internal representations of their social worlds through repeated experiences and interactions 10 . These representations encode expectations about the self, others, and social relationships, guiding how subsequent social experiences are perceived, interpreted, and navigated 14 . Sociological theory extends this from the relationship between individuals to the relationship between individuals and the wider social environment in which they live [15] [16] [17] . Notably, Coleman's 'boat' offers a framework for understanding how macro-level conditions -such as institutional functioning and features of communities -shape human behaviour through mechanisms operating at the individual level 17 . Rather than assuming a direct relationship between macro-level conditions and collective outcomes, the framework is anchored in reductionism by specifying that that macro-level conditions influence macro level outcomes via individual level mechanisms (or 'pathways') 18 . One such mechanism is how individuals' experience and perceive their wider social environment, including social groups and institutions, which then influence how individuals act and interact. These individual actions can then be aggregated to produce macro-level patterns and collective behaviours. The pathways connecting macro level conditions to individuals, however, are poorly understood 19 . For example, it is unclear why and how the same macro level conditions may be experienced very differently by different individuals, and whether it is these perceptions, or the objective conditions themselves, that are more important in determining individual behaviour and, ultimately, collective outcomes.

Despite its theoretical importance, the idea of Social World Models -internal models of our social world -have yet to be comprehensively operationalised or measured as a coherent construct. Instead, different disciplines have focused on specific dimensions that capture only one aspect of how people perceive their social worlds. For example, trust has become a commonly studied predictor of human behaviour, including political and civic participation and compliance with public health initiatives, such as vaccine uptake, as well as climate action. In the wake of recent political instability and the COVID-19 pandemic, research has increasingly focused on the fragility of public trust and its role in shaping how individuals engage with specific institutions and with society as a whole [20] [21] [22] . Social cohesion and belonging have similarly attracted attention as important constructs for understanding how well communities function and adapt ('community resilience') 5 ; especially in the face of emerging challenges, including rapid demographic change and migration, and the growing influence of online social environments 4, 23, 24 . Other literatures, such as criminology, have focused on perceptions of institutional legitimacy and effectiveness 25, 26 . Each of these constructs capture a different aspect of how individuals perceive social groups and institutions, and each has been linked to important behavioral outcomes including civic participation, mental health and wellbeing, and violence [27] [28] [29] [30] . But, because these constructs are typically conceptualised and measured in isolation, and often studied in disciplinary silos, little is known about how these different perceptions relate to one another, or how they may come together to form an comprehensive internal model that influences how individuals act and interact within their wider social world.

A second source of fragmentation relates to the target of these perceptions. That is, prior studies have typically measured each construct in relation to a single target, namely social groups or institutions. Taking the same examples, studies have examined: trust in scientists and science, belonging to a community, and the legitimacy of procedural justice. Existing research on trust has highlighted critical differences in the targets of the perceptions: in general, people do not simply trust or distrust: they trust particular people, social groups, and institutions. For instance, the same individual may trust their family but not their neighbours, trust their neighbourhood but not the police, or trust the healthcare system while distrusting the criminal justice system [31] [32] [33] . Studies have also shown that trust often clusters along specific dimensions, such that people may have high generalised interpersonal trust but low institutional trust, and vice versa. Despite the known importance of the specificity of targets for trust, the same principle has not been widely extended to other perception constructs.

Perceptions of belonging, legitimacy, fairness, and protection may similarly vary according to who or what is being perceived. Nevertheless, current measures and studies of perceptions of the social world are poorly placed to disentangle differences across social groups and institutions 34 . As a result, it is unclear how perceptions may cluster across different targets or how the interaction between different perceptions and targets are patterned and, possibly, interrelated. Measuring multiple perceptions concurrently across multiple social targets is therefore needed to unpack these sources of variation and establish how perceptions of the social world are organised and interconnected.

In this paper, we present the 'Social Matrix', an instrument designed to simultaneously and efficiently measure multiple perceptions across a range of social groups and institutions within a common framework. We systematically developed and validated the Social Matrix through critical review, expert engagement, and focus groups. We then piloted and tested the final version of the Social Matrix among participants resident in Brazil to explore the clustering of items and determine profiles of social perceptions. Rather than treating perceptions of the social world as isolated constructs tied to specific social groups or institutions, the Social Matrix provides a unifying framework for conceptualising and measuring Social World Models. In doing so, we aim to provide a foundation for advancing macro-micro theory and empirical research on how individuals perceive and interact with their wider social environments, and for investigating the mechanisms through which macro level conditions shape individual behaviour and, in turn, explain variation across societies.

Methods

We developed candidate items (literature review and expert review in English and Portuguese) and psychometrically validated (focus groups and pilot study in Portuguese) a common framework and measurement instrument, called the 'Social Matrix', that simultaneously measures key individual level perceptions of social groups and institutions (see Figure S1 for project overview). Throughout, the Social Matrix was operationalised as a cross-tabulation (i.e., matrix) of perception constructs (columns) by social groups and institutions (rows) (Figure S2).

Setting, study population and recruitment process

The research was conducted in Brazil, with the instrument development and validation process informed by the local context of Pelotas, a city in Rio Grande do Sul, southern Brazil.

A key motivation of this research was to develop an instrument suitable for integration into the five ongoing Pelotas Birth Cohort Studies (1982, 1993, 2004, 2015, and 2026), providing opportunities for future longitudinal research on social perceptions across the life course.

Accordingly, focus groups were conducted in Pelotas to ensure that the Social Matrix was culturally appropriate and comprehensible across different age groups, while the subsequent online pilot study recruited participants residing across Brazil.

The pilot study was a cross-sectional online survey that relied on age-stratified convenience samples across three distinct age groups (11-14, 15-18, and 19-59 years, corresponding to the different waves of assessment in the Pelotas birth cohort studies). Recruitment was done in two phases between October 2025 and September 2026. Participants were required to reside in Brazil, be fluent in Portuguese, have access to an internet-connected device to complete the online survey via REDCap 35 , and have no prior participation in the focus group validation phase or the Pelotas Birth Cohort Studies due to plans to incorporate the Social Matrix into prospective data collection waves. A sample size calculation to detect medium effect size (Cohen's w = 0.3) in the associations between the Social Matrix latent response profiles and socioeconomic variables, considering a chi-squared distribution and 12 degrees of freedom, indicated that a minimum of 193 participants per age group was required to achieve 80% power at a 5% significance level. Participants were invited via telephone calls and social media messages. A snowball sampling was applied, asking participants to indicate colleagues or friends with aged 11 to 59 years, as well as open dissemination using paid traffic on Instagram was carried out. We sent individual WhatsApp invitations to 415 children (11-14 years), 399 adolescents (15-18 years), and 533 adults (19-59 years). In line with ethical regulations, participants were not paid for their participation but, to encourage participation, randomly won prizes were offered for survey completion. We offered six prize draws per age group and prizes included books, umbrellas, smartwatches, boardgames, suitcases, and bedside lamps, summing up around 1,200 BRL.

Ethics and inclusion statement

The current study received ethics approval from the Research Ethics Committees of the Federal University of Pelotas's School of Medicine (#89340225.7.0000.5317), and methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant ethical regulations. This included all participants providing informed consent and, for participants aged under 18 years, informed consent was provided by their parents or legal guardians. We collected only the information essential for psychometric validation, which was fully anonymized at the point of data collection. We did not pre-register the aims and hypotheses of the current study due to its exploratory nature. This study took into account local research in understanding Brazil in general, and Pelotas specifically, by including researchers (expert review) and participants (focus groups) from the local culture to translate the survey, adapt the questions to Brazilian Portuguese and the local context, and interpret the results. The authors discussed roles and authorship from the outset of the project.

Candidate constructs and item development

We critically reviewed the literature to identify commonly measured and studied perception constructs across disciplines -that is, what perceptions have emerged as important constructs in relation to specific social groups and institutions in the study of mental health and social problems. We also looked to identify sub-components of key constructs, as well as overlap and groupings between perception constructs and sub-components. Based on these findings, we developed four primary groups relating to the key perception constructs of trust, values, competency, and social cohesion (Table S1). Further informed by the review, we developed a conceptual model detailing the interconnectedness of key perceptions and their core components (Figure S3). We then identified candidate items for potential inclusion that measured key perception constructs in relation to relevant institutions and social groups, populating the first iterations of the 'Social Matrix' in English for the three age groups 11-14, 15-18, and 19-59 years to capture different developmental stages (original version 1, Tables S2-S4).

Expert review

Sixteen field experts (7 international, 9 Brazilian) independently examined and provided indepth feedback on the version 1 of the Social Matrix in English to ensure face, content, and construct validity in June-July 2025 (Tables S2-S4). Specifically, using anoymised REDCap Survey software, experts were asked a series of questions relating to: i) the importance of perception constructs; ii) measurement items and question wording; iii) the social groups and institutions for each perception construct; and iv) additional considerations, including the transportability to a Brazilian context. Example questions included: i) trust is an important construct for understanding social problems, such as an individual's risk of violent behaviour and poor mental health symptoms; ii) "How much do you personally trust" is appropriate wording for measuring a person's level of trust? ; iii) perceptions about law and order are important in shaping social problems; and iv) "the police" is appropriate wording for measuring a person's perception of law and order in Brazil? Since we adopted a primarily quantitative approach to validation, questions were asked on a Likert-type five-point scale with the anchors of strongly disagree to strongly agree. We offered an additional free-text box response option when experts disagreed with our proposed constructs and measurement items.

We analysed all expert feedback, identifying recurrent themes, and integrated feedback and improvements to create version 2 of the Social Matrix. All feedback and improvements were shared with experts with the option to provide additional feedback on the updated version 2 of the Social Matrix (Figure S4).

Focus groups

In July 2025, we recruited participants for the focus groups using convenience sampling.

Eligibility criteria included residence in Pelotas, Brazil, to facilitate in-person discussions.

Since the aim was to develop a measurement instrument to be embedded in five existing sequential birth cohort studies (1982, 1993, 2004, 2015, and 2026 Pelotas birth cohort studies), version 2 of the Social Matrix was translated into Brazilian Portuguese and reviewed by the target population via focus groups to assess item comprehension, appropriateness, and relevance. Individuals who were already members of the existing Pelotas birth cohort studies were not eligible. While participants were recruited for the focus groups through convenience sampling, we purposively recruited participants from different sociodemographic backgrounds and age groups (Table S5). Focus groups were delivered separately for each age group. Three adapted versions of the 'Social Matrix' were considered for the age groups: 11-14 years (sample size, n=7), 15-18 years (n=7), and 19-59 years (n=6). In line with ethical regulations, participants were not paid for their participation but received reimbursement for participation-related expenses, including transportation.

Prior to the focus group discussions, version 2 of Social Matrix was administered in person by trained interviewers to each participant. After each item, participants were asked about clarity and comprehension along a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree). They were also asked whether the item required modification (yes/no) and, when applicable, invited to suggest changes. Additionally, an open-ended question explored the meaning attributed to each item, allowing the identification of potential ambiguities or interpretations that differed from those intended by the authors. Three 60minute focus groups were then conducted in-person in Brazilian Portuguese at the Center for Epidemiological Research of the Federal University of Pelotas (UFPel), one for each age group (11-14 years, 15-18 years, and 19-59 years), to further explore and discuss the Social Matrix.

Two trained psychologists ran the focus group sessions using a semi-structured format to ensure key questions were asked. Questions included: "Do you think people in your age group would understand these questions?" and "What does [trust/legitimacy/safety/fairness/belonging] mean in relation to each of these groups and institutions?". Participants were also tested on their ability to distinguish between related social groups and institutions, such as the differences between school, education, and teachers; and different levels of government. For the 11-to-14 age group, we used developmentally tailored visual prompts to facilitate discussions. All sessions were recorded and subsequently transcribed. Overall, the focus groups found that each age group showed a good understanding of the items and constructs (see Focus Group Results in Supplement).

Feedback and modifications included participants considering "has the right to make rules" to be more appropriate wording than "has the right to tell you what to do" for measuring legitimacy, while children reported that they did not understand the scientists and armed forces, therefore these social targets were removed for this age group. All focus group feedback was integrated to develop and refine a third and final iteration, version 3, of the Social Matrix, see Tables S2-S4 for modifications following each evaluation.

Pilot test of psychometric validity

We conducted a pilot test study of the instrument within a cross-sectional online survey to evaluate the criterion validity of the final (version 3, Tables S6-S12) Social Matrix. To assess criterion validity, we tested its correlation with previously established scales measuring similar or theoretically related constructs. We further assessed inter-item correlations within the Social Matrix and then explored latent response profiles to the Social Matrix, and variation by socioeconomic status. The survey was administered in REDCap Survey software and included sections on sociodemographic characteristics, the age-relevant Social Matrix (version 3), and reference scales used to test convergent validity. We selected instruments previously adapted or developed in the Brazilian context, appropriate for the age groups of the Social Matrix versions, and theoretically related to the perception and social target combination. Instruments could not be identified for all Social Matrix items, owing to the breadth of the constructs assessed and the scarcity of measures meeting these criteria. Some instruments were linked to more than one item, depending on the construct they measure. For clarity, the instruments are described below according to the social group or institution of the Matrix to which they refer. Table S13 details the 15 reference scales, including their psychometric properties and the theoretically correlated Social Matrix items. Additionally, Table S14 describes distributions of reference scales and their internal consistency across each age-specific pilot sample.

Family.

For the item on belonging with family (all age versions), we applied the Social Connectedness Scale, which assesses the subjective perception of positive and meaningful affective bonds with other people 36 , theorising a positive association. For legitimacy of family (11-14 and 15-18 years), we used two questions from the São Paulo Legal Socialization Study (SPLSS, NEV/USP) 37 , theorising a positive association.

Friends. For belonging with friends (all age versions), we applied the Social Connectedness Scale, theorising a positive association. For trust in and belonging with friends (19-59 years), we used the McGill Friendship Questionnaire -Friendship Functions (MFQ-FF), which assesses the quality of friendship relationships 38 , theorising a positive association. For belonging with friends (19-59 years), we also applied the UCLA Loneliness Scale, which assesses the subjective experience of loneliness 39 , theorising a negative association.

Neighbourhood. For trust in, security with, and belonging with neighbours (all age versions), we used the Sense of Community Index, which assesses belonging, identification, and emotional connection with the community 40 , theorising a positive association. For neighbourhood security (19-59 years), we applied the Satisfaction and Safety in the Neighborhood scale, which assesses satisfaction with the place of residence and the perception of protection against violence and other threats 41 , theorising a positive association.

School and educational institutions.

We used items from the SPLSS (NEV/USP) 37 , theorising positive associations in all cases: four questions on teacher legitimacy for legitimacy of school (11-14 and 15-18 years); four questions on teachers' procedural justice for fairness of school and educational institutions (all age versions); and five items on school belonging for belonging with school (11-14 and 15-18 years).

Police.

For legitimacy of police (all age versions), we used three questions from the SPLSS 37 , and for fairness of police (19-59 years), four questions from the same study, theorising positive associations. For trust in, legitimacy of, fairness of, and effectiveness of police (all age versions), we applied the Perceived Institutional Legitimacy measure, specifically the dimension of police legitimacy (dignified and respectful treatment, confidence in police competence, and consistent application of rules) 42 , theorising a positive association.

Politicians and governments.

For effectiveness of municipal and federal governments (15-18 and 19-59 years), we used the Legitimacy of Law and Social System dimension of the Perceived Institutional Legitimacy measure, which considers the representativeness of social values in laws, the use of laws as a tool of control by those in power, and the capacity of laws to protect people's interests 42 , theorising a positive association. For legitimacy, fairness, and effectiveness of politicians (11-14 years) and of municipal and federal governments (15-18 and 15-59 years), we applied the Anomie (breakdown of leadership factor) of the Perception of Anomie Scale (PAS), which assesses the perception of leaders and institutions as illegitimate or ineffective 26 , theorising a negative association. Finally, for trust in, fairness of, and effectiveness of municipal and federal governments (19-59 years), we used four questions on government integrity from the OECD Trust Survey, which assess the perception of probity, ethical conduct, and the resilience of public institutions to corruption, favouritism, and undue influence 43 , theorising a positive association.

Statistical analysis

We conducted complete case analyses to test convergent validity. First, we compared sociodemographic characteristics between complete and incomplete responders using Fisher's exact test for heterogeneity. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rho) was used to test convergent validity due to the skewed distribution of most Social Matrix items and reference scales, and the presence of non-linear relationships. 95% confidence intervals for rho were estimated using 10,000 bootstrap resamples. In line with prior literature, we interpreted a significant correlation of rho>0.50 as strong and rho≥0.30 as moderate 44.

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using all Social Matrix items simultaneously as continuous indicators to identify distinct subgroups of individuals based on their patterns of social perceptions. Models with different numbers of latent profiles were estimated and compared to determine the optimal profile solution. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better relative model fit, and entropy, with higher values indicating greater classification accuracy. In addition to these statistical criteria, the selection of the final model considered the interpretability and theoretical relevance of the identified profiles, the size of each profile, and the stability of the solution. Subsequently, unadjusted associations between the profiles and sociodemographic variables (skin colour, sex, family income tercile and education level) were assessed using Fisher's exact test for heterogeneity. All analyses were performed separately for each age group (11-14, 15-18, 19-59 years), and conducted in Stata (version 19), and R (version 4.6.1).

Results

The Supplement shows details of the development of the final Social Matrix questionnaire, from theoretical review, through item identification, expert review, and focus group results and adaptations arising from these processes.

A total of 784 participants piloted the final version of the Social Matrix (19-59 years, n = 377; 15-18 years, n = 210; and 11-14 years, n = 197, Table 1). Most participants self-reported to be White (range: 71.5%-85.3%), and, while there was a higher proportion of women (62.1%) among the adult age group (19-59y), gender was well balanced among the younger two age groups (range: 52.4%-52.8%). The adolescent and adult samples had relatively uniformly distributed proportions of family income, though higher family earnings were disproportionately represented among the youngest age group (11-14y). Higher levels of education were evident among the adult sample, where 56.8% had completed college education. No significant differences in sociodemographic characteristics were observed between complete and incomplete responders to the pilot survey (Table S15).

Criterion validity

Social Matrix perception constructs (trust, fairness, legitimacy, security, effectiveness, belonging) showed consistent associations with the corresponding reference scales across age groups, with all 62 tested correlations being statistically significant (p<0.001). However, the strength of these associations varied across perception constructs reflecting different levels of convergent validity. Broadly similar patterns were observed across age groups, with associations typically strongest among the adult age group, which are reported in the main text henceforth (and results for other age groups available in the Supplement). Full correlation coefficients for all age groups and p-values are reported in Tables S16-S21.

There was strongest evidence of criterion validity for trust, fairness, effectiveness and belonging (Figure 1), where the correlations with pre-existing reference scales were primarily moderate (rho≥0.30) to strong (rho>0.50). For example, trust in, and belonging to, friends was strongly associated with pre-existing measures of friendship functions (trust: rho=0.61; p<0.001; belonging rho=0.60; p<0.001), while trust and fairness in relation to the police were moderately to strongly associated with pre-existing measures of legitimacy of the police (fairness: rho=0.51; p<0.001; trust rho=0.47; p<0.001). Effectiveness was also moderately to strongly associated with theoretically relevant constructs using pre-existing measures, including legitimacy of police (rho=0.47; p<0.001) and a subscale of effectiveness of the federal government from the perceived anomie scale (rho=-0.53; p<0.001). Security yielded modest support for criterion validity with moderate associations with pre-existing reference scales, though due to the novelty to the construct and item measure the validation constructs were more adjacently related. For example, security in your neighborhood was moderately associated with sense of community index (rho=0.39; p<0.001). On the other hand, legitimacy yielded weak support with only weak associations with related constructs and similar measurement items. Notably, our measure of legitimacy of the police was significantly yet only modestly associated with two pre-existing measures of police legitimacy: rho=0.28, p<0.001 and SPLSS rho=0.23, p<0.001.

There was little evidence that the strength of associations with reference scales depended on the social group or institution being assessed. With the exception of municipal government, all examined social targets --including family, friends, neighbours, police and federal government --showed at least one strong correlation with a theoretically relevant reference scale. This suggests that evidence of convergent validity did not consistently vary by social groups or institutions. Instead, the strength of evidence differed across specific constructsocial target combinations. Figure 1 presents the 27 tested correlations between selected Social Matrix items and reference scales among adults aged 19-59 years, and Figures S5 and S6 show the criterion validity correlations tested among children and adolescents, respectively.

Inter-item correlations within the Social Matrix

When examining inter-item correlations within the Social Matrix, the overall pattern provided little evidence of substantial redundancy between Social Matrix items. Extremely high interitem correlations (rho ≥ 0.80) were rare across all three age groups, occurring for 3 of 1,485 correlations among participants aged 19-59 years, 5 of 1,540 among those aged 15-18 years, and 1 of 595 among those aged 11-14 years (red squares in Figures S7-S9). Stronger associations were clustered among perceptions of closely related social groups and institutions. For social groups, perceptions of friends and neighbours showed strong correlations, irrespective of the perception construct (i.e., trust vs fairness vs legitimacy).

There was also evidence of clustering for specific institutions independent of the perception construct, including strong associations between education and public health institutions, police and the military, and the municipal and federal government (Figure 2 for the 19-59year age group, and Figures S10-S11 for the younger age groups). When looking by social target (Figure 3 for the 19-59-year age group, and Figures S12-S13 for the younger age groups), the pattern of associations between perception constructs meaningfully varied. While legitimacy was generally the most weakly associated perception construct, the strength of its interrelatedness varied by social target. For example, trust, fairness, security, and belonging were highly associated for family, and legitimacy was weakly associated with each of these other perception constructs (rho range: 0.29-0.36). However, for the military, legitimacy was consistently moderated associated with the other perception constructs of trust, fairness, effectiveness, and security (rho range: 0.41-0.48).

Latent profile analysis

Latent profile analyses for each of the three age groups were conducted to identify distinct subgroups of individuals based on their patterns of social perceptions. These analyses identified a four-class model as optimal across all three age groups, based on higher entropy and diminishing improvements in the AIC and BIC goodness-of-fit indicators with the addition of further classes (Table S22). The average posterior probabilities of profile membership were very high across all four classes within each of the three age groups (all ≥ 0.97) (Table S23). In the 19-59 age sample, Profile 1 was characterized by generalised negative perceptions, whereas Profile 4 by generalised positive perceptions, irrespective of social group or institution. Profiles 2 and 3, on the other hand, showed medium overall levels of perception and these differed markedly in their patterns across social groups and institutions. Specifically, Profile 2 showed positive social-civic perceptions, characterized by higher ratings for the municipal and federal governments, the public health system, educational institutions, and neighbours, alongside lower ratings for the police and military. Conversely, Profile 3 represented positive security-institutional perceptions, showing higher perceptions for the police and military, but lower ratings for local and federal government, public health system, educational institutions, and neighbours (Figure 4). Despite these differences, some commonalities emerged across these four profiles. Median values for trust, security, effectiveness and fairness were broadly similar within each profile but varied across profiles (Figures S14-S15).

In the two younger age groups, a similar pattern was observed for Profile 1 (generalized negative perceptions) and Profile 4 (generalized positive perceptions). Although Profiles 2 and 3 again exhibited intermediate overall levels of perceptions, their patterns across perception constructs were less marked than in adults. In particular, median values for perception constructs exhibited greater variation within each profile in these younger groups compared to the 19-59 age group (Figures S16-S21). Notably, perceptions of a few social groups and institutions were relatively invariant across profiles and age groups. In particular, perceptions of family and science were generally favourable, whereas perceptions of gangs were consistently negative, irrespective of profile membership or age group. Among participants aged 19-59, compared to Profile 4 (generalized positive perceptions), Profile 1 (generalized negative perceptions) had a significantly higher proportion of males (55.6% vs. 29.8%, p = 0.006), individuals in the lowest family income stratum (38.9% vs. 20.0%, p = 0.031), those with the lowest educational attainment (36.1% vs. 11.3%, p = 0.002), and non-white individuals (44.4% vs. 28.2%, p = 0.072). In both younger age groups, we found no strong statistical evidence for such associations (Table S24).

Discussion

This study provides the initial validation of the Social Matrix, a measurement instrument and common framework designed to capture multiple, interacting dimensions of how individuals perceive their social worlds. We found moderate-to-strong evidence of convergent validity with established reference scales selected for their theoretical relevance, alongside evidence that individual perception items generally correlated, as expected, but rarely to an extremely high degree that would indicate redundancy. These patterns of inter-item correlations suggest that perceptions of different social groups and institutions are related but not interchangeable.

Beyond these psychometric findings, we identified distinct patterns in how individuals perceive different social groups and institutions, which varied meaningfully by sociodemographic makeup. These results suggest that, as theorised, both the types of perceptions and the social groups and institutions being perceived are important for understanding how individuals internalise and represent their wider social worlds. In order to advance theory and empirical research on how macro level conditions shape individual behaviour, both dimensions (perception constructs x social groups/institutions) need to be simultaneously considered and, ideally measured, within a common framework. This is the purpose of the Social Matrix. Further research is required to establish further validity and the substantive gains to be made from using the Social Matrix in longitudinal studies both within and beyond Brazil.

Although individual perception constructs [4] [5] [6] [7] 23, 45, 46 have been extensively studied, much of this research remains confined to disciplinary silos, limiting our understanding of how different perceptions relate to one another across social groups and institutions. Our initial findings help to illustrate the theoretical and empirical importance of these limitations.

Legitimacy, for example, has been extensively studied in criminology through the legal socialisation framework 47 , which examines how individuals develop perceptions of laws, rules and authority through social experiences, and how these perceptions shape behaviour, including delinquency, violence 25, 37, 48, 49 , and other outcomes (e.g., public support). In Brazil, the São Paulo Legal Socialisation Study (SPLSS) 37 has advanced this literature by examining how adolescents' experiences with the police and their neighbourhoods shape perceptions of police legitimacy over time. However, it is still unclear how legitimacy relates to other perceptions, or whether these perceptions generalise across social groups and institutions (or authority figures). The novelty of the Social Matrix is that it is able to shed insights on the interconnectedness of specific perceptions across various social groups and institutions. In measuring these dimensions concurrently, we found that legitimacy was only weakly associated with other positive perceptions of family, such as trust, but showed stronger associations with trust, fairness, effectiveness and security in relation to the military. These differences suggest that individuals do not unanimously develop perceptions of legitimacy that generalise across all authority figures 50 (police, teachers, parents) as has been recently suggested 49 , nor do positive or negative perceptions of a particular social group or institution necessarily extend across different perception constructs. Similar findings are emerging from the growing literature on public trust, whereby it matters what social group and institution is being perceived 31, 32, 43 , even if -as we also found -these perceptions may cluster according to adjacent institutions (e.g., military and police, public health and education institutions).

Nevertheless, there is nuance in which perceptions are held in relation to which social group and institutions. Thus, measuring constructs in isolation, or exclusively in relation to a single social target, risks obscuring both commonalities and meaningful differences in how individuals represent their social worlds.

The latent profile analyses further illustrate the value of examining social perceptions jointly, revealing distinct patterns of responses across individuals. Across all three age groups, four latent profiles (i.e., patterns) emerged, characterised by the overall positivity or negativity of perceptions and how these differed across social groups and institutions. These profiles also varied by socioeconomic position among adults, whereby lower income and educational attainment were more common among adults with generalised negative perceptions, echoing previous research showing a relationship between socioeconomic status and perceptions, such as social trust 51, 52 . The remaining profiles revealed contrasting perceptions of different types of institutions. One was characterised by generally favourable perceptions of civic institutions, including government, education and public health services, alongside less favourable perceptions of the police and military. The other exhibited the opposite pattern, with more favourable perceptions of institutions associated with security and the use of force.

These distinctions resonate with research suggesting that perceptions of macro level conditions may fundamentally reflect broader political orientations, rather than the objective conditions themselves 53, 54 . They raise the possibility that ideological differences may shape be most critical in understanding some individuals' perceptions and internalisations of their social worlds. Further research is needed to examine the role of political orientation and ideology in shaping these patterns, particularly in Brazil, where the political and historical significance of institutions, such as the police and military (cf. military dictatorship, 1964-1985), are vividly present in civilians living memory with cascading polarisation 55.

Despite the overall heterogeneity between profiles, certain patterns were consistent. Family was generally perceived favourably, as was science, whereas perceptions of gangs were consistently negative across profiles and age groups. The positive perceptions of family are consistent with theories (e.g., attachment theory) 56 emphasising the importance of close interpersonal relationships as sources of trust, belonging, and security. Favourable perceptions of science are particularly noteworthy given prominent narratives of declining public trust in scientific institutions, especially following the COVID-19 pandemic. Our findings align with recent cross-national evidence suggesting that concerns about a widespread crisis of trust in science may be overstated 57, 58 . On the other hand, the consistently negative perceptions of gangs are notable in the Brazilian context, where criminal organisations can exert considerable influence over local communities and, in some settings, assume roles in providing protection, social order and belonging 59 . Our findings suggest that, despite these potential roles, gangs are not generally perceived as positive sources of security or belonging 60, 61 among participants in this study and warrants further investigation.

Our study has several limitations, primarily related to the sampling strategy, geographical scope and initial psychometric validation. First, the pilot relied on convenience sampling through an online survey, underrepresenting individuals with limited internet access and lower digital literacy, which therefore may not be generalisable or representative of the Brazilian population. Second, although the Social Matrix was systematically developed in English through a critical literature review and international expert review, and subsequently translated and culturally adapted into Brazilian Portuguese through local expert input and focus groups, it was piloted exclusively in Brazil. Given that perceptions of social groups and institutions are shaped by sociocultural and institutional contexts, further validation across countries and languages is needed to establish its cross-cultural applicability. Finally, the weaker evidence of convergent validity for legitimacy highlights the need for further refinement and testing, particularly regarding item wording, comprehension and interpretation across social targets. These weaker associations may partly reflect limitations of the reference measures: one primarily assessed procedural fairness rather than legitimacy itself, consistent with its stronger association with the Social Matrix item measuring police fairness, while another measure of police legitimacy showed poor internal consistency 37, 42 . Further research is therefore needed to distinguish limitations of the Social Matrix items from those of existing reference measures and to establish their validity across different sociocultural settings.

In this paper we offer an initial empirical foundation for conceptualising Social World Models and operationalising the measurement via the Social Matrix as interconnected but differentiated perceptions of the people, groups, and institutions that constitute people's broad social environments. By capturing multiple perception constructs across multiple social targets, the Social Matrix offers a framework for moving beyond the study of isolated perceptions towards a more integrated understanding of how individuals develop representations of their social worlds, shape individual behaviour, health, and wellbeing and, in turn, contribute to collective behaviours and societal outcomes.

Focus Group Results

The 11-14-year-old group included 7 participants (mean age = 14.1, SD = 1.22), mostly girls (71.4%) and from high-income households (57.1%). The 15-18-year-old group included 7 participants (mean age = 17.4, SD = 1.6), mostly female (57.1%), from high-income households (66.7%), and attending high school (100.0%). The 19-and-older group included 6 participants and comprised mostly female (66.7%), with a mean age of 31.8 years (SD = 5.9), mostly from low-income households (66.7%), and with education evenly distributed across categories (33.3% each).

Overall, the groups showed a good understanding of the items and constructs, with some changes by age group. For the 11-14-year-old group, "neighbors" were replaced with "neighborhood"; "education" with "your school"; "law and order" with "police"; and "media"

with "social media". The children reported not understanding the scientists' and armed forces' group, so these group was removed. For legitimacy, "has the right to make rules" was considered more appropriate than "has the right to tell you what to do". "Protected" was considered more closely related to the perceptions of safety. Specifically, for the 15-18-yearold group, "religion" was changed to "religious centers" to focus specifically on religious institutions, and "health system" was changed to "Sistema Único de Saúde (SUS)," the Brazilian public health system. We added examples for governments and institutions and divided "media" into traditional media (television and magazines) and social media. Finally, educational institutions, religious centers, and organized crime groups were excluded from the belonging domain because they suggested active participation. The other changes followed those made for the 11-14-year-old group.
