Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

In recent years, the steady rise of economic inequality across the globe has accompanied an ever-increasing focus on the question of redistribution.

Though the majority of people believe that economic inequality is a problem, policy decisions to redistribute resources are typically highly contentious.

Previous psychological, sociological, and political science theories which attempt to explain the paradox between stated beliefs and actual behavior in redistributive choice have largely relied upon an unverified assumption that redistributive preferences are derived from perceptions about pre-existing inequality. Here, we propose a Moral Preferences Theory of Redistribution, which integrates intuitions and prior research derived from various modeling frameworks across psychology, cognitive neuroscience, and economics. This theory posits that that redistributive behavior is guided by multiple moral principles, and that the preferences that guide the selection of these principles are both context-dependent and reflect dispositional individual values. This theory was tested using a multi-methodological approach: across two experiments -a large behavioral experiment (N = 231) and a direct fMRI follow-up study (N = 50) -the data show converging evidence to support the Moral Preferences Theory across multiple levels of analysis: political, psychometric, computational, and neural.

[div-01]

One of the most urgent threats to stability of modern societies is that of economic inequality. Inequality has been consistently associated with increases in crime (Blau & Blau, 1982; Kang, 2016; Coccia, 2018), decreases in public health (Kawachi, 1997; Wilkinson & Pickett, 2011; Pickett & Wilkinson, 2015; Vega & Sribney, 2017), limitations in social mobility (Grusky & Hauser, 1984; Haveman & Smeeding, 2006; Chetty et al., 2017), and general degradation of civil society (Solt, 2008; Karakoc, 2013; Cole, 2018). However, these problems are not only symptoms of inequality -they also exacerbate the issue, leading to a vicious cycle wherein existing inequality produces greater and greater inequality over time (Birdsall, 2006; Cingano, 2014). With such drastic consequences, it is relatively unsurprising that 54% of adults across 36 nations report being very concerned about the growth of economic inequality (Wike et al., 2025).

Despite the alarm which economic inequality seems to engender, there is, paradoxically, limited public support for redistributive policies: government initiatives which seek to directly lessen inequality by transferring resources from the economically advantaged to the economically disadvantaged (Almas et al., 2022; Gelman, Kenworthy, & Su, 2010; Page & Jacobs, 2009; Saad, 2011; Shapiro & Young, 1986). Indeed, efforts to redistribute in this manner are often seen as highly controversial (Saad, 2011; Newport, 2013). While proponents of redistribution argue its necessity to ensure a free, fair, and functioning society, detractors describe redistribution as unfair in principle, and argue it threatens the long-term stability and prosperity of a society. These contradictory and polarizing aspects of redistributive policies can account for the interest in redistribution across several scientific disciplines. The following section provides an overview of two qualitatively different approaches to characterizing redistribution, those of generative theories and of descriptive models, highlighting the strengths and limitations of each approach. We conclude this section by proposing a new theory which integrates these approaches and, in doing so, moves beyond the limitations of each.

Section 1: Existing Accounts of Redistribution

Generative Theories

Across several disciplines -including psychology, sociology, and political science -several theories have been proposed to explain why fundamental disagreements about redistribution exist, with the goal of subsequently predicting which members of society will support redistribution or oppose it.

In short, these generative theories advance claims about the mechanisms by which individuals (or groups of individuals) come to either support or oppose redistribution. These theories therefore produce falsifiable hypotheses about which types of people will either support or oppose redistribution, and thus whether redistributive efforts will succeed or fail.

The disciplinary divide in theories of redistribution lies not in the nature of the predictions per se, but at the level of explanation for the predictions themselves. For example, Sociological theories such as Relative Deprivation Theory (Runciman, 1966) and Hegemonic Consent Theory (Burawoy, 2012) focus on the role of social, cultural, and economic influences on attitude formation; meanwhile theories from Political Science such as Median Voter Theory (Downs, 1957; Black, 1958) and Power Resource Theory (Korpi, 2018) seek to characterize the process of interest aggregation within institutional constraints. Psychological theories, on the other hand, attempt to characterize redistribution by defining the underlying motivational aspects of attitudinal formation. One prime example of this is System Justification Theory (SJT), which calls upon principles of cognitive dissonance, suggesting that those who are most disadvantaged by a given system are paradoxically most motivated to believe the system is fair and just (Jost & Banaji, 1994). This implies that opposition to redistribution will be most pronounced amongst the most economically disadvantaged (Jost et al., 2003). Conversely, Social Dominance Theory (SDT) predicts that individuals who are advantaged by inequality will be incentivized to perceive that it is fair, thus electing to preserve it by opposing redistribution. In the absence of this motivation, economically disadvantaged individuals are proposed to make unbiased judgments about the fairness of economic inequality (Sidanius et al. 1994; Castillo, 2011; Rodríguez-Bailón et al., 2017; Easterbrook, 2021; Buchel, Luijkx, & Achterberg, 2021).

Despite the variation in both the predictions of who will support redistribution and the level of explanation from which these predictions are derived, they collectively reflect an underlying claim of moral absolutism. Moral absolutism suggests that in any given situation, redistribution is governed by a single morally correct principle which is usually held to be related to differences in perceptions about the fairness of the original distribution. So, when inequality is judged to be unfair people will choose to redistribute resources to lessen it, while inequality judged as fair will lead people to oppose redistribution. This assumption is crucial because, without the need to define and account for idiosyncratic moral values, these theories are thus able to render predictions about which individuals will support redistribution, and why they will do so (via, for example, an assessment of fairness).

The strength of generative theories lies in their ability to render predictions about which individuals will support redistribution, and offer explanations as to why. The notion of moral absolutism is ubiquitous in theories of redistributive behavior, and the assumption that redistribution adheres to moral absolutism is broadly useful for deriving generative theories of redistributive psychology. However, this assumption is directly contradicted by empirical data in several research fields. The most direct and thorough evidence comes from research on monetary allocation in interpersonal games -in other words, the field of social decision-making (Sanfey, 2007). This body of research shows that differences between individuals in redistribution persist, even when the antecedents of inequality are unambiguous. Here, moral absolutism predicts that all people should behave the same because their perceptions of inequality, being experimentally controlled, should be the same. Consequently, the primary limitation of existing theories is that they rely on the vulnerable assumption of moral absolutism.

Descriptive Models

By combining computational modeling and incentive-compatible decisionmaking tasks, social decision-making research has been able to identify the motives underlying distributive behavior, with this body of literature consistently supporting three intuitive hypotheses. First, that distributive behavior is consistent with multiple, qualitatively distinct, moral principles (i.e. Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Bolton & Ockenfels, 2000; Cappelen et al., 2007); here, moral principles refer to prescriptive rules which dictate how people ought to make decisions. Second, people behave as if contextual factors (i.e. state dependency) influence the moral principles that people adopt (i.e. Cappelen et al., 2007; Engel, 2011; Cappelen et al., 2013; Fehr & Charness, 2025). And third, that behavior is consistent with the notion that individual differences also govern which moral principles people adopt (i.e. Cappelen et al., 2007; Engel, 2011; Almas, Cappelen, & Tungodden, 2020), and usually to a substantially greater degree than contextual factors (i.e. Engel, 2011; Zhao, Kashima, & Simillie, 2017) -directly disproving moral absolutism.

Various modeling frameworks have emerged in social decision-making which have putatively been applied to redistributive behavior (van der Weele, 2014; Almas, Cappelen et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022). However, while models in these traditions do seek to identify the ideals, or principles, that choice behavior is most consistent with, research in this area stops short of proposing that these principles mechanistically guide decision-making in these interpersonal interactions. Models in these frameworks instead suggest that people make decisions as-if such principles guided their decision-making. This view of models is consistent with the notion of instrumentalism, or the view that models are tools to describe behavior rather that psychologically realistic accounts of the mechanisms of such behavior. The distinction between psychological realism and instrumentalism is, in and of itself, philosophical, however, these models have been evaluated as descriptive accounts of behavioral differences rather than generative accounts of redistributive psychology (i.e. they describe, but do not predict differences from one context to another, or one individual to another).

The strength of descriptive models lies in the accuracy of their predictions: in contrast to existing generative theories, these modeling frameworks incorporate empirically justified claims which are likely to explain how people approach the question of redistribution. However, these descriptive models also have a substantial limitation: they provide a descriptive account of (re)distributive behavior, and thus do not predict whether a given individual will support redistribution. This body of work has produced a loose set of theoretical claims which can be used to broadly describe how redistribution might change at the group level (i.e. more unfair contexts will lead to more redistribution) and explain interindividual heterogeneity in redistribution post-hoc (i.e. observed differences in redistributive attitudes might have been due to differences in individual values). However, this approach has not yet produced a generative theory of redistributive psychology which can predict how a specific individual in a specific context will make redistributive choices.

Aim of the Current Study

Here, we integrate these approaches by advancing a Moral Preferences Theory of Redistribution (MPT). MPT's fundamental claims are those which previous modeling frameworks have already evinced behaviorally: that redistributive choice is guided by multiple moral principles, that preferences for these principles are context-dependent, and that preferences are also based on dispositional individual characteristics. Consequently, this theory overcomes the limitations of generative theories by allowing for multiple moral preferences, as opposed to simple moral absolutism.

In addition, MPT addresses the limitations of descriptive models of (re)distributive behavior by incorporating the strengths of the aforementioned generative theories. The most crucial of these strengths is the ability to derive psychologically motivated, individual-specific, a priori predictions: the prediction that a given person will or will not redistribute in a particular situation, and will do so for a psychologically interpretable reason. Therefore, we advance a theory of redistributive choice which generates testable predictions across multiple levels of analysis. Here, MPT's key claims are, thus, examined in behavioral, neural, psychological, and political opinion data.

Section 2: The Moral Preferences Theory of Redistribution

Moral Preferences Theory (MPT) can be broadly described by three causal claims about redistributive psychology: that redistribution is guided by specific moral principles, and that the adoption of these principles is both context-dependent and also based on dispositional individual characteristics.

Importantly, and distinct from earlier behavioral accounts, these claims are derived from a conceptual and mechanistic account of how people address the question of redistribution. Here, we define redistribution as a decision undertaken under an unequal distribution of resources, with this decision concerning whether this unequal distribution should be adjusted, or not. To arrive at this decision, MPT suggests that perceptions of inequality are the mechanism for context-dependence, while trait preferences are the mechanism for individual heterogeneity. Overall, perceptions of inequality form the basis for an assessment as to whether the underlying inequality is fair or unfair, with this process mediated by individual trait values (i.e. two individuals can agree about the objective features of inequality, but may disagree as to whether these features connote fairness: this disagreement would be a consequence of individual trait values). These context-specific fairness judgments and context-invariant trait preferences together determine which redistributive principle individuals will prefer, with the use of this principle ultimately guiding redistributive choice (Figure 1). Note. Context-specific perceptions of inequality and individual-specific traits determine one's redistributive choices in a given context. Proximately, perceptions of inequality are constituted by perceived features that are classed as either reducible or irreducible features of inequality: examples of such features are exemplified by the gray boxes. The former inform judgments as to whether inequality is either fair or unfair: this judgment can be moderated by trait preferences. Fairness judgments and trait preferences then determine the principle which will be used to guide redistributive choice or, more specifically, they determine the moral preferences for a set of moral principles. Irreducible features of the perceived inequality are inputs to the principles who act as decision criteria: the output of this evaluation is a decision about whether to redistribute.

Unlike other accounts of distributive behavior (i.e. Deutsch, 1982; Cappelen et al., 2007), a distinct feature of MPT is that it categorically distinguishes between different features of the choice environment in terms of their relevance to redistributive decisions. Specifically, we posit that only the irreducible attributes of a particular distribution serve as direct inputs during the decision-making process. These irreducible attributes are, namely, the initial distribution of resources and the options for changing this distribution (redistributive outcomes). Anything else is incidental information, for example how the distribution of resources was generated (i.e. via merit, via luck, etc.) or the characteristics of individual recipients (i.e. group identity, need, etc.).

According to the theory, this incidental information is incorporated into fairness judgments which in turn inform the adoption of a particular principle.

Section 3: Study-Specific Operationalizations

Moral Principles

MPT contends that moral principles are the proximate decision rules underlying redistributive behavior. The idea that redistributive behavior is shaped by principles -or rules which are referenced in order to guide decisionmaking -is advanced by many theories of redistributive psychology, and is also reflected in the public discourse around redistribution. For instance, the idea that people should oppose redistribution is often heard in calls to "pull yourself up by your bootstraps". This is the essence of Equity Theory, which proposes that benefits should be allocated according to the principle of Proportionality -namely, that resources are distributed in proportion to individual contributions (Adams, 1965). In contrast, Egalitarian Theory, often captured by sentiments such as "leveling the playing field", suggests that redistribution should by unconditionally supported to ensure that benefits are allocated equally among all individuals, thus adhering to a principle of Equality. Finally, the notion that redistributive behavior can be guided by selfinterest is less explicitly acknowledged in public discourse but is a nonetheless influential notion in the literature. To this end, Rational Choice Theory proposes that people adhere to the principle of Self-Interest -supporting redistribution when they stand to economically benefit from it while opposing it when they would economically lose from it (Scott, 2000; Green, 2002).

Incorporating only irreducible features of inequality, namely the initial distribution vector and the delta vectors per redistributive choice, MPT of Redistribution limits the range of possible principles. The aforementioned three -Proportionality, Equality and Self-Interest -are such examples, and each prescribe different patterns of redistributive behavior. Proportionality and Equality always predict specific decisions (to never redistribute or always redistribute, respectively), while Self-Interest is distinguishable by materialdependent redistribution (i.e. redistribute when it is personally beneficial, do not redistribute when it is personally costly). Although other principles fit this criteria and some have been shown to characterize redistributive behavior (i.e. Efficiency, Rank Preservation, Maximin, Hierarchy Inversion), here we commit to an operationalization of MPT which utilizes only three specific principles: Proportionality, Equality, and Self-Interest.

Reducible Features of Inequality

As presented in Figure 1, MPT decomposes Perceived Inequality into two classes of features: those which are irreducible with respect to characterizing the unequal distribution of resources, and those which are not. This latter class of reducible features is theorized to drive context-specificity in moral principle selection, in that they are inputs to a judgment about whether the observed inequality is fair or not. One crucial foundational question in this respect is thus the question of how people judge the fairness of underlying inequality (Willis et al., 2022).

Various features have been proposed to inform these fairness judgments, including which types of people are disadvantaged (van Oorschot, 2000; García-Sánchez et al., 2020), the extent of the inequality (Norton & Ariely, 2011; Starmans, Sheskin, & Bloom, 2017), and the consequences of the inequality (Alesina & Angeletos, 2005; Cappelen et al., 2013). However, the factor considered to be most crucial when judging the fairness of inequality is its underlying cause, that is, why some people have more resources than others (Thibaut & Walker, 1975; Sweeny & McFarlin, 1993; van den Bos, Lind, & Wilke, 2001; Krawczyk, 2011; Ambrose & Arnaud, 2013; Tyler, 2015; Tejero-Peregrina et al., 2025). Building on Smith's (2010) work, the current study will examine the role of four specific causes of underlying inequality on redistributionmerit, entitlement, corruption, and luck.

Psychologically speaking, causes of inequality are attributions of what make individuals better or worse-off successful in a given context. Merit reflects the belief that people who are advantaged have earned these extra resources through hard work or skill. Perceptions of meritocracy (alternatively, meritocratic perceptions) are a prevalent approach to operationalizing perceived inequality, which are intended to measure the attribution of merit as a determinant of financial success (McCoy & Major, 2007; Kluegel & Smith, 2017; Castillo et al., 2021; Tejero-Peregrina et al., 2025). In contrast to this is the notion of entitlement -that better-off people have been handed these advantages through no efforts of their own, but rather via societal hierarchies such as family opportunity, inheritance, or even via discriminatory practices (i.e. racism, sexism, ageism). In a similar, but more extreme, vein, the idea of corruption reflects the belief that economically advantaged people have taken resources by committing illegal or immoral acts. Finally, advantage can be acquired through simple happenstance, or luck. In the current study we commit to these specific causes of inequality as operationalizations of reducible features of underlying inequality. The next section outlines the methods and materials that were used to address MPT, as informed by these key operationalizations.

Section 4: Approach

Moral Preferences Theory (MPT; Figure 1) makes several important claims which span multiple levels of analysis. Therefore, a multi-methodological approach is necessary to test these claims. In what follows, we will identify these what these claims are, clarify how each methodology is suited to address specific claims, and summarize literature which informed our study design and hypotheses.

Computational Modeling of Behavior

In order to test MPT, we adopt an interdisciplinary approach which combines several different data collection and analysis methods. However, at the center of this approach is the computational modeling of experimental task behavior.

The rationale for this approach is two-fold: firstly, that incentive-compatible behavioral experiments are the best available preference elicitation procedure for social motives. This approach addresses several key limitations which apply to the standard self-report methods typically employed in this field, namely, that by assessing observed, rather than stated preferences, the potential for social desirability and observer effects are mitigated (Camerer & Hogarth, 1999; Levitt & List, 2007; Kamenica, 2012). The current study seeks to distinguish between redistributive behavior that is motivated by Equality, Proportionality, and Self-Interest by assessing how behavior aligns with that prescribed by each of these principles within the experimental task. Second, computational models can both instantiate these different principles and, subsequently, enable the classification of these motives in an a priori, theorydriven manner (Vahed, Galván, & Sanfey, 2024; Galván & Sanfey, 2025). Therefore, experimental task behavior elicits dissociable behavioral preferences for moral principles, with computational modeling subsequently characterizing these behavioral preferences (i.e. game strategies).

Computational modeling of behavior is the lynchpin of the current study's approach to testing MPT because we will use these game strategies as indicators of participants' moral preferences for redistributive principles.

Below, the rationale behind the task is described, and the model which was developed to instantiate MPT within this task is outlined.

Task

Redistribution is a phenomenon which many existing studies have putatively examined in incentive-compatible experimental tasks (i.e. Ku & Salmon, 2013; Bechtel, Liesch, & Scheve, 2018; Almas, Capellan, & Tungodden, 2020; Li et al., 2022). However, we argue that several psychological properties of redistribution, in general, are not incorporated in these existing paradigms.

One such property is that of multilateralism: we propose that an necessary feature of redistribution is that it occurs in multiagent environments. Most existing studies examine reallocation behavior in bilateral interactions, which is behaviorally distinct from redistributive behavior in multilateral interactions (Andreoni, 2007), and which we suggest is likely also psychologically distinct. Another property is limited agency for the decider: unlike mere reallocation, we propose that redistribution is characterized by constraints upon the range of choice options for deciders. That is, potential redistributive outcomes are inherently tied to initial distribution amounts such that redistribution is a lever for modifying an existing distribution of resources. Redistribution is not resource assignment with a starting position; it is rather the implementation of a resource adjustment policy within a multiagent environment (Moene & Wallerstein, 2001).

In addition to these two key psychological properties which we aim to incorporate in an experimental task, further considerations specific to our study aims are also relevant. First, the current study seeks to examine moral preferences and, thus, must avoid conflating these moral preferences with strategic considerations. This restriction excludes tasks which rely upon existing histories (Cabrales, Nagel, & Mora, 2012; Koster et al., 2025) or prospective uncertainty (Beckman et al., 2002; Durante, Putterman, & van der Weele, 2014; Amasino, Pace, & van der Weele, 2023). With respect to the present study aims, tasks which rely on a spectator or third-party design are also unsuitable because such a design will be unable to dissociate Equality and Proportionality from Self-Interest. At the time of writing, we found no experimental paradigm which met all criteria, and therefore developed a novel socioeconomic paradigm termed the Redistribution Game.

In the Redistribution Game (Figure 2), 10 anonymous players are involved in a one-shot interaction: first, 100 tokens are allocated amongst these 10 players as a function of one of four allocation contexts of either merit, entitlement, corruption, or luck, ensuring that any inequality has a distinct 'cause' (Table 1). Then, the participant has the opportunity to set a single 'Tax Rate' across all players which determines the percentage of each player's tokens that will be transferred to a community pot, which is then equally dispersed among all 10 players irrespective of each player's contribution. The tokens each player ends the game with is thus the amount retained after being taxed, plus the amount that was received from the community pot. The task is incentive compatible, as participants received a monetary payment bonus based on the number of tokens they earned on several randomly selected trials. Note. Computational Model

We formalize the MPT as a computational model in order to capture the degree to which different moral principles may guide redistributive decisionmaking. Choice behavior in decision tasks involving allocations of resources is often assessed using algorithmic models of decision-making, the most prevalent of which are the family of Social Utility Models (Konovalov, Hu, & Ruff, 2018; Vahed, Galván, and Sanfey, 2024). MPT is thus instantiated as a Social Utility Model: a computational model of choice arbitration which suggests that people derive subjective value, or utility, from both economic and social sources (Galván and Sanfey, 2025).

The model proposes that redistribution is a choice which people make under conditions of existing inequality; the participant's decision is essentially how much inequality should exist after intervention. The model further posits that the overall utility of a given choice option is a function of the utility provided by the three qualitatively distinct moral principles outlined earlier. The principle of Proportionality dictates the preservation of the initial distribution, thus utility is gleaned by conserving the difference between (presumed) contributions and payouts. In contrast, according to the principle of Equality, utility is derived by choosing a redistribution of resources which lessens the inequality between individuals. Finally, for the principle of Self-Interest, utility is gained by choosing a redistribution of resources which is most economically advantageous for oneself.

To allow an assessment of the relative recruitment of these three principles, the computational instantiation of MPT contains two free parameters. These parameters -theta and phi -range from 0 to 1 and in combination modulate the relative importance of following each principle in order to ultimately maximize utility. These parameters are estimated based on the behavior of a given participant in a given condition, and thereby reflect their moral preferences (m). Neuroimaging

One important implication of the computational instantiation of MPT is that the cognitive architecture of redistributive behavior involves utility computations of prospective choices in reference to a particular moral principle. Consequently, these models imply that the computations underlying these behaviors should be both tractable and distinct in the brain while engaging in decision-making. To investigate this event-related neuroimaging will be utilized to identify candidate regions for how these moral principles may be implemented during decision-making in the Redistribution Game.

Several previous studies have investigated the neural correlates of distributive preferences, and this body of research has implicated several networks in the decision-making process for allocation decisions.

The first, and most central, of these networks is the valuation network, which comprises the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and the ventral striatum (VS). Across many studies, both the vmPFC and VS have been identified as crucial hubs for value signal integration (Levy & Glimcher, 2012; Bartra et al., 2013), and both regions have similarly been implicated in monetary allocation decisions (Fehr & Ruff, 2014). Relatedly, the cognitive control network -which includes the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), the supplemental motor area (SMA), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) -has also been identified as relevant to social decision-making (Rilling & Sanfey, 2011; Chang & Sanfey, 2013).

Specifically, previous research has shown that the dACC, SMA, dlPFC, and AI are more active when making prosocial decisions as compared to proself ones (Tricomi et al., 2010; van Baar, Chang, & Sanfey, 2019), in line with some accounts suggesting that prosocial behavior involves an override of automatic, selfish, choices (Van't Wout et al., 2005; Greene et al., 2008; Zaki & Mitchell, 2013).

Alongside the valuation and cognitive control networks, several other networks involved in social cognition have also been associated with monetary allocation decisions. For instance, increase in activation across the mentalizing network -which includes the temporoparietal junction (TPJ), posterior temporal sulcus, and several medial sub-regions of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) -has been associated with prosocial decision-making (Sanfey, 2007; Tusche & Bas, 2021). Similarly, several regions in the social affective network including the Anterior Insula (AI), Amygdala, and temporal poles have been implicated in prosocial choice (Sanfey, 2007; Tusche & Bas, 2021).

We hypothesize that there are distinct and dissociable neural mechanisms underlying the implementation of different redistributive principles, in particular recruiting the valuation, cognitive control, social affective, and mentalizing networks.

Another prediction of MPT is that perceptions of reducible features of inequality are used to inform judgments as to whether this inequality is fair or not. Consequently, differences in activation between different conditions of the task should be predicted by differences in fairness judgments. Previous research has implicated the same networks involved in principle usage: the valuation, cognitive control, social affective, and mentalizing networks.

Canonical work in this area has tied activation in the AI to both judgments of fairness and perceptions of distributive unfairness (Sanfey et al., 2003; Hsu, Anen, & Quartz, 2008; Corradi-Dell'Acqua et al., 2013). Similarly, almost two decades of research has established that the dorsolateral PFC causally contributes to perceptions of fairness and that this causal manipulation also impacts nodes in the valuation, social affective, and mentalizing networks (Van't Wout et al., 2005; Baumgartner et al., 2011; Speitel, Traut-Mattausch & Jonas; 2019; Lin & Feng, 2024).

A final prediction made by the MPT is that dispositional differences may predispose individuals to follow one principle over others. One possibility, therefore, is that neuroanatomical differences may characterize these dispositional tendencies. Although research on the relationship between social decision-making preferences and underlying brain structure is less common than brain function, some previous work has indeed addressed this question previously. This research suggests that gray matter volume in several regions in the aforementioned networks is predictive of task preferences: namely, in the TPJ, AI, dlPFC, Striatum, and Amygdala (Morishima et al., 2012; Yamagishi et al., 2016; Yamagishi et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021). Thus, the current study will assess whether strategies used in the Redistribution Game map onto differences in both brain function and brain structure and will also investigate whether fairness judgments correspond to differences in brain function.

Psychological Measures

Another key assertion of MPT is that two factors mechanistically underlie the selection of a moral principle to guide redistributive choice (Figure 1). The first such factor is a context-specific judgment about how fair inequality is. To substantiate this claim fairness judgments will be assessed within each condition of the Redistribution Game, in order to investigate the question of whether these self-reported fairness judgments predict the moral principles that participants use in that condition.

According to MPT, the second factor which underlies the selection of a moral principle are traits. There are many proposed traits -and even more available psychometric scales to operationalize these traits -but in the current experiment we will focus on two sets of trait measures which have been shown to influence redistributive attitudes in previous research. The first of these is the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ): derived from Moral Foundations Theory which posits pluralistic, intuition-based morality. Several sub-scales (i.e. foundations) from the MFQ have been shown to predict differences in redistributive attitudes across multiple studies (Miles, 2016; Voelkel & Feinberg, 2018; Franks & Sherr, 2019). The second set of trait measures are Dispositional Envy and Dispositional Compassion, which have likewise been shown to predict differences in redistributive attitudes across multiple studies (Sznycer et al. 2017; Lin & Bates, 2021). To substantiate this claim, the current study will assess whether responses to these psychological trait scales predict the adoption of different moral principles in the Redistribution Game.

Policy Views

MPT is a theory of redistributive psychology, not merely a model of redistributive behavior. Therefore, in addition to explaining choice behavior in the Redistribution Game, MPT should also explain the primary phenomenon of interest in the real world: the views that individuals hold toward redistributive policies. In line with the approach adopted by Epper and colleagues (2024), the current study will assess whether laboratory behavior explains political opinions. Consequently, participants' opinions on a variety of different policies spanning Government Spending, Tax Policy, and Social Security were collected in the current study.

Participants

Experiment 1 was an online behavioral study. An a priori power analysis for the study was conducted based on a generic small effect size estimate of d = 0.2, with a desired power of 0.8 and a 0.95 confidence interval, Bonferronicorrected for 6 tests. This resulted in a recommended sample size of 265 participants. 265 participants were collected via Prolific. Since only completed submissions were saved and a completion code was given only once the study was completed and the data was saved, no exclusion criteria were preregistered.

The sample was comprised of English-speaking Prolific workers currently residing in the Netherlands, and was balanced on the basis of gender (1455 such workers were active on Prolific in the 90 days prior to data collection).

Data for 22 participants was lost due to errors with GitLab and Pavlovia which prevented the data from being written. Deviating from pre-registration due to inadvertent exclusion, participants who had previously participated in the pilot version of study were excluded from analysis (n = 12). Thus, the final sample for analysis was 231 participants. Demographic data for these 231 participants is provided in the Supplementary Materials (pgs. 1-2).

Task

After receiving short written instructions, participants played the Redistribution Game in 4 blocks, each of 20 trials. Context-Dependency

In the current study, the cause of inequality was manipulated as a reducible feature of inequality because Moral Preferences Theory proposes that principle use varies between contexts due to judgments about how fair inequality is, and previous work has shown that the cause of inequality influences these fairness judgments. The behavioral data indicated that the underlying cause of inequality influenced both tax rate behavior and the moral principle that was adopted as a redistributive strategy. However, in this effect was not uniform across participants in experiment 1 with many participants demonstrating strategy invariance. Data in experiment 2 enabled us to test whether this could be explained by differing fairness judgments between participants for the same cause of inequality (i.e. Condition). Herein, a linear mixed effects model was estimated with a fixed effect of criteria and only a random intercept -a random effect of Condition could not be estimated.

A Type III ANOVA revealed an omnibus effect of Condition (χ 2 (3) = 11.216, p = 0.011), though only the Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparison between the Merit and Entitlement conditions was significantly different (β= 0.384, t(49) = 3.750, p bonf < 0.001).

After showing that Condition affected fairness perceptions, we sought to assess whether fairness perceptions could explain why strategy use varied across conditions. Thus, a hierarchical, multinomial logistic regression was estimated which predicted strategy by both condition and participants' self-reported fairness judgments of that condition. This analysis revealed that the strategy that participants used was predicted by the valence (χ 2 (1) = 7.534, p = 0.006) and salience (χ 2 (1) = 6.824, p = 0.008) of participants' fairness judgments in each condition -though not the condition itself (χ 2 (3) = 0.741, p = 0.863) and explained a small portion of variance in strategy selection (Marginal R² = 0.121, Conditional R² = 0.874). Section 10: Discussion

In the current study, we advance a new theory of redistributive psychologythe Moral Preferences Theory of Redistribution (MPT). This theory advances three core hypotheses about how people decide to support efforts to lessen inequality via redistribution. These are, 1) that behavior is guided by multiple, potentially conflicting, moral principles, 2) that the selection of these principles is context-dependent, and 3) that intrinsic individual differences underlie the use of these principles. MPT goes beyond these core hypotheses however, advancing process-level claims about redistributive psychology as indicated in Figure 1 (and as recapitulated in Figure 11). To test all claims advanced by MPT, we adopted an interdisciplinary approach.

Combining the strengths of multiple methodologies, the data show converging evidence that redistributive behavior accords with MPT's predictions (Figure 11). Redistributive preferences were first elicited via decisions participants made in the Redistribution Game, which were identified and classified using a computational modeling approach. Directly testing the three main theoretical arguments, the data presented here provide converging evidence for the underlying mechanistic claims. The model's process claims were substantiated by identifying candidate psychological mechanisms, showed evidence for different principle usage using both functional and structural MRI data, and cross-validated its predictions by extrapolating to real policy views.

Capturing Interpersonal Decision-Making

MPT was first assessed in terms of its ability to capture decision-making in the context of a novel socioeconomic task -the Redistribution Game. The data show that the MPT was the best available account of redistributive decisionmaking when tested against alternative models, explaining a large amount of variance in these decisions. This directly confirmed the primary hypothesis that MPT advances, showing that people employed moral principles of Equality, Proportionality, and Self-Interest to make redistributive decisions, providing support for the idea that redistributive choice is guided by moral principles. The second and third hypotheses were also directly assessed by comparing MPT to models which did not incorporate either contextual effects or interindividual heterogeneity, confirming both of these hypotheses. In sum, both experiments in the present study showed that redistributive choice was governed by the principles of Equality, Proportionality, and Self-Interest, with these tenets being used differently by different people, and across different contexts.

Crucially, although principle usage was marked by a high degree of contextinvariance, the data show small, but consistent, shifts in redistributive decisions: these effects were statistically significant even only among those who never switched strategy across task conditions. Although experiment 1 did not assess beliefs, this pattern of behavior suggests that participants did engage with the manipulations in the experiment, and therefore believed that they were playing with other, real, participants in the Redistribution Game. In experiment 2, beliefs were assessed using implicit measures: this approach is sometimes referred to as the "funnel" approach (Bargh & Chartrand, 2000; Blackhart et al., 2012). This approach minimized the likelihood of false positive identification of disbelief; however, neither experiment maximized the likelihood of correctly identifying participants' disbelief.

The experimental control afforded by the Redistribution Game enabled us to derive explicit, unambiguous, predictions from MPT -predictions which were confirmed in the behavioral data. However, in contrast to the prevailing existing model wherein redistributive preferences are entirely dependent on the perceived fairness of inequality (i.e. Runciman, 1966; Jost & Banaji, 1994; Sidanius et al. 1994; Burawoy, 2012), the data show that redistributive preferences are additionally impacted by irreducible individual differences in moral values. Consequently, the psychology of redistribution cannot be fully understood by solely studying how people perceive inequality. Instead, we must also seek to identify what individual values are and where they come from in order to understand redistributive psychology. This conclusion is bolstered by data collected across other modalities.

Identifying Candidate Neural Substrates

After confirming MPT's hypotheses in both experimental behavior and reported political opinions, these were also addressed in neuroimaging data.

Utilizing an event-related design, we investigated which regions of the brain that might implement moral principles as a decision rule when making redistributive choices. The data suggest that multivariate neural activity patterns in the right dlPFC may implement Proportionality when making decisions in the Redistribution Game. There was also evidence to support the notion that weak representational similarity between neural activity and redistributive preferences in the Redistribution Game was present across a majority of the regions analyzed.

Although these findings indicate tentative evidence for moral principle implementation during redistributive choice, these conclusions are limited by the design of the experiment. Our adaptation of the Redistribution Game for fMRI used a slider design, which required that the unit of analysis for fMRI data was the run-level, rather than the trial level. While the slider design of the Redistribution Game ensured consistency between the behavioral and fMRI versions of the task, a binary choice paradigm would have afforded the ability to assess the trial-level encoding of Equality, Proportionality, and Self-Interest when making decisions. Instead, participants could have simply identified which principle they most preferred prior to the start of the run, and implemented the Tax Rate consistently across most trials without actively deliberating on the decision that they were supposed to be making. This interpretation would also explain why no evidence of neural responses to perceived unfairness was found: judgments about the fairness or unfairness of the token allocation criteria would be made prior to -not during -the run.

Future research which addresses this design limitation will be able to provide a clearer picture of the candidate neural mechanisms which underlie fairness judgments and decision-making during redistributive choice.

We probed further into the third hypothesis advanced by MPT, attempting to identify which whether intrinsic differences corresponding to brain structure might underlie moral preferences. Analyses revealed that usage of the Self-Interest principle was predicted by decreased gray matter across the Fusiform Gyrus, with no other regions significantly predicting any strategy. We also employed a multivariate approach to assess whether the selection of a guiding moral principle might correspond to differences in neuroanatomical organization across the voxels of these brain regions. This more nuanced analysis revealed that the distribution of gray matter within several brain regions corresponded to the preferences observed in the Redistribution Game.

Crucially, most of these regions spanned the four key networks which we previously identified: the valuation, cognitive control, mentalizing, and social affective networks. The behavioral data shows that redistributive behavior is guided by the use of moral principles, and the VBM analysis suggests that individual differences have a basis in neuroanatomical organization.

Validating Proposed Psychological Mechanisms

After confirming the use of these principles and identifying potential candidate neural mechanisms, we then sought to validate the proposed psychological drivers of principle selection: namely, judgments of the fairness of inequality and trait-like differences. The data confirmed both claims: first, fairness judgments were found to underlie context-dependence in subsequent redistributive behavior. When participants judged that a monetary allocation criteria was fair they used the principle of Self-Interest, when judged unfair they used the principle of Equality, and when inequality was assessed as neither fair nor unfair they used the principle of Proportionality. Second, the data show that moral concerns for fairness also predicted the usage of Equality: this connects the current study with broader work on trait morality within Moral Foundations Theory specific to redistribution (Miles, 2016; Voelkel & Feinberg, 2018; Franks & Sherr, 2019). Third, and finally, the data similarly shows that fairness judgments within the different conditions of the Redistribution Game were moderated by the Binding foundations of the MFQ.

Incorporating moral concerns for Authority, Loyalty, and Purity, these Binding foundations represent conservative moral values (Graham et al., 2013).

There are, however, important caveats with these results, the most central of which are the sample size and the population investigated, namely 50 mostly young university students. However, the findings of the current study align closely with those observed previously on both the MFQ (Miles, 2016; Voelkel & Feinberg, 2018; Franks & Sherr, 2019) and in judgments about perceptions of inequality (Thibaut & Walker, 1975; Messick et al., 1985; Mikula, Petri, & Tanzer, 1990; Sweeny & McFarlin, 1993; Huo, 2002; Capellan & Tungodden, 2007; Tyler, 2015). Moreover, these results align with theoretical predictions made by both Moral Foundations Theory (Graham et al. 2013; Graham & Yudkin, 2022) and Fairness Heuristic Theory (van den Bos, Lind, & Wilke, 2001). This suggests that, although the current results should be interpreted tentatively, it would be reasonable to predict that these results will replicate in larger and more representative samples.

Explaining Differing Views on Redistributive Policies

To address this question of broader ecological validity, the aforementioned three core hypotheses were assessed in terms of participants' views towards real socioeconomic policies: these data strongly supported all three hypotheses. The strategies employed in the experimental task predicted different levels of support for actual redistributive policies: for instance, those individuals who used the Equality principle most strongly supported redistributive policies. The data also indicate that Self-Interest users' support was contingent upon their material benefit from redistributive policies, while opposition to redistribution policies was strongest among users of the Proportionality principle who had bleaker financial prospects. Thus, views on redistributive policies were found to be consistent with MPT's claim that intrinsic individual differences underlie moral principle selection (hypothesis 3) and that the principles of either of Self-Interest, Equality, and Proportionality guide redistributive behavior (hypothesis 1). Finally, individuals who believed that societal inequality was due to merit were more likely to oppose redistribution; this finding is in line with the theory's second hypothesis that redistributive preferences are influenced context-dependent perceptions of inequality.

Literature on the nature, and direction, of the relationship between SES and redistributive attitudes has been rife with contradictions. Some studies show a weakly negative relationship (Evans, 1993; Sznycer et al., 2017; Buchel, Luijkx, & Achterburg, 2021; Lin & Bates, 2021), in line with the predictions made by Rational Choice Theory and Social Dominance Theory, and other studies show a weakly positive relationship as System Justification Theory posits (Jost et al. 2002; Jost & Hunyady, 2003; Dirilen-Gumus, 2011; Tan et al. 2017). MPT can intuitively reconcile these contradictory findings, and the data shows that it does. A positive relationship was found among users of the Proportionality principle, a negative relationship among users of the Self-Interest principle, and a null relationship among users of the Equality principle. Thus, instead of a single generic relationship, the relationship between SES and redistributive policy views is governed by the moral principle people use and, moreover, the redistributive principle that people use can be elicited in the novel socioeconomic task.

Section 11: Implications

In the current study, we proposed and tested a new model of redistributive psychology: the Moral Preferences Theory of Redistribution (MPT). Data from the set of studies demonstrated that the redistributive principles observed in the Redistribution Game are consistent with motives underlying policy preferences, that the implementation of these decision rules corresponds with activation across several networks of the brain, and that use of these principles are predicted by dispositional characteristics and neuroanatomical differences alike. The current work thus presents converging evidence for moral pluralism in redistributive choice confirming the three hypotheses embodied by MPT across experimental choice, self-report, political views, and the brain. The key claim advanced by MPT is that redistributive psychology cannot be entirely reduced to how people merely perceive inequality, but rather that individual differences in moral preferences must be considered.

The implications of the current study for psychological science are important, namely that to better understand the psychology of redistribution, future research should address both individual and contextual factors. For society as a whole, the implications are fundamentally the same. Previous research has shown that the tension between "leveling the playing field" and "pulling yourself up by the bootstraps" is exacerbated by the fact that proponents of redistribution refer to it as undoing a system of hereditary entitlement, while opponents of redistribution refer to it as threatening a meritocracy. As pervasive as this battle to exert influence on people's perceptions seems to be, the data presented here suggests a reality which is less often considered.

Irrespective of how fair or unfair inequality is, trait-like dispositional values will always ensure a divide between those who support redistribution and those who oppose it.
