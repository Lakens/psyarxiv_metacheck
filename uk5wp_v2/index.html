What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Public Significance Statement

This meta-analysis shows that, at each age, people differ from each other in how their personality traits change, with the most pronounced differences among people found in childhood and adolescence. Teachers, educators, and practitioners should thus be aware that typical personality trait changes do not apply to every individual, illustrating a need to better understand why people differ in their personality trajectories across the lifespan.

Individual Differences in Personality Trait Changes Across the Lifespan: A Meta-Analysis of Longitudinal Studies

Personality traitspatterns of thoughts, feelings, and behaviorswere once conceptualized as stable constructs that do not or only rarely change in adulthood (Allport, 1961; James, 1890; McCrae et al., 2000). However, this conception of traits as stable entities has been challenged by comprehensive longitudinal meta-analyses showing that personality traits change across the lifespan. Specifically, both the rank-orders and the mean-levels of personality traits follow predictable patterns across time lags between assessments and age (Anusic & Schimmack, 2016; Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2006; Roberts & DelVecchio, 2000).

These meta-analytic findings provide a solid, yet incomplete description of personality development. To more fully understand the ways personality traits develop across the lifespan, it is helpful to consider a third indicator of personality development, individual differences in personality trait changes (e.g., Bleidorn et al., 2021; Borghuis et al., 2017; Damian et al., 2019; Haehner, Bleidorn, et al., 2024; Mroczek & Spiro, 2003). Individual differences in personality trait changes bridge sample-level and individual-level description of personality development as they provide information about whether and how people deviate from the mean-level trajectory (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Graham et al., 2020; Roberts & Mroczek, 2008). For example, mean-level findings in both large-scale longitudinal studies and meta-analytic research suggest that neuroticism decreases on average in adolescence and young adulthood (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2006; Seifert et al., 2024). However, whereas some people may experience particularly sharp decreases, for others, neuroticism stays relatively stable or even increase (Borghuis et al., 2017; Graham et al., 2020; Roemer et al., 2024). In nearly all studies of personality development, individual differences in change were statistically significant, providing a clear indication that personality development varies across people. But to date, no meta-analytic investigation has synthesized these findings across studies to quantify the degree to which development varies across people nor the moderators of this variability.

A comprehensive understanding of the overall magnitude of individual differences in personality trait changes as well as moderators of these individual difference is crucial, because it is the foundation for research aimed at identifying the factors that may cause these deviations from the average trajectory (Bleidorn, 2024; Bleidorn et al., 2021; Damian et al., 2019). For instance, identifying life stages with greater or lesser individual differences in personality trait changes offers important insights into the relative contribution of idiosyncratic sources of personality development across the lifespan. Relatedly, personality development theories can be evaluated regarding their utility to explain differential personality development. The examination of individual differences in change may also guide future research by identifying samples or study designs that may be particularly fruitful to investigate the sources and moderators of personality development. For example, if individual differences in change are especially strong among certain samples, future investigation could target these samples to identify the sources of differential personality development.

In the present meta-analysis, we examined the magnitude of individual differences in personality trait changes in general and whether these individual differences vary as a function of age, time lag between assessments, trait, or methodological factors such as time of publication, sample, or assessment characteristics.

Rank-order and Mean-level Stability of Personality Traits

The questions of whether and how personality traits change has gained considerable research interest over the past decades (Allemand et al., 2007; Bleidorn, 2012; Borghuis et al., 2017; Fraley & Roberts, 2005; Seifert et al., 2024), with most research focusing on the Big Five traits: agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, neuroticism/emotional stability, and openness (Bleidorn, 2024; Soto & John, 2017). Using the Big Five taxonomy as an organizing framework, comprehensive meta-analyses have integrated evidence from longitudinal studies to illustrate how rank-orders and mean-levels of personality traits develop from childhood to old adulthood (Anusic & Schimmack, 2016; Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2006; Roberts & DelVecchio, 2000).

Rank-order stability describes the degree to which the relative positions on traits within a sample remain stable or change over time (e.g., Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). The rank-order stability of personality traits has been found to be relatively high compared to other psychological constructs (Anusic & Schimmack, 2016), with stability coefficients of r = .80 over 1 year for each Big Five trait. Furthermore, rank-order stability seems to increase from childhood to young adulthood and to remain relatively high throughout the remainder of the lifespan (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts & DelVecchio, 2000). Meta-analytic evidence also showed that the rank-order stability of personality traits decreases with increasing retest intervals, but bottoms out at around r = .20 over intervals of more than 50 years.

Mean-level change refers to the strength and direction of changes in personality trait scores averaged across individuals (e.g., Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). In contrast to rankorder stability, the pattern of mean-level changes has been found to differ across the Big Five traits. Specifically, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and emotional stability tend to increase in young adulthood (Roberts et al., 2006; Roberts & Nickel, 2017; Schwaba et al., 2022). In contrast, openness to experience and extraversion tend to increase through childhood and adolescence and then decrease throughout adulthood. Furthermore, similar to changes in rankorder stability, mean-level changes increase with longer time intervals between assessments (Bleidorn et al., 2022).

In summary, existing meta-analyses provide a robust description of mean-level and rank-order stability of personality traits across age and time lags. However, neither of these parameters speaks directly to the degree that individual personality trait changes deviate from population-level trends (Borghuis et al., 2017; Damian et al., 2019; Graham et al., 2020; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018).

Individual Differences in Personality Trait Change

Individual differences in personality trait changes quantify the degree to which individual trajectories deviate from mean-level trajectories of personality traits (Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). The absence of individual differences in personality trait changes would suggest that all people change alike and that personality development was exclusively driven by causes shared across people. In contrast, substantial individual differences in change would suggest that people show a highly idiosyncratic personality development so that mean-level trajectories of personality traits are only weakly informative for the individual. Furthermore, this finding would indicate that the sources of personality development act in unique ways across people and point to the importance of moderators of personality trait changes (Haehner, Bleidorn, et al., 2024). As such, individual differences in change provide a perspective on personality development that complements findings of rank-order and meanlevel change (see Figure 1). There can be individual differences in personality trait changes in the presence (top panels) or in the absence of rank-order changes (bottom panels). Likewise, individual differences in personality trait changes can be found in the presence (right panels) or absence of mean level changes (left panels).

Different methods can be used to quantify individual differences in personality trait changes. Individual differences in change are sometimes assessed using the reliable change index, which tallies the number of participants that changes to a substantial degree (e.g., two standard errors of measurement; Damian et al., 2019; Roemer et al., 2024). An alternative approach to quantify individual differences in change is to estimate the variability around the mean-level trajectory of personality traits, with greater variability indicating more diverse trajectories among individuals, and lesser variability indicating that individual participants tend to develop more similarly to one another. In the present meta-analysis, we opted for this latter approach to examine individual differences in personality changes as it allows to quantify of the magnitude of individual differences in change and because it can be computed based on descriptive information provided in primary research.

Existing research consistently shows that people differ substantially in their personality trajectories (Asselmann & Specht, 2021; Denissen et al., 2019; Graham et al., 2020; Helson et al., 2002; Vaidya et al., 2008). However, a meta-analysis that examines the magnitude of individual differences in personality trait changes across studies with a standardized effect size metric is needed to advance our understanding of differential personality development for at least three reasons.

First, such an approach allows us to quantify the relative importance of unique versus common causes of personality development across the entire lifespan. This approach could thus be used to evaluate and refine theoretical accounts regarding the explanation of why people do or do not differ in their personality trait changes in certain life phases (see next section for details).

Second, quantifying the degree of individual differences in personality changes with a standardized effect size provides benchmarks across studies and samples. Such benchmarks are needed to move research beyond investigating whether a sample exhibits significant individual differences in change to more specific scientific questions about whether estimates of individual differences in change are higher or lower than typically found for certain samples or ages. This approach can be particularly valuable for investigations of personality trajectories of specific samples like college students, job seekers, or psychotherapy patients, to advance our understanding of why certain populations may differ or converge in their personality development.

Third, a meta-analysis on individual differences in personality changes allows for examinations of sample-level and study-level moderators of individual differences in personality changes. Information about such moderators can provide initial insights into the sources of a differential personality development and may guide design choices in future research (e.g., whether to focus on higher-or lower-order traits to examine individual differences in personality trait changes). From a theoretical point of view, two variables should be considered as key moderators of individual differences in change across the lifespan: age and the time interval between assessments.

Do Individual Differences in Personality Trait Changes Vary Across Age?

Existing meta-analyses on rank-order and mean-level changes of personality traits indicate that personality development depends on age (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2006; Roberts & DelVecchio, 2000). Examining whether individual differences in personality trait change also vary across age is important because it offers insights into the sources of personality development across the lifespan. Evidence that individual differences in personality trait changes are more pronounced during a certain life phase would suggest that idiosyncratic sources of personality development are particularly important in this developmental period. As shown by behavioral genetic studies, both genetic and reliable nonshared environmental factors (i.e., factors that make development more different, rather than more similar, across individuals) may be such idiosyncratic sources of personality development (Bleidorn et al., 2009; Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014; Hopwood et al., 2011).

However, theoretical accounts differ in their implications of how these sources should contribute to individual differences in personality trait changes across the lifespan (see Figure 2 for an overview).

For example, the Five-Factor Theory (McCrae & Costa, 2008) proposes that personality development is caused by a genetically driven personality maturation program that should be completed around age 30, whereas environmental effects on personality development should be very rare and biologically mediated (e.g., a brain injury or a pharmacological treatment; Mõttus, 2017). Accordingly, individual differences in personality trait changes during childhood to young adulthood should be explained by genetic differences. Thereafter, individual differences in change should largely disappear once biologically driven personality development is completed.

The Theory of Genotype → Environment Effects (Scarr & McCartney, 1983) posits that personality trait changes are primarily driven by genetically determined differences in environmental experiences. Individual differences in the genetic make-up of individuals should evoke different environmental reactions throughout the lifespana constant source of individual differences in personality trait changes. In childhood, there should be additional passive effects of genetic differences, such that parents provide a rearing environment that is correlated with the genotype of the child. Then, during adolescence and young adulthood, individuals should become more active in selecting into environments that match their genotype. Applying this theory to individual differences in personality change, to the degree that people select into environments that shape their predisposed personality differences, this active process may lead individuals to deviate from a normative personality trajectory and follow their own trajectory. Thus, assuming that the active selection of environments is a particularly powerful source of differential personality trait changes, individual differences in change should be more pronounced in young adulthood than in childhood and adolescence.

Notably, according to this theoretical perspective, there should be significant individual differences in change across the entire lifespan due to other genotype-environment effects beyond active selection.

Although still acknowledging the genetic contribution to personality development, the Neo-Socioanalytic Theory (Roberts & Nickel, 2017) puts a stronger emphasizes on potential environmental influences on personality trait changes. According to this perspective, personality traits remain malleable across the entire lifespan (Bleidorn et al., 2022).

Furthermore, life events are assumed to trigger personality development by leading to changes and investments in new social roles (Bleidorn et al., 2018; Bühler et al., 2024; Lodi-Smith & Roberts, 2007). As life events may differ in their timing, perception, sequencing, and consequences across people, the occurrence of important life events has been assumed to increase individual differences in personality changes (Bleidorn et al., 2020; Haehner, Bleidorn, et al., 2024). Moreover, genetic and environmental influences may interact in shaping personality development, which may also explain why people differ in their personality development even when experiencing similar life events (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014; Kandler & Zapko-Willmes, 2017). Perspectives that highlight the role of environmental causes thus imply individual differences in personality trait changes to be most pronounced in young adulthood and potentially also in old age, because psychologically relevant life experiences, such as graduation, marriage, retirement, or widowhood, tend to occur more frequently in these life phases than in middle adulthood (Elder, 1998; Haehner, Schaefer, et al., 2024; Hutteman et al., 2014; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018).

Empirically, the question of whether and how individual differences in personality trait changes vary across age has received relatively little attention. Using nationallyrepresentative data from 16 to 84 year-old Dutch participants, Schwaba and Bleidorn (2018) found that individual differences in change were most prominent in young adulthood and least prominent in old age for four of the Big Five traits; for neuroticism, individual differences in change remained stable throughout the adult life span. Similarly, across five different representative panel studies, Wright and Jackson (2025) showed that individual differences in personality trait changes tend to decrease with age. Finally, in a study comparing emerging adulthood (16 to 19 years) and young adulthood (26 to 29 years), Pusch et al. (2019) reported that individual differences in personality changes were consistently high for neuroticism and agreeableness but tend to decrease with age for other traits.

In summary, theories of personality development and initial empirical research suggest that individual differences in personality trait changes are more pronounced in adolescence and young adulthood compared to middle and old adulthood (McCrae & Costa, 2008; Roberts & Nickel, 2017; Scarr & McCartney, 1983; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). However, even though theoretical accounts converge on this general prediction, they differ in their claims regarding the precise trajectory of individual differences in personality change across the lifespan (see Figure 2). Furthermore, theoretical accounts often lack explicit statements about individual differences in personality trait changes so that auxiliary assumptions are needed to derive testable hypotheses about personality development (Bleidorn, 2024). Finally, individual studies are limited in that they rarely sample changes across the entire lifespan, providing limited insights into individual differences in personality changes in childhood and old age.

A meta-analytic aggregation of research on individual differences in personality trait change across the lifespan is thus needed to test and refine theoretical predictions about when and for which traits differential personality development is most pronounced. However, when integrating findings across studies, not only age but also the time lag between assessments must be considered in order to disentangle age and time-related differences.

Do Individual Differences in Personality Trait Changes Differ Across Time Lags Between Assessments?

Examining the time lag between assessments in addition to age is important to disentangle whether fluctuations in individual differences in change are due to age or time (Fraley & Roberts, 2005). For example, studies of younger adults may measure personality traits more frequently than studies with older adults, and if time lag and age are not distinguished it may appear that younger people vary less in their patterns of personality change simply because they are measured more frequently. Considering time lag in addition to age be used to test whether individual differences in personality changes decrease, increase, or remain constant over longer time intervals between assessments. This question is relevant to gain insights into set-point theories of personality development.

Set-point theories of personality development posit that personality trait changes are temporary (Ormel et al., 2017). According to a strict set point-model, people may change in their personality traits after experiencing a life event, but these changes should only be temporary as people return to their pre-event personality trait levels (e.g., Haehner, Krämer et al., 2025; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2019). Consequently, individual differences in personality trait changes should emerge across any time interval as some people may currently experience temporary deviations from their set-point while others do not. However, set-point theories would not predict individual differences in change to increase with longer assessment intervals, because temporary set-point deviations are not expected to accumulate over time. In other words, across any given time lag, some people would be expected to temporarily deviate from their personality set-point, implying no association between time lag and individual differences in trait changes.

Similarly, there should be no association between time lag and individual differences in change if measurement error was the only cause of individual differences in personality trait changes, because error is assumed to be randomly distributed over time. That is, to the degree that measurement error contributes to individual differences in personality trait changes, it does so equally across time intervals of, for example, 1, 10 or, 20 years.

In contrast, a positive association between assessment interval and individual differences in personality changes would indicate that these individual differences accumulate, providing substantive information about the nature of lifespan personality development (Roemer et al., 2024). Specifically, this pattern would be in line with the corresponsive principle of personality development (Caspi et al., 2005; Roberts & Nickel, 2017). According to this principle, people select into environments that match their initial trait levels, which are then reinforced by those environments. Over time, these reinforcement processes may lead to diverging trait trajectories across individuals, resulting in a positive association between time lag and individual differences in personality change. Existing evidence on personality trait change supports this possibility, showing that both rank-order of and mean-level changes in personality traits accumulate over time (Bleidorn et al., 2022).

To date, direct evidence for associations between the assessment interval and individual differences in personality trait changes is missing. A meta-analytic integration of research on individual differences will enable a robust examination of this relationship, while taking into account the role of age and other relevant moderators.

Do Individual Differences in Personality Trait Changes Differ Across Methodological Moderators?

In addition to age and time lag between assessments, we explored the role of other potentially relevant moderators that may shape the degree to which people differ in their personality trait trajectories. Consistent with other meta-analyses on personality development (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Haehner, Buecker, et al., 2024), we focused on publication year, sample, and assessment characteristics.

Publication Year. The methods used to assess and model personality trait change have evolved over the past decades (Bleidorn, 2024; Soto & John, 2017). As such, more recent research may be more sensitive to individual differences in personality changes than earlier studies.

Sample Characteristics.

We examined three sample characteristics as moderators of individual differences in personality trait changes: gender composition, representativeness, and region. First, existing research indicated that rank-order stabilities and mean-level changes do not differ across genders (Bleidorn et al., 2022), but the association of gender with individual differences in personality trait changes has not been comprehensively examined.

Second, as representative samples may be more heterogenous than convenience or student samples, individual differences in personality trait change could be more pronounced in representative samples. Alternatively, it may be that individual differences in change are more pronounced in convenience sample studies that were specifically designed to track the personality trajectories of participants experiencing life transitions or other developmentally relevant experiences.

Third, nearly all research on personality development has been conducted in samples from Western societies, so that relatively little is known about personality development in non-Western samples (Bleidorn et al., 2022). To better understand possible cultural differences in personality development, we explored whether the region of data collection is related to individual differences in personality trait changes, with the caveat that nearly all variation is among different Western cultures.

Assessment Characteristics.

In line with existing meta-analyses on personality development, we examined six assessment characteristics as moderators of individual differences in personality trait changes: mode of assessment, breadth of the assessed trait, reliability, whether the assessed trait is intended to capture maladaptive features of personality, coding of trait assessment, and whether the measure explicitly or only indirectly assessed Big Five traits.

First, we explored whether the mode of assessment (self-report vs. other reports) was associated with individual differences in personality trait change. It is possible that observers are less sensitive to subtle changes in a person's personality, so that individual differences in change may be larger in self-report studies.

Second, we investigated whether individual differences in personality trait changes differed between broad domain-level traits and narrow facet-level traits. Bleidorn et al. (2022)found that the rank-orders of personality traits are less stable for facet-level than domain-level traits. Relatedly, individual differences in narrow trait changes may be more pronounced due to individual differences in specific environmental demands.

Third, we tested whether the reliability of a measure was related to individual differences in personality trait change. Even if there were no true individual differences in personality changes, unreliability of personality assessment over time may create spurious individual differences in personality trait changes, as measurement error may lead to deviations from the average trajectory.

Fourth, we examined differences between measures assessing normal-range personality traits (e.g., conscientiousness) and measures assessing maladaptive traits (e.g., disinhibition). People may be more motivated to change maladaptive traits when levels are high, and maladaptive traits seem to be more sensitive to change when facing challenging environments, so that individual differences in change may be more pronounced for maladaptive than adaptative traits (Haehner, Sleep, et al., 2024).

Fifth, we tested differences in personality development between scales in which high values are socially desirable (e.g., emotional stability, agreeableness) and scales in which low values are socially desirable (e.g., neuroticism, disagreeableness). To the degree that individual differences in change differ between those scales, response biases, such as acquiescence or socially desirable responding, may have influenced results on differential personality development.

Sixth, as most modern research on personality traits is organized around the Big Five taxonomy of personality traits, and given that the Big Five represents a comprehensive framework within which trait measures can be organized, we explored whether individual differences in personality trait changes differed between measures that were designed versus not designed to specifically assess the Big Five personality traits.

The Present Study

In the present meta-analysis, we examined the development of individual differences in personality trait change across time lags and age. Importantly, we used an approach that allowed us to include both studies that directly examined individual differences in personality trait change across the lifespan as well as studies that provided the necessary descriptive statistics but did not themselves estimate individual differences in change. We addressed four research questions for each Big Five personality trait: (1) What is the magnitude of individual differences in personality trait change? (2) Do individual differences in personality trait changes differ across age? (3) Do individual differences in personality trait changes differ depending on the time interval between assessments? (4) Do individual differences in personality trait changes differ across methodological moderators such as publication year, sample, or assessment characteristics?

Consistent with theories of personality development and initial empirical evidence, we tested two confirmatory hypotheses (McCrae & Costa, 2008; Roberts & Nickel, 2017; Scarr & McCartney, 1983; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). First, we expected that individual differences in personality trait change are more pronounced in young than in middle and old adulthood (Hypothesis 1). Second, we hypothesized that individual differences in personality trait changes are more pronounced over longer retest intervals (Hypothesis 2).

Data Analysis

Data analysis for this meta-analysis was conducted in R (Version 4.3.2) using the metafor package (Viechtbauer, 2010). To be comparable to existing meta-analyses, we closely followed the analytic strategy of Bleidorn et al. (2022) and Haehner, Buecker, et al. (2024).

Meta-Analytic Procedure

We estimated random-effects meta-analyses with effect sizes being weighted based on the inverse of the sampling variance so that more precise effect sizes received more weight in the analyses. As several studies contributed more than one effect size estimate, we used the three-step procedure recommended by Viechtbauer (2010) and Pustejovsky and Tipton (2022) to deal with the dependency of effect sizes. First, to estimate the relationship between dependent effect sizes, we estimated the approximate variance-covariance matrix of (2) dependent effect sizes based on working assumptions on their dependencies. As done by Haehner, Buecker, et al. (2024), we assumed that trait assessments with different retest intervals belonging to the same sample have an auto-correlation of  = .61 (Bleidorn et al., 2022) and that different traits belonging to the same Big Five domain are correlated with 1 = .48 (Pace & Brannick, 2010). Finally, we assumed that different assessment modes of the same trait have a correlation of 2 = .55 within the same sample (Connolly et al., 2007).

Importantly, while accurate working assumptions increase the efficacy of the meta-analytic estimations, the meta-analytic estimates remain unbiased even if these working assumptions are incorrect (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022). Second, based on the approximate variancecovariance matrix of Step 1, we then estimated a multilevel meta-analytic model with effect sizes nested in studies. Third, using the R package clubSandwich (Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2018), we estimated cluster-robust standard errors of the meta-analytic estimates.

To address Research Question 1, we estimated a meta-analytic model without any moderators to receive an overall estimate of the magnitude of individual differences in personality traits. We had preregistered to conduct all analyses in a trait-specific way (i.e., one model for each Big Five trait) to ease interpretability compared to a model that includes trait category as a moderator. However, to also explore whether individual differences in personality changes differed significantly across traits, we included trait category as a moderator to the meta-analytic model in an exploratory analysis.

Age and Time Lag

As a second step, we examined the relevance of our key moderators age and time lag between assessments in a meta-regression framework (Research Questions 2 and 3), which we included simultaneously in the meta-analytic models. For time lag, we subtracted 1 from the original time lag so that the intercept of the meta-analytic models describes the magnitude of individual differences in personality changes over a time interval of one year (Haehner, Buecker, et al., 2024). This transformation thus ensures that our meta-analytic estimates are comparable to other meta-analyses on lifespan development that estimate yearly change rates (e.g., Buecker et al., 2023; Orth et al., 2018).

For age, we used a piecewise approach to examine different functional forms of the relationship between age and individual differences in personality changes (see Bleidorn et al., 2022 and Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014 for similar procedures). To do so, we divided the linear age variable into different age bins. For the most fine-grained partitioning, we divided age into 5-year bins (e.g., 1-5, 6-10) including a catchall bin for people over 75 years. For example, a sample with a mean age of 33 years received a value of 5 for the first six age bins, a value of 3 for the seventh age bin describing ages 31 to 35 years, and a value of 0 for all other age bins. This procedure allows different age bins to differ in their trajectory of individual differences in personality changes. However, as a model with 16 age bins is relatively complex, we then examined whether merging certain age bins reduces model fit.

For example, we compared the fit of a meta-analytic model with one larger age bin for ages 1 to 15 years with a model with three different age bins for this age range (1-5, 6-10, 11-15). In total, we estimated and compared all possible combinations of separating and merging the five-year age bins between the most complex model (16 five-year age bins) and the simplest model (one large age bin, corresponding to the linear age variable), resulting in a total of 32,768 models for each trait. To compare model fit and to identify the best fitting model, we inspected AIC and BIC. Specifically, for each personality trait, we compared the 10 models with the lowest AIC and BIC to examine whether age shows similar relations to individual differences in personality changes across traits (Haehner, Buecker, et al., 2024).

In each meta-analytic model, we examined the average effect size (i.e., the intercept), the effects of moderators (i.e., the regression coefficients), and the heterogeneity of effect sizes (i.e., random effects). The multilevel meta-analytic procedure allowed us to estimate the heterogeneity of effect sizes both within studies (𝜎 1 2 ) and between studies (𝜎 2 2 ). Individual Differences in Personality Trait Changes Across Time Lags and Age

To examine how individual differences in personality trait changes wax and wane across the lifespan, we included the time interval between personality assessments and the mean age of samples as moderators in our meta-analytic models. As described above, we examined more than 30,000 possible ways of summarizing and separating age bins to identify the functional form of the relationship between age and individual differences in personality changes. We selected the best-fitting models of this piecewise approach based on AIC and BIC and compared the findings of models with similar model fit but different sets of age bins (see Section 4 of the https://osf.io/7wk9y/files/3sw2c).

As can be seen in Table 6 and Figure 6, age showed a similar relationship to individual differences in personality changes across all Big Five traits. For agreeableness and openness, a model with two age-related variables (Age 1 to 30 and Age 30+) fitted the data best.

Individual differences in personality trait changes significantly decreased for both traits until age 30 (agreeableness: b = -0.023, p < .001; openness: b = -0.015, p = .014) and then remained flat throughout the remainder of the lifespan (agreeableness: b = 0.001, p = .827; openness: b = 0.001, p = .740). Similarly, for extraversion and conscientiousness, a model with two age-related variables fitted the data best but with slightly different inflection points.

For conscientiousness, individual differences in personality trait changes decreased until age 40 (b = -0.013, p = .001) and then remained flat until old age (b = -0.001, p = .826), while for extraversion individual differences in change decreased until age 55 (b = -0.008, p < .001) and then remained flat (b = 0.004, p = .284). Finally, for neuroticism, we found that individual differences in change decreased until age 25 (b = -0.016, p < .001), continued to decrease, but less rapidly, between ages 25 and 75 (b = -0.004, p = .044), and were flat after age 75 (b = 0.017, p = .180).

The general pattern of decreases in the magnitude of individual differences in personality trait changes throughout childhood and young adulthood followed by stability in the magnitude of individual differences in change during old age was consistent across models with similar fit but different age bins. Overall, these findings were in line with our Hypothesis 1 that individual differences in trait changes are more pronounced in young adulthood than in middle and old adulthood. However, the largest individual differences in change were not observed in young adulthood but in childhood.

Regarding time lags, we found that a longer assessment interval was related to more pronounced individual differences in personality trait changes for agreeableness (b = 0.027, p = .009), extraversion (b = 0.018, p = .001), neuroticism (b = 0.017, p < .001), and openness (b = 0.012, p = .024). For conscientiousness, the relationship between time lag and individual differences in personality changes was not statistically significant (b = 0.020, p = .071). These results were thus largely consistent with our Hypothesis 2 that individual differences in personality trait changes are more pronounced over longer assessment intervals. Methodological Moderators and Individual Differences in Personality Changes

As next step, we tested methodological moderators like the publication year, the region of data collection, or the assessment mode by adding them to the best-fitting piecewise model identified in the previous step. As preregistered, we conducted these analyses separately for each of the Big Five traits. However, because the age-related trajectories of individual differences in change were similar across Big Five traits, we additionally conducted an exploratory analysis with moderators combined across traits.

As can be seen in Table 7, the results slightly differed across traits (see Section 5 of the https://osf.io/7wk9y/files/3sw2c for graphical illustrations). The most consistent effect was that individual differences in change were smaller in magnitude for traits assessing the Big Five compared to traits not designed to assess the Big Five personality traits. This difference was found for agreeableness (b = -0.183, p = .001), conscientiousness (b = -0.164, p = .003), extraversion (b = -0.183, p = .002), and in the combined model (b = -0.138, p = .005), but not for openness (b = -0.177, p = .074) and neuroticism (b = -0.030, p = .589).

Furthermore, individual differences in changes in conscientiousness (b = 0.184, p = .001), extraversion (b = 0.212, p = .001), and openness (b = 0.400, p = .010) were more pronounced for facets than domains, although this difference was not significant in the combined model (b = 0.126, p = .052). Moreover, in the combined model, we found that individual differences in personality changes were lower in representative samples compared to non-representative samples (b = -0.119, p = .035). In each of the trait-specific models, these findings were similar in direction but not statistically significant possibly due to their lower power.

Finally, there were some additional trait-specific significant effects such as individual differences in conscientiousness being lower for more reliable trait assessments, but since these effects were only found for single traits, we interpret them with caution (see Table 7 for details). Publication Bias: Small-Sample Bias

We used two strategies to examine whether our results are influenced by the publication of results stemming from studies with small samples. First, we visually inspected funnel plots which showed that effect sizes were systematically distributed around the population effect size. Second, we examined the association between the standard deviation of effect sizes and effect size estimates. This test was not significant for any of the Big Five traits (see Section 6 of the https://osf.io/7wk9y/files/3sw2c for details). Together, these findings suggest that our results were not systematically biased by small-study effects. Notably, heterogeneity of effect sizes across studies (e.g., as reflected by dispersion of effect sizes in the funnel plots) complicated drawing definite conclusions and other forms of publication bias could still be present in the data. However, given that most studies included in this metaanalysis were not designed to examine individual differences in personality trait changes, it seems unlikely that the present findings effects are affected by publication bias.

Discussion

Existing meta-analyses on personality development have provided convincing evidence that the rank-orders and mean-levels of personality traits change across the entire lifespan (Anusic & Schimmack, 2016; Bleidorn et al., 2022; Roberts et al., 2006; Roberts & DelVecchio, 2000). However, a thorough examination of individuals differences in personality trait changes across the lifespan is required to better understand the causes and course of personality development. In the present meta-analysis, we used data from 229 studies and over 200,000 participants to examine how individual differences in personality trait changes develop across the lifespan. We found that there are significant individual differences in change in all Big Five traits, with people differing the most in their neuroticism trajectories, and least in their agreeableness and openness trajectories. Furthermore, for all traits, individual differences in change were most pronounced in childhood, decreased during young and middle adulthood, and then remained flat throughout the remainder of the lifespan.

Finally, individual differences in personality trait changes were stronger over longer retest intervals, for facet-level traits, and for measures not designed to specifically assess Big Five traits.

Magnitude of Individual Differences in Change

Existing research has consistently shown that people differ significantly in their personality development across the lifespan (Damian et al., 2019; Denissen et al., 2019; Roemer et al., 2024; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018; Wright & Jackson, 2025). The present metaanalysis confirmed this finding and extended it in two ways.

First, all Big Five domains showed significant individual differences in change (SDPOMP = 0.13 to 0.15), suggesting that there are sources of personality trait changes that lead to an idiosyncratic development in all Big Five traits (Table 5). However, our exploratory analysis also revealed small but significant differences across trait domains: Neuroticism showed the most pronounced individual differences in change, whereas individual differences in change in openness and agreeableness were lower than for the other traits (see Figure 5).

These subtle trait differences raise the question of whether there are sources of personality development that are more potent to change specific trait domains. For example, in the case of neuroticism, individual differences in trait changes may be more pronounced because this trait seems to be more responsive to strong environmental influences such as negative events or psychotherapy than the other Big Five traits, potentially leading to differential patterns of personality development (Bühler et al., 2024; Haehner, Bleidorn, et al., 2024; Haehner, Sleep, Miller, et al., 2024; Roberts et al., 2017). Conversely, there may be fewer life events and experiences that have differential effects on openness and agreeableness.

Second, the present meta-analysis provided benchmarks for the effect sizes of individual differences in personality trait changes that can be used for comparisons across samples, study designs, or constructs. To facilitate such comparisons, we developed an interactive https://personalityresearch.shinyapps.io/Shiny/ that can be used to calculate individual differences in personality trait changes and compare estimates with the present meta-analytic results. Such comparisons could be used to investigate whether individual differences in change are higher or lower in specific samples, which, in turn, may advance our understanding of the sources of personality development. For example, examining individual differences in change in samples that recently experienced an important life event may help to evaluate whether life events are a source of differential personality development (see Figure 2).

Similar comparisons could also be useful to better understand the effects of personality trait interventions (Haehner, Wright, & Bleidorn, 2024; Hudson, 2021; Stieger et al., 2021).

For example, significant individual differences in personality change in response to interventions would highlight the need for personalized approaches that account for personal or contextual factors that moderate intervention effects (Haehner, Wright, et al., 2025; Modersitzki et al., 2025; Nahum-Shani et al., 2015). Finally, a comparison of the magnitude of individual differences in change across related constructs offers insights into their developmental similarity and conceptual overlap (Lawson & Robins, 2021). For example, recent research has compared the development of personality traits and personality functioning, including individual differences in change in these constructs, to evaluate their conceptual similarity (Haehner, Sleep, et al., 2024). In general, similar change patterns across different change metrics may indicate the influence of common factors that shape the development of these constructs (Weber et al., 2023; Wright et al., 2026).

In summary, the present meta-analysis indicates that people differ significantly in their personality development and it provides effect sizes regarding the magnitude of the differential development of the different Big Five traits that may serve as benchmarks for future research on the sources of these individual differences.

Development of Individual Differences in Personality Changes Across the Lifespan

In addition to quantifying the overall magnitude of individual differences in personality trait changes, a second goal of this meta-analysis was to evaluate the development of individual differences in personality trait changes across time lag and age.

Individual Differences in Personality Changes Across Age

The identification of life stages during which individual differences in personality trait changes are most pronounced offers insights into the relative importance of unique versus common causes of personality development in different life phases (Bleidorn, 2024). We expected individual differences in personality changes to be more pronounced in young adulthood than in middle and old adulthood. Even though this pattern emerged for most traits, individual differences in personality trait changes were most pronounced in childhood and adolescence (see Figure 6). These findings suggest that childhood and adolescence are life phases characterized by relatively more idiosyncratic personality development, in which individuals deviate more from the average trait trajectory than during adulthood.

The development of individual differences in personality trait changes across the lifespan did not exactly fit any of the theoretically proposed trajectories (see Figure 2). For example, a genetically driven personality development program that is completed by age 30 as suggested by the Five-Factor Theory (McCrae & Costa, 2008) is not suited to explain why individual differences in personality trait changes were significant across the entire lifespan.

Likewise, extant theories that focus on environmental causes of personality development offer no explanation for why individual differences in personality trait changes were most pronounced in childhood and adolescence (Bleidorn et al., 2020; Wrzus & Roberts, 2017).

Theoretical accounts emphasizing the relevance of both genetic environmental causes of personality development seem to be best suited to explain the observed lifespan trajectory of individual differences in personality trait changes (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014; Roberts & Jackson, 2008; Roberts & Nickel, 2017). Combined with existing evidence on mean-level and rank-order changes, the present findings refine these theoretical perspectives in two ways.

Specifically, the pronounced but decreasing levels of individual differences in personality change in childhood that coincide with increases in rank-order stability suggest that there are sources of personality development, including genetic differences, that unfold in different ways and at a different pace across children but that these sources stabilize in the first life decade (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014). Furthermore, stable levels of individual differences in change from middle adulthood onward paired with mean-level increases in emotional stability and decreases in conscientiousness suggest that age-graded life experiences like parenthood in middle adulthood or cognitive decline or retirement in later adulthood may trigger directionally similar personality trait changes across people.

However, there are at least three caveats related to these interpretations. First, auxiliary assumptions were needed to derive testable predictions about the lifespan development of individual differences in personality trait changes from existing personality development theories (Figure 2). The present conclusions thus depend on the validity of these assumptions.

Second, there are systematic differences in the assessment of personality traits across the lifespan. For example, in childhood, other-reports of temperament are frequently used to assess personality traits (e.g., Bornstein et al., 2019; Holzman, 2018; Lawson et al., 2023) whereas self-report questionnaires are more common in adulthood (e.g., Borghuis et al., 2017; Haehner, Sleep, et al., 2024; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018). These methodological differences may have contributed to the observed age-related differences in individual differences in personality trait changes. However, they are unlikely to explain them entirely as our moderator analysis showed that assessment characteristics are only weakly related to individual differences in personality trait changes (see below for details).

Third, knowledge about the genetic and environmental contributions provide limited insights into the specific factors that drive personality development (Wagner et al., 2020). For example, within the domain of environmental factors, individual differences in personality changes could result from differences in the occurrences of important life events, differences in the ways people react to these events, and differences in how these events interact with personal dispositions (Briley & Tucker-Drob, 2014; Bühler et al., 2024; Haehner, Bleidorn, et al., 2024; Schwaba et al., 2023).

Together, these considerations highlight the need for both theoretical and empirical work to better understand and explain personality trait changes across the lifespan. The present meta-analysis may guide these efforts in three ways. First, our study illustrates that existing verbal theories of personality development lack the precision to derive testable hypotheses about the causes and course of personality trait changes (Bleidorn, 2024).

Formalized theoretical models on personality development that use mathematical expressions to describe predictors, mechanisms, and outcomes of personality trait changes would help to overcome this limitation (Eronen & Romeijn, 2020; Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019).

However, such models require a broad set of robust empirical phenomena constraining the theoretical space by limiting the degrees of freedom in setting up these models (Eronen & Bringmann, 2021). Our findings are an important contribution to the development of a formalized personality development theory because they provide robust and novel insights into the temporal unfolding of personality trait changes across the lifespan (Bleidorn, 2024).

For example, personality development theories need to explain why rank-order changes stabilize around age 25 while individual differences in change stabilize between age 30 to 55.

Second, our meta-analysis highlights assumptions that are necessary to translate personality development theories into predictions about the development of individual differences in personality trait changes. For example, most research and theory to date has operated under the assumption that life events increase individual differences in personality trait changes (Denissen et al., 2019; Haehner, Bleidorn, et al., 2024; Haehner, Wright, Krämer, et al., 2024; Schwaba & Bleidorn, 2018; Van Scheppingen & Leopold, 2020).

However, there is also some contrary evidence that individual differences in change are constant in magnitude before and after events (Jackson & Beck, 2021). Future research is needed to clarify these conflicting findings. The results of this meta-analysis may facilitate such research by providing benchmarks for comparisons and insights about how study design and sample characteristics impact findings.

Third, the present results inform the design of future studies on the specific causes of personality development. For instance, observational research on the sources of individual differences in change would be particularly fruitful in childhood and adolescence, given the larger magnitude of individual differences in personality trait changes in these as compared to later life stages. Relatedly, research investigating trait differences may ideally focus on early middle adulthood (age 30 to age 45) when individual differences in change have leveled off in some but not all traits. Such research could, for instance, help to identify life experiences that cause differential personality development in specific traits.

Individual Differences in Personality Changes Across Time Lags

Beyond age, we examined the time interval between assessments as a moderator of individual differences in personality trait changes (Table 6). In line with our hypothesis, we found that individual differences in personality changes increased over longer retest-intervals.

This finding is inconsistent with strict set-point models of personality development that postulate that personality changes are only temporary (Ormel et al., 2017). If personality trait changes were only temporary and evenly distributed across time and individuals, this developmental pattern would have implied a null relationship between assessment interval and individual differences in personality trait changes.

Furthermore, the positive association between time lag and individual differences in personality trait changes suggests that the observed individual differences in change are not just driven by measurement error but that differential personality development accumulates over time (Roberts & Nickel, 2017; Roemer et al., 2024). This finding is practically relevant as it illustrates that individual differences in personality changes do not vanish over time but that they are meaningful to our understanding of personality development (Bleidorn, 2024; Bleidorn et al., 2021; Schwaba et al., 2023). Finally, the time interval between assessments should thus be considered when comparing the magnitude of individual differences across studiesif a study does not find significant individual differences in change, a lack of statistical power or a short timeframe between assessments may be the culprit.

Methodological Moderators

Individual differences in personality trait changes also differed depending on some assessment characteristics. Specifically, we found that individual differences in personality trait changes tended to be stronger for narrow, facet-level traits that were not designed to examine the Big Five trait taxonomy (Table 7). These findings are in line with the metaanalytic results on rank-order and mean-level stability of personality traits, suggesting that broad Big Five domains tend to be more stable than other, narrower traits (Bleidorn et al., 2022). A possible explanation for this result could be the level of aggregation: Aggregating different facets of a trait may mask the differential development of these facets so that broad traits show less pronounced individual differences in change. However, the effect sizes of almost all methodological moderators were smaller than the effects of age and time lag, suggesting that the consideration of age and time lag is more important to understand the development of individual differences in personality changes.

Limitations

The present meta-analysis has several limitations. First, although we were able to draw on an extensive set of studies on personality development with more than 2,000 effect sizes and 200,000 participants, we still had to exclude a relatively large share of studies from existing meta-analytic datasets on personality development. Specifically, in older studies, the minimum and the maximum score of personality trait measures, which were required for the present meta-analysis, were rarely reported. To mitigate this problem, we contacted 130 authors to request the missing information butagain particularly for older studiesinformation on the minimum and maximum personality score was frequently not retrievable.

Second, the results of the present meta-analysis are affected by limitations of the studies it included. Because existing research on personality development has mostly been conducted in Europe and North America, it is an open question whether our findings generalize to personality development in all cultures (Bleidorn et al., 2022; Haehner, Naudé, et al., 2025). We tested whether individual differences in change differed across cultural contexts, but this test was underpowered to detect differences on par with the effect sizes of our other moderator variables (see Table 7). Future research on personality development in non-Western countries is needed to better understand which findings on personality development are culturally universal and which are specific to Western countries (Henrich et al., 2010; Van De Vijver, 2013).

Third, the majority of studies included in the present meta-analysis were not designed to examine individual differences in personality changes. As a result, we were not able to examine some theoretically interesting moderators of personality development, such as the occurrence of life events, because they were not assessed in the studies included here.

Relatedly, given our focus on personality development in the general population, we excluded studies with clinical samples and interventions. Future meta-analytic research may extend their focus to also examine the role of individual differences in change in these populations and under these conditions. Relatedly, we did not rate the methodological quality of included primary studies but instead examined relevant sample and assessment characteristics (e.g., reliability of the personality trait measure) as meta-analytic moderators, as we deemed this approach more appropriate for naturalistic longitudinal studies than common methods checklist.

Fourth, our meta-analytic approach relied on effect sizes that were calculated based on the descriptive statistics of personality traits assessed at two measurement occasions. In doing so, we were able to incorporate a large number of studies, including those that were not explicitly designed to assess personality trait change. Another advantage of this approach is that the estimates can be easily compared with those from existing and future research.

However, a potential disadvantage of this approach is that the estimates based on two assessment waves may be less reliable than estimates of individual differences in personality change derived from growth curve models and multi-level models that rely on multiple assessment waves. For a meta-analytic aggregation of individual differences in change estimates from those models, standardized analyses and reporting practices would be required.

Fifth, by integrating effect sizes across studies, we could only investigate the effects of moderators at an aggregate level (e.g., the association between the percentage of female participants in a sample and individual differences in personality trait changes). Individual participant data meta-analysis could be a useful step for future research to gain more finegrained insights into the conditions under which individual differences in personality trait changes emerge.

Conclusions

People differ in their personality development across the lifespan. The present metaanalysis quantified the degree of these individual differences and provides benchmarks for future research (https://personalityresearch.shinyapps.io/Shiny/). Individual differences in personality trait changes were most pronounced in childhood and adolescence, decreased in young adulthood, but were nonetheless significant across the entire lifespan. These findings indicate that idiosyncratic sources of personality development are most relevant early in life, likely reflecting a combination of genetic and environmental sources of personality development. Furthermore, individual differences in trait changes tended to increase over longer assessment intervals, suggesting an accumulation of these differences over time.

Overall, the present meta-analysis provides an initial test of different theoretical accounts of personality development and a starting point for a more systematic investigation of internal and external sources of individual differences in personality changes and for the development of formalized theoretical models of personality development.

(2024) each conducted systematic literature searches on personality development spanning different publication years: Roberts and colleagues covered the literature published until January 2005, Bleidorn and colleagues included studies published between January 2005 and January 2020, and Haehner and colleagues covered research published between January 2020 and October 2023. Merging their meta-analytic datasets-as done by Haehner, Buecker, et al. (2024)-provides comprehensive descriptive information on personality development from primary empirical research. These meta-analytic datasets were based on systematic literature searches on PsycINFO using the same search string: (personality OR trait OR temperament) AND (test-retest OR longitudinal). Furthermore, studies were only included in the meta-analytic datasets if they fulfilled the following inclusion criteria: (1) Empirical, quantitative design (i.e., no reviews, comments, or qualitative studies) (2) Conducted with human subjects (3) Written in English (4) Longitudinal design (i.e., two or more assessments of the same sample) (5) Retest-interval of 6 months or more (6) Assessment of personality traits 1 at two or more measurement occasions (7) Nonclinical sample and no intervention (8) Homogenous age of the sample (i.e., SD of age at baseline ≤ 3) In addition, we only included studies in the present meta-analysis that reported (a) the standard deviation of a personality trait measure at baseline (T1) and a follow-up assessment (T2), (b) the minimum and maximum of the personality trait measure, (c) the age at baseline, (d) the time lag between assessments or age at T2, and (e) the sample size at T1. As information on minimum and maximum of personality trait measures was not included in the meta-analytic datasets of Roberts et al. (2006) and Bleidorn et al. (2022), we added this information to their meta-analytic datasets in an additional coding step if available (see next section for details). If information on the minimum and maximum was not provided, we contacted authors to request the missing information or otherwise excluded the study. We contacted authors of 130 studies; 51 replied but only 23 authors were able to retrieve information on the minimum and maximum of the personality trait measure.
