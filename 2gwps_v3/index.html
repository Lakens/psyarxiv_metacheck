Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Caregivers often face stressful multitasking situations in which household-or work-related tasks compete with engaging with their young child. Caregivers' experiences of stress can affect how they interact with their children and may also be transmitted to the child. This "stress contagion" may be particularly pronounced with caregivers who have difficulties in regulating stress responses. In the current study, we examined how inducing stress in 62 mothers shaped the dyadic interaction (expression of facial affect and cardiovascular activity) with their 6-38-month-old children, and how stress-induced changes varied depending on self-reported maternal emotion regulation difficulties and child age. Mothers' and children's positive and negative facial affect, inter-beat intervals (IBIs) and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), as well as synchrony in each measure, were compared between two-minute face-to-face interactions before and after a stress induction in the mother (pre-and poststress). Maternal stress impacted the dyad differently depending on the child's age. In interactions with younger children, both mothers and children showed less positive facial expressions and were less synchronized in their expressions post-versus pre-stress. Older children, in contrast, showed stronger physiological arousal (i.e., decrease in IBIs and RSA), but more positive facial expressions post-stress.

In addition, mothers with more emotion regulation difficulties expressed more negative affect, and their heart rates were less synchronized with their children's postversus pre-stress. Both child age and mothers' regulatory abilities are thus shaping the impact of acute maternal stress on mother-child interaction quality and should be considered in interventions on parental stress

[div-01]

Caregivers often navigate complex environments where direct childcare competes with household or work-related tasks. In the Netherlands, childcare is still primarily provided by mothers, despite advances towards gender equality in the division of work and childcare (Statistics Netherlands [CBS], 2022). Caregivers' frequent engagement in "mental labour", such as planning groceries, preparing for appointments, or engaging in social situations while attending to their child, has been associated with increased stress in mothers, among other negative outcomes (Offer & Schneider, 2011; Reich-Stiebert et al., 2023). Zooming in on how maternal stress due to multi-tasking influences the dynamics of mother-child interactions could provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the transmission of stress and negative consequences thereof in early development.

Synchrony in Mother-Child Interactions

Mother-child dynamics are often governed by bio-behavioural synchrony, defined as the temporal coordination of physiology (i.e., heart rate or respiratory sinus arrhythmia [RSA]) and behaviour (i.e., facial expressions) between interaction partners (Feldman, 2007). Within a caregiving context, synchrony is often interpreted as a reflection of co-regulation (Bornstein & Esposito, 2023; Mata et al., 2026). For example, on the level of non-verbal emotional expressions, synchrony in positive facial affect has been proposed to serve the regulation of positive arousal in play situations (Feldman, 2003).

Synchrony can also be observed in measures of the autonomic nervous system, such as cardiovascular signals, as a physiological indicator of arousal and arousal regulation. RSA synchrony, that is coordination in adaptive irregularities in the cardiac (sinus) rhythm based on respiration, typically acts as index for parasympathetic co-regulation (Pinto & Figueiredo, 2023). Especially in the behavioural domain, higher levels of synchrony in mother-child interactions have been associated with more positive child outcomes in the long-term (Leclère et al., 2014). In the physiological domain, a more complex model is proposed in which parents are required to move in and out of synchrony with their children to regulate their children's (physiological) state (Montirosso & McGlone, 2020). In highstress contexts, more synchrony may instead facilitate stress contagion (Engert et al., 2019). Here, the mother's "fight-or-flight" response, characterized by sympathetic activation and reduced parasympathetic regulation, is transmitted to the child (Blasberg et al., 2023), overriding the dyad's ability to co-regulate.

Effect of Stress on Interacting Mothers and Children

Most studies on the effect of stress on mother-child interactions used the Still Face Paradigm to induce stress in the child (Tronick et al., 1978). This paradigm demonstrates that infants respond to maternal withdrawal with reduced positive affect, heightened heart rate, and decreased RSA (Busuito et al., 2019; Jones-Mason et al., 2018; Mesman et al., 2009). Moreover, mother-child synchrony in parasympathetic activity has been shown to decrease after inducing stress in children (Li & Lunkenheimer, 2025; Stallworthy et al., 2024), whereas synchrony in positive affect increases, potentially buffering stress and re-establishing affiliation (Somers et al., 2025).

Despite the often-overwhelming demands of everyday life and parenting, only a handful of studies have induced stress in mothers and captured its transmission to their children. These typically used a modified version of the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST; Kirschbaum et al., 1993), in which mothers prepare and deliver a speech in front of evaluators, resembling a job interview. This psychosocial stress induction indeed results in lower self-reported positive affect and higher selfreported negative affect, as well as higher cortisol levels and heightened cardiac sympathetic arousal in the mothers, reflecting a stress response (Blasberg et al., 2023; Waters et al., 2014 Waters et al., , 2017)). Importantly, in two studies with 12-14-month-old infants, the stress was induced while the dyad was separated, meaning infants were neither in the stress situation themselves nor exposed to the mothers' immediate stress responses. Still, infants showed greater cardiac sympathetic synchrony with their mothers in an interaction following the stress induction compared to beforehand, likely indicating stress contagion (Waters et al., 2014 (Waters et al., , 2017)). Furthermore, greater RSA synchrony, reflecting parasympathetic coregulation, was only observed in the post-interaction for the control condition (mothers who relaxed), not after the stress induction, suggesting that stressed mothers lacked the regulatory resources to coregulate their infants (Waters et al., 2017).

Because the dyads were separated during the stressor, these findings only capture contagion following the actual stressor. The question of what happens when a mother manages a social-evaluative threat while interacting with her child has been studied with school-aged children: children aged 8-12 who observed their mothers complete a TSST showed greater cardiac sympathetic synchrony with their mothers, increased personal distress, and heightened cortisol release relative to children in a control condition, indicating stress contagion via direct observation (Blasberg et al., 2023). Whether younger children, who rely more heavily on co-regulation, are similarly or even more affected after directly witnessing their mothers' stress responses remains an open question that the current study addresses.

Child Age and Maternal Difficulties in Emotion Regulation

A crucial factor determining the dynamics of mother-child interactions is the age of the child.

Depending on maturational processes, communication shifts from autonomic changes and linked behavioural expressions in early infancy to a more diverse behavioural repertoire and symbolic communication in toddlerhood (Lipschits & Geva, 2024). Consequently, young infants rely heavily on caregivers for coregulation due to their limited ability for autonomic regulation (Porges & Furman, 2011), whereas self-regulatory abilities grow stronger in later infancy and toddlerhood (Posner & Rothbart, 2000). While these developmental changes would suggest a decrease of mother-child synchrony on the autonomic level, together with an increase on the behavioural level throughout the first year of life, evidence remains mixed. Reviews of past studies did not find evidence for an influence of age on the strength of behavioural and affective synchrony between 3-9-month-old infants (Golds et al., 2022), nor on RSA synchrony from infancy to adolescence (Miller et al., 2023).

However, these null findings stem from studies with narrow age ranges, and differences in protocols and samples may obscure age effects across studies. Examining dyads across a wider developmental window during an acute stressor is therefore necessary to capture how age shapes stress contagion and co-regulation.

Beyond child age, the resources that mothers can employ during interactions depend on their own regulatory abilities. Maternal difficulties in emotion regulation, which can also be observed in anxiety, depression, and various other mental health conditions (Zitzmann et al., 2024) may have detrimental effects on co-regulation between mothers and children. Crucially, the impact of maternal emotion regulation depends on the modality of synchrony being measured. When looking at physiological co-regulation, mothers who report greater emotion regulation difficulties show more dysregulated arousal during caregiving (Leerkes et al., 2020) and synchronize less strongly with their children in RSA following a stressor (Gao et al., 2023), compared to mothers with less difficulty in emotion regulation, indicating that they might have been unable to co-regulate. When looking at facial expressions, however, mothers with more difficulties in emotion regulation seem to synchronize more strongly with their children in their affect, both before and after stress induction (Lotzin et al., 2016).

This highlights an important distinction: while successful co-regulation is reflected by high physiological (RSA) synchrony after an acute stressor, high affective synchrony may only be adaptive if synchrony in positive affect acts as a stress buffer, and not if mothers are generally overresponsive to children's expression (Mitsven et al., 2022), including their distress. Moreover, research needs to be extended to situations in which stress is directly induced in the mother, and not the child, to study the effect of regulating mothers' personal distress on mother-child synchrony.

Objectives of the Current Study

In the current study, we examined how acute maternal stress changes the behavioural and physiological dynamics of interactions between mothers and their young children. We employed a shortened version of the TSST protocol (Birkett, 2011) to induce stress in the mother while her child was present, and recorded video and electrocardiograms during face-to-face interactions before and after the stress induction. Because the study was not preregistered, we distinguish between effects for which we had directional hypotheses based on previous work and exploratory questions, and we interpret the latter with particular caution. At the individual level, we hypothesized an increase in physiological arousal (shorter inter-beat intervals [IBIs]) post-versus pre-stress in both mothers (reflecting stress reactivity) and children (reflecting stress contagion). We further analysed whether mothers and children would show behavioural withdrawal (less positive or more negative affect) or compensatory affiliative behaviour (Mayo & Heilig, 2019), and whether RSA would decrease poststress.

At the dyadic level, we hypothesized an increase in IBI synchrony post-stress, reflecting stress contagion. Changes in synchrony in positive facial affect and in RSA were investigated, as synchrony might either break down due to maternal withdrawal or increase as the dyad attempts to repair the interaction (Somers et al., 2025).

Finally, we examined two moderators of these stress-related changes. We hypothesized that mothers with greater emotion regulation difficulties would be less capable of buffering the stressor, resulting in lower RSA synchrony post-stress, whereas the role of maternal emotion regulation for changes in affect synchrony was explored (Lotzin et al., 2016). Given the maturation of self-regulatory capacities across the first years of life (Posner & Rothbart, 2000), child age was examined as a moderator of all stress-related changes.

Methods

Participants

In total, 86 mothers visited our laboratory in [BLINDED FOR REVIEW] with their 6-to 38-monthold children between December 2024 and August 2025. The study consisted of different subtasks, and we obtained complete and usable data for at least one of the outcome variables for 62 mothers (mean age = 35.46 years, SD = 4.71; 1 missing) and their children (mean age = 18.7 months, SD = 8.85; 36 female). The remaining dyads did not complete all phases of the task of interest. Most mothers were highly educated (78.9% vocational education or university degree; 1 missing), and 54.8% of families reported a gross monthly household income above €6,000 (11.3% did not report their income).

Families were Dutch (40.3%) or from another Western country (35.5%, versus non-Western countries, 1 missing). The experiment was either conducted in Dutch (51.6%) or in English (see Table S1 in the Supplemental Material for further demographic information). For the different analyses, we had to additionally exclude participants due to a lack of the respective measure (e.g., missing affect data due to cameras not working properly, missing physiological data when children did not accept electrocardiography [ECG] stickers) or due to poor data quality (e.g., movement artifacts). Therefore, facial affect data were available for 56 mothers and their children. Physiological data were available for 41 mothers and 44 children (individually), and 37 mother-child dyads.

Participants were recruited via flyers in public spaces, such as childcare centres and GP offices, and via social media platforms (Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn). All mothers provided informed consent for both themselves and their child before participation. Once the study was completed, mothers were reimbursed for their travel costs and received a 10€ voucher for an online shop, as well as small gifts for their child. The study procedure was approved by the local ethics committee of the [BLINDED FOR REVIEW].

Sample Size Justification

Sample size was planned a priori with G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Erdfelder et al., 2009) for the withinsubject effect of the stress induction (pre-vs. post-stress), assuming a correlation of r = .80 between repeated observations. The required total sample sizes were 81, 37, and 22 for small (f = 0.10), smallto-medium (f = 0.15), and medium (f = 0.20) effects, respectively. Given the substantial data loss that is common in developmental samples, we aimed to obtain usable data from at least 37 dyads per outcome. Data collection stopped when the funded period ended (see Participants), independently of the observed results. A simulation based on the stress-related change in infant IBIs reported by Waters and colleagues (2017; M = 13.94 ms, SD = 32.36) further indicated a power of .99 to detect a change of this size in the child physiology subsample (n = 44) (Caldwell & Lakens, 2025).

Because these a priori calculations did not take the moderation tests into account, we additionally conducted sensitivity power analyses for each model as analysed (Lakens, 2022; Table S16 and Figure S3 in the Supplemental Material). Power was derived analytically from the fitted models and verified with Monte Carlo simulations. With 80% power (α = .05, two-sided), the analysed samples allowed us to detect pre-post changes of dz ≥ 0.38 (55 dyads) to dz ≥ 0.47 (37 dyads), and moderation effects in which the moderator correlated at r ≥ .37 (55 dyads) to r ≥ .44 (37 dyads) with the pre-post change.

For the binomial models of negative affect, only very large moderation effects (odds ratios of approximately 5 to 6 per SD) could be detected reliably. The study was thus adequately powered to detect medium-sized stress effects.

Procedure

To acclimate, mother and child first played in a comfortable play area as they usually would. For the task of interest, they moved to a separate testing area in the lab with a standard chair for the mother and an age-appropriate chair for the child in front of a computer screen (see Figure 1A). The experimenter and an assistant then applied disposable ECG electrodes to mother and child. Once the signal quality was approved, a functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) cap was placed on the child's head. As many children did not tolerate the cap and we only obtained data for a subset of participants and phases, fNIRS analyses are not included in this manuscript. During preparation, mothers assisted with sensor placement. Children could sit on their mothers' lap and watch a video of choice as distraction if necessary. Across the four experimental phases, mother and child behaviour and physiology were continuously recorded via video and ECG recordings: Baseline: the dyad sat side-by-side facing the screen and watched a 2-minute aquarium video together. They were alone in the room and instructed to interact only if interaction was strictly necessary (e.g., the child became fussy). Interaction before stress induction (pre-stress phase): the screen was turned off, and the chairs were turned around so that mother and child faced each other. The mother was instructed to play with her child as she usually would for two minutes. Stress induction: the experimenter sat next to the mother and the child (triangular setup) for two minutes during which the mother underwent the modified TSST. Interaction after induction (post-stress phase): the mother was once again asked to play with her child for two minutes in experimenter's absence (see Figure 1B). Afterwards, additional tasks unrelated to this study were conducted. Participants could take breaks whenever necessary but were encouraged to take them before the stress induction to standardize the timing of the manipulation.

Stress Induction: Trier Social Stress Test (TSST)

To simulate a stress-inducing multi-tasking experience within the laboratory setting, we used an adjusted, shortened version of the TSST protocol (Birkett, 2011). Namely, we included only the arithmetic portion of the TSST, reduced to two minutes to match the duration of the experimental phases. In contrast to previous studies (Waters et al., 2014 (Waters et al., , 2017)), we did not separate mothers from their children to induce stress, as the goal was to create a naturalistic setting in which the parent needs to perform additional tasks during caregiving.

The experimenter entered the room after the first face-to-face interaction, sat next to child and mother, and first asked the mother to rate her current stress level on a scale from 1 -10. The experimenter then introduced the task as a 'multitasking task' and provided the standard TSST arithmetic instructions, asking mothers to count from 1022 in steps of 13. Throughout the task, experimenters provided reminders to answer quickly, and, in case of wrong replies, mothers had to restart from 1022. The child was given a soundless toy to play with while the mother was completing the task. Interaction with the child was allowed if the child called for attention but the importance of continuing the task was highlighted. After two minutes, the experimenter stopped the task and asked the mother to rate her stress level again; these two ratings served as the manipulation check.

Measurements

Behavioural coding: Nonverbal emotional expressions

The entire experimental session was recorded from four angles using high-resolution cameras (1920x1080 px) at a framerate of 30 Hz. Video recording was controlled via VISO (version 11.1.8767.40077) and implemented through Observer 16. Event markers for the onset and offset of each task and phase were sent from a separate PC to both Observer and the ECG recordings, synchronizing the measures.

Behavioural microcoding was performed offline in Observer 16 and 17 by five trained raters for the two interaction phases (pre-and post-stress induction). Coded behaviours comprised facial affect (positive, negative, neutral, not visible) and gaze direction (gaze to other's face, gaze to other's body, gaze elsewhere, gaze not visible), and levels within each behaviour group were mutually exclusive (see coding scheme provided in the Supplemental Material). Twelve of the 56 videos (21%) were double-coded to monitor reliability between raters after training. Interrater reliability for coding affect was acceptable (κ = 0.76). For individual-level analyses, we exported the duration of the coded behaviours and calculated the percentage of each behaviour relative to the time that the behaviour was visible (Table S2 in the Supplemental Material). Negative affect was rare (i.e., zero-inflated) in both mothers and children. Therefore, percentages of negative affect were recoded into binary variables, indicating the presence (1) or absence (0) of negative affect per individual and phase. As negative affect occurred in very few dyads (12.5%), synchrony analyses were restricted to positive affect. Time series of positive affect expressions were resampled to 1 Hz within each phase. Dyadic coordination in positive affect was operationalized as the simultaneous or time-lagged presentation of positive affect in mother and child, with a maximum lag of 5 s based on previous research (Marx et al., 2025). We analysed the resulting categorical time series with Cross-Recurrence Quantification Analysis (CRQA) in R (package crqa; (Coco et al., 2024). Details about this analysis and descriptive statistics (maximum recurrence values, lags and the recurrence of shuffled data) are provided in the Supplemental Material (Table S2 and S3).

Heart rate

Disposable electrodes were attached to the upper bodies of the mother and the child to record their ECG. To facilitate child recordings, the standard Lead-II configuration was adjusted for both participants by putting the ground electrode on the back of the neck (seventh cervical vertebrae). The Vin+ and Vin-electrodes were placed below the left and right collarbones respectively. ECG was recorded with in-house amplifiers at a 1000 Hz sampling frequency and wirelessly transmitted to the in-house recording software Fysio Recorder (version 2.2). ECG signals of mother and child were processed offline as described previously (Abney et al., 2021; see Supplemental Material for a detailed description). Signals were band-pass filtered (4-30 Hz), R-peaks were detected in ARTiiFACT (Kaufmann et al., 2011) and visually inspected and manually corrected by a trained researcher (J.F.), IBIs shorter than 200 ms were removed, and remaining artifacts were corrected by cubic spline interpolation. IBI time series were resampled to 5 Hz, and RSA was derived by detrending the IBI series with a 51-point cubic moving polynomial, band-pass filtering it in the respiratory frequency range (mothers: 0.12-1.0 Hz; children: 0.3-1.3 Hz), and computing the natural logarithm of its variance in 15-s sliding windows. For individual analyses, we averaged the IBI and RSA time series per person and phase (see Table S2 in the Supplementary Material).

For dyadic analyses, we also used CRQA to quantify synchrony of physiological signals.

Given the slower changes in the RSA signal, we operationalized synchrony as concurrent changes in the time series of mother and child with a maximum lag of 10 s (Nguyen et al., 2021). A detailed description of this analysis and related descriptive statistics can be found in the Supplemental Material (Table S2 and S3).

Questionnaires

As part of the larger research project, mothers (n = 61, 1 missing) completed an online survey (Dutch or English) on demographics, pregnancy/childbirth, child development, and maternal and child characteristics. For the present study, we used the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation (DER) Scale (Gratz & Roemer, 2004), administered as a validated short form (18 items; three items per subscale).

This questionnaire assesses self-reported difficulties in emotion regulation across six domains: nonacceptance of emotional responses, difficulties engaging in goal-directed behaviour, impulse control difficulties, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to emotion regulation strategies, and lack of emotional clarity. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale;1 = almost never to5 = almost always.

Higher scores reflect greater difficulties in emotion regulation. Internal consistency for the total score in the present sample was good (α = .86).

Data Analysis

All analyses distinguished pre-versus post-stress phases. As a manipulation check, we compared the self-reported stress levels before and after the stress induction using a paired-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

In preliminary analysis, we examined zero-order correlations between the outcome variables (apart from the binomial variables negative affect child/mother), as well as between the potential moderators Child Age, maternal DER and the outcome variables, in the two phases of the experiment separately. Results are reported in the Supplemental Material (see Preliminary Analysis and Figure S1). We additionally tested whether the children's biological sex (male or female) or mothers' ethnic background (Dutch, other Western, or other non-Western) were associated with potential differences in the outcome variables in both phases, using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Kruskal-Wallis tests, respectively. None of the tests were significant (ps > .05); therefore, neither variable was included as a covariate.

To test effects of the stress induction on the individual and the dyad level, we fitted linear mixedeffects models with the fixed effects Phase (post-versus pre-stress), Child Age and maternal DER, the interactions Phase × Child Age and Phase × DER, and random intercepts for dyad. Phase was effectcoded (pre-stress = -0.5, post-stress = 0.5) and Child Age and DER were mean-centred. Models were estimated with restricted maximum likelihood, and fixed effects were tested with F tests using Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (R packages lme4 and lmerTest; Bates et al., 2015; Kuznetsova et al., 2017). As effect sizes, we report standardized coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals.

Response variables on the individual level (mother and child separately) were the percentage of positive affect, the mean IBI and the mean RSA per phase; response variables on the dyad level were the maximum recurrence in positive affect, IBIs and RSA. Negative affect (presence vs. absence per individual and phase) was analysed with binomial generalized linear mixed-effects models. To control the false discovery rate (FDR) across the eleven models, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) within families of tests that each addressed one research question: the effect of the stress induction (Phase; 11 tests), its moderation by child age (Phase × Child Age; 11 tests) and its moderation by maternal DER (Phase × DER; 11 tests). The between-person effects of Child Age and DER, which were not the focus of this study, formed a separate family (22 tests). We report uncorrected and FDR-adjusted p values (pFDR). Effects with p < .05 but pFDR ≥ .05 are described as nominally significant and interpreted as exploratory. Results under alternative correction schemes (FDR correction within each model; Holm correction across all 33 stress-related tests) are reported in Table S18. In addition, permutation tests (child age or DER shuffled across dyads; 10,000 permutations; Fisher's combined p across the nine linear models, computed on pre-post difference scores) tested whether the interactions with each moderator were, as a set, stronger than expected by chance (Table S19).

To probe significant interactions with Phase, we tested whether the slopes of DER differed from zero within each phase (Holm-adjusted across the two phases), and compared the two phases at the first quartile (10 months), median (18 months) and third quartile (27 months) of the child age distribution. Lastly, if we did not observe any hypothesized effect on an outcome variable, we fitted Bayesian (generalized) linear models on the outcome, using the same effect structure as in the Frequentist analyses, to explore evidence for the null hypothesis. As we did not have empirical data to base our prior probabilities on, we defined three different prior distributions, ranging from narrow (normal(0, 1)) to medium (normal(0, 2.5)) and wide (normal(0, 5)). Bayes Factors (BFs) > 10, > 3 and 1-3 were interpreted as strong, moderate and weak/inconclusive evidence for the alternative hypothesis, and BFs < 0.1, 0.1-0.3 and 0.3-1 as strong, moderate and weak/inconclusive evidence for the null hypothesis (Jeffreys, 1961).

Discussion

In the current study, we tested how stress experiences in mothers influence behavioural and physiological dynamics in subsequent interactions with their children. As hypothesized, the effects of a maternal stress induction on child and mother's affective and physiological activity, as well as their synchrony, depended on child age and maternal emotion regulation difficulties, although only the moderation of IBI synchrony by maternal emotion regulation difficulties survived correction for multiple comparisons, and the age-related effects should therefore be considered exploratory. Contrary to expectations, stress-related changes in mothers were limited to their subjective ratings and expressive behaviour: mothers, particularly those of younger children, showed less positive affect post-stress, but no physiological changes. Compared to younger children, older children tended to show more positive affect, shorter IBIs and lower RSA post-stress, and dyads with younger children tended to become less synchronized in positive affect. Mothers with more emotion regulation difficulties showed a relative decrease in IBI synchrony post-stress, were more synchronized with their children in positive affect across both interactions, and tended to express negative affect more often.

Facial affective expressions are highly relevant nonverbal communication tools in social interactions (Cowen & Keltner, 2020; Frith, 2009). Positive facial affect may either decrease in stressful situations, reflecting an individual's distress, or increase as a "tend-and-befriend" strategy to foster affiliation. Although women have been suggested to primarily follow this latter affiliative strategy (Mayo & Heilig, 2019), we observed a decrease in maternal positive facial affect post-stress, which, however, did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. Importantly, unlike previous studies in which women interacted with adult strangers, mothers in our study interacted with their own children. Given the strong existing affiliative bond, increasing positive affect may not be an effective or necessary social-communicative response to stress within the mother-child dyad. Moreover, the decrease in positive affect was most pronounced in mothers of younger children. This may have been because mothers of younger children displayed more positive facial affect in the pre-stress interaction, potentially leaving more room for a stress-related decrease. This difference pre-stress aligns with the developmental shift in affective communication: While non-verbal channels dominate in infancy, affect is increasingly shared via verbal communication and complex behaviours in toddlerhood (Harrist & Waugh, 2002).

Similar to their mothers, younger compared to older children tended to display less positive facial affect (but not more negative facial affect) after the maternal stress induction, although differences between phases were not significant at any of the tested ages. Mothers' and children's positive facial affect displays were also synchronized in both interaction phases. Mirroring the reduction of positive facial affect in both mothers and young children post versus pre-stress induction, mothers and younger children also tended to be less synchronized after the stress induction compared to mothers and older children. Thus, in dyads with younger children, stress may disrupt positive affective synchrony by reducing mothers' positive affect. As children age, the mother-child dyad accumulates shared interactional experience, potentially rendering their affective attunement more robust and resilient to acute stressors (Markova & Nguyen, 2023).

Indeed, compared to younger children, older children seemed to rather show more positive affect post-versus pre-stress induction. With co-regulation becoming more bi-directional throughout development, older children may have initiated more positive affective interactions to "cheer up" their mothers or bounce them back from the stressful experience. In line with this, dyadic affect regulation (or "affective mutuality") has been shown to increase in interactions as children age, while shared periods of high positive affect (or "mutual engagement"), in contrast, are more prominent in younger children, and decrease over time (Gajewski-Nemes et al., 2025). When previous studies induced stress in 6-month-old children instead, mother-child synchrony in positive affect increased afterwards, primarily led by the mother. Hence, synchrony in positive (facial) affect may act as a tool to jointly overcome stressful situations, led by mothers if the child requires regulation, or even by older children if the mother is experiencing stress.

When experiencing heightened levels of stress, as mothers reported in our study, the sympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system typically activates while the parasympathetic branch deactivates. However, we did not observe evidence for an increase in maternal physiological arousal (indexed by IBI or RSA) post-versus pre-stress induction. The correspondence between self-reported stress experiences and physiologically assessed stress responses is known to be inconsistent in the literature (Campbell & Ehlert, 2012). Although we tried to minimize any delay between the stress induction and the second interaction phase, effects on maternal physiology might have already started to vanish. Thus, stress-induced increases in arousal in mothers were either too short-lived to capture or the stress induction was not impacting cardiac indices of arousal (regulation).

Children, in contrast, tended to show changes in the two physiological indices, which depended on their age. Compared to younger children, older children showed a reduction in both RSA and IBIs post-versus pre-stress, although these interactions did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and the difference between phases was significant only for IBIs at 27 months. Because the mothers did not exhibit changes in physiological arousal themselves, this suggests that the children did not simply mirror maternal autonomic activity. Rather, they may have appraised the stressful situation via changes in maternal affective behaviour, pointing to a cross-modal transmission of stress. Findings of past studies indicate that children can pick up on their mothers' stress both early and later in infancy (Mueller et al., 2021; Waters et al., 2014). However, these studies did not compare children of different ages, and our findings tentatively suggest that toddlers, rather than infants, can become physiologically aroused due to a stressful situation for the mother, even in the absence of corresponding changes in maternal physiology. Age effects on physiological activity of children overall were in line with typical child maturation processes: from the first month after birth, heart rate is known to decrease and RSA to increase at rest (Fleming et al., 2011; Sheinkopf et al., 2019).

Individual characteristics of the mother modulated the effect of the stress induction on motherchild physiological synchrony, and this was the only stress-related effect that survived correction for multiple comparisons. We did not observe the expected overall increase in IBI synchrony post-stress induction (Waters et al., 2014 (Waters et al., , 2017)). Instead, we found that mothers with more difficulties in emotion regulation showed higher IBI synchrony in the pre-stress phase, but a relative decrease in synchrony post-stress. This result suggests that maternal difficulties in emotion regulation, also observed in relation to anxiety and depression (Cisler et al., 2010; Joormann & Stanton, 2016), may be linked to hyper-vigilance or over-attunement to the child's state, such that these dyads show higher IBI synchrony in a typical play situation (Doba et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2022). This aligns with findings that optimal child development is supported by midrange maternal responsivity rather than constant, high synchrony (Beebe & McCrorie, 2010; Mitsven et al., 2022). Moreover, mothers should be able to flexibly adjust their responsivity to buffer acute stressors: less anxious mothers become particularly responsive when their child's arousal peaks and then co-regulate their child's distress, whereas more anxious mothers respond more to smaller-scale stressors and are less able to switch to co-regulation (Smith et al., 2022). Correspondingly, RSA synchrony after a stress induction was only observed between mothers with low emotion regulation difficulties and their 7-month-olds, likely indicating coregulation, and the children of those more regulated mothers were also more likely to return to RSA homeostasis (Gao et al., 2023). We found no conclusive evidence that maternal emotion regulation difficulties were related to RSA synchrony, possibly because the broader age range and inducing stress in the mother rather than the child reduced the need for co-regulation.

In line with our expectations and previous research (Lotzin et al., 2016), mothers with more emotion regulation difficulties were more synchronized with their children in positive affect across both interactions, and this association survived correction for multiple comparisons. Similar to the higher IBI synchrony of these dyads before the stress induction, this may reflect heightened responsivity to the child's cues rather than adaptive co-regulation. Mothers with higher parenting stress have been found to synchronize more with their 6-month-olds' facial affect during play, without this resulting in more positive child affect, underlining that higher synchrony is not necessarily functional (León et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2024). In addition, mothers with more emotion regulation difficulties were more likely to express negative affect across both interactions, although this association did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. In line with their reported difficulties, they might have struggled to regulate the experience, or at least the expression, of negative affect in the interaction with their child. Under high stress, adaptive emotion regulation strategies seem to help caregivers maintain sensitive parenting, fostering better child mental health (Iwanski et al., 2025).

Emotion regulation training for caregivers with emotion regulation difficulties may thus help to prevent the transmission of a high stress responsivity to their young children and, thereby, the potential development of psychopathology.

By investigating the influence of maternal stress induction on spontaneous mother-child interactions in a laboratory setting, this study balanced experimental control with ecological validity while capturing multi-modal dyadic dynamics. However, the study has several limitations. First, the sample is quite homogeneous, consisting predominantly of highly educated families from Western backgrounds. Structural factors like socioeconomic status (SES) dictate the resources available for daily interactions and influence parental stress and infant self-regulation (Hoyniak et al., 2021; Jones-Mason et al., 2018). Children in low-SES environments may be more susceptible to developing high stress responsiveness and less able to cope with it (e.g., Suveg et al., 2016). Similarly, our sample reported relatively low levels of emotion regulation difficulties, with only 13% scoring above the clinical cut-off (Barrett et al., 2025). The adverse effects of maternal emotion regulation difficulties on co-regulation often become most apparent in the context of severe psychopathology (Feldman et al., 2009; Granat et al., 2017; but, see also Hakanen et al., 2019). Future research must replicate these findings in more diverse samples, specifically including families facing chronic stress, low SES, or clinical psychopathology.

Methodologically, our adapted version of the TSST arithmetic task successfully induced subjective stress in mothers, but it did not elicit the sustained physiological responses typically seen when the full protocol is utilized (Birkett, 2011; Blasberg et al., 2023). We opted for this naturalistic, multi-tasking design to keep the child in the room, reflecting daily caregiving demands (Schieman & Young, 2015). However, because children observed the stress induction directly, their subsequent responses may reflect a combination of direct appraisal ("vicarious stress") and indirect transmission via the mother ("stress contagion", Marheinecke et al., 2025). To better isolate the mechanisms of stress contagion, future research should utilize standardized protocols specifically designed for assessing maternal stress in dyadic contexts, such as the CASP paradigm (Mueller et al., 2021).

Finally, future work should consider child temperament, particularly negative emotional reactivity, which influences the strength and directionality of synchrony (Hibel et al., 2015), as well as explore these dynamics in interactions with fathers and diverse family structures (Girod et al., 2025).

Conclusion

Affective and physiological synchrony in caregiver-child interactions facilitates the exchange of information and emotion regulation and contributes to long-term social bonds (Wood et al., 2021), particularly in early childhood, when children rely on their caregivers to regulate their physiological states (Atzil et al., 2018). Our findings indicate that parental stress affects these delicate dynamics, depending on the parent's emotion regulation abilities and, tentatively, on the child's developmental stage. Mothers with more emotion regulation difficulties were more synchronized with their children's heart rate during typical play, but less so after the stressor. While younger children seemed to mirror their caregivers' behavioural stress responses, older toddlers showed tentative signs of cross-modal stress contagion, with increased physiological arousal and more positive facial affect. Interventions that bolster maternal emotion regulation and sensitive responsivity, such as "Tuning in to Toddlers" (Cloud et al., 2025) or "Mindful with your Baby" (Zeegers et al., 2019), may therefore help prevent the transmission of chronic stress and buffer its adverse effects on child development.
