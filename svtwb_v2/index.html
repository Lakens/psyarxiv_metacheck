Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

We present a theory-based approach to psychometric rating-scales based on the hypothesis that their putative measurands are internal discriminative stimuli that are shaped by operant selection of relational responding. According to the formal requirements of representational measurement theory, we argue that a measurable internal discriminative stimulus emerges, if and only if relational responses are selected such that the resulting structure is homomorphic to a numerical structure such as the real numbers. We further embed the concept of internal discriminative stimuli in the theory of operant selection, where selection is understood as a multilevel process that shapes behavior with regard to its expected fitness consequences. The theory has wide implications for the interpretation of psychometric rating-scales, as well as for the experimental study of measurement in psychology.

1 Introduction

Numeric ratings on predefined scales ("rating-scales" hereafter) play an important role in modern society, starting with the ubiquitous five-star online reviews, over clinical assessments of symptom severity, to the usage of rating-scales for virtually any imaginable construct in psychological research and diagnostics. Rating-scales are also quite common in behavioral studies involving human subjects, where the ratings are treated as verbal reports that indicate certain behavioral tendencies.

Despite the wide use of rating-scales, the mechanisms generating numeric verbal responses are still poorly understood. The traditional psychometric approach to rating-scales builds on the idea that they are some kind of measurement instrument. Psychometricians often try to establish an analogy with physical measurement, which is construed as the assignment of numerals to physical objects (Stevens, 1946). In much the same way, psychological measurement is claimed to involve the assignment of numerals to psychological (or mental) objects, such as sensations, beliefs, attitudes, agreement and others. Despite considerable controversy about the methodological, epistemological, and ontological status of psychological measurement (see Borsboom, 2005 for a conceptual overview and Michell, 1999 for a historical perspective), psychometrics has produced a well-established modus operandi that is hardly ever questioned within mainstream psychology, such as the calculation of reliability and validity estimates based on classical test theory (Lord & Novick, 1968), the construction of probabilistic response models in item response theory (e.g., Rasch, 1960), or the statistical modeling of latent variables based on structural equations (cf. Hoyle, 2012).

Here, we propose a radically different approach to rating-scales by treating them as a special class of verbal behavior that has been shaped by environmental contingencies at different levels of selection.

The result is an explicit theoretical link between what is traditionally called psychological measurement and the principle of selection by consequences (cf. Skinner, 1981). The core claim of our approach is that rating-scales may in fact be genuine measurement devices -but their putative measurands are not mental objects, but discriminative stimuli in the context of relational response behavior. The theory is laid out in an informal manner to make it accessible to a wide audience.

However, we also present a formalized version of its core claims in Appendix A2.

The remaining sections are structured as follows. In section 2, we lay out the conceptual foundation for our approach by integrating the representational theory of measurement (Krantz et al., 1971) with the semantic view of scientific theories (Borgstede & Eggert, 2023a , 2023b). The main result from section 2 is that measurement requires an empirical relational structure that can, a) be formally represented by a numerical structure, and b) follows from a substantive formal theory about the phenomena underlying the empirical structure. In section 3, we apply this conceptual framework to psychological measurement, leading us to the study of relational response behavior (Marsh, 1967), which is understood as a special class of operant behavior that is shaped via selection by consequences (cf. Baum, 2017; Borgstede & Eggert, 2021). The main result from section 3 is that, if there are any measurable attributes in psychology, they must be discriminative stimuli generated by an individual in the context of relational responding. This theoretical embedding of relational responding in an overarching theory of operant behavior is then applied to psychometric ratingscales. Finally, the theoretical and practical implications of our approach are evaluated and discussed in section 4.

2 Theory and Measurement

2.1 The Representational Theory of Measurement

The representational theory of measurement was developed as an axiomatic framework for the empirical (re-)construction of measurement scales at an abstract level, such that it equally applies to measurement across all scientific disciplines. The central idea of representational measurement is that a scale measures something, if and only if it constitutes a mapping from an empirical structure (e.g., comparisons of objects with a beam balance) to a numerical structure (e.g., the real numbers), such that each level of the attribute is assigned exactly one number and at least some empirical relations (e.g., "heavier than") are preserved in the corresponding numerical structure (mathematically speaking, the mapping needs to be a homomorphism).

In classical physics, a paradigmatic example for representational measurement is the application of a beam balance to measure an object's mass. The empirical relational system (ERS) under study is construed by of a set of physical objects that can be put into the pans of a beam balance (e.g., a collection of uncalibrated weights) and the mechanical characteristics of the scale (e.g., two pans that are mounted to a symmetric lever). Suppose that arbitrary subsets of the weights can be put into each of the pans, which will result in the beam balance being either at equilibrium or tipping towards one of the two sides. The empirical relational system thus consists of a set 𝐴𝐴 of weights, a dominance relation ≼ ("not tipping to the left") on the cross-product of 𝐴𝐴 × 𝐴𝐴, and a concatenation operation ⨁ ("adding a weight to a pan") that maps the cross-product of 𝐴𝐴 × 𝐴𝐴 back onto the set of weights (i.e., ⨁: 𝐴𝐴 × 𝐴𝐴 ↦ 𝐴𝐴). The totality of all possible comparisons of subsets of weights by means of the beam balance yields a set of inequalities (such as 𝑎𝑎⨁𝑏𝑏 ≼ 𝑐𝑐 for 𝑎𝑎, 𝑏𝑏, 𝑐𝑐 ∈ 𝐴𝐴). These inequalities can be solved by assigning exactly one real number to each weight (thereby fixing one of the weights as a unit), because the ERS imposed by the beam balance follows a specific type of structure, which is known as an extensive measurement structure (see Figure 1).

Figure 1: Representational measurement as a homomorphism from an empirical relational system (ERS) to a numerical relational system (NRS). The homomorphism implies that all empirical objects (such as weights u, v and w) are assigned to exactly one numerical object each (such as the numbers 1,2,3), such that the empirically observed relations and operations performed on these objects (such as ≼,⊕, representing the manipulation of a beam balance) correspond to mathematical relations and operations (such as ≤, +).

The representational theory of measurement specifies exactly which kinds of empirical structures can be mapped onto which kinds of numerical structures (Krantz et al., 1971). It does so by stating the structural requirements for the construction of different types of measurement scales. For example, extensive measurement is possible if and only if the relation ≼ forms a weak order and the operation ⨁ is commutative, distributive and continuous. Depending on the type of ERS, different mappings onto numerical systems are possible (commonly known as levels of measurement). For example, empirical equivalence classes can be mapped onto a nominal scale, and empirical comparisons that form a weak order can be mapped onto an ordinal scale. There are also various types of ERS that allow for a metric embedding, such as additive conjoint structures (Suppes, 1951), difference structures (Suppes & Winet, 1955), or bisection structures (Pfanzagl, 1959). Furthermore, if the ERS is stochastic in nature, it may even be possible to construct metric scales from simple pair comparisons (Bradley & Terry, 1952) or binary test responses (Rasch, 1960). The theory of representational measurement is neither restricted to specific types of empirical systems (such as classical physics), nor to a particular numerical system (such as the real numbers). It provides a general framework for the construction of measurement scales for arbitrary domains, relations and operations.

For some physical attributes (such as mass or length) the structural requirements for extensive measurement may appear trivial. However, even a system as simple as a set of sticks that are put next to each other (dominance relation) or end to end to combine the length of two or more sticks (concatenation operation) requires a very restrictive geometry to enable extensive measurement (cf. Holzhauser & Eggert, 2019). For example, if the sticks are compared by placing them on a curved surface, the ERS will no longer be extensive and the intended mapping onto the real numbers will not be successful. In other words, the choice of the objects and the definition of the relations and operations that are believed to constitute a measurable ERS are crucial for the construction of measurement scales.

2.2 Theory-based Measurement

The representational theory of measurement gives an explicit definition of what measurement is and specifies how one can, in principle, find out whether a certain experimental procedure allows for the construction of a measurement scale. In other words, the representational theory of measurement provides a meta-theoretical background for answering whether psychometric rating-scales measure something or not. However, it does not tell us anything about the nature of the measurand. In the representational view, the measurand is completely determined by the ERS that is being used to construct the scale (Buntins et al., 2017). Consequently, if we find out that a certain ERS allows for the construction of, say, a ratio scale, all we know is that this particular empirical system has a quantitative 1 structure. We do not know, however, why the system has a quantitative structure or how the structure originated. For example, if the measurement of mass was solely defined in the context of weights that can be put in the pans of a beam balance, we would not have the faintest idea what we are measuring. The fact that we can construct a quantitative scale merely tells us that there might be something quantitative inherent to the ERS, but not, what this something might possibly be. Moreover, we would have no justification for the assignment of mass-values to any object that cannot be put into the pan of a beam balance, such as a subatomic particle or a planet.

To justify the introduction of a theoretical concept like "mass" we need to embed the ERS under study into an overarching formal theory that links the behavior of different ERSs to that concept.

Given such a theory, the characteristic properties of the different ERSs can be used to test the theory and may be used to measure the theoretical concept by means of different ERSs thus leading to different operationalizations. In other words, we need a theory about the generative processes behind the ERSs under study. For the case of mass, classical mechanics provides such a theory. In classical mechanics, mass is defined by Newton's second law of motion, which states that the force (𝐹𝐹) acting on an object equals exactly the product of its mass (𝑚𝑚) and its current acceleration (𝑎𝑎): 𝐹𝐹 = 𝑚𝑚𝑎𝑎. The theoretical framework of classical mechanics can be applied to arbitrary macroscopic physical systems, including beam balances. In both cases, the theory predicts exactly the behavior that gives rise to the construction of the respective measurement scales. For the beam balance, classical mechanics predicts that the beam balance is at equilibrium if and only if the sum of masses in both pans are equal, and that any deviation from equality will result in the beam balance tilting towards the side with the higher mass. In other words, the behavior of the empirical system under study can be explained by the objects' masses as formally defined in classical mechanics, we can be certain that any quantitative representation of the structure (if such a representation exists), is a quantitative representation (i.e., a measure) of the objects's masses 𝑚𝑚, with all its theoretical implications within the framework of classical mechanics (see Figure 2). Furthermore, if we are confident that our theory is empirically adequate (which we may well be in the case of classical mechanics when applied to macroscopic objects with comparably low masses and low velocities), we may even identify the results of completely different procedures (such as solving Newton's equations of motion for two celestial bodies) as measurements of mass 𝑚𝑚. Therefore, we may ascribe mass to a planet without ever putting it in the pan of a beam balance, because the theoretical meaning of "mass" is not intrinsic to the measurement procedure, but stems from a formalized theory about the generative processes underlying the measurement procedure.

At a more general level, the example of physical mass illustrates how measurable quantities can be given theoretical meaning. Whereas representational measurement tells us how to find out whether we measure something, the question what we measure can only be answered by substantive formal theory. Substantive formal theory fixes the meaning of an abstract concept (e.g., "mass") by providing a very strict grammar for the theoretical terms used in the theory. The application of such a theory to an empirical system implies a formal model of the system, whose parameters correspond to the theoretical terms used in the theory (see Figure 2). From this theory-based perspective, measurement means to apply a substantive formal theory to an empirical system in such a way that parameters of hitherto unknown value can be inferred from the behavior of the system (Borgstede & Eggert, 2023b).

3 A Selectionist Theory of Psychometric Rating-Scales Following the representational theory of measurement, measurement scales are mathematical models for certain classes of empirical structures. These structures are composed of empirical relations and operations that act on certain classes of objects. According to theory-based measurement, the meaning of the measurand further requires a general theory about the generative processes behind the empirical relational structure that is being represented.

If we apply this measurement theoretic background to the responses of an individual to a ratingscale, the first question to answer is which empirical relational structures might underly the observed rating-scale behavior. The second question concerns the mechanisms generating these putative relational structures. What then are the empirical relational structures underlying rating-scale behavior? For example, if we ask a person to indicate how much they like certain commodities using a numeric rating-scale, we implicitly ask which of the commodities the individual would prefer when given a choice (i.e., if the person rates option A higher than option B, we would expect them to choose A over B). Similarly, if we instruct a person to rate how attractive they find another person, we implicitly ask which of the presented individuals they would rather approach in a potential mating context. And if we instruct a person to indicate their agreement to a collection of statements, we implicitly ask them to state which of the statements they are more likely to support in a conversation.

In general, we may thus say that the class of empirical relational structures underlying rating-scales is given by relational behavior such as choice, preference or avoidance, among others. The empirical relational structures underlying rating-scale behavior are hence nothing but behavioral responses that relate certain stimuli to each other. Therefore, the key to understanding how rating-scales work naturally leads to the study of relational responding, understood as a special class of operant behavior. In the following, we hypothesize that the structure of relational responses is shaped by the environment in which they have evolved, and that this selection process necessarily leads to the emergence of internal discriminative stimuli that act as context-dependent predictors of evolutionary fitness changes (Borgstede & Eggert, 2021).

3.1 Relational Responding

Relational responding has been characterized as a special kind of operant behavior where the discriminative stimulus is based on at least two individual cues (e.g., two or more lights) and a response is selected (e.g., by contingent food delivery) based on a certain relation between the cues.

For example, given two keys that are illuminated at different levels of luminescence, an individual may be selected to choose the key that appears brighter than the other one. In contrast to operant behavior that has been established using a singular cue, relational responding does not depend on the absolute value of the relevant stimulus characteristics (e.g., a certain luminescence), but on its relative values (e.g., luminescence relative to a reference light). For example, an individual that has been trained to choose the key that is illuminated with the highest brightness using luminescence A, B and C (following the order A < B < C) will choose a novel stimulus D over all other three stimuli if it is brighter than the three training stimuli -although, in contrast to the training stimuli, it has never been selected by positive consequences (such as food delivery). In other words, responding generalizes to novel stimuli according to the selected relation between cues, and not according to their absolute characteristics (Köhler, 1938; Marsh, 1967). Furthermore, it is possible to establish distinct prompts for different relations between the same objects. For example, an individual may choose the brightest out of two stimuli when prompted by a tone of a certain pitch, and to choose the largest when prompted with another tone, even if these two characteristics do not align (Cook et al., 1997).

Arguably the most prominent kind of relational behavior refers to structures that fulfil the requirements of an equivalence relation (Sidman, 1994 (Sidman, , 1997)). Equivalence relations are characterized by three structural properties. First, they need to be symmetric (i.e., if 𝑎𝑎~𝑏𝑏, then 𝑏𝑏~𝑎𝑎 for all elements 𝑎𝑎 and 𝑏𝑏), second, they need to be reflexive (i.e., 𝑎𝑎~𝑎𝑎 for all elements 𝑎𝑎), and third, they need to be transitive (i.e., if 𝑎𝑎~𝑏𝑏 and 𝑏𝑏~𝑐𝑐 then 𝑎𝑎~𝑐𝑐 for all elements 𝑎𝑎, 𝑏𝑏, 𝑐𝑐). If these three conditions hold with regard to an individual's relational behavior, the stimulus domain underlying the structure can be represented by a set of nonoverlapping classes (i.e., equivalence classes). Following the representational theory of measurement, such equivalence classes can be represented by a nominal scale and hence form a measurable attribute. Relational responding that forms an equivalence structure is a fundamental feature of behavior in that it allows the organism to treat different stimuli as if they were one and the same because both have the same consequences (cf. Sidman, 1994). Therefore, the behavior of the organism is not under the direct control of external events, but of equivalence classes that align with anticipated consequences. In other words, equivalence classes can be understood as discriminative stimuli for operant behavior. In the following, we argue that this holds for all structures of relational response behavior, including those that are based on rank orders and, potentially, quantitative relational response structures (cf. Hayes et al., 2001).

3.2 Selection of Relational Responding

An important characteristic of relational responding is that it is not necessarily contingent on physical relations (such as brightness or pitch) because -being the result of selection -relational responding is shaped by environmental consequences, which need not align with the values of physical quantities. For example, color perception (understood as relational responding that has been selected at an evolutionary time scale) does depend on the wavelength of light (which is a physical quantity). However, when prompted to choose the color that most closely matches violet, humans consistently choose red over green, although the difference in wavelength between violet and green is much smaller than that between violet and red (cf. Robertson, 1992). Similarly, it is not guaranteed that physical quantities map onto psychological quantities, as routinely assumed in most psychophysical studies (cf. Krantz, 1972). For example, although differences in pitch physically depend on the wavelength of sound waves (which are physical quantities), it is by no means guaranteed that an individuals' relational responding to different pitches also has a quantitative structure. In fact, depending on their specific learning histories, some individuals may be selected to respond to different pitches according to consistent quantitative intervals (e.g., a child that is raised in a family of musicians), while others merely distinguish between lower and higher pitch (e.g., a child that has had musical education). Finally, quantitative and qualitative aspects of relational responding to a set of stimuli may be effective simultaneously and produce quite complex relational behavior. For example, responding to color cues has proven to be highly context dependent and involve complex interactions among contrast, brightness, and other contextual cues (Beau Lotto & Purves, 2002; Witzel & Gegenfurtner, 2018) and even includes culturally and individually different distinctions between various hues (Özgen, 2004). 2 The case to be made here is that relational responding is not "just there," but in fact shaped by its environmental consequences due to selection at both, the individual level (i.e., learning), the population level (i.e., socialization), and the species level (i.e., evolution) (cf. Maule et al., 2023). Consequently, being the result of selection on relational responding, the putative measurands of rating-scales are not representations of the world as it is (i.e., of external objects), but of the expected consequences of behavior. In other words, they are internal stimuli that are generated by the individual. This is necessarily the case because, by definition of relational responding, there simply are no external cues corresponding to the selected relational structures. The structure of relational responding lies in its consequences, not in the physical world. Therefore, individuals can only respond according to a selected relational structure if they produce the corresponding discriminative stimulus themselves. We propose that these internally generated discriminative stimuli are the putative measurands of psychometric rating-scales and in this way constitute what some might call "psychological attributes."

The main theoretical claim of this paper is thus that the putative measurands of psychometric ratingscales are the result of selection by consequences acting on relational responding and that they are measurable if and only if the behavior of interest and the observed test behavior was selected such that its structure corresponds to the intended numerical mapping (e.g., a ratio scale). We further argue that, since relational responses are shaped by their consequences (and not determined by external cues), the organism has to produce corresponding discriminative stimuli internally -it is these internal discriminative stimuli that constitute psychological attributes. Consequently, if ratingscales measure anything, they measure discriminative stimuli that are produced by the individual such that they align with the consequences of the underlying relational response behavior.

3.3 Relational Responding and Fitness Prediction

If relational responses are selected by consequences, their structure is determined by the specific selection contingencies that gave rise to them. In other words, the structure of relational responding is a mirror of an individual's (phylogenetic and ontogenetic) selection history. There is, however, no obvious reason to believe that relational responses are selected such that they allow for a quantitative representation (e.g., the mapping onto a numeric scale). As argued above, the mere existence of quantitative physical properties (such as wavelength of light) does not imply that an organism is selected to distinguish between these physical properties in a quantitative fashion.

Selection is not determined by antecedents, but by consequences. Therefore, structures that emerge from selection on relational responding can only be understood if the structure of their consequences is known.

Notably, the theory of multilevel behavioral selection provides a strong a-priori argument for the existence of quantitative structure of behavioral consequences. As demonstrated by Price (1970), selection only acts on attributes that covary with evolutionary fitness (or a statistical predictor thereof). This is most obvious in the context of natural selection, where evolution is driven by average fitness consequences, but likewise holds for selection on individual behavior (Borgstede, 2024; Borgstede & Eggert, 2021). Evolutionary fitness, defined as the long-term contribution of an individual's genes to the future population, is a genuine quantity with a natural point of zero (zero fitness means an individual contributes nothing to the future population). Hence, consequences at an evolutionary level always involve quantitative information in the sense that an attribute may result in higher or lower fitness, and that the amount of expected fitness change can be expressed as a real number (McGraw & Caswell, 1996). For example, a physical threat, such as the presence of a predator, directly changes the probability of survival, and thus implies a reduction in evolutionary fitness. Depending on the average probability of death in the presence of the predator, the expected change in fitness may be stronger or weaker -giving rise to potential selection for a quantitative response structure when relational responding to different threats is selected for. In other words, given a relational response structure such as avoidance behavior in the presence of physical threat, the fact that different threats quantitatively vary in their expected effects on evolutionary fitness constitutes a selection pressure for a quantitative response structure and, hence the necessity to generate an internal quantitative discriminative stimulus (we may call it "fear"). The same holds for events that yield an expected gain in evolutionary fitness, such as the relative benefit of different food items (we may call it "food preference"). Depending on the different combinations of contexts and selected behaviors, such quantities should further be expected to be qualitatively distinct. For example, despite them both being quantitative predictors of expected evolutionary fitness, "fear" and "food preference" are qualitatively distinct because they depend on different contexts (i.e., physical threats vs. food items) and different selected behaviors (i.e., flight behavior vs. foraging).

Their quantitative character is determined by their predictive value for changes in evolutionary fitness. Their qualitative character is determined by the unique combination of context, action, and consequences.

Furthermore, internally generated discriminative stimuli may be effective even if there are no physical (i.e., environmental) cues present. They may thus bridge temporal gaps between environmental fitness predictors and an individual's behavior. For example, the sudden loss of a spouse is a strong environmental cue for drastically changing contingency structures with regard to evolutionary fitness. Virtually all fitness predictors that were related to the spouse cease to be effective. If at least some of these fitness predictors were effective in the absence of the spouse (such as a certain time of the day that used to predict the spouse's availability for a phone call), an internal stimulus that signals the absence of the spouse (we may call it "grief") will prevent the individual from showing the behavior that used to be effective (such as dialing the spouse's phone number) and thus foster the exploration of new (more adaptive) behavior. Similarly, when meeting a new potential mate, an internal stimulus that signals the availability of a potential mating opportunity -even if the person of interest is not present -(we may call it "desire") will motivate the individual to engage in otherwise costly courtship behavior that may ultimately increase evolutionary fitness.

The context dependence of selection on relational responding further implies that their structure may differ between individuals. At the level of species wide adaptations (i.e., regarding the effects of natural selection), the dependence of relational responding on environmental context is highly relevant for comparative psychology. Moreover, behavior is also selected at the level of populations (cultural selection) and at the level of the individual (operant selection). Therefore, we should expect relational response structures to differ between individuals depending on their cultural background and their individual learning histories. Importantly, these differences should not only manifest in quantitative variation, but also in qualitatively different discriminative stimuli, depending on the specific environmental consequences.

3.4 Behavioral Psychometrics

Having provided the necessary conceptual and theoretical background to clarify what the putative measurands of psychometric rating-scales might be and how they are shaped by environmental consequences, we can now give a full behavioral account of rating-scales.

The most important insight from our theoretical investigation is arguably that the administration of a rating-scale alone does not guarantee that the resulting numeric responses are genuine instances of measurement. If the putative measurands of rating-scales are in fact internal discriminative stimuli that are shaped by selection on relational responding, some of them might be quantitative, whereas others are not. For internal discriminative stimuli without quantitative structure, any attempt to "measure" them on a numeric scale is futile. On the other hand, the mere existence of quantitatively structured relational responses does not guarantee that the underlying psychological attribute can be assessed by means of a rating-scale. For example, a common way of pain assessment in humans is to present a ten-point scale (either on paper, on a screen, or verbally) and prompt the individual to indicate their current pain on this scale in relation to the worst pain they can imagine (which is anchored at the maximum value of the scale). Such pain ratings are routinely treated as if they corresponded to the actual ratio between the currently experienced pain and the maximum possible amount of pain (which would imply a ratio scale). Applying the theoretical framework outlined above, a rating-scale may be established as a genuine instance of measurement if and only if, a) the structure of a person's response behavior (e.g., their responses to numeric scales) and of their relational responding to the target stimuli (e.g., actual pain stimuli) are both homomorphic to the structure of the intended numerical representation (e.g., the positive real numbers with multiplication), and b) the two relational structures are themselves related by a one-to-one mapping.

Each of these two conditions may or may not hold for a specific combination of target stimuli and rating behavior within a particular individual as well as across individuals. For example, a person may well differentiate between various pain stimuli in a manner that is structurally equivalent to the positive real numbers (i.e., their pain related behavior may indeed be quantitative) but fail to map this quantitative structure to a numeric scale (i.e., condition b does not hold). Another possibility would be that an individual does consistently map their pain related behavior to the numeric scale (i.e., condition b holds), but neither of the underlying empirical structures are isomorphic to the positive real numbers (this may be the case if a person can only perform an ordering of pain stimuli and, at the same time, responds to the numeric scale in accordance with the cardinal numbers, rather than the positive reals). Both the internal structure of relational response behavior to numeric scales, as well as the mapping between an arbitrary quantitative structure and a numeric scale must be demonstrated empirically prior to any claims that the rating produces genuine measurements. In other words, we need to establish a theoretically sound and empirically adequate link between a measurable attribute (such as an internal discriminative stimulus with a quantitative structure) and an individual's numeric ratings of that attribute (cf. Hasselman, 2023; Uher, 2018).

Although theoretically straightforward, empirical tests of such links may actually be hard to conduct, especially if the relational structure underlying an internal discriminative stimulus is not known because we have limited access to an individual's history of operant selection. Nevertheless, the question of how people establish mappings between different relational response structures (such as pain related behavior and numeric rating behavior) might be investigated at a more general level using well-established experimental methods. Specifically, one may conduct operant experiments where a certain (predefined) relational response structure is shaped by providing corresponding feedback to a subject's relational behavior. In other words, instead of speculating about the existence of quantitative mental objects (as implied by the traditional psychometric approach), one might just as well construct specifically designed internal discriminative stimuli as an experimental model for further investigation. For example, one could program the corresponding schedules of reinforcement such that they establish one response structure that is quantitative and one that is not quantitative, and then explore what happens if the subject is instructed to map the two onto a common numeric scale. Such an experimental procedure would answer the question whether the ordering and additivity of quantitative response structures are preserved in such mappings.

Moreover, it would reveal how subjects deal with lacking information about differences on the rating scale when one of the established response structures has merely ordinal structure.

Finally, our framework may be used to investigate variability in relational responding within and between subjects. For example, numeric ratings on a pain scale might be consistent mappings from one quantitative response structure (the one that gives rise to pain related behavior, such as avoidance or escape) to another (the one used in the assessment procedure, namely a numeric rating-scale) for one subject but not for another. Moreover, an individual might be able to map some relational response structures onto numeric scales but not others. Our framework suggests putting these questions to empirical test by comparing different mappings between experimentally established relational structures. Specifically, one could select an individual's relational responses such that two separate quantitative response structures emerge by using two different stimulus sets with different spacings between the training stimuli. Subjects could then be instructed to map a preexisting internal discriminative stimulus (i.e., one that has not been established in the experiment) onto the two experimentally established quantitative structures, independently. If these two mappings yield the same spacings of judgements on both stimulus sets, there is good reason to believe that the ratings were actually based on a consistent mapping based on a quantitative relational response structure (i.e., that they are genuine measurements). If, however, subjects produce different spacings on the two pre-trained quantitative structures, we would conclude that the internal discriminative stimulus under consideration is not measurable on a quantitative scale.

These examples show that the theoretical account proposed here is empirically very rich in that it implies several novel hypotheses about the basic processes underlying the data generated by psychometric rating-scales. Moreover, the fact that relational behavior can be shaped by behavioral selection at the individual level (i.e., operant selection) allows for the construction of specific experiments to test these hypotheses, some of which are outlined in more detail in Appendix A1.

4 Discussion

We have presented a behavioral account of the putative measurands of rating-scales that integrates the representational theory of measurement with theory-based semantics, building on the wellestablished principles of operant behavior. The fundamental claim of our theory is that the measurands of rating-scales are internal discriminative stimuli that underly relational response behavior. Whereas their qualitative mode is determined by the context and the type of action, their quantitative structure is shaped by selection such that it allows for the prediction of gradual changes in evolutionary fitness.

For behavioral domains that are directly linked to changes in evolutionary fitness (e.g., foraging, escape, and avoidance behavior), it appears at least plausible that quantitative relational response structures should emerge. In other contexts, especially in those where the environmental feedback is mediated by complex interaction between individuals (such as those involved in person judgements), there is less reason to expect a quantitative response structure. Furthermore, if our theory is correct, we should expect variation between individuals, cultures, social groups etc., unless the structure of the environmental feedback is identical. Moreover, the same behavioral domain may have qualitatively different structures between individuals.

Of course, a psychometric rating-scale may still yield useful results even if it does not provide genuine measurement of internal discriminative stimuli. As a social practice, the use of rating-scales may still be meaningful, irrespective of their scientific scope (cf. Buntins, 2014; Buntins et al., 2016 Buntins et al., , 2017)). For example, the use of a numeric pain scale may be a useful communicative tool between a physician and a patient even if pain ratings do not actually correspond to a quantitative relational structure of pain related behavior. Similarly, some psychometric rating-scales may serve as evaluative indices (rather than measurements) that help to justify certain decisions or social selection processes (e.g., in the context of personnel selection). Nevertheless, one should not confuse psychometrics as a social practice with genuine measurement and be careful to differentiate between social utility and scientific validity.

An open question raised by our theory is how relational response structures relate to subjective experience. Although the theory itself is formulated in purely behavioral terms, the main implications of our theory on relational responding appear to be in good accordance with subjective experiences.

For example, the different regulatory functions of avoidance behavior in various contexts (e.g., going to the dentist or awaiting an exam) does not only give rise to qualitatively different relational structures at a behavioral level, but seems to be in line with qualitatively different experiences of "fear." Such resemblances point towards the possibility that relational responding might play a pivotal role in explaining the nature of subjective experiences. If selection on relational response behavior consistently aligns with verbal reports of subjective experiences, this would open a whole new field of research for behavior analysis, including phenomena commonly thought to be "private" (i.e., only accessible to the individual under study), such as sensation and emotions. Moreover, we would expect the structure of relational responding to vary with an organism's capability of relational learning, which might explain why humans -being the only known species capable of arbitrary relational learning (cf. Hayes et al., 2001) -have such a variable and complex range of subjective experiences.

The main advantage of such an experimental approach over traditional psychometric methods is that, given the individual is capable of relational learning, the experimenter has full control over the relational response structures under study. It is thus possible to generate model structures with known relational properties and then investigate whether (and if so, how) these structures are mapped onto other response structures -such as numeric rating-scales. Consequently, if our main theoretical claim is correct (i.e., if internal discriminative stimuli actually emerge from relational responding), we may establish specific internal discriminative stimuli as independent variables in a controlled experiment.

The general procedure for the proposed experimental tests is thus: a) choose a set of neutral stimuli (such as geometric patterns or pseudo-words), b) specify a relational structure on the stimulus set, c) perform operant training using a match-to-sample paradigm, using the pre-defined structure as the criterion of selection, d) test whether the individual has acquired the relational structure by means of test trials with untrained stimulus combinations. The independent variables that can be manipulated in this procedure include the type of the relational structure for the training (equivalence, order, similarity, specific operations…), as well as the consistency of the scheduled feedback.

A 1.2 Empirical Predictions

In the following, we will use the relational match-to-sample (RMTS) procedure to derive a collection of critical hypotheses and some open questions from our theory. The list of empirical hypotheses is not meant to be exhaustive, but rather to cover a wide range of potential applications of the theory and address some of the fundamental theoretical claims.

The first critical hypothesis to be tested builds on the claim that internal discriminative stimuli are emergent properties of relational response behavior. This claim can be tested empirically using the RMTS procedure by means of a simple manipulation check after the training session. Specifically, if the structure of relational response behavior emerges from selection by consequences, individuals should generalize the trained relations and operations to stimulus combinations that have not been part of the training set. We know from research on equivalence learning that such emergent properties do exist in humans for "same/different" relations (i.e., human subjects are able to form equivalence classes of neutral stimuli and generalize the relational structure to untrained relational cues). However, it remains open to empirical test whether the same is true for more complex relational structures, and particularly quantitative structures.

The second prediction in the context of emergent variables concern generalization of a learned relational structure to operations that are implied by the structure but have never been specifically selected. For example, a continuously ordered structure with an additive operation (such as concatenation of object stimuli) implies that the mid-point between any two stimuli is well-defined.

Therefore, an individual that has been trained to relate stimuli such that the properties of additivity are fulfilled, should also be able to find the mid-point between two stimuli. We therefore hypothesize that individuals who have been trained on two disjunct sets of stimuli according to the same type of relational structure (e.g., a quantitative structure), but using different operations during training (e.g., addition vs. averaging) with different cues, should consistently apply these operations in both sets when the corresponding cue is present, even if they have never been selected to do so.

Moreover, if internal discriminative stimuli are generalized to other contexts, it should be expected that one quantitative structure can be consistently mapped onto another quantitative structure. We therefore hypothesize that individuals who have been trained on two disjunct sets of stimuli according to the same type of relational structure (e.g., a quantitative structure), but using different operations during training (e.g., addition vs. averaging) with different cues, should consistently map the objects of the two stimulus sets onto each other, such that the relational structure is preserved.

The theory furthermore suggests that internal discriminative stimuli -understood as emergent properties of relational responding -might evoke verbal responses that have been acquired as cues in the context of structurally similar relational domains. For example, during stimulus equivalence trainings, subjects sometimes say that certain pairs of stimuli are "the same," even though this verbal response has been acquired in a completely different context. Analogously, when a quantitative relational structure has been established in an RMTS experiment, individuals might utter verbal responses that have been acquired in other contexts that bear a similar relational structure, such as "more," "difference," or "half as much." We therefore hypothesize that, once a quantitative structure has been acquired by means of a RMTS procedure, when prompted to describe their experiences during the test session, subjects will provide verbal reports about quantitative judgements, including, but not limited to, the use of numerals or other mathematical concepts.

Another central claim of the theory is that internal discriminative stimuli should have different qualities when they involve different behavioral domains. Therefore, depending on the mode of responding established in the RMTS training, we would expect functionally different response structures that should be qualitatively distinct to the subject. Following the same rationale as before, we thus hypothesize that, if two different quantitative structures have been trained using the same stimulus set, but different behavioral domains (e.g., approaching vs. avoiding the target stimulus) that are signaled with two different cues, when prompted to describe their experiences during the test session, subjects will provide verbal reports about qualitative differences regarding the cues used to distinguish between the two structures during training.

The next two predictions are derived from the claim that internal discriminative stimuli will only have a quantitative structure if they provide consistent information about the amount of expected change in evolutionary fitness. Suppose that a quantitative relational structure is trained using a RMTS procedure, but the contingent feedback is only provided with a certain probability smaller than 1 (i.e., the selection procedure involves, to a certain degree, unreliable feedback). This will decrease the available information about the quantitative structure, such that the individual cannot properly acquire the relational response structure that is being selected. Since order is more robust with regard to random noise than additivity, we hypothesize that when a quantitative structure is being trained using a RMTS procedure with added random noise, there will be a critical value of acceptable error above which the individual will only acquire an ordinal relational response structure instead of a quantitative relational structure.

If, however, the amount of noise is itself predictive of the expected value of change in evolutionary fitness, the lack of information about the additive structure may be compensated to a certain degree.

Particularly, if the probability of erroneous feedback during the training of a quantitative response structure is a monotone decreasing function of the pre-defined differences of the training stimuli, we hypothesize that the individual should be expected to respond according to a probabilistic quantitative structure.

A 1.1 The Relational Match-to-Sample Paradigm (RMTS)

Relational learning has been well-studied in human and non-human individuals, mostly in the context of equivalence class formation (Sidman, 1994). Equivalence learning experiments usually employ a match-to-sample task, where individuals have to choose one of several stimuli (the response options) such that it matches a certain target stimulus (Premack, 1983). To avoid confounding factors that might affect the response, the stimulus sets are usually chosen such that they bear no intrinsic information about the relational structure one wishes to establish. Stimulus material often includes abstract geometric shapes or pseudo-words -the rationale being that the stimuli should be as neutral as possible to the individuals. Before the training begins, a relational structure needs to be defined on the stimulus set that determines which responses are counted as "correct" and which as "incorrect". The result is a contingency structure that specifically selects those responses that are consistent with the pre-defined relational structure. The training is usually followed by a test trial where hitherto untrained combinations of stimuli are presented as targets and response options. For example, if the aim is to establish a relational structure where the stimuli a, b, and c are part of one equivalence class, the training trials may include the stimulus combinations a-b and b-c (but not a-c).
