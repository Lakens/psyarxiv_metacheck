Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Memory conversations substantially impact the development of children's autobiographical memory and other capabilities. However, if its impact on memory accuracy is brought into focus, exposure to maternal elaborative conversations may turn out to make children susceptible to suggestions by others; inspiring the question of how these mnemonic practices exactly figure into children's memory development. We argue that suggestibility might be a consequence of how children are socialized to remember, and thus can be explained by their tendency to rely on the input of other people while recalling their memories. This may result in memory errors when conversation partners have mistaken assumptions about the past.

Nevertheless, experience from these mnemonic conversations could also positively impact a number of cognitive abilities -theory of mind, epistemic vigilance or metacognition -which may serve as control processes during retrieval and gradually allow children to discern when to trust their own or other's testimony about the past, resulting in better memory capabilities in the long run.

Introduction

Human beings are fond of discussing their past: referring to past events occurs at around 5 to 7 times per hour during family conversations (Miller, 1994). Children are involved in such conversations as soon as they start to speak: these early dialogues are, of course, quite asymmetric with infants contributing a few words, based on which caregivers build a complex narrative (Fivush, 2019; 2022). For instance, after arriving home, a toddler may look at the parent, and say "ball", upon which the mother starts speaking about their experiences at the playground ("Yes, we were playing with a ball at the playground, and it has rolled under the bush. A lady helped us out, and brought the ball back to us. We were very happy!"). As children age, their accounts of the past become more rich and detailed, and around the age of 3.5, they become able to form a narrative about past events independently (Fivush et al., 1995). By the end of preschool, these narratives become even more complex: children not only describe what happened, but also mention to their partner when and why these events occurred ("In the morning, we went to the playground. I have kicked the ball too hard and it has rolled under the bush."), and relate the events that happened to themselves ("First, I was very worried that we might never find the ball!") (Reese, 2002).

Thus, during the years, children become competent participants of such joint family conversations. In fact, as children become increasingly able to actively enrich the conversation with their own memories, parents do not step back, but instead become more and more involved, contributing new details.

Therefore, the development of these reminiscing conversations could be considered a collaborative spiral in which the parents' responsiveness also plays an increasingly nuanced role (Haden et al., 1997). The socio-cultural account (Nelson & Fivush, 2004; Fivush & Nelson, 2004) proposes that caregiver input in these conversations -predominantly provided by mothers -constitutes an important mechanism through which memory development takes place.

Relatedly, certain linguistic tools have been demonstrated to have a positive impact on children's memory performance (Fivush & Fromhoff, 1988). Specifically, the elaborativeness of a caregiver supports children's developing mnemonic abilities by a tendency to both provide and ask for novel information during the unfolding conversations -through open ended, yes/no questions and statements which entail new information; and also a proclivity to express that they value the children's contributions (evaluative statements). Importantly, there is variance in caregivers' conversational practices: some tend to provide a coherent narrative and highlight associations for their children, and refer to internal states (high elaborative reminiscing style). (For a more detailed account of how elaborative style is measured, see Supplemental Material). Others are more redundant with what they share and the strategy of whom reflects a goal to specifically probe certain information (low elaborative reminiscing style) (Fivush, 2018). This style is stable across time and between siblings (Harley & Reese, 1999; Reese et al., 1993; Haden, 1998), but many caregivers also adapt their conversational strategies as children age: while they use more elaborative statements and yes/no questions in the case of younger children, open questions become more frequent by the end of preschool (Fivush et al., 2006; Haden et al., 2009). Furthermore, while most of the research concerns the conversational style of mothers, these individual differences have also been identified in the case of fathers (Reese & Fivush, 1993; Reese et al., 1996; although see Zaman & Fivush, 2013, in which fathers were found to be generally less elaborative). Interestingly, some investigations show that caregivers are more elaborative with daughters, as opposed to sons (Reese & Fivush, 1993; Fivush et al., 2003; Reese et al., 1996), but this effect does not appear consistently (see Fivush & Wang, 2005; Zaman & Fivush, 2013). This variation in conversation strategy has been shown to impact children's memory development: preschoolers are more likely to recall those details of events which were discussed with a parent either as it was unfolding (Haden et al., 2001) or after it had concluded (McGuigan & Salmon, 2004; Conroy & Salmon, 2006). Furthermore, children of highly elaborative parents show better memory performance -in the form of better structured narratives with more details -even when controlling for other factors such as language, child temperament or attachment (Farrant & Reese, 2000; Reese, 2002); a robust finding that has been substantiated by a recent metaanalysis (Waters et al., 2019). Parental elaboration has an impact on preschoolers' performance on memory tasks that are unrelated to joint reminiscing, suggesting that it contributes to the acquisition of mnemonic strategies as well (Léonard et al., 2024). Additionally, these conversational practices boost the development of other abilities, such as theory of mind, language, emotional development, and literacy skills (Haden et al., 2021; Fivush, 2019 Fivush, , 2022;; Waters et al., 2019; Fivush & Salmon, 2023). Despite the robust finding that reminiscing style impacts memory development, there are some factors which have been investigated with regards to whether they have a modulatory role.

On the one hand, neither educational level (Farrant, 2000 cited by Fivush et al., 2006), nor ethnic or economic background had been demonstrated to substantially impact reminiscing practices (Leyva et al., 2008). However, parents from working class backgrounds seem to be more directive (Burger & Miller, 1999). Notably, there are cultural differences in mnemonic practices: caregivers from cultures which value relatedness, rather than autonomy -such as Chinese or Japanese -tend to be less elaborative (Wang, 2021). Also, mothers have been shown to differ with regards to how much they tend to control a conversation, which has been named autonomy support, and used as a further possible classification of parental conversational style (Cleveland & Reese, 2005; McDonnell et al., 2022). Parents who are more autonomy supportive tend to follow the lead of their child during such discussions, while some are more controlling and thus steer the conversations more according to their own agenda.

All in all, elaborative conversations have been demonstrated to boost memory performance.

However, the impact of joint reminiscing on the children's memory is often measured by the richness of detail or narrative structure of their account -which do not necessarily reflect an accurate recall. This highlights an important issue with regards to joint reminiscing: namely, whether these jointly created representations necessarily reflect an accurate account about the past. Additionally, previous findings also highlight that elaborative recall practices prompt children to rely on and build in information provided by adults into their memories about the past. But what if these adults have mistaken beliefs about the past or are motivated to orient the children towards ascertaining a certain account of previous events? Furthermore, the main goal of an elaborate conversation about a past event is often not to recall the specifics of an event, but to bond or to highlight some general information about the world for the children (Fivush & Nelson, 2004; Kulkofsky & Koh, 2009). For instance, reminiscing about a doctor's appointment may not only entail discussing the details of the event, but also processing the emotions with regards to the particular happenings and strengthening the child's knowledge of how one should behave during such visits, and to make them understand why it is important to go in the future.

Related to these latter points, children may face some challenges stemming from this socialization of remembering: on the one hand, the accurate recall of specific events may be influenced -or even compromised -by the tendency of such conversations to highlight generalizable information. However, children also need to recall specific details of past events when solving some everyday problems -such as finding some toys at their last seen locations -, which requires memories that are accurate and specific.

Furthermore, the accuracy of memories may also be compromised if their caregivers prove to be unreliable sources of information about the past -since children build on the input of their mothers to a great extent. Also, as children age, they gain a growing number of experiences that are not shared with their parents -thus, while discussing these events, the mother may not be as knowledgeable as the child with regards to what happened. They also come to converse about the past with other people more frequently. This highlights further potential challenges, such as what if these conversational partners are unreliable or malevolent. Thus, while in some aspects, children may arguably approach these conversational situations similarly to joint reminiscing about shared events with their mothers -as adults are in fact often reliable sources of information about the past -, they also need to learn to be more cautious in trusting the contributions of other people. We argue that these two facets of retrieval challenges are related: in order to recall accurate information one has to monitor the source with respect to reliability. How may mnemonic practices then impact both the way children remember and their ability to cope with such challenges?

In the current proposal, we highlight that while the developmental benefits of joint reminiscing are undeniable (Fivush, 2019), if accuracy is brought into focus, this socialisation of remembering also brings about certain risks. These include children being easily guided by their mothers and/or other people. Furthermore, as these conversations often serve social functions -such as promoting a sense of belonging -, children may have a hard time noticing when accuracy should be prioritized over reaching a shared understanding. Nevertheless, we suggest that the constructive process of socialization on how to remember through joint reminiscing offers a solution to this challenge as well, since memory conversations also enable children to gradually employ metacognitive abilities -e.g., source monitoring, theory of mind or epistemic vigilance -that support them in realizing that people's accounts of the past may diverge and are fallible. In other words, through participating in these conversations, they come to understand the representational and perspectival nature of memories (Fivush & Nelson, 2006). We propose that consequently, through their refinement, these abilities come to serve as control mechanisms for ensuring appropriate remembering, potentially promoting a balance between reaching a shared understanding and veridicality. All in all then, while joint reminiscing may initially increase the probability of some memory errors, through promoting certain cognitive abilities, elaborative practices of conversing about the past also equip children with tools to counteract the influence of others -which may be manifested in children of elaborative parents learning to resists suggestions more rapidly and efficiently by and following the end of the preschool years.

In the current paper, we further elaborate the proposal, and discuss what we know so far about memory conversations and children's vulnerabilities to other people's suggestions. These two lines of research -memory conversations and suggestibility -have had a different focus with regards to children's memory abilities: while investigations targeting memory conversations highlighted the social nature of remembering and how these practices figure into the development of the richness and narrative structure of children's memories, the suggestibility literature emphasized the need of accuracy in retrieval and the faillability of memory processes. Nonetheless, we believe that both provide interesting insight into how children adapt to challenges concerning remembering. We review findings up to date with regards to these later phenomena and the development of theory of mind, metacognition and epistemic vigilance, and highlight venues for future research.

Memory socialization: benefits and challenges

Expanding on this proposal, as early reminiscing is characterized by a high proportion of input by the mother, children are socialized to build on both her input, and then later on, potentially the input of others while reconstructing their memories. At first, since the mother is usually knowledgeable about events discussed with the child (Fivush, 2022), accepting her as a reliable and alternative source for remembering is warranted, and as a consequence, children are accustomed to building on the information she provides. These experiences may prompt children to turn to others as trustworthy conversation partners, which could be reflected in a tendency to be guided by others' input at recall. We argue that findings concerning the investigation of how suggestible children are by misinformation provided by others could be relevant in order to understand how this socialization of joint reminiscing impacts remembering (Ceci & Bruck, 1993). Suggestibility refers to exploring how the way people -or in this case -children encode, store, report or retrieve past events may be influenced by social or psychological factors (Ceci & Bruck, 1993). This question is especially relevant in forensic and therapeutic contexts, concerning how children may be supported in adequately recalling events that happened to them.

Investigations often target situations which could be considered a "subcategory" of conversations about the past: when the goal is to reconstruct a specific past event as accurately as possible, usually through a conversation with an unfamiliar adult. Relatedly, we argue that the high extent to which young preschoolers are suggestible by others is partly the consequence of this socialization of remembering, which may generally prompt children to rely on the input of others' while recalling a specific event. As a consequence, we would expect that this suggestibility is modulated by some factors: it may be more pronounced if the (mis)information is coming from the mother and if her reminiscing style is elaborative, as children of these parents are accustomed to receiving and building on additional information during memory conversations.

Nevertheless, we also propose that these conversations promote a context in which further cognitive abilities -such as metacognition, epistemic vigilance and theory of mind -are fostered and used in an integrated manner. How would these abilities specifically figure into memory conversations? When recalling a past event, mothers often refer to others' mental states, such as knowledge or belief, or offer the possibility of perspective change. These linguistic forms may -more or less explicitly -convey to children that people remember different things from the same event (for some examples, see Table 1; also Fivush & Nelson, 2006). Such experiences highlight that one should monitor their own and others' beliefs about events, and provide a "practice" ground for related reflective control practices (such as ToM and metacognition abilities). Furthermore, children may learn that others are not always reliable informants with regards to the past, and that they themselves may have relevant information to share or could even contradict others.

In other situations, they may learn information based on which they come to doubt their own account. All in all, experiencing such contradictions between accounts could prompt children to be more vigilant about the sources of their knowledge, whether that be first hand or based on others' accounts. Thus, monitoring the sources of their beliefs (source memory) and monitoring the reliability of others (epistemic vigilance) can be integrated and put to use during memory conversations. As a consequence, we would expect that children become less suggestible as they age, especially those with more elaborative mothers, the reason partly being that these conversations boost cognitive abilities that allow children to monitor their memory retrieval. Next, we explore findings up to date with regards to the complex role of memory conversations in memory development, with a focus on suggestibility and the mentioned abilities.

Reminiscing practices and suggestibility

Children's proneness with regards to others' suggestions are measured with versions of so-called misinformation paradigms (Klemfuss & Olaguez, 2004): children witness some information -through observing an event in person or on video -and then they subsequently receive further, false informationin the form of misleading questions or false narratives -and then they are asked to report the original event. For instance, after the video they may hear the testimony of another person describing the video, but with false details (say that somebody was wearing a red shirt, instead of a blue one in the video). It is also possible to explore how children change their own testimony due to suggestive feedback following a first recall. In all of these cases, we can measure the amount to which children's reports conform to the information suggested by an (unfamiliar) adult. This is often measured based on two types of changes: yielding refers to a person assenting to a misleading information shared by another, while shifting means changing one's response depending on some ambiguous, negative feedback (Gudjonsson, 1984). Based on studies involving these paradigms, preschoolers seem more suggestible than older children or adults (Bruck & Ceci, 1999; Ceci et al., 2016) (although sometimes, older children appear to be more suggestible, Ceci et al., 2007; Otgaar et al., 2018). In more detail, they are more likely to conform to suggestive questions (Thompson et al., 1997) or be confused by misinformation (Ceci et al., 1987; Gobbo, 2000). Some common practices, such as reinforcing expected responses by positive feedback (Garven et al., 2000) or prompting children to visualize events as if they have happened (Ceci et al., 1994), result in preschoolers endorsing false information. Thus, input from others while recalling memories has a great impact on young children's reports.

Remarkably, some characteristics of suggestive interviews -such as asking guiding questions or giving feedback -resemble elaborative conversational practices (Fivush, 2022). Principe and London (2022) highlight that certain instances of elaborative reminiscing -concerning discussions of nonshared events with a parent who tends to be elaborative, but also controlling, thus taking the lead in a discussion -may have a potential "dark side"; namely, that these types of conversations are more likely to result in memory errors in the case of the children, especially if a parent is misinformed. Similarly, but more broadly, Lawson and colleagues (2025) have also proposed that maternal elaboration may indeed be considered a double edged sword when it comes to memory development, focusing on its potential impact on memory accuracy. Relatedly, we would argue that frequent experiences with such conversations could not only lead to errors in some specific cases, but may make children more sensitive to suggestions generally. Evidence shows that elaborative mothers are more likely to behave akin to a suggestive interview if they are misinformed about non-shared events. Also, preschool-aged children of such mothers are more likely to recall the suggested information both in conversations with her and others (Principe at al., 2013; 2017; Lawson et al., 2025). Importantly, Principe et al. (2017) find that preschoolers of elaborative mothers are more likely to actually commit source errors -e.g. reporting actually seeing a rabbit -following a suggestion by their mother. Thus, in young children, elaborative conversational practices may even potentially influence how they remember the source of their memories (seeing something as opposed to being told by someone).

Empirical evidence also highlights that conversation context matters: mothers tend to ask more leading questions from younger children if their goal is to gather information (Klemfuss & Peplak, 2023).

Studying older children -6-year-olds -, Klemfuss and colleagues (2016) found that the extent to which children follow the suggestions of the mother does not depend on elaborative style. However, they have demonstrated that being susceptible to the suggestions of other adults actually depend on reminiscing practices: children of elaborative mothers are less likely to be confused by suggestive questions from another adult (for details of the studies, see Table 2). Notably, in this study, the suggestions to be applied by the parent (and the unfamiliar adult) were embedded in the forms of pre-scripted suggestive yes/no questions, as opposed to in the studies of Principe and colleagues (2013; 2017) in which parents were instructed to discuss events with their children more freely. This is an important difference in methodology with potentially important implications: arguably, pre-scripted yes/no questions differ from the types of conversations children of elaborative parents are accustomed to having, and as a consequence, suggestions embedded in this form may be less "effective" in influencing children of such parents. Furthermore, elaborative parents may also be more persuasive in case they are allowed to converse more freely. Nevertheless, it is also possible that since this study involved slightly older children, their more developed monitoring abilities could have aided them in avoiding misinformation.

In light of the above results, future investigations are needed to explore the scope of the influence of mother's elaborative style, including its impact on suggestibility by others. Longitudinal investigations could shed light on whether the developmental changes in children's suggestibility are modulated by the mothers' reminiscing practices. An explanation to the seeming contradiction -between the above introduced findings from Principe et al., 2013 , 2017 and Klemfuss et al., 2016 -might be that while at first, children of elaborative parents may be more open to suggestions coming from their mother and even others, this tendency could decrease more dynamically compared to children with less elaborative mothers. Furthermore, the long term effect of suggestions would also be interesting to explore; it is possible that suggestions coming from an elaborative mother are more permanently embedded in the children's account, compared to suggestions from others or of different forms. Overall, while further investigations are needed, the findings do suggest that an elaborative approach may put children at risk for suggestions; especially if these are coming from the parent. These findings again highlight a potential contradiction between how elaborative practices promote memory performance, and that these shared representations about the past may not necessarily be accurate -since the (mis)informed parent may (mis)lead the child.

Details of some focal studies investigating how parental reminiscing practices are related to children's suggestibility

How are cognitive control abilities related to reminiscing practices and/or suggestibility?

In the following we provide an overview of findings up to date with regards to the potential interrelatedness of metacognition, epistemic vigilance and ToM with parental reminiscing practices as well as suggestibility, and point out underexplored areas of investigation.

Reasoning about the self: metacognition Memory conversations may figure into the development of metacognitive abilities as they provide a context in which children are able to reflect on both the representational nature of memories and the process of remembering. According to the current proposal, we would expect that children with more elaborative caregivers are characterized by a more rapid improvement in metacognitive abilities as they age, which in turn, could support them in resisting suggestions. Unfortunately, as of yet, no study has specifically targeted this three way relationship, however, we believe longitudinal investigations of such interrelatedness would be a fruitful venue for future research. Nevertheless, in the following sections, we will focus on describing a collection of studies which have targeted exploring how parental reminiscing and children's suggestibility may separately be related to children's developing sense of self, and two further metacognitive abilities (uncertainty monitoring and metamemory).

Uncertainty monitoring concerns one's "ability to evaluate their subjective feeling of the likely (in)accuracy of potential responses" (Lyons & Ghetti, 2011). Children as young as around 18-20 months of age seem to adequately modify their behaviour in some situations involving more or less certain outcomes, suggesting that they possess some rudimentary form of this ability (for an overview, see Goupil & Kouider, 2019). This ability -among others things -concerns how confident one is with regards to the accuracy of one's memory, and thus is relevant when it comes to suggestibility since if one is able to adequately assess whether they remember something accurately and this is reflected in more certainty, they would arguably be less likely to be swayed by others' inaccurate testimony. Metamemory is a more complex ability that can be defined as one's knowledge of and awareness of memory (Flavell & Wellmann, 1975). Indeed, in a certain way, one may consider monitoring uncertainty with regards to the details of a past event to constitute a part of metamemory (in Shao (2010) mentioned below, uncertainty monitoring is actually considered to be a procedural metamemory task). Similarly, more developed metamemory abilities may support children in noticing when (not) to trust their own memories, as well as reflecting on the failability of both their own, and others' recall processes, thus resulting in reduced suggestibility.

On the relationship between reminiscing practices and children's metacognitive abilities, it seems that maternal elaborativeness promotes self-awareness: children with elaborative mothers seem to pass the visual self-recognition task (mirror task) earlier (Harley & Reese, 1999 cited by Nelson & Fivush, 2004), and mental state references used by mothers are shown to influence the complexity of children's self-evaluations (Wang et al., 2010). Likewise, some parental reminiscing practices -such as metamemory talk used by the parent ("I have such a poor memory.") -and children's metacognitive abilities indicate a positive relationship (Léonard et al., 2023; Geurten & Léonard, 2023). Thus, related to the above described prediction, while elaborative practices indeed seem to be positively related to the development of some metacognitive abilities, the specific impact of elaborative talk -focusing on comparing high and low elaboration -still needs to be explored with regards to the development of self-awareness.

With regards to the other factors from the prediction, a few studies have investigated if children's suggestibility is positively related to their performance in certain metacognitive abilities. On the one hand, Shao (2010, Experiment 1, Procedural Metamemory task) shows that preschoolers -aged around 4.3 years on average -who are better at adequately monitoring their (un)certainty are less suggestible. Roebers (2002) demonstrates, however, that even quite older, 8 to 10 year-old children's assessment of confidence with regards to the accuracy of their responses is greatly impacted by contextual factors: while it is stable in case of neutral questioning, it is impaired in case their partner is misleading and assertive.

Nevertheless, even in this study, those who were more likely to report being uncertain about their incorrect response to a misleading question had better overall recall accuracy scores. This suggests that even though children's metacognitive monitoring performance may be fragile, there already seems to be an association between better sensitivity to inaccurate responding and memory performance. Thus, further studies are needed to explore the robustness of a potential positive association between uncertainty monitoring and resisting suggestions, while also focusing on how certain conversational factors may impact such a relationship. Whether children's better metamemory performance is related to diminished suggestibility has also received attention. Geddie and colleagues (2000) demonstrate that preschoolers showing better metamemory performance are indeed less suggestible. Similarly, Mazzoni (1998) shows that school-aged -9-year-old -children who are more confident in their memory abilities are less likely to incorporate information suggested by others. However, Shao (2010, Experiment 1, Declarative Metamemory task) finds that younger, preschooler aged who perform better at a task measuring their explicit reasoning about others' memory processes -through responding to questions such as who would win a memory contest, the one who rehearsed the information or the one who did not -are more likely to shift their response following feedback from adults. Note, on the one hand, that children were younger in the study of Shao (2010), than in Mazzoni (1998). Furthermore, Mazzoni (1998) sensitized children to monitor their own mnemonic abilities, Shao (2010) did the same for those of others; thus, they might have introduced different biases in monitoring that resulted in different responses to social suggestive interactions.

Additionally, there are methodological differences in the two studies; for instance, Mazzoni (1998) employed a recognition task concerning neutral, veridical and misinformation, while Shao (2010) has shown the effect when it comes to children's responses to negative feedback by adults. Nevertheless, future investigations could clarify the reason for this difference, and also explore if there is indeed a positive relationship -suggested by some studies -between metamemory and resisting suggestions whether parental conversational style may play a role in such an association.

Reasoning about the sources of our memories: source monitoring Monitoring the sources of memories could be important for a number of reasons; for instance, one may be able evaluate the validity of their knowledge through retaining the source from which it came -which could mean recalling whether information is of first-or second-hand, or a specific person as the source (Sperber et al., 2010). With regards to our proposal, we would expect elaborative conversations to boost the development of source memory, which in turn, could support children in resisting suggestions. No specific study has explored source monitoring performance, parental reminiscing practices and suggestibility at the same time; similarly to metacognition, we believe this would be an important interrelatedness to investigate. Nor are there any investigations specifically targeting if the children of more elaborative parents show better source monitoring performance. Nevertheless, Nelson (2021) demonstrates that the frequency of mental state references by a mother during a memory conversation is associated with better source accuracy in preschool-aged children. Thus, while this study did not explore elaboration per se, these findings further warrant the exploration of the potential association between elaboration and source monitoring..

With regards to if source monitoring supports children in resisting suggestions, a number of investigations substantiate that superior source monitoring abilities seem to be associated with reduced suggestibility in the case of preschoolers (Mazzoni, 1998; Poole & Lindsay, 2001; Poole & Lindsay, 2002; Giles et al., 2002; Thierry et al., 2001; Bright-Paul et al., 2005). For instance, children appear less suggestible when source-related tasks precede a suggestive questioning (Giles et al., 2002; Thierry et al., 2001; Bright-Paul et al., 2005); and this is especially true in the case of older children (Poole & Lindsay, 2001; Poole & Lindsay, 2002). These findings altogether highlight the relevance of studying how other aspects of reminiscing conversations -for instance, by comparing high and low elaboration specificallymay relate to source monitoring development, and if the rather convincing association between better source monitoring performance and diminished suggestibility is somewhat moderated by parental reminiscing practices.

Reasoning about others: epistemic trust/vigilance Furthermore, memory conversations may help children in honing their epistemic vigilance skills, such as discerning the trustworthiness of other people. More specifically, if children display mistrust in others based on some cues early on, this may protect them from being suggested by them. Studies suggest that some aspects of this "reliability monitoring" may already be quite early developing: for instance, 12-months-olds differentiate between knowledgeable and ignorant others while facing uncertainty (Bazhydai et al, 2020). Relatedly, we would argue that these early competences might be more rudimentary, being manifested in a more rigid, all-or-nothing discarding of information from sources deemed unreliable. Memory conversations may support the further development of such abilities, involving more complex, context sensitive reasoning about others' reliability, with a special focus on how this could be ascertained with regards to past events. Thus, we would expect children of more elaborative parents to be better at assessing if they should trust their partners' account(s) about past events, which could also help them in resisting suggestions when warranted.

While no specific study investigates the role of elaborative conversations in this respect, findings show that children are not equally suggestible by all others; for instance, children are less likely to follow suggestions of adults who are labelled as silly (Lampinen & Smith, 1995) or are uninformed (Robinson & Whitcombe, 2003). Furthermore, they are less susceptible to suggestions from other children (Ceci et al., 1987). These results may stem from children's ability to identify attributes which may be important when it comes to discerning the reliability of another person's past account, since it seems warranted not to let uninformed others or those who are generally less knowledgeable (such as other children) to influence their own account of the past. Resistance to suggestions by certain others may be one sign that preschoolers can in fact "protect" themselves from mistaken influence in some situations. However, Shao (2010, Experiment 4) finds that children who are better at discerning whether another person is a reliable source of information are not actually less suggestible. These findings nonetheless pinpoint that the reliability of others can manifest itself in many ways, either through their behavior or through the expression of their opinions, and its assessment is also part of the development process. Thus, these highlight the relevance of investigating whether maternal elaborativeness is related to children's reasoning about reliability and trustworthiness.

Reasoning about others: theory of mind Conversations about past events provide ample opportunities for children to gain experience with how others' mental representations -such as accounts of past events -may (mis)align with their own.

According to this proposal, we would assume that children with elaborative mothers are characterized by better ToM abilities, and that the application of these abilities in remembering contexts could be reflected in decreased suggestibility. One study did already examine the connection between reminiscing style and suggestibility, which also included a theory of mind measure: Klemfuss and colleagues (2016) find that parental elaboration is positively associated with children's resistance to false suggestions; however, ToM performance did not play a role in the extent to which children are suggestible by the mother or others.

Future investigations could explore whether this is a robust finding, and whether ToM plays a mediating role in the case of younger children (around an average age of 3 to 5).

Whether children of elaborative parents are indeed characterized by better ToM performance, findings convincingly show that this is the case (for as metanalysis, see Waters et al., 2019). For instance,, an intervention targeting the promotion of elaborative conversational practices -involving children from the age of 19 to 44 months -resulted in improvement in theory of mind performance of children who initially showed worse linguistic performance (which competence is highly related to theory of mind scores) (Taumoepeau & Reese, 2013). Interestingly, in a study which investigated the impact of elaborative practices and maternal explicit mental state references simultaneously, elaboration turned out to be a stronger predictor of ToM performance (Ontai & Thompson, 2008). Thus, this aspect of reminiscing seems to specifically promote the development of children's reasoning about other people's minds.

When it comes to a potential positive relationship between diminished suggestibility and better ToM performance, however, reviews demonstrate mixed findings (Bruck & Melnyk, 2004; Klemfuss & Olaguez, 2020): seven studies find that superior ToM performance is related to reduced suggestibility; six investigations did not show ToM to be related to resistance to suggestions; yet, in two studies, more developed ToM was actually associated with increased suggestibility. Further investigations show a complex picture: some studies indicate that better ToM performance shields children against suggestions (Bright-Paul et al., 2008), especially below the age of 4.6 (Pérez-Mata et al., 2022). However, Melinder and colleagues (2006) find that ToM ability does not predict resistance to suggestions if chronological age and inhibition are taken into consideration. All in all, further studies would be needed to clarify why ToM is associated with decreased suggestibility in some situations, but not in others, and if this could be related to the mother's conversational style; potentially with involving a more precise and broader measurement of ToM competencies, like the ToM Scale (Wellmann & Liu, 2004). Furthermore, while metacognitive and ToM abilities are arguably linked to each other -for instance, ToM abilities may serve as a metacognitive tool to aid monitoring of mental states, including memories -, the motivation to understand others, such as keeping track of their perspectives even over time (see Király et al., 2018), may also result in ToM abilities contributing to the emergence of more sophisticated memory abilities.

Overall, findings about the relationship between parental reminiscing practices, suggestibility and various socio-cognitive abilities present a complex picture, and further investigations are needed in order to understand exactly how memory conversations allow the unfolding of these cognitive processes. Yet, we would argue that these highlight the important contribution mnemonic conversations may have with regards to children's emerging abilities to monitor both their own mental states and the mental state of others.

With regards to alternative explanations, many potentially relevant abilities develop greatly during the preschool years, which may support children in resisting suggestions. For instance, abilities related to executive functions have been shown to mature substantially between the ages of 3 and 6 (Zelazo et al., 2003; Carlson & Moses, 2001). Relatedly, the development of inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility are pertinent when it comes to resisting suggestions suggestibility since children need to be able to recall their memories and uphold keeping to their own account, which processes rely on these abilities to a great extent. We would argue, however, that while the development of such abilities may in themselves already result in the reduction of suggestibility, elaborative practices may have further beneficial effects. Thus, we expect to still find that differences in parental elaborative practices may contribute to individual differences in suggestibility between children in groups with similar levels of development when it comes to executive functions. Currently, this remains an empirical question.

Although the impact of reminiscing practices have been studied extensively, some venues for future research also remain, which may be of relevance when it comes to this current proposal. On the one hand, it would be fruitful to more extensively scrutinize how children behave during such reminiscing conversations and how these behaviours may impact the parents, and if these behaviours are related to the variation in parental practices in any way. For instance, Lawson and colleagues (2024) have measured the extent to which children contradict or correct their parents while discussing the past, and find that around 30% of participants did so, and older children -6 to 7-year olds -were more likely to do so than younger children (4 to 5 years of age). We believe it would be interesting to explore if this aspect of children's contribution is related to elaborative style and the target abilities. We would expect children of elaborative, autonomy supportive to be more active in correcting their parents, and that this could be positively related to their developing metacognitive or epistemic vigilance related abilities on the long run. Furthermore, while some studies have focused on exploring the elaborative practices of fathers (Reese & Fivush, 1993; Reese et al., 1996; Zaman & Fivush, 2013), most of the investigation still focus on the mother-child dyad while considering the impact of elaboration. We consider more extensively studying the influence of further family members to be also of importance, especially as the social circle of children increasingly widens during the preschool years (Bohanek et al., 2009).

Conclusions

Primarily, mnemonic conversations create a context in which children learn about how memories are formed, used and shared, and how making inferences based on specific events allow them to predict what may happen in similar circumstances. During such conversations, mothers continuously help children in recalling their memories; through adding details and asking questions. These practices may understandably orient children to build on the input of mothers, which is reflected in potential memory errors in case she is misinformed. However, in this proposal, we emphasize that the way children generalize their experiences from such conversations remains to be further investigated, along with whether children with elaborative mothers, or more responsive mothers, are more suggestible by others generally. The suggestibility literature implies that young children are especially likely to accept false information from others, but further studies could explore if there is indeed a variability depending on the mothers' reminiscing practices and the impact this later may have on the developmental trajectory of children's suggestibility.

In this novel proposal, we pinpoint that while elaborative conversational practices may at first make children indeed vulnerable to suggestions, the content of such conversations highlight to them that both their own and the cognitive processes of others can and need to be monitored. Furthermore, these conversations allow them to practice control processes in an integrated manner, with the scaffolding of experienced others. In the long run, this may result in children learning to flexibly adapt to the changing goals of remembering, and thus becoming able to focus on accuracy, even if this requires discarding others' or their own (potentially fallible) account. Systematic research is still needed to explore how such control processes figure into memory conversations. Nevertheless, we believe that the findings from pairwise investigations targeting the potential interrelations of reminiscing practices, the development of ToM, metacognition, source memory and epistemic trust imply that this constitutes fruitful venues for future research.

Supplemental Material 1. Challenges of Operationalizing Mother-Child Reminiscing

After more than three decades of research on the various aspects of parental reminiscing, the central problem of quantifying parent-child memory conversations still remains to be refined. The foundations of assessing maternal reminiscing style have been established by Fivush and colleagues (Nelson & Fivush, 2004; Fivush & Nelson, 2004), and was later advanced by several researchers (Haden, 1998; Reese & Brown, 2000; Laible, 2004; Kulkofsky, 2011; Principe et al., 2017). This approach posits that the reminiscing style of the mother is highly elaborative if it contains a high number of propositions that move the conversation forward, add new details and encourage the child to expand on their statements. The coding unit of this original coding system (Reese & Fivush, 1993) is defined as unique maternal utterances which can be classified as elaborations, repetitions or evaluations. Thus, elaborativeness can be calculated by summing the number of adjectives or adverbs, "Wh"-type questions (What, Why, Where, How, What kind), yes-no questions, associations, and positive or negative evaluations, yielding an absolute frequency score.

While most studies still adapt this frequency-based coding system from Reese and Fivush (1993) and code maternal propositions into four categories -"Memory question elaborations", "Yes-no question elaborations", "Memory question repetitions", "Yes-no question repetitions" or evaluations -, the specific computations used to quantify elaborativeness as a construct vary across the literature (Waters et al., 2019). The most influential alternative coding system calculates the ratio instead of the frequency of elaborations using a 5-point scale (Laible, 2004), providing a "macro" perspective of the overall tone (Leyva et al., 2019) and capturing elaborativeness within the dynamic dyadic turn-taking of a mother and child. However, by restricting scoring to a 5-point range and dividing elaborations with the total number of utterances, ratio data obscure the individual variability in mothers' elaborations, better reflected through absolute frequency scores.

Two recent reviews assessing studies of maternal reminiscing style (Leyva et al., 2019; Waters et al., 2019) analyse these key differences in operationalizing parent-child interactions in detail and highlight the diversity of the specific procedures implemented to compute the elaboration codes with either of the approaches. Crucially, such inconsistencies in compositing maternal elaboration scores make it difficult to accurately determine the predictive role and significance of the various components of maternal elaboration in the observed differences in children's cognitive and affective development.

Therefore, the methodological challenge of defining appropriate coding procedures and that also of identifying how exactly the variations in the detected effects are determined by the implemented approach has been at the forefront of research on memory conversations. The recent literature has highlighted the importance of well-planned, conscious choice of coding systems and demonstrated that while there is not necessarily a best, "one-size-fits-all" coding method, a larger consensus on the notable components of these conversations, a more consistent approach for the operationalization and data analysis along with more dynamic modelling, could better capture the naturalistic and transactional nature of this fundamental developmental process (Waters et al., 2019).
