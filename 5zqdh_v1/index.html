Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

Abstract

Survey research is ubiquitous in psychological science, but self-reports may be biased by how people communicate their internal states. The Latent State and Response Style (LASARS) model separates variation attributable to latent states from variation attributable to response styles, enabling response-style-adjusted estimates of psychological constructs. To make the method accessible, we developed both an R package and a module for the point-and-click statistical software jamovi, and this tutorial demonstrates their use. The jamovi implementation uses maximum-likelihood estimation and requires no programming experience, while the R package uses hierarchical Bayesian estimation and provides full posterior distributions. Using an empirical worked example, we illustrate data preparation, model specification, output interpretation, and how applying LASARS may alter substantive conclusions. We also describe additional functions for model evaluation and flexible application. This tutorial aims to encourage broader adoption of response style modelling in psychological and survey research.

The Latent State and Response Style (LASARS) Model: An Introductory Tutorial Introduction

Self-report measures are a foundational tool in psychological science (Paulhus, Vazire, et al., 2007), but responses reflect more than the latent states 1 researchers seek to measure. When completing Likert-type questionnaires, with items like the one in Figure 1A, respondents differ systematically in how they use response scales, with some tending to favour extreme (Batchelor & Miao, 2016; Clarke III, 2000), midpoint (Chyung et al., 2017), or otherwise particular response categories regardless of item content (Paulhus, Vazire, et al., 2007). These response styles can distort measurement and complicate the interpretation of survey scores (Baumgartner & Steenkamp, 2001; Schauberger & Tutz, 2022). The Latent State and Response Style (LASARS) framework provides a means of disentangling substantive variation from variation arising through the response process. By leveraging existing statistical techniques (H.-S. Lee & O'Mahony, 2007; M. D. Lee & Ke, 2022; Liddell & Kruschke, 2018), the framework simultaneously estimates latent states and individual response tendencies, allowing researchers to quantify and adjust for response style effects and providing a theoretical interpretation or survey responding. 1 The term 'latent state' is used to describe any construct, trait, behaviour, attitude, or emotion that a survey instrument aims to measure. We intentionally use this broad term because the methods described in this paper are applicable across all domains of self-report instruments. We encourage readers to substitute the term 'latent state' with the specific constructs assessed in their own research to more clearly understand how this method may benefit them. 1.

The LASARS framework is motivated by the view that response styles reflect stable individual differences in how people communicate latent states, rather than merely random measurement error (Grimmond et al., 2026). Separating response style variation from substantive latent state variation is, therefore, important for improving the precision of latent state measurement and allows the causes, correlates, and consequences of response style to be investigated. This has the potential to improve the psychometric performance of self-report measures by reducing systematic measurement error and producing continuous latent state estimates. Given these many benefits, we have developed both a jamovi module and an R package that automate model estimation and reduce the barriers to implementationfoot_0 . The jamovi module provides an intuitive point-and-click interface with quick model application and minimal technical overhead, while the R package offers greater flexibility and control, including implementation within a hierarchical Bayesian framework and advanced modelling options. In this tutorial, we provide a practical guide to using both platforms to model response styles and obtain latent state estimates that are less affected by individual differences in scale use to make the model accessible to all researchers who use Likert-scale surveys.

Worked Example: Background and Research Question

Throughout this paper, we use a worked example based on an attempt to replicate the findings of Gärtner et al. (2024) in a general population sample. Gärtner et al. (2024) found that personality traits, measured using the HEXACO model (Ashton & Lee, 2009), predicted the need for cognitive closure (NFCC; measured with a German translation (Schlink & Walther, 2007)) among undergraduate medical students. NFCC refers to an individual's desire for certainty and a preference for definite answers over ambiguity or uncertainty (Webster & Kruglanski, 1994) and Gärtner et al. (2024) argue that this is particularly relevant in medical contexts, where a strong desire for closure may increase the likelihood that clinicians prematurely settle on a diagnosis or cease further clinical reasoning before all relevant possibilities have been considered.

Their results indicated that higher levels of Openness, Extraversion, and Honesty-Humility, and lower levels of Conscientiousness and Emotionality, were associated with significantly lower NFCC scores (Gärtner et al., 2024). Although the reported effects were generally small, Openness emerged as the strongest predictor of NFCC. In the present example, we examine whether these findings generalise beyond a sample of medical students and whether a similar pattern of associations is observed when alternative measures of personality and NFCC are used.

The LASARS Modelling Framework

The LASARS framework is based on an ordinal-probit model of survey responding in which observed Likert responses arise from two distinct sources of variation: respondents' underlying latent states and their characteristic response styles. The model assumes that latent states exist on continuous dimensions and that observed responses are generated by sampling from this internal distribution and mapping the sampled value onto a finite set of ordered response categories. Figure 1B visually represents this process and shows how the same location on the latent state continuum can yield different survey responses because of different threshold placements. These threshold placements reflect people's response styles and the three examples shown in Figure 1B reflect hypothetical response styles that represent high values in the three response parameters captured by the lasars model. Importantly, the model assumes that each respondent applies the same set of response thresholds across all latent states measured by a survey instrument, meaning that threshold differences are interpreted as properties of the response process rather than of any particular construct. This allows the model to simultaneously estimate latent state locations and response style tendencies, separating variation attributable to the constructs being measured by the survey from variation arising through systematic differences in scale use. The latstat model remains available on both platforms for applications where researchers require maximum flexibility or wish to model threshold locations directly, and it is included in the worked example presented in this tutorial in the interest of completeness.

Worked Example: Data Setup

The data used in this study were collected in 2021 as part of a larger project incorporating multiple surveys and behavioural tasks. A total of 207 participants were recruited through Prolific Academic and completed the Need for Closure Scale (NFCS; Webster and Kruglanski (1994)) and the 50-item International Personality Item Pool (IPIP) questionnaire (Goldberg et al., 1999), among other measures, over several days in a counterbalanced order. For both surveys, the data were reshaped into an item-wise format, such that each row corresponded to a single survey item response.

The NFCS consists of 41 items rated on a 6-point scale, with response options labelled 'Strongly Disagree', 'Moderately Disagree', 'Slightly Disagree', 'Slightly Agree', 'Moderately Agree', and 'Strongly Agree'. The scale comprises five subscales which capture preference for structure and organisation (Order; 10 items, three reverse-scored), discomfort arising from uncertainty (Ambiguity; 9 items measuring), the tendency to reach conclusions quickly and confidently (Decisiveness; 6 items), preference for consistency across situations (Predictabil-ity; 8 items, three reverse-scored) and an unwillingness to challenge existing beliefs (Close-Mindedness; 8 items, five reverse-scored). The 50-item IPIP measures the Big Five personality traits, with 10 items assessing each of the traits -Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism (Goldberg, 1992; Goldberg et al., 1999). Each subscale con- tains approximately equal numbers of positively and negatively worded items.

Respondents rate the accuracy of each statement on a 5-point scale with labels 'Very Inaccurate', 'Moderately Inaccurate', 'Neither Accurate Nor Inaccurate', 'Moderately Accurate' and 'Very Accurate'.

An example data set is available through the jamovi data library, allowing users to explore the required data structure and reproduce the analysis shown in Figure 2.

Worked Example: Running the Analysis in Jamovi

To first replicate the findings of Gärtner et al. (2024), we scored the IPIP and NFCS using the same approach as the original study. Henceforth referred to as the conventional scoring method, this involved reverse-scoring negatively keyed items and summing scores to obtain total scores for all IPIP and NFCS latent states. The NFCS latent state scores were then summed to produce one overall score for each participant, with higher values indicating a stronger need for cognitive closure. A multiple regression was performed on conventional scores with each of the five IPIP constructs as predictors and total NFCC score as the outcome variable.

We then applied the lasars model to the IPIP data. This allows us to estimate both latent state and response style parameters simultaneously. For the NFCS data, we instead applied the latstat model. This choice was made primarily for demonstration purposes, as it provides an opportunity to illustrate the use of both modelling approaches in a single tutorial. In practice, we would generally recommend applying the lasars model to both surveys. An alternative approach would be to combine the two surveys into a single data set and analyse them jointly, assuming that participants' response style parameters are consistent across tasks. Although this is a stronger assumption than fitting each survey separately, recent evidence suggests that response style tendencies may be sufficiently stable across different tasks for such an approach to be reasonable in many applications (Grimmond et al., 2026).

Running the Analysis in R

For users who prefer working within R, the lasars package is available on CRAN and provides access to a hierarchical Bayesian implementation of the model. By default, the package estimates model parameters using Particle Metropolis within Gibbs (PMwG; Kuhne et al., 2026), a sampling-based approach that returns full posterior distributions for both group-and subject-level parameters, along with estimates of the covariance structure among model parameters (Gunawan et al., 2020). This differs from the jamovi implementation, which uses maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). As a result, the lasars R package provides richer information about parameter uncertainty, individual differences, and relationships among model parameters, albeit at greater computational cost; a detailed comparison of the PMwG and MLE methods is provided in the Supplementary Information. The hierarchical Bayesian framework implemented in lasars also enables a wider range of modelling options and output summaries than are currently available in jamovi. To support these more advanced analyses, the package includes a comprehensive vignette with additional examples, detailed descriptions of package functionality, and guidance on common analytical workflows.

Input

To fit the lasars model in R, users must provide the name of the data frame, the maximum number of response options available on the survey instrument (specified as a numeric value), and the column names of the subject, response, and latent state variables (specified as character strings). In addition, users must specify whether the directional preference parameter is to be estimated using a Boolean TRUE/FALSE argument. When directional preference is included, the name of the directional-coding variable and the level identifying reverse-scored items must also be supplied as character strings. These arguments are always required when fitting the latstat model. Users may also provide additional PMwG sampling settings, including the number of particles, the number of iterations, the target acceptance probability, and the number of processing cores to use. Detailed descriptions of these options are provided in Kuhne et al., 2026, and any PMwG settings may be passed to the run_lasars() and run_latstat() functions. The default settings run the model for 3,000 iterations with 50 particles at each stage of the PMwG algorithm, which will be sufficient for estimation in most applications and can generally be left unspecified.

For testing, debugging, or exploratory model specification, users may wish to temporarily reduce these values (e.g., to 100 iterations and 10 particles) to obtain rapid feedback before fitting the full model. Alternatively, the sampling_method argument can be set to "mle" to estimate the model using maximum likelihood methods. This approach produces results equivalent to those obtained from the jamovi implementation and offers substantially faster estimation times, albeit at the expense of the additional uncertainty and covariance information provided by PMwG estimation.

Worked Example: Running the Analysis in R

To run the analysis in R, we first applied the model independently to the IPIP and NFCS data by loading the lasars package and calling the run_lasars() function for the IPIP data and the run_latstat() function for the NFCS. Upon completion, we extracted the posterior mean of each subject-level parameter and combined the IPIP and NFCS estimates into a single data set. As with the jamovi analysis, a total NFCC score was calculated by summing the five NFCS latent state estimates and used as the dependent variable in a multiple regression with the five personality traits entered as predictors.

Output

The R implementation will return all the parameters described in the jamovi Output section. When the MLE sampling method is used in R, the returned output is a simple data frame containing subject-level parameter estimates. This format mirrors the subject-level estimates available through the jamovi implementation, whereby each row corresponds to a single participant and contains point estimates for all estimated model parameters, along with information regarding model convergence. However, the default estimation method in the R package is PMwG, meaning the output contains the full posterior distribution of samples generated during model estimation. Specifically, the output is a nested PMwG object containing information about the fitted data, prior distributions, group-level parameter samples (theta_mu), subject-level parameter samples (alpha), and covariance parameter samples (theta_sig). The object also stores information from each stage of the PMwG sampling procedure, allowing users to inspect convergence, evaluate sampling performance, and access posterior draws for custom analyses. Conventional Scoring Method

By applying the conventional scoring approach used by Gärtner et al. (2024), we were able to replicate most of their reported findings. Model-Adjusted Scores

In many respects, the results were similar after accounting for response style. Consistent with the conventionally scored analyses, both the jamovi and R model-adjusted analyses indicated that higher levels of Conscientiousness and Neuroticism significantly predicted NFCC, while neither Agreeableness nor Extraversion was a significant predictor. Importantly, however, Openness, which emerged as the strongest predictor of NFCC in Gärtner et al. (2024), was no longer a significant predictor when the regression was performed using model-adjusted latent state estimates.

This pattern suggests that some of the association between Openness and NFCC observed under conventional scoring methods may reflect shared response style variance rather than substantive overlap between the underlying constructs. By accounting for response style tendencies while estimating latent states, the lasars framework removes variance attributable to differences in scale use, resulting in a weaker relationship between Openness and NFCC. Consequently, for researchers relying on conventional significance thresholds, the substantive conclusions regarding the role of Openness in predicting NFCC would differ depending on whether or not response style effects were accounted for during measurement.

Additional Package Functions

The R package also includes a number of additional functions that allow users to evaluate model fit, customise model specification and apply the framework in more advanced ways. The gen_pp_data() function generates synthetic data sets from fitted model estimates, enabling posterior predictive checks and other forms of model assessment. This functionality can be useful for evaluating how well the model reproduces characteristics of the observed data and for exploring the response patterns associated with particular parameter values or response style profiles. Users may also use the make_priors() function to override the default prior distributions and specify priors that are better aligned with their application or substantive knowledge. This allows experienced users to implement alternative estimation approaches beyond PMwG and MLE, or to incorporate the models into broader modelling frameworks as required. Expanded description of the additional package functions is provided in the vignette accompanying the R package.

Application Considerations

Although the model is broadly applicable across a wide range of Likert scale formats, there are some important considerations and circumstances in which workarounds may be required. Most notably, the model requires information from multiple latent states to distinguish response style variation from substantive responding, although these states may represent related facets of a broader construct. For genuinely unidimensional measures, the model can still be applied by jointly analysing a second instrument that uses the same response format. Because the data requirements for successful estimation remain unclear and likely vary across estimation methods, users should routinely inspect convergence diagnostics and model fit indices, particularly when working with small samples, few items, or unusual questionnaire designs. If there is insufficient constraint in the data to estimate the model, both jamovi and R methods will indicate issues with model convergence.

The model produces continuous latent state estimates, offering greater precision than summed scores and reducing some limitations associated with ordinal data, such as edge effects and score compression. However, these benefits come with an important trade-off: the resulting estimates are expressed on a different scale from the raw or summed scores used in most normative and benchmark-based assessments. Although de-biased scores on the original response scale can be derived with modification to the likelihood functions, no universally optimal approach currently exists. For this reason, we have not implemented options for such transformations in either the R package or the jamovi module. Nevertheless, users interested in generating de-biased scores on the original scale are encouraged to explore these possibilities in ways that best align with their specific applications and research objectives.

Finally, it is important to emphasise that accounting for response style will not always produce large or substantively meaningful changes in the results. However, the magnitude and direction of response style bias is typically unknown prior to analysis, making it difficult to determine whether observed relationships reflect substantive associations between latent states or shared aspects of the response process. Consequently, even when response style adjustment has little impact on the final conclusions, applying the model provides greater confidence that the resulting estimates more accurately reflect the latent states of interest.

General Summary and Conclusion

Response style can bias Likert-scale scores and distort inferences about the latent states that surveys are intended to measure, yet it may also represent a meaningful and stable individual difference in its own right (Grimmond et al., 2026). To help researchers separate substantive latent state variance from response style effects, we have developed implementations of the LASARS modelling framework in both jamovi and R. This tutorial has demonstrated the application of the lasars and latstat models across both platforms, outlining data requirements, key input options, outputs, model selection considerations, and available estimation methods. Through a worked example reflecting a typical survey-based workflow, we showed that these methods can be incorporated into existing analyses with minimal disruption and that substantive conclusions may differ when response style variance is explicitly modelled, highlighting the value of accounting for response style when analysing self-report data.

By reducing the technical barriers associated with model fitting, we hope these tools increase the accessibility and adoption of methods that explicitly separate response style from latent state expression. The modelling framework can be applied retrospectively to most Likert scale data sets, regardless of the construct, attitude, or behaviour being measured, allowing data to be re-analysed in ways that account for response style effects. We hope that accounting for response style becomes a routine consideration when analysing Likert scale data and we look forward to seeing the diverse ways in which these methods are applied across research domains, disciplines, and methodological traditions. As both packages are still under active development, we encourage users to report bugs, suggest improvements, and provide feedback on their experiences. Such input will play an important role in guiding future development and ensuring that the software continues to meet the needs of the research community.

Threshold locations can be parameterised in different ways and the LASARS framework currently implements two paramterisation alternatives which may each be appropriate for different research questions and survey designs. The Latent State Only (latstat) model estimates threshold placement as absolute locations, providing a highly flexible representation of response behaviour but yielding threshold estimates that are somewhat difficult to interpret substantively. In contrast, the Latent State and Response Style (lasars) model imposes a more parsimonious parameterisation by expressing thresholds in terms of three psychologically interpretable response style parameters. These parameters capture theoretically meaningful response tendencies, including preferences for central versus extreme response options, odd versus even response options, and directional response preferences, resulting in a multidimensional profile of response style for each respondent. Although the latstat model is mathematically more flexible, our simulation studies indicate that it provides little practical advantage in parameter recovery, even for scales with larger numbers of response options and that any gains arising from its greater flexibility are generally offset by the increased complexity of estimating a much larger number of parameters. Consequently, we recommend the lasars model as the preferred analytic approach due to its greater parsimony and psychological interpretability.
