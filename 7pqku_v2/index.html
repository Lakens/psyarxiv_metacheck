How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Listening-related fatigue is common in individuals with hearing loss and may affect decisions to continue with or disengage from listening, yet its objective assessment remains challenging. We used an effort-based decision-making paradigm to investigate whether subjective fatigue ratings predict objective eye-and face-related measures and whether the combination of subjective and objective measures predicts listen-or-rest decisions during a listening task.

Twenty-seven German-speaking adults with normal hearing (NH; 35.7±13.1 years) and thirty Danish hearing-aid users (HA; 73.7±7.1 years) each completed 126 speech-in-noise trials with varying difficulty and reward, while on each trial they could decide whether to listen or to rest. Subsequently, subjective fatigue ratings were collected after each trial. Eye and facial features were recorded throughout the entire study. Analyses were conducted within groups and proceeded in two stages. First, linear mixed-effects models examined how subjective fatigue, task difficulty, reward, and response correctness predicted each eye and facial metric. Second, these metrics, along with the same predictors, were used to model trial-by-trial rest decisions.

In NH participants, greater subjective fatigue was consistently associated with reduced eye openness and increased gaze velocity (small-to-medium effects), as well as higher blink rates (small effect). In HA users, greater fatigue similarly predicted reduced eye openness and increased gaze velocity (small-to-medium effects), together with a small increase in negative facial expressions. In both groups, rest decisions were more likely when task difficulty was higher, reward was lower, and subjective fatigue was higher. In NH participants, incorrect response and greater eye openness in the preceding trial additionally increased the probability of choosing rest.

This work supports the assumption that ocular and facial indicators may serve as promising markers of preceding subjective fatigue and decisions to rest in people with hearing impairment. Recognizing these markers could play an important role in assessing and managing listening-related fatigue.

Introduction

Globally, more than 430 million people live with disabling hearing impairment, substantially affecting daily life (World Health Organization, 2021). Individuals with hearing impairment frequently report elevated levels of listening-related fatigue in everyday communication (see review Philips et al., 2023). This fatigue has been documented across clinical and self-report studies and is associated with sustained listening effort in challenging auditory environments (Edwards, 2007; Holman et al., 2019; Kramer et al., 2006; Sweetow, Henderson-Sabes, 2006; Pichora-Fuller, Singh, 2006; Wang et al., 2017). Accumulated fatigue may influence decisions to engage in or withdraw from social situations, potentially reducing social participation and quality of life, particularly among older adults (Mick et al., 2014; Heffernan et al., 2016; Shukla et al., 2020). Given these consequences, it is important to further investigate listening-related fatigue from multiple perspectives. Questionnaire-based measures, which are commonly used to assess fatigue (e.g., Vanderbilt Fatigue Scales, Hornsby et al., 2022 Hornsby et al., , 2023)), may be insensitive to rapid fluctuations and are vulnerable to limited interoceptive awareness and reporting biases (Shiffman et al., 2008; Heine et al., 2016), highlighting the need to complement self-report measures with more objective indicators. Promising candidates for such indicators are ocular and facial measures, as they are largely involuntary and sensitive to rapid changes in cognitive and affective states. The present study examined behavioral changes in listening task performance by integrating task demands, subjective fatigue ratings, and objective psychophysiological measures, specifically eye-and face-based metrics. It further investigated how listening-related fatigue develops over time and how it affects eye and facial measures, as well as how the combination of subjective and objective metrics can predict listen-orrest decisions in normal-hearing participants and hearing-aid users.

Related work Fatigue

Generally, fatigue is defined as a subjective state of exhaustion induced by sustained effort (Chaudhuri & Behan, 2004; Herlofson & Larsen, 2002; Müller & Apps, 2019), and task demands increase fatigue in both physical and cognitive domains (Kuppuswamy, 2017; Lorist, Boksem, & Ridderinkhof, 2005 Early theories viewed fatigue as energetic depletion and used it as an indicator of performance decline (Hockey, 2013). More recent findings challenge this view, showing that performance can recover under external motivation despite fatigue (Boksem et al., 2006; Hockey, 2013; Hopstaken et al., 2015). This has led to conceptualizing mental fatigue as a "stop emotion" that promotes disengagement before actual capacity is exhausted (van der Linden, 2011), reflecting an adaptive conflict between persisting and pursuing alternative actions (Hockey, 2013). This view presents fatigue as a reluctance to exert further effort and, behaviorally, as reduced vigor and disengagement from task exploitation in favor of alternative activities (Boksem, Meijman, & Lorist, 2006; van der Linden, Frese, & Sonnentag, 2003). From an evolutionary perspective, fatigue-related disengagement reflects a trade-off between task exploitation and environmental exploration (Inzlicht et al., 2014), guided by motivational cost-reward evaluations (Aston-Jones & Cohen, 2005; Cohen et al., 2007; Boksem & Tops, 2008). When goals are attainable with current effort, fatigue is minimal; when goal attainment fails, fatigue increases and triggers reassessment of goals and effort allocation. High-value goals may justify sustained or increased effort despite fatigue, whereas low-value goals may lead to goal adjustment or disengagement (Eckert et al., 2016). Although Hockey's model did not explicitly address hearing impairment, qualitative evidence suggests that listening-related fatigue in this population is consistent with this theoretical framework (Davis et al., 2021). For example, in a work meeting, a listener may shift from active participation when the perceived effort demands outweigh the expected value of the outcome. In this sense, fatigue may be reflected in disengagement or restrelated decisions during listening activity.

Effort-based decision-making

Humans are typically averse to exerting effort and often prefer lower-effort options unless the additional reward sufficiently compensates for the effort required. This is commonly referred to as effort discounting, in which rewards lose subjective value as effort demands increase (Apps et al., 2015; Chong et al., 2017; Kool & Botvinick, 2018; Matthews et al., 2023). This phenomenon is commonly studied using effort-based decision-making (EBDM) tasks, in which this cost-benefit evaluation process can be monitored by having individuals choose between easier or more demanding versions of the same task for differing rewards (Chong et al., 2016). EBDM studies demonstrate aversion to both cognitive and physical effort across manipulations of attentional switching, task switching, mathematical complexity, motor actions, and force production (Apps et Although existing conceptual frameworks of listening effort and listening-related fatigue, such as the FUEL model (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016), propose an interaction between task demands, motivation, and listening fatigue, there are currently no experimental designs in hearing research that allow this interaction to be examined systematically, nor to evaluate how hearing impairment influences it. This gap is particularly relevant given that listening-related fatigue may contribute to the increased risk of social isolation observed among individuals with hearing loss (Shukla et al., 2020). Communication and speech-processing difficulties are known to contribute to fatigue, especially later in the day (Hornsby & Kipp, 2016 , Alhanbali et al., 2017; Huizinga et al., 2026), and this accumulated fatigue may influence decisions about whether to engage in or withdraw from social situations, particularly in noisy environments. An EBDM framework provides a useful account of these decisions by proposing that individuals continuously evaluate the potential benefits of an activity against the effort required to achieve them. In the absence of substantial fatigue, individuals may be willing to invest effort even when demands are high or rewards are relatively low, if the expected value of the outcome justifies the cost. However, as fatigue accumulates, the subjective cost of effort may increase, shifting this cost-benefit balance and making effortful activities with limited rewards less worthwhile. In other words, decisions about social engagement may involve a form of effort discounting, whereby the perceived value of participation is reduced as listening effort and fatigue increase. To address this gap, the current study examines performance in listening tasks under systematically varied demandreward conditions, allowing us to investigate the interaction between task demands, motivation, and listening fatigue, with potential implications for populations with hearing impairments.

Subjective vs. objective indicators of fatigue

Despite substantial supporting evidence (Boksem & Tops, 2008; Hopstaken et al., 2015), few studies have simultaneously measured subjective fatigue and momentary willingness to exert effort (Müller & Apps, 2019; Matthews et al., 2023). Questionnaire-based measures are often insensitive to subtle or rapid changes and are susceptible to reporting biases, limited interoceptive awareness, and retrospective distortions (Shiffman et al., 2008; Heine et al., 2016; Matthews et al., 2023). Objective, rapid measures may therefore be better suited to capturing mental fatigue dynamics. Eye-tracking and facial video recordings are non-invasive, require minimal preparation, and allow relatively natural behavior compared with neuroimaging methods. They provide high temporal resolution, support continuous measurement over extended periods, reduce reliance on self-report, and can be deployed in ecologically valid and scalable experimental settings.

Face-based indicators of fatigue

In addition, facial expressions were extracted as a potential indicator of mental fatigue. Emotional responses to speech have received limited attention, and existing studies have largely relied on subjective rating scales, which are limited by difficulties in emotion identification, response biases, and post hoc rationalization (Venkitakrishnan & Wu, 2023). Although no established objective measures of emotional responses in hearing loss currently exist, evidence from psychological studies suggests that subjective and objective emotional responses may align when social regulation is minimal (Matsumoto et al., 2011). Accordingly, facial expressions, defined as externally observable indicators of emotion, offer a promising objective complement to self-report measures by capturing both conscious and unconscious emotional and psychophysiological states. Francis and Oliver (2018) reported that adverse listening situations in individuals with hearing loss are more commonly associated with emotions such as frustration and annoyance rather than more intense emotions like anger or fear. Measuring these emotional responses may therefore provide an index of listening difficulty in challenging conditions. Consistent with this view, qualitative interviews revealed that individuals with hearing loss experience a range of emotions (including fear, frustration, sadness, stress, embarrassment, doubt, and anger) as consequences of sustained listening effort, which they attributed to increased effort and missed conversational information (Marinelli, 2017). Moreover, Francis and Love (2020) highlighted substantial overlap between the physiological systems underlying listening effort and affective responses, characterizing listening effort as both an affective and cognitive phenomenon. A recent empirical study by Venkitakrishnan & Wu (2023) observed increases in confusion and frustration as listening difficulty increased. The combination of pupil response and facial expression analysis was also used by Smith et al. (2025) to predict individual severity of tinnitus and sound sensitivity disorder, who showed promising results. These findings suggest a close link between emotional experience and both listening effort and fatigue.

Listening effort vs. listening-related fatigue

Recent years have seen increased attention to listening effort in clinical hearing science (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016; Zekveld et al., 2018), often motivated by its presumed link to mental fatigue. However, despite the clear relevance of mental fatigue for individuals with hearing impairment, the mechanisms linking listening effort to fatigue remain unresolved. One key reason is that listening effort and listening-related fatigue are typically conceptualized at different temporal scales and measured using different paradigms. In the present paper, and in line with Smith (2025; dissertation) listening effort is defined as moment-to-moment fluctuations in the allocation of mental resources over short timescales (approximately 2-10 s), whereas listening-related fatigue is conceptualized as a longer-term state emerging over extended periods (typically ≥30 min). Beyond temporal differences, listening effort reflects active engagement with a specific stimulus or event, while fatigue reflects the capacity or willingness to sustain such engagement over time. Importantly, although effortful listening is connected with higher fatigue, recent experimental evidence suggests that this relationship is more complex. For example, Alfandari et al. (2023) demonstrate that listening effort is not determined by task demands alone, but emerges from an interaction between sustained load, fatigue, and motivation. These findings challenge the assumption that increasing task difficulty can be used as a direct proxy for either listening effort or fatigue. The present study therefore examines how systematically manipulated task demands influence performance, listen-or-rest decision, and fatigue, without relying on listening effort as an intermediate proxy.

Study aim

Given the multifaceted nature of fatigue, the primary aim of the present study was to examine listening-related fatigue using multiple indicators of task (dis)engagement. Because fatigue can manifest across multiple levels, including subjective experience, behavioral choices, and observable physiological responses, a multidimensional approach is necessary to capture its dynamic development during listening. In particular, ocular and facial measures may provide sensitive objective indicators of fatigue-related changes in engagement, as they can reflect subtle physiological alterations that are not always accessible through self-report. Such measures may therefore capture fatigue-related changes earlier or more continuously than subjective ratings or rest decisions, which rely on conscious awareness and explicit reporting. Within an effort-based decision-making framework, we hypothesized that fatigue accumulation would alter the perceived cost-benefit balance of engaging in effortful listening. Specifically, as fatigue increases, the effort required to complete a listening task may become more costly, leading to a devaluation of low-reward/highdemand options and an increased likelihood of choosing rest. Thus, rest decisions were considered a behavioral indicator of fatigue-related changes in effort-reward evaluation. This approach enabled us to collect multidimensional subjective and objective data on a momentary, trial-by-trial basis and test predictive models including multiple determinants of fatigue-related behavior. Specifically, subjective fatigue ratings, rest decisions, and eye-and facial measurements were used as complementary indicators of listening-related fatigue, reflecting subjective experience, behavioral choices, and physiological responses, respectively.

The study had four sub-goals: (i) to investigate the development of listening-related fatigue in two distinct samples, specifically younger normal-hearing adults and older hearing-aid users, without direct comparison, but with detailed examination of fatigue dynamics within each group; (ii) to explore the dynamics of rest decisions as a behavioral proxy of subjective fatigue, with the prediction that fatigue accumulation would increase the likelihood of disengagement from effortful listening, particularly under high-demand and low-reward conditions; (iii) to test whether subjective fatigue ratings, together with task parameters (difficulty and reward) and performance, predict eye-and facial features; and (iv) to test whether subjective fatigue ratings, task parameters, performance, and eyeand facial measurements can predict listen-or-rest decisions, thereby evaluating the feasibility of applying EBDM to listening tasks.

Method

This article presents two independent studies that followed a design inspired by Matthews et al. (2023) and Müller et al. (2021). Each study involved a different sample performing distinct listening tasks. Given the considerable differences between the samples and study designs, the two studies were not intended for direct comparison; rather, the focus lies on the individual performance patterns within each sample. Unless otherwise specified, the methodological descriptions apply to both studies.

Participants

Study 1.

Twenty-seven adults with self-reported normal hearing (13 females, 14 males) participated in the study. Participants' ages ranged from 19 to 63 years (mean = 35.7, SD = 13.1). All were native German speakers with normal or corrected-to-normal vision, no current outer-ear pathology, and no self-reported cognitive impairment. Participants received compensation of approximately 20 EUR per hour, in addition to travel compensation of 7 or 13 EUR depending on the travel distance.

Study 2.

Thirty adult hearing-aid users (14 females, 16 males) took part in the study. Participants' ages ranged from 60 to 83 years (mean = 73.7, SD = 7.1). All were native Danish speakers with sensorineural hearing loss, and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Pure-tone average thresholds (PTA; 0.5, 1, 2, 4 kHz) for the better ear ranged from 27.5 to 70.0 dB HL (mean = 45.75, SD = 12.7). As part of their standard enrollment in the database, participants have been provided with free hearing aids as well as access to audiological care as compensation for their participation in experimental studies. In addition, participants were compensated for travel costs.

Sample sizes were determined based on feasibility considerations, including the availability of eligible participants during the recruitment period and available study resources. The study was designed as an exploratory investigation of potential markers of listening-related fatigue, using within-participant repeated measurements and mixed-effects modelling approaches to examine trialby-trial changes. All participants in both studies provided informed consent. The experimental procedures were approved by the Science-Ethics Committee for the Capital Region of Denmark (reference: H-16036391). Experiments were conducted at Eriksholm Research Centre, Denmark. Before the experiment, potential participants were asked to carefully read a document about the procedure and the related risks. The entire session lasted approximately 2.5 hours for Study 1, whereas Study 2 comprised two visits, lasting approximately 1 hour for the first session and 2.5 hours for the second session.

Apparatus

The experiment was conducted in an acoustically and electromagnetically shielded booth. Participants were seated in a comfortable office chair positioned at a reference point in the center of the room, with their ears aligned to the height of the loudspeakers. A 23.8-inch monitor and a computer keyboard were placed on a desk in front of them. A Tobii Pro Spectrum eye tracker (Tobii AB; 1200 Hz sampling rate) was positioned behind the keyboard. Ambient light was maintained at a moderate level of 75 lux at the reference point. A Logitech C270 HD Webcam recorded a video of each participant's face throughout the experiment The stimuli were presented from a frontal loudspeaker (Genelec 8030C) positioned at 0° azimuth (above the screen) and at a 1 m radius from the reference point. A separate loudspeaker positioned on the right side was used for communication with participants. The loudspeakers were connected to an RME Madiface with a Ferrofish Pulse 16 I/O extension, which was linked via USB to a personal computer running Microsoft Windows 10. The computer hosted PsychoPy (version v2024.2.1) and Tobii Eye Tracker Manager, with calibration performed using a custom app written in Matlab that controls calibration via the https://developer.tobiipro.com/ . Audio playback and control were handled through PsychoPy. The screen placed beneath the front loudspeaker displayed task instructions and stimuli.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) data were also collected, but in the current article we do not report any results from fNIRS 4.

Stimulus material

Speech materials consisted of the Oldenburger Satztest (OLSA, Wagener et al., 1999) in Study 1 and Danish Sentence Test (DAST, Kressner et al., 2025) in Study 2, for which only recordings from female speaker 1 (F1) were used 5 . From each test, 126 sentences were selected based on two criteria: (1) highest naturalness ratings (see Kressner et al., 2024) and (2) proximity to the mean of the psychometric function. This selection aimed to minimize sentence-related effects on performance and choices across conditions.

Background noise

In Study 1, a 16-talker babble noise was created by concatenating the 120 OLSA sentences in random order 16 times. The resulting 16 tracks were overlaid with random onset differences between 0.5 and 1.5 seconds. The first and last 2 seconds of the combined file were trimmed, and the root-mean-square (RMS) amplitude was normalized to the average RMS of the original speech files.

In Study 2, background noise consisted of a 16-talker babble derived from F1 DAST sentences. First all available sentences were concatenated in random order 16 times. Four intermediate 4 talker babble signals were created by summing the mixes in groups of four and then temporally offset (0, 2, 4, and 6 s), combined, and trimmed to yield the final 16 talker babble. To ensure spectral similarity between background noise and speech material, the multi talker babble noise was matched in long term average spectrum (LTAS) to all F1 sentences used throughout the experiment as reference corpus. Each babble waveform was then spectrally equalized to the reference LTAS using an FIR based LTAS equalization procedure. After equalization, babble signals were normalized to a fixed RMS level corresponding to -26 dB RMS. In total, 42 independent noise segments were extracted from the long babble noise.

Speech-in-noise files

Prior to the experiment, speech-in-noise stimuli were generated by combining OLSA (Study 1) or DAST (Study 2) sentences with babble noise. In Study 1, each sentence was mixed with a random 10-second segment of 16-talker babble noise including 0.5 s fade-in and fade-out ramps. In Study 2, noise segments were trimmed to match sentence duration with 3 s pre-and post-sentence padding, gated with 1 s linear onset and offset ramps, and RMS-normalized (-26 dB RMS). Across both studies, sentence levels were adjusted relative to noise to achieve target SNRs using individual speech reception thresholds (SRTs), defined as the signal-to-noise ratio at which 50% of the speech material is correctly recognized. For each trial, a unique speech-in-noise file was created, and stimulus presentation was controlled via Excel files specifying trial order for each participant and experimental part.

Familiarization part.

In both studies, 15 speech-in-noise files were generated, representing five difficulty levels (three per level), and the reward levels were presented in fixed order.

Pre-task part. In both studies, 75 speech-in-noise files were generated (5 difficulty levels and 5 reward levels with each of the 25 combinations occurring 3 times). Study 1 used a pseudorandomization procedure in which the 25 difficulty-reward combinations were repeated three times and randomized separately within each half of the pre-task, whereas Study 2 presented stimuli in fixed order. Following Müller et al. (2021), 90% of the trials were sham trials to minimize fatigue effects.

Main task part.

In both studies, 126 speech-in-noise files were generated (42 per difficulty level; three levels). In Study 1, sentences were randomly assigned to different signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels for each participant (with six sentences reused). The difficulty-reward conditions were evenly distributed across the two halves of the main task and randomized within each half. In Study 2, three SNR-sentence mappings and four sentence orders were generated to control for stimulus-specific effects, ensuring balanced distribution of difficulty and reward across experimental halves.

In Study 2, calibration was achieved by adjusting the amplitude in PsychoPy, such that background noise alone was played at a sound intensity of 65 dB LAeq. Individual SRT estimation

Each participant's SRT was determined individually to calibrate difficulty levels. In Study 1, before SRT estimation, participants were familiarized with the OLSA material to minimize potential learning effects due to word repetition. Two test lists of 20 sentences each were presented for this familiarization. In Study 2, custom Matlab scripts were used to present DAST sentences to estimate of each participant's SRT50, closely following the procedure described by Keidser et al., 2013. Three rounds of SRT estimation were conducted on maximum 30 sentences each, whereas the first round was considered as training round and familiarization and was not considered for the SRT calculation.

SRT estimation followed a sentence-based scoring procedure, with a success criterion of at least four out of five words repeated correctly in Study 1 and at least two out of three key words repeated correctly in Study 2. To account for potential variability due to the novel noise material, the estimation included two test lists of which the average was taken to set the individual difficulty levels. In Study 1, the resulting SRT₅₀ value defined difficulty level 3 (the hardest level in the main task -Hard). Difficulty levels 2 (Medium) and 1 (Easy) corresponded to SRT₅₀ + 1.5 dB and SRT₅₀ + 4 dB, respectively, while levels 4 and 5 were defined as SRT₅₀ -1.5 dB and SRT₅₀ -4 dB, respectively. In Study 2, the resulting SRT₅₀ value defined difficulty level 4. Difficulty level 5 (very hard) corresponded to SRT50-1 dB, difficulty level 3 (Hard in the main task and further in data analyses) corresponded to SRT50 + 1 dB), level 2 (Medium) to SRT50 + 3 dB, and level 1 (Easy) to SRT50 + 8 dB.

Experimental paradigm

Both studies employed the experimental paradigm described by Matthews et al. (2023), adapted by replacing the original cognitive task with a speech-in-noise task. Following the terminology from the original article, we use the term "work-rest decisions" to describe participants' choices between "work" (listening to a sentence in noise while viewing a 'default' star image on the screen) and "rest" (listening to noise while viewing a picture of Copenhagen on the screen). Each study consisted of three consecutive parts: a training part (familiarization with listening effort levels), a pre-task (minimizing fatigue effects), and the main task. Before each part, an eye-tracking calibration was performed.

Procedure

Familiarization

A familiarization part preceded the main experiment. The aim of the familiarization was to introduce participants to the task (see Fig. 1), in which they chose between working in a speech-in-noise listening task for reward or taking a rest, and to expose them to different difficulty levels, providing subjective fatigue ratings, and time constraints. During this part, participants completed 15 practice trials, that were presented in order of increasing difficulty (easy; medium; hard; very hard; and extremely hard, with specific corresponding SNRs for each study), with reward values varying in a random ascending manner for demonstration purposes. The difficulty levels were visualized using color-coded speedometers, with increasing amounts of red indicating higher difficulty. Each difficulty level was presented three times, and participants were instructed to choose the listening option at least once for each level to ensure adequate exposure. Participants also experienced both listening and resting conditions and were informed that no reward would be received for missed trials or incorrect answers. After the first five practice trials, time constraints and fatigue rating procedures were introduced. Before proceeding to the main experiment, participants were asked whether they understood the task and were given the opportunity to ask questions.

Pre-task

The pre-task served to assess participants' baseline fatigue levels and to capture effort-based decisionmaking behavior before fatigue accumulation, providing a measure of initial effort-reward evaluation in a low-fatigue state. The pre-task included five difficulty levels crossed with five reward levels, resulting in 25 conditions, each presented three times (75 trials in total). Participants made decisions on every trial; however, only 10% of the trials (pseudo-randomly preselected) required them to perform the task if they had chosen to work. For sham trials where participants opted to work, a brief blank screen appeared before the next trial began. Points were awarded for all sham trials: 2, 4, 6, 8, or 10 points for correct responses (depending on the reward level), and 1 point for choosing to rest. No fatigue ratings were collected during the pre-task.

Main task

By this stage, participants were informed of the reward for successful task completion and the associated difficulty level, as introduced during the familiarization and pre-task parts. The final sequence of experimental stages for each trial was as follows: offer (decide to work or rest) → listening to the sentence (if work was chosen) → repeating the sentence → providing a subjective fatigue rating → receiving feedback on response correctness → receiving feedback on cumulative points. When choosing to rest, participants viewed images of Copenhagen, matched in luminance to the standard experimental display. The reward for rest was fixed at 1 point. A detailed illustration of the trial procedure is presented in Fig. 1. The main task comprised 9 conditions selected from the pre-task, specifically, the three easiest difficulty levels (1-3) paired with the three highest reward levels (6, 8, and 10 points). In Study 1, the background noise level was fixed at 65 dB SPL, while speech level varied according to task difficulty: the hard condition corresponded to the individual SRT₅₀, the medium condition to SRT₅₀ + 1.5 dB, and the easy condition to SRT₅₀ + 4 dB. In Study 2, the background noise level was also fixed at 65 dB SPL, while speech level varied according to task difficulty: the hard condition corresponded to the individual SRT₅₀ +1 dB, the medium condition to SRT₅₀ + 3 dB, and the easy condition to SRT₅₀ + 8 dB. The different offsets between the studies resulted from piloting a functional range in the two participant groups. Performance was scored using a criterion of at least four out of five words repeated correctly in Study 1 and two out of three key words repeated correctly in Study 2, to earn the points associated with that trial's reward condition.

Each condition was presented 14 times, resulting in a total of 126 trials. Trials were randomized but balanced across the two halves of the task, such that each condition appeared seven times per half. This ensured that all conditions were equally affected by potential fatigue-related changes over time. A total of 120 OLSA sentence files were used for the 126 trials, with six sentences randomly repeated. The repeated sentences were distributed randomly across the difficulty × reward conditions and trial order and treated like all other sentences. In the main task, all decisions were made at the beginning of each trial, and participants could earn rewards on every trial. At the end of each trial, participants received feedback indicating whether their response was correct or incorrect by showing how many points were earned, as well as the cumulative points they had earned up to that trial.

Data modalities

All subsequent analyses across data modalities were conducted using data from the main task only.

Eye measurements

We recorded eye tracking data at a sampling rate of 1,200 Hz. For each trial, the raw eye tracking data consisted of vectors, one for each eye, indexing pupil size (in mm) 1 , eye openness (the distance between the eyelids, in mm), gaze coordinates on the screen (standardized 0, 1 coordinates), and PsychoPy triggers indicating the trial phase (offer, listening, etc.), as well as missing values at time points where the eye could not be detected. Missing values could occur due to blinks, but also other noise in the image processing during the eye tracking.

The preprocessing of both eye openness and gaze velocity data was based on the pupil data preprocessing 1 . Samples before and after consecutive missing values were removed to avoid artifacts related to blinks (Winn et al., 2018). We applied this rule only to consecutive missing values longer than 10 samples. For these instances, we set the samples within the time range of 35 ms before (i.e., 42 samples) and 100 ms after (i.e., 120 samples) to missing values. We calculated the percentage of missing values for each eye in every participant-by-trial observation. Trials with at least 30% missing samples in both eyes were excluded from further analyses. Only the listening phase (~10 s, Fig. 1) of each main-task trial was used for subsequent analyses involving eye and facial measurements. For each participant, the "better eye" was determined as the eye with the greater number of valid trials. All subsequent eye-tracking analyses were conducted on the preprocessed data (as described above), using each participant's better eye. The data were downsampled to 300 Hz for easier handling of large datasets. All summary statistics remained exactly the same after downsampling.

Eye openness data. Eye openness data were provided directly by the Tobii Pro Spectrum as the distance between the eyelids in millimeters. These data were used both as a standalone indicator and as the basis for blink detection.

Gaze velocity.

Gaze coordinates were recorded on a normalized 0-1 scale and converted to screen pixels by multiplying by the screen resolution (1920 × 1080) and rounding to the nearest integer. For each participant and trial, eye positions were ordered by timestamp, and the Euclidean distance between successive gaze points was calculated. Gaze velocity was then defined as this distance divided by the inter-sample interval, yielding a measure in pixels per millisecond.

Blink data. Blinks for each participant-by-trial observation were calculated from the eye openness data using an algorithm described in Nyström et al. (2024) with default parameters (version 15/09/2023). For each blink, the algorithm outputs its onset and offset, eye openness at onset, offset, and amplitude peak, the timing of the amplitude peak, maximum closing and opening velocities, the times at which these velocities are reached, duration, and opening and closing amplitudes.

Facial measurements

For each participant, video recordings of their face were captured during all trials of the main task. The recordings were subsequently split into individual trial videos, and facial landmarks were tracked for each frame using https://colab.research.google.com/github/googlesamples/mediapipe/blob/main/examples/face_landmarker/python/%5BMediaPipe_Python_Tasks%5D_Face_Landmarker.ipynb , yielding 468 facial landmarks per frame. Based on the facial landmark tracking, emotion detection was performed using a series of convolutional neural networks (VGGNet) 7 , providing seven emotion categories: 'happy', 'neutral', 'surprise', 'sad', 'angry', 'disgust', and 'fear'. These emotions were further grouped into three valence categories, specifically 'positive', 'neutral', and 'negative', and the average proportions of these valence categories were calculated across participants and trials. In further analyses, we included only the trial-by-participant observations of negative emotional expression percentages.

Work-or-rest decisions

At the beginning of each trial during the offer phase, participants were presented with the potential reward for successful task completion and the associated difficulty level. They then decided whether to work or rest for that trial. These decisions were subsequently used in the analyses as potential indicators of fatigue, following the approach described in Matthews et al. (2023).

Subjective fatigue ratings

In each of the 126 main-task trials, participants provided a fatigue rating on a scale from 1 to 100 immediately after repeating the sentence. Before beginning the main task, participants gave an initial fatigue rating with no time limit. For all subsequent trials, the cursor began at the previous position, and participants had 8 seconds to record their rating. Ratings were adjusted using the arrow keys: the up and down keys changed the value in increments of 1, and the left and right keys in increments of 5.

Statistical analyses

Only data from the main task are presented in the article. Most statistical analyses reported in this article were exploratory, aiming to investigate and describe various potential indicators of listeningrelated fatigue in their full complexity. Based on previous research (Smith, 2025), fatigue typically emerges after prolonged task engagement and requires time to manifest. Eye and facial features are similarly expected to change over time. Accordingly, we analyzed correlations between eye and facial measurements and subjective fatigue with trial order, using Pearson correlations calculated individually for each participant. This approach accounts for within-subject variability, which could influence subsequent modeling (see description of modeling below). Descriptive statistics were also calculated for most measurements to provide a comprehensive view of both individual differences and average tendencies.

We applied a two-stage modeling approach. In stage 1, linear mixed-effects models examined how fixed effects, specifically task difficulty, reward, preceding subjective fatigue, and response correctness, and the random effect of participant predicted each eye and facial metric (Fig. 2A). Separate linear mixed-effects models were built for each dependent variable because our focus was on how each measure individually responded to the predictors, rather than on correlations among the dependent variables. Standardized regression coefficients (β) were reported as measures of effect size, with values of approximately 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively (Cohen, 1988). This modelling direction was chosen based on the assumption that eye-and facial measures represent observable manifestations of an underlying latent fatigue state rather than determinants of fatigue itself. Conceptually, subjective fatigue ratings were treated as the closest available approximation of this latent state, whereas eye-and facial measures were considered potential objective indicators of fatigue-related changes in task engagement. Accordingly, eye-and facial features were modelled as outcomes that may vary as a function of subjective fatigue, task demands, and task performance.

In stage 2, we extended the model used in Wienen et al. (2026, under review) to test whether ocular and facial measurements explained additional variance in trial-by-trial rest decisions beyond the behavioral factors previously shown to predict work/rest decisions. Trial-by-trial rest decisions were predicted using generalized binomial logistic regression, with predictors chosen to capture (i) current task demands and incentives (task difficulty, reward), (ii) internal state and recent performance (fatigue rating and correctness from the previous trial), (iii) sequential dependencies in decisionmaking (previous trial decision), (iv) time-on-task effects (experimental half), and (v) physiological markers of effort and fatigue (eye and facial measurements from the previous trial). Participant was included as a random effect. (Fig. 2B). Prior to model fitting, numeric predictors were examined for multicollinearity by inspecting variance inflation factors (VIFs) and pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients. To identify the best model, we performed an exhaustive model search, testing all possible combinations of candidate fixed effects. Models were compared using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC), and the model with the lowest AIC was selected as the best-fitting model.

Eye-and facial-related measures (Stage 1) were included not only as potential objective indicators of fatigue-related changes in engagement, but also because they may capture such changes more continuously than rest decisions representing the outcome of an effort-reward evaluation (Stage 2). The two-stage modelling approach therefore allowed us to examine both whether these objective measures were sensitive to fatigue-related factors and whether they contributed to the prediction of subsequent work-rest decisions.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.5.2.

Discussion

The study examined how listening-related fatigue develops over time and how it relates to eye and facial measures, as well as work-rest decisions, in normal-hearing participants and hearing-aid users.

It further tested whether subjective fatigue, task parameters, and performance could predict both objective physiological responses and behavioral disengagement, as reflected in rest decisions. Across both studies, subjective fatigue increased steadily over time and emerged as the most consistent predictor across analyses, reliably predicting eye and facial measures. Work-rest decisions were primarily driven by subjective fatigue and task value (difficulty and reward), with limited contributions from physiological or performance-based measures.

A notable finding across both studies was the very strong association between time-on-task and subjective fatigue ratings, with mean individual correlations of 0.9 among NH participants in Study 1 and 0.85 among HA users in Study 2. Such high correlations suggest that subjective fatigue ratings were heavily influenced by task progression, raising the possibility of a task-induced or time-on-task bias in self-reported fatigue. This pattern is consistent with previous work showing that fatigue ratings generally increase during prolonged or repetitive tasks, even when task demands remain constant (Hopstaken et al., 2015; Matthews et al., 2023). Importantly, this dependence does not necessarily invalidate subjective fatigue measures. Rather, it highlights that self-reports may partly reflect participants' expectations about how fatigue should evolve over time, in addition to their momentary internal state (Hockey, 2013). Asking participants to rate their fatigue frequently may further strengthen this effect by repeatedly directing attention to how tired they feel and how long the task has lasted, which can anchor ratings to task progression rather than momentary experience. In this sense, the observed correlations likely reflect both actual accumulation of fatigue and a systematic reporting bias induced by the experimental structure.

One potential methodological solution is to reduce the frequency of fatigue ratings, for example by collecting them at predefined intervals or only at key points during the experiment. Intermediate fatigue levels could then be estimated using linear or nonlinear interpolation, minimizing repeated introspection while preserving sensitivity to gradual changes. Alternatively, combining sparse subjective ratings with continuous objective markers (e.g., eye-based measures) may help dissociate true fatigue-related changes from time-on-task effects. Such approaches may improve the interpretability of subjective fatigue measures and reduce confounding with task duration in future studies.

Consistent with previously reported behavioral analyses (Schulte et al., 2026; Wienen et al., 2026, under review), rest decisions were strongly influenced by the joint effects of task difficulty and reward, with substantially more rest occurring in the hardest condition and at the lowest reward level, particularly among younger NH participants. This pattern suggests that participants were often able to overcome subjective fatigue and reengage with the task when sufficiently motivated. Such findings are consistent with the view that mental fatigue functions primarily as a motivational control mechanism that regulates the allocation of cognitive resources rather than reflecting a fixed depletion of capacity (Hopstaken et al., 2016). In contrast, HA users appeared to base their disengagement decisions primarily on task difficulty, showing little sensitivity to reward level. In this regard, we got different results than McLaughlin et al., 2020, who reported that older adults discounted the value of effortful listening more strongly than younger adults, preferring lower monetary rewards to avoid more challenging signal-to-noise ratios. Within the older group, poorer hearing and reduced working memory capacity were associated with a greater tendency to select easier trials, whereas no such relationships were observed in younger adults (McLaughlin et al., 2020).

This divergence between the two groups in our studies suggests differences in motivational processes underlying rest decisions. One possible explanation is that compensation structures differed across the study groups: HA participants received non-monetary compensation (specifically free hearing aids for regular participation in the experiments at the Eriksholm Research Center), whereas NH participants were compensated monetarily on an hourly basis. Differences in incentive structures may therefore have attenuated the motivational impact of rewards for HA users. Additionally, generational differences in responsiveness to gamified elements of the experimental design may play a role. Younger NH participants may have been more sensitive to the point-based reward system, whereas older HA participants may have been less responsive to such game-like features. At the same time, subjective fatigue significantly predicted rest decisions in both samples, highlighting its role in regulating engagement decisions.

Interestingly, in Study 1 with NH individuals we observed a proportion of rest decisions that was very close to that reported for the cognitive task The first-stage modelling analyses revealed that subjective fatigue was the most consistent and robust predictor of eye and facial measures across both samples, supporting the assumption that mental fatigue is associated with changes in different measurements of oculomotor behavior (see review Bafna & Hansen, 2021). Our main result is that eye and facial parameters, previously mainly investigated in the context of mental fatigue during visual tasks, generalize to listening tasks, showing similar trends: across both NH participants and HA users, higher subjective fatigue was strongly associated with reduced eye openness and increased gaze velocity, with additional group-specific effects observed for increased blink rate (among NH individuals) and increased negative facial expressions (among hearing-aid users).

Focusing on gaze behavior, increased mean gaze velocity was reliably predicted by increasing subjective fatigue across the experiment in both samples. One possible explanation is that fatigue reduces the stability of oculomotor control, leading to less efficient and more variable eye movement patterns. Fatigue has been shown to impair top-down attentional control and increase neural noise, which may manifest as less precise and more erratic gaze behavior (Di Stasi et al., 2013; Hopstaken et al., 2015). Increased gaze velocity may therefore reflect reduced inhibitory control over eye movements under fatigued conditions. Our results are consistent with multiple studies across other domains, such as free visual exploration tasks (Cazzoli et Reduced eye openness in both samples as a function of fatigue is consistent with findings from PERCLOS (percentage of eye closure)-based studies, although the measures are not identical. PERCLOS is typically computed over longer time windows and reflects accumulated drowsiness, whereas our measure captures more fine-grained temporal dynamics. Nevertheless, both measures index reduced alertness and increased propensity for eye closure, and in the light of this, fatigue is expectedly associated with decreased activity in arousal-regulating systems, leading to increased eyelid closure and reduced eye openness (Dinges & Grace, 1998; Johns, 2003). While PERCLOS was originally developed for detecting drowsiness in driving contexts (Wierwille et al., 1994), our results extend this relationship to a listening task, suggesting that similar physiological mechanisms underlie listening-related fatigue.

Our results showing increased blink rate as a function of fatigue, as well as reduced blink rate under higher task difficulty in NH individuals, are consistent with prior research on both visual and nonvisual tasks (Stern et al., 1984; Eckstein et al., 2017). A reduction in blink frequency during task engagement was attributed to increased attentional demands, followed by a compensatory increase in blinking after task completion (Stern et al., 1984). Eckstein et al. (2017) further suggested that reduced blinking during task performance leads to ocular dryness, which subsequently triggers increased blinking over time. From this perspective, the increase in blink rate observed with fatigue may reflect a combination of reduced sustained attention, physiological compensation for ocular strain, and changes in neuromodulatory systems. Additionally, fatigue-related disengagement from task demands has been associated with increased spontaneous blinking (Hopstaken et al., 2015), providing further support for this interpretation. Consistent with a motivational rather than a purely demand-driven account, Minagi et al. (2025) reported that blink rate during sentence repetition increased at -5 dB SNR relative to 0 dB but did not increase further at -10 dB, where self-reported motivation was lowest, and found no significant association between their objective and subjective indices, suggesting that blink rate tracks the joint influence of task demand and willingness to engage rather than either measure serving as a proxy for the other.

In contrast to subjective fatigue, task difficulty showed more selective and measure-specific effects. While increased difficulty decreased both gaze velocity and blink rate in NH participants, as well as eye openness and gaze velocity in HA users, these effects were less consistent than those of fatigue. One possible explanation is that task difficulty primarily affects momentary allocation of cognitive resources (i.e., listening effort), whereas fatigue reflects a more cumulative state that influences overall system functioning. According to resource theories of attention (Kahneman, 1973) and frameworks such as FUEL (Pichora-Fuller et al., 2016), increased task demands may initially lead to greater focused engagement and suppression of non-essential processes (e.g., reduced blinking), but do not necessarily translate into fatigue unless sustained over time. This distinction may explain why task difficulty effects are more transient and less consistent across physiological measures.

Reward and response correctness showed even weaker and less consistent effects across both samples. Specifically, we observed a significant effect of the highest reward on increased eye openness in NH individuals, while incorrect responses were associated with reduced blink rate (potentially reflecting increased alertness following negative feedback) and decreased eye openness in HA users (possibly indicating demotivational effects). However, these relatively inconsistent and weaker effects appear to contrast with prior evidence suggesting that motivational factors, including reward, can modulate listening effort and associated physiological responses (e.g., Alfandari et al., 2023). One possible explanation is that reward effects primarily operate at the phase of decisionmaking about whether to continue or disengage (i.e. rest decisions at the offer phase), rather than being directly reflected in continuous physiological measures during task performance (i.e. the analysed listening phase). Alternatively, the reward manipulation in the present study may not have been sufficiently strong to elicit consistent physiological modulation, as discussed above.

An interesting divergence between the two samples emerged in the effects of rest trials. Among NH participants, rest trials were associated with increased gaze velocity and blink rate, whereas no such effects were observed among HA users. This may indicate that NH participants show clearer physiological disengagement or recovery during rest periods, while HA users maintain a more stable or constrained physiological state. One possible interpretation is that older HA users experience a more sustained fatigue state, leading to reduced physiological differentiation between task and rest conditions. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that individuals with hearing impairment report higher baseline levels of fatigue and may show altered recovery dynamics (Hornsby & Kipp, 2016; Huizinga et al., 2026). Another complementary explanation relates to age-related differences in cognitive flexibility and adaptive control, whereby older adults may exhibit reduced ability to rapidly shift between states of engagement and disengagement, potentially leading to more stable but less dynamically modulated physiological responses across task contexts (Mayr, 2001; Braver & West, 2008).

Interestingly, facial measurements, specifically, a higher percentage of negative emotional expressions, were significantly associated with higher subjective fatigue only in HA users. Although similar approaches have shown promise in prior work (e.g., Kawamura et al., 2020), where facial expressions during speech were used to estimate fatigue with relatively high accuracy, effects in the present study were more limited. This may be due to the constrained experimental setup, which included fNIRS recording and restricted movement, potentially reducing the natural variability of facial expressions. Additionally, older adults may express fatigue through facial behavior differently, than younger adults. Future studies conducted in more ecologically valid settings are needed to further evaluate the utility of facial expressions as indicators of fatigue.

Taken together, the findings of the first-stage modelling support a model in which subjective fatigue represents a central correlate of physiological and behavioral variation during listening tasks, beyond the effects of task difficulty and performance-related factors. Although standardized effect sizes were generally small to moderate, fatigue-related predictors showed highly consistent associations across multiple objective measures and samples. Given the multifactorial nature of fatigue and the inherent variability of physiological measures, these effect sizes represent meaningful changes in objective indicators associated with changes in subjective fatigue. Importantly, the results also highlight that physiological manifestations of fatigue are not uniform across populations, underscoring the need to consider individual differences, such as age and hearing status, when interpreting objective fatiguerelated measures.

Comparing the best-fitting models at Stage 2 of the modelling across both NH and HA samples reveals both converging and diverging patterns in the predictors of trial-by-trial rest decisions. Across both samples, subjective fatigue in the previous trial appeared as a robust predictor, with higher fatigue reliably increasing the likelihood of choosing to rest. This convergence supports theoretical accounts that conceptualize fatigue as a signal of rising effort costs, biasing decisions toward disengagement or rest (Hockey, 2011 (Hockey, , 2013;; Kurzban et al., 2013; Shenhav et al., 2017). In this framework, fatigue is not merely a consequence of task demands but it serves an adaptive function signaling the need to regulate effort expenditure and supporting effective control of action and motivation (Hockey, 2013). Similarly, incorrect responses in the previous trial were associated with an increased probability of resting in both studies. This finding aligns with evidence that performance errors reduce motivation and increase perceived effort costs, thereby influencing subsequent task engagement (Botvinick & Braver, 2001 , 2015; van der Linden et al., 2003). Together, these shared effects suggest that both internal state (fatigue) and recent performance outcomes shape effort-based decisions during sustained listening.

Despite these similarities, notable differences between the two studies were observed. Among younger normal-hearing participants, both intermediate and high task difficulty levels and multiple reward levels significantly influenced rest decisions. Harder tasks increased the likelihood of resting, whereas higher rewards promoted continued task engagement, consistent with cost-benefit models of effort allocation (Westbrook & Braver, 2015; Shenhav et al., 2013). In contrast, for older hearingaid users, only the highest difficulty and largest reward levels significantly affected decisions, suggesting reduced sensitivity to moderate changes in task demands and incentives. This pattern may reflect a more conservative decision threshold in older adults or differences in motivation, possibly related to the incentive structure in this sample as discussed above (free hearing aids rather than hourly compensation), particularly under conditions of increased listening effort (Peelle, 2018). Another difference concerned the contribution of time-on-task. Descriptively, rest decisions increased slightly across the experiment in both samples, with a larger increase among HA users (NH: 7.1% vs. 7.6%; HA: 2.8% vs. 4.3% in the first and second halves, respectively). However, experimental half did not remain a positive predictor after accounting for fatigue and other trial-level factors. In fact, among NH participants, the second half was negatively associated with rest decisions in the best-fitting model, suggesting that the observed increase in rest choices over time was primarily explained by accumulating fatigue rather than elapsed time itself. Among HA users, experimental half did not remain in the best-fitting model, potentially reflecting the lower overall number of rest decisions in this sample.

Finally, objective ocular and facial measures contributed to the likeliness of rest decisions differently across studies. For NH individuals, previous eye openness predicted subsequent rest decisions, suggesting that ocular behavior captured aspects of the internal state relevant for effort regulation (for blink rate it was shown e.g. in Caffier et al., 2003 and Coupal et al., 2025; for pupil dynamics in Hopstaken et al., 2015), although the direction of this relationship did not simply mirror the fatiguerelated reduction in eye openness observed in Stage 1. In HA users, however, none of the eye or facial measures contributed significantly to rest decisions, despite several of these measures being significantly associated with subjective fatigue in Stage 1. This suggests that physiological indicators of fatigue do not necessarily translate directly into behavioural disengagement, and that the factors guiding rest decisions may differ from those underlying the physiological manifestations of fatigue. Across both studies, subjective fatigue remained a consistent predictor of subsequent rest decisions, together with task-related factors such as listening difficulty, reward, and, in NH participants, previous-trial correctness. Taken together, these findings suggest that ocular and facial measures may provide complementary information about the internal state associated with listening-related fatigue, but their contribution to behavioural decisions to disengage from listening may be limited and context-dependent.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, subjective fatigue ratings may have been influenced by task-related factors, raising the possibility of task-induced bias in selfreports. Second, the rest periods implemented in the experiment were relatively short and constrained (i.e. participants remained seated in front of the monitor without the possibility to move or engage in alternative activities), which limits their ecological validity and may not reflect real-world recovery processes. Third, the motivational manipulation based on game-like reward points may not fully capture real-life motivational dynamics, potentially reducing the generalizability of findings related to reward effects. Fourth, the use of the fNIRS setup imposed restrictions on participants' movement and posture, which may have affected the natural expression of facial behavior and limited the ecological validity of the listening context. Less constrained wearable approaches, such as the glasses-type device used by Minagi et al. (2025) to record eye blinks and head tilt during speech-innoise listening, offer one route to more naturalistic measurement, and head-pose information is in principle already contained in our facial-landmark recordings but was not analysed here. Finally, the two studies differed in several aspects, including participant characteristics (age and hearing status), task language, incentive structures, and overall design, which constrains direct comparisons between samples and limits the ability to attribute observed differences to specific factors. Future research should address these limitations by employing more ecologically valid designs, more naturalistic rest conditions, and more comparable experimental frameworks across populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study provides converging evidence that subjective listening-related fatigue plays a central role in shaping both physiological responses and behavioral disengagement during listening tasks. Across two samples, normal-hearing individuals and hearing-aid users, fatigue increased over time and emerged as the most consistent predictor of eye and facial measures, supporting the view that it reflects a latent internal state that manifests across multiple response systems. In contrast, task parameters such as difficulty and reward showed more selective effects on physiological measures but, together with subjective fatigue, strongly influenced listen-or-rest decisions, consistent with effort-based decision-making frameworks. Importantly, physiological measures showed limited predictive value for behavioral disengagement, suggesting that they may reflect fatigue-related processes without directly driving listen-or-rest decisions. Differences between the two samples further indicate that the expression of fatigue in physiological and behavioral domains may vary as a function of age and hearing status. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of considering listening-related fatigue as a key mechanism linking listening demands to both physiological responses and real-world behavior, while also underscoring the need for multimodal approaches to capture its complex and dynamic nature.
