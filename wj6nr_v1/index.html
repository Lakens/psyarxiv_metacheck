Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Background: Insomnia is strongly associated with symptoms of depression and anxiety during emerging adulthood, yet the mechanisms underlying these relationships remain poorly understood. Numerous constructs have been proposed as potential explanatory pathways, but few have been systematically screened and independently replicated. This study applied a structured associational variable screening approach to identify and replicate candidate constructs associated with relationships connecting insomnia and depression and anxiety in emerging adults.

Methods: Data were drawn from two independent samples of emerging adults. Study 1 (N = 333) provided exploratory analyses and Study 2 (N = 1,555) provided independent confirmation. Insomnia symptoms were assessed using the Sleep Condition Indicator (SCI) and depression and anxiety symptoms using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS). Candidate associative variables included psychological inflexibility, emotion regulation difficulties, behavioral and experiential avoidance, maladaptive sleep-related behaviors and attitudes. Following Weems et al. (2025), candidates were evaluated using sequential analyses of zero-order associations, multivariable associations and incremental explanatory power (ΔR²), attenuation of the sleepsymptom association, equivalence under reversal of the proposed associative pathway, and moderation.

In Study 1, emotion regulation difficulties, psychological inflexibility, and behavioral avoidance met screening criteria as potential path variables between insomnia and depression, while emotion regulation difficulties and psychological inflexibility met criteria as potential path variables between insomnia and anxiety. These candidate associations were replicated in Study 2. For depression, emotion regulation difficulties (ΔR² = .277), psychological inflexibility (ΔR² = .286), and behavioral avoidance (ΔR² = .122) demonstrated substantial incremental explanatory power. For anxiety, emotion regulation difficulties (ΔR² = .175) and psychological inflexibility (ΔR² = .182) met all screening criteria, with internalizing demonstrating the largest incremental contribution. Moderation models were estimated but consistently explained negligible additional variance (ΔR² ≤ .006).

Conclusions: Across exploratory and confirmatory samples, psychological inflexibility and emotion regulation difficulties emerged as consistent candidate associative variables linking insomnia with both depressive and anxiety symptoms. Behavioral avoidance additionally emerged for depression. Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal or mechanistic inference, these findings narrow a broad set of potential explanatory constructs and identify promising candidates for longitudinal and experimental research examining relationships between sleep disturbance and depression and anxiety during emerging adulthood.

INTRODUCTION

Emerging adulthood is a period of heightened vulnerability to sleep disturbance, depression, and anxiety (Arnett, 2000; Suvisaari et al., 2009; Taber-Thomas & Perez-Edgar, 2015). The transition from adolescence into adulthood is accompanied by substantial changes in social roles, educational and occupational demands, relationships, living environments, and independence over health behaviors, while neurobiological and circadian development continues to shape patterns of sleep and emotional functioning. Against this backdrop, symptoms of depression and anxiety are highly prevalent among emerging adults (Lipson et al., 2022; Roy et al., 2025; The Healthy Minds Network, 2024) and represent major contributors to functional impairment, reduced quality of life, and suicide risk (Crowley et al., 2007; Dahl & Lewin, 2002; Gaultney, 2010; Hershner & Chervin, 2014; Lund et al., 2010; Taylor et al., 2013). Sleep difficulties are similarly common during this developmental period, with young adults frequently experiencing insomnia symptoms, insufficient sleep, irregular sleep-wake schedules, and poor sleep quality. Identifying factors that are associated with co-occurrence of sleep disturbance and internalizing symptoms and potentially to the progression from sleep disturbance to internalizing disorders during emerging adulthood is an important public health and clinical priority. A substantial literature demonstrates robust associations among disturbed sleep and both depression and anxiety (Baglioni et al., 2011; Blaxton et al., 2017; Breslau et al., 1996; Buysse et al., 2008; Pigeon et al., 2008). Insomnia is among the strongest prospective predictors of depression, predicting first onset, worsening symptoms, and recurrence even after accounting for baseline depressive symptoms (Baglioni et al., 2011; Cho et al., 2008; Gallo et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016). Sleep disturbance may precede depressive episodes by months or years, and persistent sleep problems are associated with more severe and chronic courses of depression. Sleep disturbance is also strongly associated with anxiety symptoms and disorders (Richards et al., 2020; Staner, 2003), and longitudinal evidence suggests bidirectional relationships in which poor sleep predicts subsequent anxiety while anxiety-related arousal and worry further interfere with sleep (Kalmbach et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2024). During emerging adulthood, these relationships may be particularly consequential because shifting developmental, academic, occupational, and interpersonal demands can simultaneously disrupt sleep and increase vulnerability to internalizing symptoms. Importantly, the relationship between poor sleep and internalizing psychopathology appears to extend beyond their concurrent symptom overlap. Experimental and interventional research suggests that manipulating sleep can produce changes in emotional functioning. Experimental sleep loss results in reduced positive affect, increased anxiety, and increased depressive symptoms (Palmer et al., 2024). Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), the first-line treatment for insomnia, (Qaseem et al., 2016) reliably improves sleep and has also been associated with reductions in depressive and anxiety symptoms (Bei et al., 2018; Blom et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2017; Furukawa et al., 2024; Gebara et al., 2018; Irwin et al., 2022; Suh et al., 2013; Ubara et al., 2022). Treatment of insomnia may reduce risk for subsequent depression, including among individuals without clinically significant depression at treatment initiation (Cheng et al., 2019). This literature suggests that sleep may represent a modifiable contributor to internalizing psychopathology rather than simply a correlate or consequence of psychological distress. However, substantially less is known about the psychological processes that may account for these relationships. Several candidate processes may plausibly contribute to associations among sleep disturbance, depression, and anxiety. Emotion regulation difficulties represent one particularly compelling candidate. Adequate sleep supports cognitive and affective regulatory functioning, whereas sleep disruption is associated with increased emotional reactivity, reduced regulatory capacity, and greater difficulty recovering from negative emotional experiences (Goldstein & Walker, 2014; Palmer & Alfano, 2017). Difficulties regulating emotions, in turn, are strongly implicated in both depression and anxiety (Cisler & Olatunji, 2012; Visted et al., 2018). Thus, individuals experiencing poor sleep may become increasingly vulnerable to internalizing symptoms in part because they have greater difficulty modulating emotional responses to everyday stressors. Psychological inflexibility and experiential avoidance may represent additional transdiagnostic processes connecting sleep with internalizing symptoms (Pantesco & Schanke, 2025; Zakiei et al., 2024). Psychological flexibility refers broadly to the capacity to remain in contact with difficult internal experiences while adapting behavior in accordance with situational demands and personally meaningful goals (Doorley et al., 2020; Kashdan, 2010). In contrast, psychological inflexibility and experiential avoidance involve rigid efforts to control, suppress, or escape unwanted thoughts, emotions, and physiological sensations (Chawla & Ostafin, 2007). Poor sleep may reduce individuals' capacity to flexibly respond to distress, while attempts to avoid or control uncomfortable internal experiences may further contribute to both sleep-related difficulties and internalizing symptoms (Dorrian et al., 2019). These processes are particularly relevant to depression and anxiety because avoidance can provide short-term relief while maintaining longer-term patterns of withdrawal, worry, and reduced engagement with reinforcing activities. Sleep-specific cognitive and behavioral responses may also contribute to these relationships (Harvey, 2002). Individuals experiencing sleep problems often develop maladaptive behaviors intended to compensate for or control poor sleep, such as altering activities based on anticipated fatigue, spending excessive time in bed, or rigidly attempting to manage sleep (Espie, 2002). Similarly, dysfunctional beliefs and attitudes about sleep may amplify the perceived consequences of a poor night's sleep and increase cognitive and physiological arousal (Carney et al., 2007). Although intended to manage sleep disturbance, such responses can perpetuate sleep difficulties and may contribute independently to psychological distress. More broadly, dispositional characteristics associated with negative affectivity, internalizing tendencies, and social or emotional detachment may create shared vulnerability to both sleep disturbance and symptoms of depression or anxiety. Despite a growing literature identifying potential explanatory processes, it has been difficult to determine which constructs are most relevant to the sleep-mental health relationship. Candidate variables have often been examined individually, making it challenging to determine whether their associations persist when considered alongside competing constructs. Moreover, statistical significance alone provides limited information about whether a candidate variable meaningfully contributes to an association between two constructs. Variables may correlate significantly with insomnia and internalizing symptoms without substantially accounting for their shared variance, and constructs identified as explanatory variables may instead operate as moderators, consequences, or indicators of broader shared vulnerability. These limitations are particularly important given substantial conceptual and empirical overlap among constructs such as emotion dysregulation, experiential avoidance, psychological inflexibility, and internalizing personality traits.

A recently proposed approach for evaluating associative variables provides a systematic method for identifying candidate processes when temporal or experimental data are not available to establish mediation (Weems, 2025). Rather than relying on a single indirect-effect test, this approach evaluates candidate constructs across multiple criteria, including their zero-order relationships with predictor and outcome variables, unique associations when competing variables are considered simultaneously, incremental explanatory variance, attenuation of the original predictor-outcome association, bidirectional equivalence, and potential moderation. Such an approach is particularly useful as an initial screening strategy for identifying constructs that warrant prioritization in subsequent longitudinal and experimental research. Replication in an independent sample can further distinguish potentially robust associations from samplespecific findings. The current study applied this framework to systematically evaluate psychological constructs that may help account for associations between insomnia symptoms and symptoms of depression and anxiety during emerging adulthood. Using two independent samples, we first conducted exploratory analyses to identify candidate associative variables and then tested those variables in a larger independent confirmatory sample. Candidate constructs included psychological flexibility, emotion regulation difficulties, experiential avoidance, maladaptive sleep-related behaviors and practices, and psychopathology dimensions (internalizing, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and anankastia) (Wright et al., 2025). Given the substantial overlap but also meaningful differences between depression and anxiety, associative variables were evaluated separately for each outcome. We expected that more severe insomnia symptoms would be associated with greater symptoms of both depression and anxiety and that several transdiagnostic constructs (particularly emotion regulation difficulties, psychological inflexibility, and internalizing tendencies) would emerge as candidate associative variables. By systematically comparing multiple candidate processes and evaluating their replication across independent samples, this study sought to identify the transdiagnostic psychological constructs most strongly associated with the sleep-depression/anxiety relationship and thereby prioritize targets for future longitudinal, experimental, and intervention research.

METHODS

The overall study was in two parts. In the first (Study 1), exploratory analyses were conducted to identify candidate constructs to be tested in the confirmatory study (Study 2). Procedures Study 1 was conducted through recruitment of college students directly from two colleges or universities in the northeastern United States. Students were eligible to participate if they were enrolled in the introduction to psychology research pools at the two schools. Participants indicated interest in the project through Sona and completed the survey with Qualtrics. They were compensated with course credit. A total of N = 410 participants started the survey. Of those, n = 44 did not complete key demographic questions (e.g., age, gender) and 60 failed at least two of the three attention check questions (e.g., "I sleep on a pillow made of spaghetti"). Given the overlap between those two categories, a total of n = 77 participants were excluded, leaving a sample of N = 333 who completed sufficient measures to be included in the analysis. See Table 1 for demographics and results of key variables. Study 2 was conducted through Prolific in November of 2025, with Qualtrics as the survey platform. Potential participants were required to be enrolled as undergraduate students in a twoor four-year program and had to be based in the United States. Participants were compensated $3.75 USD through Prolific upon completion of the nine-minute survey. A total of 2,156 participants started the Qualtrics survey. Of those, n = 188 completed the survey in under 240 seconds (an impossible speed), n = 95 did not provide their age or other critical demographic information, n = 88 failed one or more of the Prolific and Qualtrics bot/AI completer checks, and n = 455 indicated that they were not currently undergraduate students. Given the overlap between those categories, a total of 601 participants were excluded, leaving a sample of N = 1,555 who completed sufficient measures to be included in the analysis. See Table 2 for demographics and results of key variables. Note that Study 2 was designed such that not every participant received every measure. To reduce participant burden, we used a planned missingness design (Graham et al., 2006). (Graham et al., 2006) All participants completed a core battery: demographic questions, the depression and anxiety subscales from the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (Henry & Crawford, 2005), the Sleep Condition Indicator (SCI) (Espie et al., 2014), and the first four items of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (Buysse et al., 1989). We used these PSQI items only to calculate total sleep time (TST) and sleep efficiency (SE) and did not compute a PSQI global score. Each remaining measure was assigned an estimated completion time based on its number of items. Measures were then randomly selected and presented in random order until the next measure would push total survey time past nine minutes. Because assignment depended only on this random procedure, missing data on these measures are missing completely at random (MCAR) by design. Participants completed every item of each measure they received, so missingness was at the measure level only and no imputation was needed. Analyses of each measure included all participants who received it (see Table 2 for sample sizes).

Measures

Depression and anxiety: Depression and anxiety were measured with the respective subscales from the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21). The stress subscale was not used in this analysis, and was thus not presented to participants. A total of 14 items were presented (seven each for depression and anxiety), and responses given on a four-point Likert-style scale ranging from zero ("Did not apply to me at all") to 3 ("Applied to me very much, or most of the time"). Depression items include statements like "I felt that I had nothing to look forward to", while anxiety items included statements like "I felt scared without any good reason." In keeping with the original 42-item DASS, items within each subscale were summed and multiplied by two, with lower scores indicating lower levels of depression or anxiety. Observed Cronbach alphas for Study 1 were acceptable, with .78 for anxiety and .90 for depression. For Study 2, alphas were also acceptable, with .84 for anxiety and .92 for depression. These values are about the same as in the original validating work (alpha = .88 for depression and .90 for anxiety) (Henry & Crawford, 2005). Insomnia symptoms: Insomnia symptoms were measured by the Sleep Condition Indicator, a well-validated measure of insomnia. The measure consists of eight Likert-style items such as "Thinking about a typical night in the past month, how long does it take you to fall asleep?", with five options. The option reflecting the best sleep is scored a 4, while the option reflecting the worst sleep is scored a zero. Higher scores reflect better sleep, unlike other commonly-used insomnia scales. Observed Cronbach alpha for Study 1 was acceptable, alpha = .81, with a similar value for Study 2, alpha = .86. These values are consistent with the alphas from the original validating work (alpha = .86) (Espie et al., 2014). Acceptance of Sleep Difficulties: Acceptance of sleep difficulties was measured by the Sleep Problem Acceptance Questionnaire (SPAQ) (Bothelius et al., 2015). The measure consists of eight Likert-style items such as "I lead a full life even though I have sleeping problems," with responses scored from zero to six. Note that several items are reverse scored, and the final scale is such that higher scores reflect more acceptance. Although the scores can be summed to form a single acceptance score, both the original validating article and our analyses suggest a two-factor model. One factor (AE) reflects active engagement (e.g., "Although things have changed, I am living a normal life despite my sleeping problems"), and the second reflects willingness to experience sleep problems (WILL; e.g., "It's important to keep on fighting these sleep problems", reversed). Alphas in the original validating work were acceptable for the subscales (AE alpha = .89, with .73 for willingness), which were consistent with the results from our work (Study 1 AE alpha = .87, WILL alpha = .76; Study 2 AE alpha = .91, WILL alpha = .80). Emotion Regulation: The ability to regulate emotion was measured by the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS-18) (Victor & Klonsky, 2016), a well-validated 18-item version of the original scale (Gratz & Roemer, 2004), Items such as "I am confused about how I feel" are assessed on five-point Likert-style scale scored from one through five, with higher scores reflecting greater difficulty managing and processing emotion. Internal consistency for the 18-item scale is acceptable, with alpha = .91 in the original validating article (Victor & Klonsky, 2016), and a comparable set of alphas in our sample (Study 1 alpha = .88; Study 2 alpha = .90). Psychological Inflexibility and Avoidance: Avoidance was assessed through the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire, version 2 (AAQ-2), a seven-item, Likert-style scale including items such as "Emotions cause problems in my life," scored from one (never true) to seven (always true), such that higher scores reflect greater inflexibility. The original validating work found acceptable internal reliability of alpha = .84 (Bond et al., 2011), which was consistent with our findings (Study 1 alpha = .86 and Study 2 alpha = .92). Experiential Avoidance: Experiential avoidance is related to the psychological inflexibility measured by the AAQ2, but measures behaviors associated with avoiding distress or discomfort, as opposed to beliefs about the acceptability of distress and discomfort. Experiential avoidance was measured with the Brief Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire (BEAQ), a 15-item, Likertstyle scale which includes items such as "I work hard to keep out upsetting feelings." Each item is scored on a scale from one to six, and the total scale (after a single item reversal) is scored such that higher scores reflect greater behavioral avoidance. Internal consistency from the original study was acceptable (alpha = .86), as was observed with our data (Study 1 alpha = .83; Study 2 alpha = .85). Insomnia Safety Behaviors: The Sleep-Related Behaviors Questionnaire (SRBQ) measures behavioral strategies to avoid undesired sleep outcomes (e.g., insomnia) (Ree & Harvey, 2004). It is a 32-item Likert-style measure scored from zero to five, with higher scores reflecting more sleep-related safety behaviors. Items such as "During the day, I block thoughts about sleep out of my mind." . Internal consistency in the original validating article was acceptable (alpha = .92), which was similar to our findings (Study 1 alpha = .88; Study 2 alpha = .91).

RESULTS Study 1

Study 1 was exploratory. The goal of these analyses is thus to identify candidate constructs for confirmatory analyses using the larger and more diverse sample of Studies 2. Given that data for Study 1 was collected cross-sectionally, causal directionality cannot be determined (Maxwell & Cole, 2007) and should not be presumed beyond what existing evidence has suggested. In this case, we sought to expand on the well-known bidirectional relationship between sleep and depression and anxiety (e.g., Alvaro et al., 2013; Cousins et al., 2011; Kahn et al., 2013; Leahy & Gradisar, 2012; Peltz et al., 2017; Philbrook & Macdonald-Gagnon, 2021; Tavernier & Willoughby, 2015; Vandekerckhove & Wang, 2017) by exploring intermediate variables.

To do this, we adopted the stepwise associational variable analysis approach described by Weems (Weems, 2025). This approach screens candidate associative variables through a series of sequential steps. In step one, we examined the zero-order correlations among sleep condition, each candidate associative variable, and the outcomes of depression and anxiety. In step two, we conducted regression analyses to test three criteria. First, including the associative variable had to explain a meaningful amount of additional variance in the outcome (ΔR² of .10 or greater). Second, the association between sleep condition and the outcome (c) had to be meaningfully attenuated once the associative variable was included (c′). Third, the association of the associative variable with the outcome (b) had to be comparable in strength to the association obtained when the model was reversed, with the associative variable regressed on the outcome (b′). This last comparison shows that the results are not solely reliant on a directional interpretation. In step three, we tested whether the associative variable instead moderated the relationship between sleep condition and the outcome. Steps one and two are exclusionary: if a variable triplet did not meet the criteria at either step, it was not carried forward to Study 2. Step three is expository and is meant to prompt consideration of moderation as an alternative explanation, rather than to exclude candidate variables. Note that Weems (Weems, 2025) describes this as a four-step process; for simplicity and to reduce the number of tables, we report two of these steps together as step two. Thus, while our description lists three steps, the procedure follows the Weems approach.

Study 1: Zero-Order Associations (Step 1)

Correlations are reported in Table 3. Sleep condition was strongly and significantly correlated with both depression and anxiety. For each candidate construct, we then checked whether it was significantly correlated with both sleep condition and the outcome. Following Weems (Weems, 2025), the Condition column in Table 3 summarizes the pattern. "All Associated" means that the candidate was significantly correlated with both. "One Set" means that it was significantly correlated with only one of the two. Only candidates showing the "All Associated" pattern passed this step. Willingness to experience sleep problems (a subscale of the Sleep Problem Acceptance Questionnaire) showed the One Set pattern and did not pass. It appears in the later tables for completeness but was not considered further.

Study 1: Direct and Indirect Associations (Step 2)

Regression results for all candidates, including those that did not pass the first step, are reported in Table 4 for completeness; results for candidates that did not pass the first step are not interpreted. We excluded candidates that added less than .10 to the explained variance in the outcome, which was our conservative a priori threshold. We also excluded candidates whose inclusion did not meaningfully reduce the association between insomnia symptoms and the outcome, because this indicates that the candidate does not account for a meaningful part of that association. Finally, we compared the association between the candidate and the outcome in the original and reversed models. We did not treat differences between the two as evidence of direction; similar values were taken to indicate that the association likely runs in both directions or has no clear direction, consistent with Weems (Weems, 2025). The following candidate-outcome combinations passed this step: For depression, emotion regulation difficulties, psychological inflexibility, and behavioral avoidance; for anxiety, emotion regulation difficulties and psychological inflexibility. Emotion regulation difficulties and psychological inflexibility were therefore the strongest candidates for both outcomes.

Study 1: Moderation (Step 3)

We next examined whether each candidate changed the strength or direction of the association between sleep condition and the outcome. As shown in Table 5, this effect was statistically significant for some of the combinations, but each added little explained variance (.01 to .05), and none reversed the direction of the association. Summary of results

We used a small exploratory study (N = 333) to identify candidate associative variables that may be a part, mechanistically or otherwise, of the relationship between sleep condition (SCI) and depression (DASS-depression) and/or anxiety (DASS-anxiety). Because we had cross-sectional, non-experimental data, we used Weems' (Weems, 2025) approach, which is a step-wise screening to identify potential alternative explanations for the relationship. After identifying candidate variable sets in the exploratory dataset, we replicated the analysis in a larger (N = 1,555) independent sample to confirm the exploratory findings. The results were very similar. These findings suggest that, for the relationship between sleep condition (SCI) and depression (DASS-depression), psychological flexibility (AAQ2) and emotion regulation (DERS) are the strongest candidates. DISCUSSION

This study systematically screened a theoretically informed set of psychological constructs that might help account for associations between symptoms of insomnia and depression and between symptoms of insomnia and anxiety, using an exploratory sample followed by independent confirmation. For depression, emotion regulation difficulties, psychological inflexibility, and behavioral avoidance emerged as robust candidate associative variables across the two samples. For anxiety, emotion regulation difficulties and psychological inflexibility consistently met the path criteria across both samples. Psychological inflexibility and emotion regulation difficulties may be particularly informative because they represent potentially modifiable processes. Rather than suggesting different processes for depression and anxiety, the findings point to a combination of shared transdiagnostic processes and additional constructs that may be more specifically relevant to depression. Psychological inflexibility may be an important transdiagnostic candidate linking sleep with internalizing symptoms. Psychological inflexibility explained substantial incremental variance in both depression (ΔR² = .286) and anxiety (ΔR² = .182) in Study 2. These findings are consistent with emerging literature linking greater psychological inflexibility with insomnia severity and poorer sleep quality across clinical and nonclinical samples (Ahn et al., 2024; El Rafihi-Ferreira et al., 2022; Zeng & Gan, 2026). Recent work examining specific dimensions of psychological inflexibility has similarly found associations between experiential avoidance, cognitive fusion, reduced present-moment awareness, and poorer sleep quality, in part through greater pre-sleep arousal (Mastin-Purcell et al., 2025). Several processes could plausibly contribute to these associations. Sleep difficulties may diminish individuals' capacity to respond flexibly to distressing internal experiences, while inflexible responses to fatigue, negative affect, or sleeprelated distress may contribute to rumination, withdrawal, and reduced engagement in valued activities. Psychological inflexibility may therefore represent one candidate process relevant to the association of sleep difficulties with internalizing symptoms broadly, rather than with depression specifically. However, given longstanding concerns regarding the construct and discriminant validity of the AAQ-II, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The AAQ-II has been criticized for substantial content and statistical overlap with negative affect and general psychological distress (Tyndall et al., 2019; Wolgast, 2014), raising the possibility that its comparatively strong associations with both depression and anxiety may partly reflect shared measurement content rather than a distinct transdiagnostic process. Emotion regulation may represent a second potentially modifiable process relevant to associations between insomnia and internalizing symptoms. Difficulties in emotion regulation demonstrated substantial incremental explanatory power for both depression (ΔR² = .277) and anxiety (ΔR² = .175) in Study 2. Adequate sleep is thought to support adaptive emotion regulation, whereas sleep disruption and sleep loss have been associated with heightened emotional reactivity and diminished capacity to regulate negative affect (Gruber & Cassoff, 2014; Meneo et al., 2023; Palmer & Alfano, 2017). A recent meta-analysis further demonstrated robust associations between insomnia symptoms and multiple dimensions of emotion dysregulation, including greater reliance on maladaptive regulatory strategies and dispositional difficulties in emotion regulation (Samea et al., 2025). The current cross-sectional findings cannot establish the direction of these associations: sleep disturbance may compromise emotion regulation, emotion dysregulation may interfere with sleep, internalizing symptoms may contribute to both, or these processes may operate reciprocally over time. The identification of emotion regulation as a candidate associative variable is nevertheless potentially important because emotion regulation is modifiable, providing a rationale for investigating these associations longitudinally and experimentally. Some of the mental health benefits of successfully treating insomnia may occur in part because improvements in sleep restore greater capacity to regulate affect. Consistent with this possibility, a recent randomized trial among adults with depressive symptoms found that digital cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) improved both emotion regulation difficulties and depressive symptoms relative to sleep hygiene education; moreover, early improvements in emotion regulation difficulties statistically mediated subsequent improvements in depression (Tamm et al., 2025). Conversely, interventions that directly strengthen emotion regulation skills may have downstream benefits for both sleep and emotional symptoms. Examining depression and anxiety separately revealed both shared and potentially symptomspecific patterns of association with insomnia. Psychological inflexibility and emotion regulation difficulties survived the screening procedure for both outcomes, suggesting that these constructs may reflect processes relevant across internalizing symptoms. In contrast, behavioral avoidance and detachment emerged as candidate associative variables for depression but not anxiety. This pattern is conceptually consistent with models of depression emphasizing behavioral withdrawal and reductions in response-contingent positive reinforcement: avoidance can reduce engagement with rewarding or meaningful activities, while detachment similarly encompasses diminished social and emotional engagement, anhedonia, and reduced positive affect (Carvalho & Hopko, 2011; Dimidjian et al., 2011). Together, these findings raise the possibility that insomnia intersects both with broad transdiagnostic processes and with constructs that may be more closely related to particular forms of internalizing psychopathology. In particular, anxiety may involve additional sleep-relevant processes that were not well represented in the candidate set, including worry and repetitive negative thinking, intolerance of uncertainty, cognitive arousal, threat sensitivity, and anxiety sensitivity (Clancy et al., 2020; Harvey, 2002). Several strengths of the present study increase confidence in the pattern of findings. Rather than examining a single hypothesized explanatory variable in isolation, we systematically compared multiple theoretically plausible constructs using prespecified screening criteria that required more than statistical significance alone. The use of an exploratory sample followed by an independent confirmatory sample provided an opportunity to determine which initial findings replicated in a separate and substantially larger sample. Examining depression and anxiety separately further allowed us to distinguish constructs that emerged across both internalizing outcomes from those that appeared more specific to depression. Finally, analyses considered competing explanations for observed associations, including moderation and reverse or bidirectional associations, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of the candidate variables than would a conventional cross-sectional mediation approach. These strengths should be considered alongside several limitations. Most importantly, both studies were cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish temporal ordering, mediation, or causality. The prioritized constructs should consequently be understood as associative variables rather than demonstrated mechanisms. All constructs were assessed using self-report measures, increasing the possibility that shared method variance contributed to observed associations. Insomnia was assessed with the SCI, which captures insomnia symptoms but does not provide objective measures of sleep or represent the broader range of sleep disturbances that may relate to internalizing symptoms. Similarly, the DASS assesses dimensional symptoms of depression and anxiety rather than clinical diagnoses; patterns of association may differ across specific depressive and anxiety disorders. Interpretation is also complicated by potential conceptual and item-content overlap among several constructs. This concern is particularly relevant to psychological inflexibility as measured by the AAQ-II, emotion dysregulation, internalizing, and the depression and anxiety outcomes. Finally, although the candidate set was intentionally broad, it was not exhaustive. Potentially important processes (including worry, repetitive negative thinking, and cognitive arousal) were not comprehensively represented, and the present results should not be interpreted as identifying all variables that may contribute to associations between insomnia and internalizing symptoms. The number of candidate variables and analyses also raises the possibility of chance findings associated with multiple testing. However, the structured screening procedure, requirement that candidates meet multiple criteria, and use of an independent exploratory-to-confirmatory design reduce concern that the principal findings reflect isolated statistically significant associations. In conclusion, the present studies identified psychological inflexibility and emotion regulation difficulties as candidate associative variables relevant to both depression and anxiety in the context of insomnia symptoms, while behavioral avoidance emerged specifically for depression. These findings illustrate the convergence of findings across an exploratory sample (N = 333) and a substantially larger independent confirmatory sample (overall N = 1,555, with analytic Ns varying by measure availability), strengthening confidence in the prioritized constructs and narrowing a broad theoretical space to a smaller set of candidates. Longitudinal, experimental, and intervention studies are now needed to determine whether the identified candidates temporally or causally contribute to associations between sleep and emotional symptoms. Clarifying these processes may ultimately help explain the broader mental health benefits of treating insomnia and identify additional targets for preventing the onset or recurrence of depression and anxiety among emerging adults.
