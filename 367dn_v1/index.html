Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .051 Across the nine designs that contain both families, the Spearman correlation between mean indicators per factor and the log ratio of median F M for cross-loadings to residual covariances is .80 at magnitude 0.3 (p = .009) and .67 at 0.2 (p = .051).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

A structural equation model can fit its data even when the design had little chance of detecting substantively relevant alternatives. A blind-spot map enumerates single-parameter alternatives to a fitted model, applies each at a standardized magnitude that holds implied variances fixed, and reports the probability that a stated decision rule would flag the model if the alternative were true, beside the probability that the same rule flags a correctly specified model. Analytic detection from the noncentral chi-square is paired with a simulated path that measures each rule's finite-sample size, and equivalence that follows from a saturated latent structure is certified by proof instead of by a numerical threshold. In ten published designs with N from 20 to 2,566, 2,035 alternatives are detectable, and all 177 admissible directed-path and latent-covariance alternatives are certified equivalent, so the fits test only the measurement structure. Median detection under the likelihood-ratio test rises from .071 at N = 20 to .996 at N = 2,566, and the largest design still has three blind spots, residual correlations of 0.3 detected at most 9.6 percent of the time against a size of 5.8 percent. The RMSEA > .06 rule flags 457 alternatives below .50 that the likelihood-ratio test detects above .80, and its measured size reaches .434 at N = 20. A declared block of correlated residuals among reverse-keyed items is detected with probability .998 where no single pair exceeds .520. Independent R and Julia packages generate reproducible artifacts.

1 Introduction

Several plausible structures can fit the same observed data in a structural equation model (MacCallum, Wegener, Uchino, & Fabrigar, 1993; Raykov & Marcoulides, 2001; Stelzl, 1986). Whether a fit decision tells the reader anything about a particular alternative depends on the analysis model, which is the model actually fitted, on the sample size, and on the decision rule that was applied. Where a study had little power against a specified alternative, that alternative stays compatible with the reported fit, even in a case where the population covariance it implies differs from the covariance of the fitted model (Satorra & Saris, 1985).

Power analysis gives the probability of detecting a specified misspecification (MacCallum, Browne, & Sugawara, 1996; Satorra & Saris, 1985). Analytical tools and software support that calculation for a pair of models, under the likelihood-ratio test and under rules based on the RMSEA (Jak, Jorgensen, Verdam, Oort, & Elffers, 2021; Moshagen & Bader, 2024), but the competing models and their magnitudes are still selected by the analyst. This paper proposes a blind-spot map, a report that answers the same question for a whole catalogue of competing models generated from the fitted model instead of for one pair chosen by hand. Each entry of the catalogue frees one parameter that the fitted model holds fixed, a position referred to below as a coordinate, and sets it to a standardized magnitude under a single magnitude convention. The entries are grouped in families, where a family consists of all the alternatives that one generating rule produces, such as every cross-loading from a factor to an indicator it does not load on, or every residual covariance between two indicators. The catalogue exposes the covariance-equivalent alternatives as well, whose implications for structural interpretation are established in the SEM literature (MacCallum et al., 1993; Stelzl, 1986). Saris, Satorra, and van der Veld (2009) argue that overall fit indices do not indicate the size of a misspecification, and they evaluate restricted parameters one at a time with the expected parameter change, the modification index and the power of the modification-index test. The map proposed here shares that view of the parameters. The question it asks of them is a different one, whether the decision rule that a study applied to its overall fit could have rejected the model had a given misspecification been present. An answer is given for every eligible coordinate in the families that the analyst declares for the run, four of which are enumerated by default. Each coordinate carries a standardized magnitude, an admissibility status that records whether the alternative yields a proper population covariance to which the analysis model can be fitted, and a detection probability, which is the probability that the rule rejects the model when that alternative holds. The detection probabilities of a design, reported beside the probability that the same rule rejects a correctly specified model, make up its detection profile, and an alternative whose detection lies at or near that second probability is a blind spot of the design. Sensitivity analysis in observational research makes the effect of a specified unmeasured structure explicit (VanderWeele & Ding, 2017). The proposed map does the same for a specified structural alternative by evaluating the chance that the fitted SEM would be flagged if that alternative had generated the data. Its scope is a decision rule applied to a covariance model. The substantive interpretation therefore still depends on assumptions that lie outside the calculation of fit.

Exact covariance equivalence has an interpretation of its own, because two models that imply the same population covariance cannot be told apart by their fit at any sample size (Raykov & Marcoulides, 2001; Stelzl, 1986), whereas a positive population discrepancy leaves detection low at the available sample size and lets it rise in a larger study (MacCallum et al., 1996; Satorra & Saris, 1985). The blind-spot map therefore keeps the two cases apart. Where an alternative changes only the latent covariance structure and the analysis model leaves that structure saturated, the equivalence is certified by proof. For every other entry the classification as equivalent rests on a stated numerical tolerance, and the map records which of the two bases applies.

The blind-spot map is made up of five elements. Its enumeration is exhaustive within the declared families, and the perturbations it applies are standardized so as to preserve the implied marginal variances.

Equivalence is certified where a proof applies and classified elsewhere. The detection probability under each stated decision rule is reported with the size of that rule, a size that is measured by simulation and never assumed. The last element is the sample size at which an alternative would reach a chosen detection level, as in sample-size planning for a specified alternative (MacCallum et al., 1996; Moshagen & Bader, 2024).

The software writes a structured file, the artifact, which records the catalogue with every quantity needed to verify its entries, and from it a reader can regenerate the catalogue and recompute any entry.

Applications in psychology frequently interpret approximate-fit indices alongside the likelihood-ratio test or in its place (Jackson, Gillaspy, & Purc-Stephenson, 2009; Ropovik, 2015). The operating characteristics of a fixed cutoff depend on the model and on the sample size (F. Chen, Curran, Bollen, Kirby, & Paxton, 2008; Groskurth, Bluemke, & Lechner, 2024). Across the detectable alternatives of the ten designs analyzed below, median detection is .239 under the likelihood-ratio test and below .001 under the RMSEA > .06 rule (Hu & Bentler, 1999), while the measured size of the RMSEA rule ranges from .434 to below .001. Model-specific calibration of the cutoffs offers another route to stated error rates (McNeish & Wolf, 2023). The blind-spot map complements that route by reporting the detection of each declared alternative once a rule is specified. Pesigan, Cheung, Wu, Chang, and Leung (2026) also generate a set of neighboring models from the fitted model, and they weigh the plausibility of each given the observed data by a Bayesian posterior probability computed from the Bayesian information criterion. The two approaches are complementary in that posterior probabilities compare the alternatives in the light of the data in hand, whereas the blind-spot map states how often the decision rule of the design would have rejected the fitted model under each alternative. The latter is a frequency property of the design, and it holds before the data are consulted. Neither probability is a verdict on whether a published model or any competing model generated the observed data. A blind-spot map is a falsifier and not a verifier, in the sense that it can show that a design could not have distinguished the fitted model from a stated alternative and can never show that the fitted model is correct.

Applied to ten published designs, the proposed map shows three properties that a fit statistic does not reveal. In every design with latent variables the covariance evidence bears on the measurement structure alone, because all directed paths and latent covariances among the factors are certified equivalent (Lee & Hershberger, 1990; Stelzl, 1986). The actual size of the likelihood-ratio test departs from its nominal level in the three smallest designs, by amounts that the asymptotic theory does not show and that small samples lead one to expect (Curran, Bollen, Paxton, Kirby, & Chen, 2002; Moshagen, 2012). The RMSEA cutoff leaves almost every enumerated alternative unflagged, which is a property of the rule that a sensitivity statement can make explicit.

2 The blind-spot map

2.1 Setup and notation

The analysis model is written in reticular action notation (McArdle & McDonald, 1984). The directed coefficients are held in an asymmetric matrix A and the variances and covariances in a symmetric matrix S, while a selection picks the observed indices out of the m modeled variables. With E = (I -A) -1 , the implied covariance of the p observed variables is the observed submatrix of ESE ′ , written Σ(θ) for a free parameter vector θ. Because reticular action notation represents directed paths, residual covariances and mean structures in one algebra (McArdle & McDonald, 1984), one perturbation mechanism reaches every family of alternatives that the map enumerates.

The covariance-structure fits use the sample covariance C and the normal-theory discrepancy of maximum-likelihood factor analysis (Jöreskog, 1969) (1) Under the Wishart likelihood C has divisor N -1 and the test statistic is T = (N -1)F ML .

The normal likelihood divides by N instead, with T = N F ML . Both conventions are offered by lavaan under its likelihood option (Rosseel, 2012). A model fitted to G groups minimizes a weighted sum of the group discrepancies, where the weights are (N g -1)/(N -G) under the Wishart likelihood and N g /N under the normal likelihood, and its statistic is that sum multiplied by N -G or by N . The artifact records the convention of the fit, and every formula below uses the recorded one, which for the corpus is the Wishart likelihood with divisor N -1.

2.2 The alternative catalogue

An alternative is a single competing model, obtained by freeing one parameter of the analysis model at one coordinate and setting that parameter to one magnitude. The catalogue is the enumerated set of alternatives for a fitted model. A family is the set of alternatives that one generating rule produces. Four families are enumerated in every design, the first being a cross-loading from a latent variable to an indicator outside its analysis-model indicators and the second a residual covariance between two observed variables whose residuals are uncorrelated in the analysis model. Cross-loadings are often observed in applications and frequently disregarded, and omitting one that exists in the population biases the loadings and the structural parameters of the fitted model (Navarro & Schermelleh-Engel, 2025). A covariance between two latent variables that the analysis model leaves uncorrelated forms the third family, and a directed path between latent variables where none is specified forms the fourth. A fifth family applies when the model is fitted to several groups with loadings constrained equal across them, which is the metric step of an invariance test (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). Each of its alternatives raises one constrained loading in one of the later groups while the first group keeps the common value. The map thereby states which departures from the assumed invariance the design could have detected.

Enumeration is exhaustive within each family, in the sense that every coordinate the analysis model leaves fixed becomes an alternative at each magnitude of a declared grid. By default the grid is 0.2, 0.3 and 0.4 for cross-loadings and 0.1 and 0.2 for loading differences between groups, while the other families use 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3. An analyst who needs a different grid can declare one. The artifact records the catalogue rule by name, which lets a reader regenerate every entry with its coordinates and magnitudes and compare the result with the recorded catalogue. Any missing alternative that would change the reported detection profile is exposed by that comparison.

Declared alternatives can be added to the enumerated ones. A declared alternative moves several coordinates at once, for instance the residual covariances among all the indicators that share a method (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). It is applied as a single perturbation (Section 2.3), with the result that a pattern of small associations is evaluated together with its components. Enumeration respects the available design information as well. A family whose data requirements are not met, such as a loading difference between groups in a model fitted to the pooled sample (Appendix B), appears in an exclusions list together with its reason.

2.3 The magnitude convention

A magnitude has to mean the same thing in every cell of the catalogue, or detection probabilities cannot be compared across cells, and comparison is the entire purpose of a map. Stating the magnitude that a power analysis needs is a known difficulty (Moshagen & Bader, 2024). The convention adopted here is that a perturbation of standardized magnitude m is applied together with an adjustment to the corresponding residual variance that holds the implied marginal variances of the observed variables at their fitted values.

Without the adjustment, adding a cross-loading inflates the variance of the affected indicator, and the design then detects a mixture of the structural change and the variance change, in proportions that differ from cell to cell. The derivation of the adjustment is given in Appendix A. A loading that differs between groups is scaled in the same way within the group concerned, and a declared alternative applies all its components before the residual variances are adjusted once.

The marginal-variance adjustment requires positive residual variances, and an adjusted residual variance at or below zero gives the status inadmissible_dgp at the declared magnitude, in which case the entry retains its magnitude and receives no detection probability.

2.4 Detection under a stated decision rule

Let Σ A be the implied covariance of an alternative A, treated as the data-generating covariance, and let be the discrepancy remaining when the analysis model is fitted to Σ A .

Under a sequence of local alternatives the test statistic follows a noncentral chi-square with noncentrality λ = (N -1)F M and the degrees of freedom of the model (Satorra & Saris, 1985), with N -G in place of N -1 for G groups. The map uses the asymptotic result at fixed alternatives and measures its finite-sample accuracy for each design (Section 2.7).

The detection probability of A is the probability that a stated decision rule flags the fitted model when A is true, and throughout the paper the design means the analysis model, the sample size and the decision rule taken together. Detection is a property of the design under a stated alternative, so a map built from it is a falsifier and not a verifier, in the sense that a high value says that the design could have rejected the fitted model and never says that the fitted model is right.

The map also reports detection under the approximate-fit rules used in applied work (Ropovik, 2015). Since the sample RMSEA (Browne & Cudeck, 1992; MacCallum et al., 1996) is a monotone function of T , the probability that it exceeds a cutoff c, such as the conventional .06 (Hu & Bentler, 1999), is available in closed form, and the test of close fit gives a third rule, whose null hypothesis specifies an RMSEA of ε 0 > 0 (Browne & Cudeck, 1992; MacCallum et al., 1996) and whose critical value comes from the noncentral distribution with noncentrality (N -1) df ε 2 0 .

For G groups the sample RMSEA carries the factor √ G, so the cutoff term in eq. ( 4) becomes df (N -G) c 2 /G. The comparative fit index (Bentler, 1990) depends on the statistic of a baseline model as well and is not mapped. Every detectable alternative also carries its population RMSEA, G F M /df, which states its misfit on a scale that does not depend on the sample size.

A detection probability is uninterpretable on its own. Every rule is therefore reported with its size, the probability that the same rule flags a correctly specified model, which is obtained analytically by evaluating the same expression at F M = 0 and is also measured by simulation. For the likelihood-ratio test the analytic size is α by construction, and the measured size can exceed it in small samples. Fixed fit-index cutoffs have a size that depends on the design even analytically (F. Chen et al., 2008; Marsh, Hau, & Wen, 2004), and across the ten designs the measured size of the RMSEA cutoff ranges from .434 to below .001 (Section 5.3).

Reporting the size beside the detection makes the false-positive behavior of each rule visible. An admissible alternative whose detection probability lies at or near the size of the rule is a blind spot of the design.

2.5 Equivalent alternatives

Exact covariance equivalence means F M = 0 and λ = 0, so that detection equals the size of the rule at every sample size (Stelzl, 1986). The software assigns the status equivalent_model when the optimized discrepancy satisfies F M < 10 -10 , excludes these entries from the detection summaries, and reports them separately with the basis of the classification. When the alternative changes only the covariance structure of the latent variables and the analysis model leaves that structure saturated, the equivalence holds by construction, as Proposition 2 in Appendix C proves. The software checks the conditions of the proposition from the recorded coordinates and certifies such an entry instead of classifying it numerically. Every other equivalent entry carries a numerical basis, and what it implies for arbitrarily large samples rests on the tolerance.

The interpretation of an entry depends on the population discrepancy. A positive F M lets detection rise with the sample size, whereas an exact zero leaves the covariance evidence unchanged at any sample size.

2.6 Admissibility and conditioning

Three numerical conditions govern admissibility, of which the first is that a perturbed covariance must be positive definite to represent a distribution, so that a failure receives an inadmissible status. Conditioning is checked next, and when the spectral condition number of the perturbed covariance exceeds 10 10 the entry is reported as ill-conditioned and carries no detection probability, because optimizing against a near-singular target gives an unstable result.

The third condition concerns the minimizer in eq. ( 2). The noncentral approximation assumes that the parameter value which best approximates Σ A lies inside the parameter space, while structural misspecification can instead drive that value to a negative variance in the population (Kolenikov & Bollen, 2012), in which case the discrepancy surface is flat in the direction of the improper estimate. Such an entry receives the status improper_minimizer, and the map reports its F M and the offending variance, withholds the analytic detection probability, and measures detection by simulation (Section 2.7). The refit itself is accepted only at a stationary point, with the gradient below 10 -5 relative to the discrepancy, and an entry that does not reach one is reported as nonconverged and carries no number, nonconvergence and improper solutions being familiar outcomes of maximum-likelihood estimation (Anderson & Gerbing, 1984; Boomsma, 1985).

2.7 Measured calibration and simulated detection

Equations ( 3) and ( 4) use the asymptotic reference distribution, under which the likelihood-ratio test has size α exactly. In finite samples the statistic is inflated, more so at small N and as the number of observed variables grows (Curran et al., 2002; Moshagen, 2012), and the map therefore measures the calibration instead of asserting it. Replications are drawn from the implied covariance of the fitted model, the analysis model is refitted to each of them, and the rejection rate of every rule is recorded with its Monte Carlo standard error (Morris, White, & Crowther, 2019). Simulation from the model-implied distribution is the general route to power where no closed form applies, and it extends to distributionally misspecified models (Irmer, Klein, & Schermelleh-Engel, 2024). Here it serves to measure the accuracy of the closed form in the design at hand. A calibration block whose likelihood-ratio rejection rate departs from α by more than three standard errors marks the artifact as miscalibrated.

The simulated path repeats the procedure under the covariance Σ A of each alternative and records detection under all three rules beside the analytic values. Every alternative and the calibration run share one seed, so that contrasts between entries carry common random numbers (Glasserman & Yao, 1992) and not independent noise. A replication counts when its refit ends at a stationary point, with the gradient below 10 -4 relative to the discrepancy, whatever exit label the optimizer assigns, and a solution with a negative variance counts as well, because the analyst would still obtain its test statistic and act on it. Analytic and simulated values are reported together and are never substituted for one another. Where the calibration is close to nominal the two agree, and where it is not the simulated values are the operating characteristics of the design.

By default each replication draws a sample covariance from the Wishart distribution, which describes a normal population with the covariance Σ A . When the raw data are available, two further generators measure the calibration and the detection for the distribution of the data themselves. The modified bootstrap of Bollen and Stine (1992) resamples the rows of the centered data Y after transforming them to the target covariance as Y C -1/2 Σ 1/2 A , which keeps the joint distribution of the data apart from its covariance, and the ordinal generator cuts latent normal variables with the correlations of Σ A at thresholds set from the observed category proportions, as in the model for ordered categorical indicators of Muthén (1984). The data never enter the artifact, which records instead a digest that binds the simulated values to them.

2.8 Planning from a map

Since F M is a population quantity that does not change with N , eq. ( 3) can be inverted for the sample size at which an alternative reaches a chosen detection probability (MacCallum et al., 1996). Where no closed form exists, the same sample size is found by modelling simulated power as a function of N (Irmer et al., 2024).

The function bsm_n_for_detection returns the smallest such N , and an infinite value for an equivalent alternative, so that a blind spot becomes a design statement about the sample a study would have needed for that alternative to reach, for instance, detection .80 under the likelihood-ratio test. The inversion can also run over the magnitude at the sample size of the design. For each coordinate, bsm_min_detectable searches the smallest magnitude at which the alternative reaches the chosen detection, and it reports the coordinate as not reaching it when the admissible range of the magnitude convention ends first.

3 Implementation

3.1 The artifact

The output of a map is a documented artifact for independent inspection. It records the provenance of the fit, the RAM coordinates and the sufficient statistics. The catalogue rule, the computation settings and the results for every alternative follow, with two digests over its canonical contents. Participant-level records remain outside the artifact, which keeps only a digest of them when the simulated path resamples the raw data.

Moments make the artifact shareable, because a covariance matrix, the sample size and the model suffice for the calculations of Section 2. An author can therefore publish a checkable detection profile and keep the participant-level data private.

3.2 Two packages

The method is implemented twice, as the R package blindspot and as the Julia package Blindspot.jl (Bezanson, Edelman, Karpinski, & Shah, 2017). The two packages share no code and meet only in the artifact format. Each of them enumerates the catalogue, computes the analytic and the simulated map, and writes, reads and verifies artifacts, including those written by the other package. The R package takes a model fitted with lavaan (Rosseel, 2012). It also accepts lavaan model syntax together with raw data, a covariance or correlation matrix, or one matrix per group, in which case it fits the model with lavaan itself, and apart from a JSON parser it imports only packages distributed with R. The Julia package reads the same model syntax and fits it by maximum likelihood with the defaults of lavaan, minimizing with the BFGS method of the Optim package (Mogensen & Riseth, 2018). A design can therefore be mapped without R. Both packages print a summary ordered from the least to the most detectable alternative, with the measured calibration at its head.

The R extractor copies the fitted parameterization, in the sense that it reads and reshapes the output of the fitting software and recomputes the implied covariance from the coordinates of the artifact as a selfcheck, refusing to write when the two disagree beyond 10 -10 . The covariance check prevents a transcription error from becoming a silent change of the model.

The extractor also refuses every fit whose test statistic the analytic map does not model, which covers inequality constraints and bounds, robust test statistics (Satorra & Bentler, 2001), estimators other than maximum likelihood, ordered indicators (Muthén, 1984), sampling weights, multilevel structure, constrained mean structures, and full-information likelihood on incomplete cases (Enders & Bandalos, 2001), and each refusal names its reason. A pointwise covariance check cannot catch these cases, because a constrained fit reproduces its implied covariance exactly while having more degrees of freedom than an unconstrained one.

Equality constraints within and between groups are represented exactly, through a map from coordinates to shared parameters, so that a multi-group fit with equal loadings keeps the degrees of freedom that lavaan reports. The map reads the sample-size convention of the fit and applies it to every rule.

For observed exogenous variables that lavaan treats as fixed, the full-covariance RAM representation frees the exogenous covariance block as saturated nuisance parameters, and the food-market model consequently retains the two degrees of freedom of the fitted model when its alternatives are evaluated.

In both packages the refit of the analysis model to a perturbed covariance runs a scaled quasi-Newton search, followed by a second optimizer started from its result, keeps the lower discrepancy, and accepts it only at a stationary point. In the alienation design of Section 5, whose observed variances differ by a factor of 47, a plain quasi-Newton search stops short of the minimum. The simulated path draws each Wishart replication by the Bartlett decomposition, starts each refit from moment-based values (Boomsma, 1985), with loadings taken from the covariances with the marker indicator so that a reverse-keyed item starts with the right sign, and records the statistic, the exit status and the termination gradient of each replication. Replication r of the calibration run and of every alternative draws from its own random stream, keyed by the seed and by r, which in R is a stream of the combined multiple-recursive generator of L'Ecuyer (1999), obtained as in L'Ecuyer, Simard, Chen, and Kelton (2002), and in Julia a Xoshiro256++ generator (Blackman & Vigna, 2021). The results therefore do not depend on the number of worker processes, and the alternatives share their draws in the way that Section 2.7 requires. Analytic and simulated values are reported side by side, because the analytic path rests on an asymptotic reference distribution whose adequacy in finite samples is an empirical question (Curran et al., 2002).

Since the two packages meet only in the format, either of them can verify the output of the other, and the format, and not one of the programs, is the contract between the analyst who reports a map and the reader who checks it.

3.3 Verification tiers

Verification is reported at one of three levels, and a verifier states which one it used. Structural verification confirms that the artifact parses, that its dimensions are consistent and that its digests recompute, and it regenerates the auto-generated part of the catalogue exactly from the recorded rule. The structural level needs no raw data and still catches a narrowed catalogue. Statistical verification recomputes every analytic value, which has to agree within 10 -6 , and reruns the simulated values from the recorded seed with either package. A rerun value is accepted when it lies within z 1-0.01/(2k) combined Monte Carlo standard errors of the recorded one, where k counts the simulated alternatives and the calibration run. The whole artifact is thereby held to a family-wise error rate of one percent. Exact verification reruns the artifact with the package, the version and the platform that produced it and requires bit-identical output.

A simulated artifact records the package, its version and the platform. The verifier checks these three before it compares outputs bit for bit, whereas comparisons across packages and platforms use the statistical tier (Appendix D). All ten corpus artifacts validate against the JSON Schema of the format, regenerate their catalogues exactly and pass structural verification in both packages. Each package also verifies the artifacts of the other at the statistical tier (Section 4).

4 Validation

Validation compares the numerical components with lavaan, with independent power calculations, with direct refits to population covariances and with simulated rejection rates, and it compares the two packages with each other, so that the comparisons establish together the numerical accuracy and the finite-sample limits of the reported probabilities.

Five comparisons with independent implementations assess the discrepancy, the power calculation, the rejection behavior that the map predicts and the simulated path, and the last of them compares the two packages with each other.

Analytic detection probabilities agree with semPower (Moshagen & Bader, 2024; Moshagen & Erdfelder, 2016) to 2.2 × 10 -16 on three alternatives spanning two families. The discrepancy F M of eq. ( 2) was checked against lavaan (Rosseel, 2012) refitted directly to every perturbed covariance in the corpus of Section 5, 2,284 refits in all. The largest relative difference over the 2,035 detectable alternatives is 1.6 × 10 -9 and the median 8 × 10 -12 , and for 185 of the 186 equivalent alternatives lavaan also returns a discrepancy below 3 × 10 -14 , whereas for the remaining one, a certified directed path in the big-five design, it reports no converged solution.

The third anchor tests the claim that the map makes, with data generated from a planted alternative, a cross-loading at standardized magnitude 0.20 with F M = 0.0629 at N = 150 on eight degrees of freedom, to which the analysis model was fitted in 4,000 independent samples. The map predicted a detection probability of .554 and the observed rejection rate was .548, a difference of 0.74 Monte Carlo standard errors. The same test confirms that the map points at the right coordinate, because among the 58 admissible alternatives in that catalogue the planted one is the unique nearest entry to the generating covariance, at a discrepancy of zero against a runner-up of 0.152.

The fourth anchor compares the simulated path with an independent simulation, in which replications were drawn in four designs spanning N = 20 to N = 403 from the fitted model and from three alternatives and were refitted with the R package and, separately, with lavaan, 4,000 replications each. Across the 16 conditions the two rejection rates of the likelihood-ratio test differ by at most 2.02 combined Monte Carlo standard errors, and the rates of the RMSEA rule by about one percentage point at most. Under the fitted model the two implementations agree on sizes between .065 and .098 in the designs with N = 20, N = 75 and N = 403, so that the finite-sample departures described in Section 5.3 are properties of the designs and do not arise from either program.

The fifth anchor compares the two packages, which share no code. Mapping the ten corpus designs from the same fitted models, they assign the same status to all 2,583 entries and the same basis to every equivalent one, and their analytic detection probabilities differ by at most 1.7 × 10 -11 . Their simulated detection probabilities are drawn from different random streams, and over the 2,098 entries that both packages simulate, five standardized differences exceed three in absolute value, against 5.7 expected by chance, the largest being 3.35. Starting instead from the raw data or the published moments, the Julia package fits the ten models itself and recovers the parameter structure of lavaan in all ten of them, with estimates within a relative 3.9 × 10 -6 of those of lavaan and analytic detection within 5.7 × 10 -6 of the R map. Each package verifies the ten artifacts of the other at the structural and at the statistical tier.

Five invariants are checked across the entries of the catalogue. Multiplying an indicator by a positive constant and refitting must leave every detection probability unchanged, since a standardized magnitude cannot depend on the arbitrary units of an indicator, and reordering the variables must leave the map unchanged up to relabeling, because a dependence on order indicates an indexing error. The tests check that detection does not decrease across the declared magnitude grid of each coordinate or across sample sizes for a fixed alternative, and that a zero magnitude returns the size of the rule. Every entry that the software certifies as equivalent by Proposition 2 must also return F M < 10 -10 from the optimizer, so that the proof and the numerics check each other.

5.2 What the fits can test

All 18 latent-covariance alternatives and 159 of the 192 structural-path alternatives are covariance-equivalent to their analysis models, and the software certifies every one of the 177 by Proposition 2, while the other 33 structural paths are inadmissible. The certificate applies because each of the nine designs with latent variables leaves its latent covariance structure saturated, seven of them being confirmatory factor models with freely correlated factors and two being structural models whose latent part is a complete recursive system. In all nine, the covariance evidence bears on the measurement structure and on nothing else. No fit statistic from these designs, at any sample size, could have distinguished a directed path between latent variables from a correlation, or one direction from another (Lee & Hershberger, 1990; Stelzl, 1986). The map makes this explicit in every artifact instead of leaving it to a reader to notice.

The remaining nine equivalent entries carry a numerical basis and all belong to the alienation design, in which each factor has two indicators. They are the residual covariances between the two indicators of the same factor, at three magnitudes. Freeing such a covariance alongside the factor's loading and variance leaves the model unidentified, with a singular expected information matrix, so the analysis model absorbs the perturbation exactly.

The 63 alternatives with an improper minimizer, 60 of them cross-loadings, mark misspecifications that drive the analysis model to a negative variance in the population (Kolenikov & Bollen, 2012). Seventeen are cross-loadings in the alienation design, whose factors each have two indicators, a concentration that parallels the higher frequency of improper solutions in samples from models with few indicators per factor (Anderson & Gerbing, 1984). The analytic path withholds a detection probability for these entries. Their simulated detection has a median of .713 and ranges from .093 to one, so that a misspecification strong enough to produce a population Heywood case is usually detected, although not always.

5.3 Measured calibration

Table 2 reports the measured size of each rule beside its analytic value. The likelihood-ratio test rejects a correctly specified model 8.9 percent of the time in the food-market design (N = 20), 10.2 percent in the political-democracy design (N = 75) and 7.5 percent in the ability design (N = 145), and each of these rates departs from .05 by more than three Monte Carlo standard errors, as small samples and large covariance matrices lead one to expect (Curran et al., 2002; Moshagen, 2012). The quality-of-life design, with seventeen observed variables at N = 403, rejects 6.5 percent of the time, 2.7 standard errors above .05, and the other six designs reject between 4.7 and 5.9 percent of the time and are not flagged. The RMSEA > .06 rule flags a correctly specified model 43.4 percent of the time at N = 20, against an analytic 34.4 percent, and 21.5 percent at N = 75, against an analytic 13.4 percent.

For the four designs whose raw data are distributed, the bootstrap generator of Section 2.7 (Bollen & Stine, 1992) measures the same sizes under the distribution of the data, again from 2,000 replications. The likelihood-ratio test then rejects a correctly specified model 19.6 percent of the time in the food-market design, 13.8 percent in the political-democracy design, 8.8 percent in the mental-ability design and 26.6 percent in the big-five design, against 8.9, 10.2, 5.2 and 5.8 percent under the Wishart generator. The big-five items have six response categories, and the ordinal generator, which cuts normal variables at the observed category proportions, gives a size of 6.6 percent for that design. Discreteness alone therefore accounts for little of the inflation, which comes from the joint distribution of the responses, as nonnormal data inflate the normal-theory statistic of a correctly specified model (Curran, West, & Finch, 1996).

The inflation carries over to detection, as fig. 3 shows by plotting simulated against analytic likelihoodratio detection for all 2,035 detectable alternatives under the Wishart generator. In the three flagged designs the median absolute difference is .044, .062 and .039, in the quality-of-life design it is .036, and in the other six designs it is at most .013. Pooled over the corpus, median detection is .239 analytically and .278 by simulation. For a small design the analytic value understates how often the stated rule rejects, and the measured size shows that much of the difference consists of false positives instead of added sensitivity.

5.4 Detection by family and by sample size

Residual-covariance detection tracks sample size across the ten designs, as the noncentrality λ = (N -1)F M implies for single-element perturbations of similar size. The Spearman correlation between log N and the median analytic detection is .964 at standardized magnitude 0.2 and .952 at 0.3. Cross-loading detection tracks N less closely, with correlations of .62 and .47, because a cross-loading's effect also depends on how many indicators its factor carries.

A residual covariance changes one off-diagonal element of the implied covariance. A cross-loading from factor f to indicator j changes the covariances of y j with every indicator of f , so the affected block widens with the number of indicators per factor. Across the nine designs that contain both families, the Spearman correlation between mean indicators per factor and the log ratio of median F M for cross-loadings to residual covariances is .80 at magnitude 0.3 (p = .009) and .67 at 0.2 (p = .051). Residual covariances have the larger median F M only in designs with at most three indicators per factor, and every design with more than three has the cross-loading median ahead.

The ordering of median detection follows the same pattern without being uniform, with cross-loadings leading in seven of nine designs at magnitude 0.2 and in six of nine at 0.3. The health-care design has the largest reversal, with median cross-loading F M between a quarter and a fifth of the residual-covariance median. Its four factors are the most strongly correlated in the corpus, with a mean absolute correlation of .81, which is consistent with a cross-loading onto a nearly collinear factor being largely absorbed by the indicator's own loading. Because the two families' magnitudes are standardized on different scales (Appendix A), these comparisons describe the corpus instead of a general ranking of the families.

Modification indices can localize a residual association after model rejection (Saris et al., 2009; Sörbom, 1989). The detection probabilities here quantify the chance of reaching rejection under each declared alternative.

5.5 Detection under conventional cutoffs

Across 75 published latent-variable studies in eleven psychology journals, Ropovik (2015) found frequent reliance on approximate-fit indices, and Jackson et al. (2009) found the chi-square test reported alongside them almost universally, so the map evaluates RMSEA-based rules beside the likelihood-ratio test. Over the 2,035 detectable alternatives, median detection under the RMSEA > .06 rule (Hu & Bentler, 1999) and under the test of close fit (Browne & Cudeck, 1992) is below .001, analytically and by simulation, against .239 and .278 for the likelihood-ratio test.

For 457 alternatives the likelihood-ratio test rejects with probability above .80 while the RMSEA rule flags the model with probability below .50, and the simulated path gives 456 such alternatives, as fig. 4 shows. The cutoff behaves as it was built to behave, in that its rejection region tolerates population misfit below an RMSEA of .06. The population RMSEA of the corpus alternatives, F M /df, has a median of .021 and exceeds .06 for only 2.4 percent of them. The comparison therefore turns on a substantive choice instead of a statistical defect, the choice being whether a residual correlation of .3, or a cross-loading of .3, is a misspecification that a study may leave unflagged. A design reported only with the cutoff makes that choice implicitly. The map makes it explicit, alternative by alternative, and pairs each rule with its size, because fixed cutoffs have model-and sample-dependent operating characteristics (F. Chen et al., 2008; Groskurth et al., 2024; Marsh et al., 2004; Savalei, 2012).

5.6 Blind spots in the ten designs

The weakest detectable alternatives are residual covariances of standardized magnitude 0.1 in nine of the ten designs, with analytic detection between .050 and .078. Against the measured size of each design's rule, rule flags a correctly specified model. In the political-democracy design at N = 75, residual correlations of 0.3 are detected between 10.5 and 34.2 percent of the time, against a size of 10.2 percent. In the big-five design at N = 2,566, the median residual correlation of 0.3 is detected with probability near one, yet the three weakest are detected 6.1, 9.5 and 9.6 percent of the time against a size of 5.8 percent.

A shared method is a pattern across several coordinates (Podsakoff et al., 2003), and the map evaluates it when an analyst declares it (Section 2.2). Four of the fifteen Big Five items, A1, E1, E2 and O2, are reversekeyed, a wording that carries a method effect of its own (DiStefano & Motl, 2006), and a declared block that correlates their residuals pairwise at 0.1, with a population RMSEA of .020, is detected with probability .998, whereas none of its six pairs alone exceeds .520. At 0.2 the block is detected with probability 1.000 and the weakest of its pairs with .773, so that a design which would often miss each component of a keying artifact can still be likely to reject the model when the components occur together.

These numbers describe how often each model would be flagged if the stated residual association generated the data. A blind-spot map is a falsifier and not a verifier, in the sense that it shows which alternatives these designs could not have distinguished from the fitted models and supplies no evidence that any of these associations exists in the published data or that the fitted models are correct.

6 Worked example

The worked example maps the mental-ability model of Holzinger and Swineford (1939), with nine indicators on three correlated factors at N = 301, from the raw data that lavaan distributes. In R the model syntax and the data go to a single function, which fits the model with lavaan, enumerates the catalogue, computes the analytic map and, with simulate = TRUE, runs the simulated path with 2,000 replications for the fitted model and for every alternative.

hs <-lavaan::HolzingerSwineford1939 map <-blindspot(model, data = hs, likelihood = "wishart", simulate = TRUE) summary(map) bsm_write(map, "hs.bsm.json")

The catalogue has 180 entries, of which 144 are detectable. The summary opens with the measured calibration, in which the likelihood-ratio test rejects the fitted model itself in 5.2 percent of the replications, with a Monte Carlo standard error of 0.5 points. The asymptotic approximation is therefore adequate for this design, and the analytic and simulated values below agree closely. Run with a single worker process, threads = 1, the call takes about 16 minutes on a laptop. The artifact it writes holds the map, the calibration and the seed from which the simulated values can be rerun.

Three entries of the map illustrate the three outcomes of Fig 1. Among the thirty-six residual covariances at standardized magnitude 0.1, the weakest, between x4 and x5, has analytic detection .052 and simulated detection .057 against a measured size of .052, which means that a residual association at that coordinate would lead to rejection about as often as a correctly specified model does. Cross-loadings at magnitude 0.3 have a median detection of .565 and range from .263 to almost one across twelve detectable indicatorfactor pairs, while three more are inadmissible at that magnitude and three have an improper minimizer.

All eighteen directed paths between the factors are certified equivalent, because the model leaves the three factor covariances free, so that the fit carries no information about direction among the factors.

The planning functions of Section 2.8 turn the weakest entries into statements about the design. A residual correlation of 0.2 at the median coordinate has F M = 0.0214, for which bsm_n_for_detection returns N = 1,053 for detection .80 under the likelihood-ratio test, three and a half times the sample of the study, and the weakest residual correlation of 0.1 would need about 60,000 observations. At the sample size of the study, bsm_min_detectable(map, target = 0.8) searches each coordinate for the smallest magnitude with detection .80, which lies between 0.30 and 0.49 for 28 of the 36 residual covariances and between 0.20 and 0.42 for nine of the 18 cross-loading coordinates, whereas the other coordinates leave the admissible range of the magnitude convention before they reach it and the six directed paths are equivalent at every magnitude.

Measurement invariance is usually assessed in configural, metric and scalar steps before groups are compared (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000), and the metric step, which holds the loadings equal across groups, is part of the analysis model only when the model is fitted to the groups separately. A second call fits the model in this way to the two schools of the study, passing the grouping column and the equality constraint on to lavaan.

by_school <-blindspot(model, data = hs, group = "school", group.equal = "loadings", likelihood = "wishart", simulate = TRUE)

The multi-group fit, with n = 156 and n = 145, keeps the 54 degrees of freedom that lavaan reports for it, and its catalogue contains, besides the four families in each group, 12 alternatives that raise one constrained loading in the Grant-White school by a standardized 0.1 or 0.2. Of these, 11 are admissible, with analytic detection between .053 and .068 at 0.1 and between .060 and .159 at 0.2, so that a loading differing between the two schools by these amounts would have left the fit of the invariant model almost unaffected.

Simulated detection exceeds the analytic value by 1.4 to 3.2 points for every one of them, an excess of the order of the measured size under the fitted model, .064 with a Monte Carlo standard error of .0055, which reflects the calibration of the test in groups of about 150 and adds no sensitivity to the loadings.

When the raw data are available, the calibration can also be measured under the distribution of the data themselves, with the bootstrap generator of Section 2.7 in place of the Wishart draw. Section 5.3 reports the result for this design and for the three other designs whose data are distributed.

bsm_simulate(map, ids = character(0), generator = "bootstrap", data = hs)

The Julia package maps the same design without R. It reads the model syntax and the data from a file, here written from R with write.csv(hs, "hs.csv", row.names = FALSE), fits the model itself, and the last line of the following code verifies the artifact that R wrote, recomputing every analytic value and rerunning the simulated ones from the recorded seed. 7 Discussion

7.1 What a blind-spot map licenses

A blind-spot map reports the probability of rejecting a fitted model under a specified alternative, at a specified magnitude and sample size and under a specified decision rule. A blind-spot map is a falsifier, not a verifier. It can show that a design could not have distinguished the fitted model from a stated alternative, and it can never show that the fitted model is correct. High detection across the declared catalogue shows that the design could have rejected the model under those alternatives and supplies no probability that the model is true, so that a fitting model remains one explanation among the structures compatible with the data (MacCallum et al., 1993; Raykov & Marcoulides, 2001; Stelzl, 1986).

The probability reported for a residual correlation of 0.30 describes detection on the assumption that this correlation generated the population covariance, whereas evidence about the structure that actually generated the data requires substantive assumptions or additional data, with the consequence that the corpus supports statements about the sensitivity of the designs under declared alternatives.

7.2 Implications for practice and review

A sensitivity statement, of the kind that observational research reports for unmeasured confounding (Van-derWeele & Ding, 2017), can report three things beside a fit decision. The first is the set of alternatives whose detection lies near the measured size of the rule, and the second the families that the analysis model cannot distinguish at any sample size. The third is the sample size at which a substantively relevant alternative would reach a chosen detection level. A sample large enough to detect an omitted cross-loading is a planning requirement that has been stated for nonlinear structural models as well (Navarro & Schermelleh-Engel, 2025). The map and its calibration block supply the first two and the planning functions of Section 2.8 the third. Together they tie a reported fit decision to the alternatives that the design could and could not have examined, in a form that a reviewer can check against the artifact.

Two checks from the corpus apply to any submitted model and need no new data. If the analysis model leaves its latent covariance structure saturated, as all nine latent-variable designs here do, its fit supports no directional claim among the latent variables (Lee & Hershberger, 1990; Stelzl, 1986), and the map certifies this property instead of leaving it to inspection. If the sample is small or the covariance matrix large, the measured size of the likelihood-ratio test can exceed its nominal level (Curran et al., 2002; Moshagen, 2012), as it does in the three smallest of the ten designs, and a calibration block shows whether a reported rejection or non-rejection rests on a test that operates at its stated level.

The artifact permits structural verification of its dimensions, its digests and its generated catalogue without the participant data, and a reviewer can inspect whether a relevant coordinate appears among the enumerated alternatives and can declare a further alternative for a specific concern.

Applied across a larger corpus, in the manner of earlier audits of published applications (MacCallum et al., 1993; Ropovik, 2015), the same catalogue rule could summarize the share of enumerated alternatives with high detection in a body of research, and the ten designs of the present corpus illustrate the quantities that such a study would need, including the size of the rule and the admissibility status of each entry.

7.3 Structural alternatives and causal structure

The catalogue encodes directed paths and residual covariances as statistical terms of a model. A directed path can receive a causal interpretation when the design and its assumptions support that interpretation (Rohrer, 2018), whereas a residual covariance can reflect several sources of shared variation, and the map evaluates whether the implied covariance structures can be distinguished under a specified rule of fit. A positive F M may yield low detection in a finite sample, while an exact equivalence persists at every sample size.

In every corpus design with latent variables, all admissible directed paths and latent covariances are certified equivalent to the fitted model, 177 entries in all. For these designs covariance fit leaves direction among the latent variables unresolved at any sample size, and a directional reading rests entirely on assumptions about the processes the paths represent (Grosz, Rohrer, & Thoemmes, 2020; Rohrer, 2018).

An analogous sensitivity analysis for causal-discovery graphs would require a different detection event, such as the recovery of a specified edge, whereas the present estimand is the probability of rejecting a fitted covariance model under a stated alternative.

7.4 Limitations

The analytic map applies to covariance-only fits with continuous indicators and normal-theory maximum likelihood, in one group or in several. Other fits are refused by the software instead of being mapped (Section 3.2). Robust test statistics (Satorra & Bentler, 2001) and ordinal indicators (Muthén, 1984) would need reference distributions of their own. A model with a constrained mean structure, such as one that holds intercepts equal across groups in the scalar step of an invariance test (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016), would need a map of the means as well as of the covariances, which is why the invariance alternatives of the present catalogue stop at the loadings.

The analytic path and the default generator describe multivariate normal data with the fitted covariance, whereas nonnormal data inflate the normal-theory statistic of a correctly specified model (Curran et al., 1996). Where the raw data are distributed, the bootstrap generator measures the size of each rule under the distribution of the data, and for the six-point Big Five items it raises the measured size of the likelihood-ratio test from .058 to .266 (Section 5.3), whereas the six designs that are available only as covariance matrices can be calibrated under normality alone. The bootstrap treats the observed sample as the population, with the consequence that its measured size describes data distributed like the sample at hand and carries the sampling error of that sample.

Detection probabilities are comparable within a family under the stated standardized-magnitude convention. Across families equal nominal magnitudes are standardized on different scales (Appendix A) and change different numbers of covariance elements, as Section 5.4 shows. The population RMSEA (Browne & Cudeck, 1992) reported with every detectable alternative places all families on one scale of misfit, although that scale is itself insensitive to some omitted cross-loadings and to clusters of correlated residuals (Savalei, 2012), and ordering the alternatives by it would produce a different ranking that answers a different substantive question.

The analytic path rests on an asymptotic reference distribution whose finite-sample error the map measures instead of assuming, and in three of the ten designs the measured size of the likelihood-ratio test exceeds .05 by more than three Monte Carlo standard errors, so that there the simulated values are the ones to read. Alternatives with an improper minimizer receive only a simulated detection probability.

Each enumerated alternative perturbs a single coordinate, and a misspecification that combines several coordinates, such as a pattern of correlated residuals from a common method (Podsakoff et al., 2003), enters the map only when an analyst declares it, as the method block of Section 5.6 illustrates. The map is therefore exhaustive within its single-coordinate families and nowhere else. The ten corpus designs are canonical and redistributable and were not sampled from published research, which means that they show what a map reports and support no estimate of how common blind spots are in the literature.

Catalogue size grows with the square of the number of indicators, and the seventeen-variable design produces 597 alternatives. The ordered profile in fig. 2 condenses the distribution of the detection probabilities, although a large catalogue still requires a substantive selection of the alternatives that matter for interpretation.

Appendix B Pooled invariance is covariance-equivalent

A model fitted to the pooled sample cannot represent a loading that differs between groups, so the catalogue of a single-group fit excludes such alternatives and lists them among its exclusions, whereas the multi-group catalogue of Section 2.2 contains them.
