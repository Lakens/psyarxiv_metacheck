Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Clinical psychological science has contributed a range of methodological and treatment innovations that have improved human well-being. At the same time, our field can play a larger role in studying and addressing a range of societal problems and, crucially, in training future clinical scientists in this work. Applying the case example of firearm violence research, we describe ways to expand concentrations and training in "public" clinical psychological science to increase support for social problems research and its reach to impacted communities. We outline ways in which clinical science is well-positioned to advance such research and highlight potential reforms needed to create a sustainable pipeline of investigators interested in public health and clinical psychological science. The article presents an integrated agenda for building interdisciplinary collaborations, embedding cultural and policy competence into training, and ensuring sustained mentorship in the study and prevention of social problems, including firearm violence.

Introduction

The field of clinical psychological science stands at a crossroads between traditional psychological science and the urgent social realities shaping human health. For decades, psychological scientists have made calls to more closely align our science to the goal of helping ameliorate social problems (Chavis et al., 1983; Miller, 1969), arguing that "the integrity of our scholarship must depend upon its being set in the real world of everyday experience…" (Bevan, 1991, p. 475). For the field of clinical psychology in particular, a reckoning of sorts has come from the recognition that despite advances in our knowledge of psychological disorders and the development of efficacious treatments for a slew of psychological problems, the mental health burden in the US has actually gotten worse (Berenbaum et al., 2021; Insel, 2022). One reason for this gap likely reflects this decades-long concern that we are not exerting enough effort to address urgent social problems and certainly not uniformly training the new generation of clinical psychological scientists to do so.

Our call in this article is consistent with more recent movements in clinical psychological science that emphasize a broader focus on dissemination and implementation science (Shoham et al., 2014) and new approaches to delivering mental health services (Kazdin & Blase, 2011).

These movements acknowledge that a change in approach is needed to increase our potential to reduce the disease burden that decades of clinical psychological research have not overcome. At the same time, because the mental health disease burden does not stand in a vacuum and is embedded within social and political structures, this call goes beyond advocating for novel approaches to expand access to treatments. It advocates for greater investments in "public" scholarship within clinical psychological science. As Eaton and colleagues (2021, p. 1212) noted, "to aspire toward public psychology, psychologists will need to enter into community and public spaces and embrace their roles as public agents …. and to share research and practice with the public as experts and equals."

Consistent with this, we define public clinical psychological science as the creation of high-quality knowledge directly aimed at both helping to ameliorate a range of problems society faces and working in close collaboration with relevant communities and decision makers to do so (see calls for public scholarship in related fields; Bridger & Alter, 2010; Miller, 1969; Uggen & Inderbitzin, 2010). Not all clinical psychological scientists need be involved in public scholarship, with many focusing on basic science questions around the nature of psychopathology and putative causal mechanisms or environmental influences, for example.

Nonetheless, the ability to make more immediate impacts on urgent social problems increases with greater translation into community and policy realms. As such, clinical psychological science training should expose students to a public clinical science approach and collaborationbuilding skills to facilitate the translation of research findings to the community and policymakers.

In the present paper, we highlight potential opportunities, innovations in graduate training, and field-wide reforms needed to overcome existing barriers that can move us closer to a public clinical psychological science. We leverage the case example of firearm violence prevention, a multidisciplinary area in which psychology and clinical psychological science have a growing presence, although much of the firearm violence field's research infrastructure has been built within public health and medicine with collaborations in criminology, law, and social work (Pirelli et al., 2020; Schmidt et al., 2022). This is despite the fact that firearm violence relates to key constructs and behaviors studied by psychologists (e.g., community violence, intimate partner violence, suicide; Nickerson et al., 2020; Thomson et al., 2025; Turner & Leyens, 2019), and that the development, evaluation, and increasingly the broad implementation of evidence-based psychological interventions are central strengths of clinical psychological science (Barlow et al., 2018; Beidas et al., 2025). Indeed, the field of firearm research has benefitted greatly from the growing engagement of clinical scientists, particularly in regard to firearm suicide prevention, who are studying effects of firearm access on suicide, lethal means safety interventions, and the communication of suicide prevention science to the public and policymakers (Anestis et al., 2021; Anestis & Daruwala, 2021; Bond et al., 2024; Stanley et al., 2020; Thomson et al., 2025). Examples and lessons learned from the study and prevention of firearm violence can provide a template that the field of clinical psychological science, training programs, and even individual research teams can apply as they shift toward addressing a range of societal problems that benefit from the greater engagement of clinical psychological scientists.

Extant Psychological Science as a Guide

The history of psychological science has many examples of its direct impact on understanding and informing potential solutions for social problems. In the 1940s, Mamie and Kenneth Clark conducted developmental research that spoke directly to the effects of racial segregation on the wellbeing of Black children (Clark & Clark, 1947), evidence that was used in Brown v. Board of Education (1954) to challenge school racial segregation. About a decade later, the field of community psychology was formed with the goal of formally centering context to study and create social change at a system or organizational level (Trickett, 2009). Interestingly, community psychology grew into its own subfield after splintering from clinical psychology, which was criticized for its too frequent reliance on individual-level explanations of human suffering and experienced as overly-pathologizing of individuals' efforts to navigate unjust environments.

Despite justified critiques of clinical psychological science, important socially relevant research has been conducted and continues to be conducted by clinical psychology researchers.

Clinical psychological scientists are addressing a major societal problem, the burden of mental illness, by studying risk and protective factors, the development of psychopathology, and treatment approaches and their implementation (Berenbaum et al., 2021). Many are also engaging with other social problems that intersect with mental health, such as the plight of migrant children (e.g., Patel-Syed et al., 2025), structural racism and health disparities (e.g., Neblett, 2019), sexual reproductive health policy (e.g., Tabachnick et al., 2026), poverty (e.g., Hwang & Kong, 2024; Wadsworth, 2012), inequities in the criminal-legal system (e.g., Johnson et al., 2025; McCarthy et al., 2026), and community violence (e.g., Gresham et al., 2025).

Despite this, the presence of clinical psychological science remains too limited in scale, visibility, and institutional infrastructure relative to the scope of the social problems we face and the relatively more established presence of adjacent fields.

In this article, we argue that the modal training approaches in our field fail to adequately center social impact and application as an immediate goal of research, and there is infrequent reference to public scholarship in clinical psychology. For the sake of the public good and to ensure the continued relevance of clinical psychological science, training should be expanded to emphasize 1) the study and prevention of a range of social problems; 2) contextual, systemic, and cultural influences (e.g., Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2024); and 3) community-engaged and policy-relevant collaborations (Giusto et al., 2024). Indeed, Bronfenbrenner's (1979) Social Ecological Theory highlights how behavior (e.g., psychopathology, functioning) is the product of nested, interacting systems ranging from individual characteristics to broader sociopolitical structures. These systems include the microsystem (e.g., family, peers), mesosystem (e.g., interactions between school and neighborhood), exosystem (e.g., local governments, media), and macrosystem (e.g., policies, cultural norms). The ecological model is useful for organizing multilevel investigations of risk factors and interventions (see Verona & Fox, 2025, for its application to the study of antisocial behavior and incarceration risk). In the area of firearm violence, for example, it is important to take the nested systems into account for ensuring that major influences and points of intervention are addressed together, including at the levels of the individual (e.g., emotion regulation), family and peers (e.g., parenting practices, peer mentorship), and community (e.g., disorganization, neighborhood green space, and youth-led civic engagement) (Sadatsafavi et al., 2022; Sullivan et al., 2025).

Beyond these proximal ecological levels, the work of clinical psychological scientists needs to increasingly address exosystem and macrosystem influences. Firearm violence prevention scholars, for example, recognize the economic and racial segregation policies that have maintained concentrated poverty (Benns et al., 2020; Kim, 2019) and this country's cultural norms around gun access and use (Lemieux, 2014) to understand the problem and inform prevention. The latter are areas that research in clinical psychological science is less likely to incorporate and consider, but to which trainees can be exposed to better position the next generation to address broader social problems (e.g., Anestis et al., 2021).

Clinical Psychological Science's Position of Strength

We contend that the reach of clinical science can be expanded through channeling our key strengths to help embed us in the spaces where our research can do the most good. The field can readily draw on its methodological rigor and grounding in clinical practice to collaborate with public health, medicine, law, education, and community partners on important societal issues. Clinical psychological science's long-held expertise in advanced data analyses and research design can be leveraged to mine large datasets of panel and administrative data (e.g. shootings dashboard, policy enactment), synthesize results to inform hypotheses about psychological processes that influence individual behavior (e.g., carrying of firearms, retribution mindsets), and implement sophisticated evaluations of prevention and intervention strategies at individual, family, and community levels. Clinical psychology's strengths in assessment could also be leveraged to improve on and develop psychometrically-sound instruments to assess processes that help maintain or ameliorate social problems (e.g., attitude change, decisionmaking, public perceptions of policy).

Science communication is another area in which clinical psychological scientists can make important contributions. Borrowing from public criminology, the public clinical psychological scientist must come equipped with "peer-reviewed evidence, clear points, and plain language" (Uggen & Inderbitzin, 2010, p. 730). In the area of firearm violence, for example, rhetoric on the causes of violence often comes with substantial misinformation, particularly the confounding of mental illness with dangerousness (Anestis & Daruwala, 2021; Varshney et al., 2016). Clinical psychological scientists are well-positioned to speak on mental health determinants of behavior and the pitfalls of predicting low base rate behaviors, as well as to cite evidence showing that those with severe mental health problems make up a small proportion of perpetrators of violent crime (Ciak & Cuddeback, 2025). At the same time, effective science communication depends on who delivers it. In the area of firearm suicide prevention, research suggests that health professionals, including physicians and related clinical providers, are often not viewed as the most credible messengers on safe firearm storage, whereas law enforcement personnel, current military personnel, and veterans are often preferred sources (Anestis et al., 2021). Thus, clinical psychological scientists should not assume that they will always be the most persuasive public voice. Instead, they may often have their greatest impact by helping to develop, test, and refine evidence-based messages while partnering with trusted community messengers who are more likely to be heard by the intended audience.

Communication is also more likely to succeed when messages are delivered in plain language, reflect the values and concerns of the intended audience, and avoid a moralizing or overly prescriptive tone.

A unique strength of clinical psychological science is the ability to both generate scientific knowledge and translate that knowledge into prevention and intervention strategies.

Clinical psychologists are often trained and licensed to assess and intervene on conditions directly tied to major social problems, including trauma, suicidality, and interpersonal conflict.

Along with that practical expertise, they also bring expertise in assessment, treatment development, and outcome evaluation, which are critical for identifying recipients of care, refining intervention strategies, and evaluating the effectiveness of prevention efforts. These dual competencies position clinical psychological scientists to serve as key translational partners in community and health system responses to social problems, highlighting an important opportunity for doctoral clinical psychologists. As master's-level clinicians play an increasingly central role in delivering mental health services, the distinctive contribution of doctoral clinical psychological scientists is not simply the provision of direct care, but the integration of science, assessment, intervention, and translation.

As such, advancements in clinical science training can help position doctoral clinical psychologists to contribute leadership in dissemination and implementation, science communication, prevention research, and multidisciplinary collaboration aimed at addressing complex social problems. The prevention of firearm suicide illustrates this point. Clinical psychologists are already trained to conduct suicide risk assessments that incorporate both static and dynamic factors and to develop individualized, collaborative safety plans with clients.

Evidence suggests that safety planning approaches that incorporate firearm-specific counseling can improve safety-related behaviors and support treatment engagement (Stanley et al., 2020). In this context, clinical psychological scientists interested in reducing firearm mortality can be involved in developing, adapting, and evaluating firearm safety planning approaches in health care and community settings.

A persistent challenge is that interventions and prevention programs that demonstrate effectiveness often fail to reach affected communities. Dissemination and implementation science is a growing area in clinical psychological science and offers a framework for addressing this gap by examining not only whether interventions work but also how they can be adapted, sustained, and scaled in diverse contexts (Beidas et al., 2025). Clinical psychologists can play a central role in concentrated initiatives that apply dissemination and implementation principles to addressing various social problems. In the area of firearm violence, clinical psychological scientists have been serving as part of larger teams of researchers and practitioners in hospitalbased violence intervention programs (HVIPs), where their support is shown to disrupt cycles of reinjury and retaliation (Aboutanos et al., 2025; Cunningham et al., 2023). Similarly, clinical psychological strategies targeting individuals and families have been shown to complement place-based strategies for preventing community violence, such as neighborhood greening and vacant lot remediation (Branas et al., 2018; Kondo et al., 2018). Importantly, clinical psychological scientists would be able to inform on the research-practice gaps that need to be addressed to improve uptake of evidence-based strategies in multiple settings. These examples highlight clinical psychological science's advantage in embedding theory-driven research and interventions within team-based, multilevel systems.

The Need for Expanded Training Models

As the above illustrates, clinical psychologists have the competencies, presence, and collaborative orientation to make social problem prevention efforts more effective and In practice, this would first mean graduate programs building training opportunities through formal partnerships among psychology, medicine, public health, criminology, public policy, and other programs so that trainees develop shared analytic frameworks and fluency in cross-sector collaboration. For example, a trainee can be co-mentored by faculty from psychology and faculty from a different discipline, such as public health, medicine, or criminology. In firearm violence research, such collaborations can include joint projects with engineers to design and evaluate firearm safety technologies, or with policy scholars to study how regulations affect risk behaviors and injury outcomes. This exchange breaks the echo chamber of discipline-specific questions and answers, fostering innovation that neither psychology nor any single field could achieve alone. Such experiences represent the on-theground application of Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems model, allowing students to see how psychological processes both shape and are shaped by the social, environmental, and policy contexts in which social problems occur.

A second component of a changing training landscape would extend this collaborative approach beyond academic partnerships to real-world settings, grounded in the lived realities of communities most affected by social harms. Most graduate programs in clinical psychological science already offer field placements and co-mentorships in applied settings, at least for clinical training. These placements can and should be expanded to enable trainees to frame research questions in collaboration with practitioners and residents rather than from detached academic perspectives. For example, a trainee interested in firearm violence might help a hospital-based violence intervention program track reinjury outcomes, work with a school district to evaluate a prevention initiative, or partner with a community organization to refine how program materials are delivered and understood. This approach reflects the argument that the most effective applied research emerges when institutional inquiry aligns with the priorities and values of those directly impacted (Mears, 2010). To achieve sustainable prevention, researchers and practitioners must build long-term relationships grounded in mutual accountability and shared ownership (Guillot-Wright & Temple, 2025). Furthermore, because many on-the-ground practitioners and professionals (e.g., case workers, law enforcement) may read academic publications infrequently or lack access to them, translational efforts that target information sharing (e.g., training) are essential (Ashby, 2020; Telep, 2024).

Diversified pipeline and embedded cultural competence.

The majority of social problems disproportionately affect marginalized communities (Armstead et al., 2021; Benns et al., 2020), making cultural competence, equity, and structural literacy indispensable components of psychological training. For example, firearm violence research trainees must learn to recognize how systemic racism, residential segregation, and economic inequality interact with individual risk factors to perpetuate cycles of harm (Jacoby et al., 2018). However, developing equitable prevention science requires more than cultural understanding; it requires structural inclusion.

Students and early-career scientists from the most affected communities bring essential lived experience and contextual knowledge that can deepen the relevance, ethics, and impact of research. Yet these trainees often face disproportionate barriers to entering and thriving in academic and research careers, including financial hardship, limited mentorship networks, and institutional climates that undervalue community-informed expertise (Ramakrishnan et al., 2025; Walsh et al., 2025). Diversifying the pipeline is not only imperative but also scientifically warranted: diverse research teams are more innovative and better equipped to produce socially responsive solutions to complex public health challenges (Hofstra et al., 2020). To achieve equity, programs must intentionally recruit, mentor, and support trainees from underrepresented backgrounds. This includes providing paid research opportunities and institutional mechanisms that treat lived experience as a source of scientific expertise. There have been various efforts by institutions and programs in recent years to enlarge support and mentoring to early-career and underrepresented trainees, including fellowships in firearm violence offered by the University of Michigan and the New Jersey Gun Violence Research Center and the "diversifying clinical science" recruitment events hosted by top psychology graduate programs in the country.

Integrating equity into clinical psychological research training ensures that prevention science is shaped not only by those who study it but also by those who have lived and understand it.

Faculty and mentors should be especially mindful to avoid divisive or moralizing language that may alienate trainees or colleagues with differing perspectives (Honeycutt & Jussim, 2023). Mentorship practices set the tone for inclusivity in research, and subtle cues of ideological bias can discourage open dialogue and narrow the diversity of thought essential to scientific progress (Honeycutt & Freberg, 2017). In firearm violence research, for example, mentors should prepare students to engage respectfully with firearm-owning populations, who are often underrepresented in research yet central to prevention success. They have an inordinate influence on public opinion and the framing of messages regarding firearm regulations. In an increasingly polarized political environment, cultural competence must extend beyond marginalized communities to include firearm owners who may perceive research as a threat to ownership or constitutional rights (Boine et al., 2022). These groups are not mutually exclusive. National data show that roughly one in four Black and Hispanic adults in the United States own a firearm, and rates of firearm ownership have increased among lower-income households (Brown et al., 2017), and among women (Miller et al., 2022). These patterns challenge the stereotype that firearm ownership is confined to White men or rural conservative populations and underscore more generally the importance of engaging with impacted communities in a way that centers their experiences over our own agendas.

Policy translation and advocacy skills.

Clinical psychological scientists have expertise that position them to help connect scientific evidence with public policy, yet most receive little formal training in policy fluency or in translating evidence for decision-makers as part of their graduate education. We recognize that these are substantial areas of training in their own right, such that clinical psychology programs will not necessarily have the bandwidth or faculty expertise to prepare all trainees to become policy specialists. Rather, graduate training should provide foundational exposure to how legislation shapes behavior and prevention capacity (Zeoli & Webster, 2019), with the goal of promoting collaborative fluency, not full specialization in policy, media, or implementation domains. This type of initial training can strengthen multidisciplinary collaboration by helping trainees understand how clinical psychological science interfaces with broader systems and decision-making contexts. For example, studies of firearm risk-based laws have shown that their effectiveness depends on careful implementation, public understanding, and the integration of behavioral insights into program design and evaluation (Studdert et al., 2020; Zeoli & Webster, 2019). Clinical psychologists can effectively work on these issues by assessing the behavioral determinants of compliance and public trust, as well as by identifying mechanisms that enhance policy uptake and reduce unintended consequences.

Clinical psychology graduate training for those interested in social problems research should also include strategies for engaging policymakers and journalists and crafting public messages that convey complex data in clear, value-neutral terms. Guillot-Wright and Temple (2025) have shown that scientific evidence is most likely to influence policy when researchers establish mutual trust, communicate clearly; and align their recommendations with policymakers' practical and moral frameworks, highlighting shared goals such as safety, family stability, and community well-being. Training on evidence-based strategies for communicating research findings in ways that are accessible and useful to policymakers would enhance the work of clinical psychological scientists and prepare them to navigate politicized environments without compromising scientific integrity (Bond et al., 2024). Importantly, we believe that policy translation is a core function of applied science, through which research findings inform policymakers' evidence-based decisions and prevention priorities. A trainee and researcher trained in policy analysis and communication can thus extend the reach of clinical psychological science from academic research into legislative, public health, and community decision-making arenas, fulfilling psychology's ethical commitment to use science in service of the public good. In the face of these structural constraints, clinical psychology must take a proactive role in maintaining research continuity. Departments and training programs can help coordinate research consortia that share data, mentorship, and analytic infrastructure across academic units and institutions, enabling progress to continue even when federal support falters. Applying for training grants and lobbying for state budget dollars are needed to fund such consortia and expand them into state-level research centers, offsetting federal reductions. As mentioned, embedding trainees as interns within community organizations, health systems, or school districts allows them to conduct pragmatic evaluations and generate data for theses or dissertations that directly inform local policy. These embedded research experiences also help students and postdoctoral fellows build professional networks that extend beyond academia, increasing the likelihood that their skills will be retained regardless of funding fluctuations.

The Need for Field-Wide Reforms

Beyond what individual programs or research teams can do to enhance training opportunities, collaborations, and community-engaged work, academic psychology as a field and institutions of higher learning must also undertake reforms to support more social problems research. The academic incentives currently in place not only fail to support research and training in public scholarship, but may actually discourage early career investigators and students from undertaking it. Our own observations are that many clinical psychology students often enter graduate school with an interest in improving society but can often feel discouraged to pursue such work due to what is perceived as competing priorities and incentives. Some academic psychologists may fall for the false dichotomy that rigor (too often defined as quantitative and/or biological research) and societal relevance cannot co-occur. This positioning is reinforced by the often-dismaying perspectives of our natural science colleagues or even the lay public that situate psychology as a "soft" or "not real" science. The research of clinical psychological scientists is even more likely to be scrutinized in this way, given that it is uniformly considered "applied research". We argue that this field-wide insecurity may overly-determine the priorities and training models we have chosen to pursue.

Field barriers.

For decades, the field's seeming reaction to these perceptions has been to double down on scholarship that prioritizes biobehavioral mechanisms as putative causes of behavior and to espouse perspectives that prioritize biological constructs as causal to psychological or social phenomena (cf. Miller, 2010). Although biobehavioral research has expanded understandings of psychopathology processes, and can indeed inform social problems research (see Verona et al., 2023, in the area of firearm violence), this type of research tends to prioritize internal validity with less consideration of how the research reflects the larger context in which psychological phenomena occur (Lin et al., 2021). The critique is not with the methods or contributions of mechanism research, per se, but with the field's apparent over-valuing of certain types of knowledge and methods at the expense of other types. This prioritization has often translated into diverting material and training resources away from research directly applicable to social problems. For example, the increasing demands for external funding upon tenure and promotion in academic departments of psychology often dictate that research programs be guided by what is "fundable." The apparent prioritization of biologically-based research by funding agencies like the NIH for several decades (Lilienfeld, 2014; Teachman et al., 2019) may have served to limit funded research on social and contextual factors, although that can be debated (Pacheco et al., 2022).

Beyond funding, the academic incentive structures for purposes of tenure, promotion, and recognition in the field primarily reward publishing a large number of peer-reviewed articles per year in field-specific journals, which can work to discourage research agendas that would have direct impacts on society. The push to publish several empirical papers a year can compel many researchers to rely on convenience samples or laboratory proxies to reduce the time lag between research idea and publication. This can undermine external validity and opportunities for immersion in community-based projects. In the area of firearm violence in particular, the low base rates (among convenience samples) and contextual occurrence (community-integrated) of gun violence do not align well with these methodological choices. Community-engaged work is often incredibly time-consuming, as one must first develop trusting partnerships with agencies and community members, even before funding can be secured; and those partnerships need to be entered into with good faith and a long-term commitment to the community as a way of preventing skepticism and feelings of betrayal when beneficial resources are removed after the project ends. This type of investment may mean that research products take longer to move from idea generation to data collection to publication, but the faculty engaging with communities and larger systems are evaluated under tenure and promotion guidelines that fail to appreciate this complexity or the important impact such work can have on academic-community partnerships and university reputations.

The dominant norms in psychology also fail to encourage greater interdisciplinarity.

Promotion and tenure committees often expect investigators to publish their work in the recognized journals in their field and show independence, increasing the level of insularity of psychology vis-à-vis other sciences. Because interdisciplinary efforts are vital to addressing complex social problems that are multi-determined, current practices risk different fields of study duplicating efforts and hinder the creation of robust teams of researchers and practitioners that tackle different aspects of the problem. Academic incentives to publish in journals that are geared toward other academics also make the research much less accessible to the public (due to both technical language and monetary costs to access the journals). As such, there is less opportunity to "give away" (cf. Miller, 1969 Miller, , p. 1071) ) clinical psychological science to the communities and constituents that could most use it, including disseminating research to policymakers and practitioners who implement programs or enact policies (Laub, 2012). In the area of firearm violence, for example, rigorous research that speaks to the effectiveness of regulations that limit access to firearms is less likely to translate into policy or practice change unless collaborations are forged with decision makers and the science can directly speak to the interests of the public (Guillot-Wright & Temple, 2025).

Potential reforms.

Balancing these historical trends will require challenging academic cultures and incentive structures, including norms on the types of research that are valued and how productivity is rewarded in science. We must address the persistent biases embedded in much of psychological academic culture, and that many of us were implicitly trained to believe, which overwhelmingly value quantitative research methods, randomized trials, and mechanism studies, at least relative to other forms of knowledge acquisition such as qualitative, mixed methods, or community-based participatory research. Despite perceptions in some quarters that community-engaged or qualitative research is not rigorous or "scientific" enough, the fact is that conducting that type of work requires high methodological standards, creativity and innovation, and adherence to the highest ethical imperatives.

Fortunately, a cultural shift has already started to occur within clinical psychological science, if not in practice at least in theory, from which we can build momentum. There have been recent calls to expand incentives for and training on research methods and topics that are more directly applicable to working toward solutions of social problems as they exist in the real world (e.g., Giusto et al., 2024; Rodriguez-Seijas et al., 2024). These initiatives would include regularly introducing qualitative, mixed methods, and community-based participatory designs into core curricula (e.g., Fitzpatrick et al., 2025) alongside quantitative research methods and statistics courses to help establish these tools as vital to the work of clinical scientists. Further, if a greater number of academic mentors are seen to be expanding research repertoires to include field studies (e.g., including RCTs, Ehrenreich-May et al., 2025; MacKenzie, 2012; Wolff, 2000) and community-based research (Collins et al., 2018), trainees will have trusted models from which they can build innovative public scholarship programs, with confidence that their work will be well-received by other clinical scientists.

Reforms would also have to occur at the level of institutional evaluations of research impact. Although publications and citations in scholarly journals represent an important aspect of science communication, the field can institute other ways to evaluate contributions and achievements. That is, traditional metrics used in evaluating productivity and making decisions about tenure and promotion can be complemented by newer metrics of social impact that are growing in acceptance in various fields. A recent special issue in the Journal of Participatory Research Methods recommended ways in which tenure and promotion guidelines can accommodate valid evaluations of research portfolios focused on the "scholarship of engagement" (Worthen et al., 2025). Recognized achievements growing from community relationships would include public dissemination of original work, entrepreneurship, and evidence that one's work led to significant changes in evidence-based practice or administration.

Teaching and learning scholars have introduced an "article utility" metric (Medina & Draugalis, 2019) that helps to quantify the extent to which research publications were used by applied researchers or practitioners to design evaluations, develop assessment tools, or refine practices, even when such users do not publish their applications in the scholarly literature.

Demonstrations of broader impact can also include mentions in press releases, newspaper articles, or political debates that stem from academic work (Fabry & Fischer, 2017), as well the dissemination of guideline or policy documents, patents, and socially-useful intellectual property (e.g., assessment instruments, treatment protocols; Bouter, 2010). Bond and colleagues (2024) suggested recognizing efforts in communicating science (e.g., webinars, press releases, published editorials) as part of faculty evaluations of research, service, and teaching contributions. Moving beyond the current metrics of faculty success would help incentivize programs of research with more social impact.

Conclusions and Moving Forward

As we have noted throughout this article, clinical psychological scientists are desperately needed and well-equipped to contribute to social problems research at higher levels. At the same time, playing a larger role in studying and helping to ameliorate social problems will require engaging more directly with impacted communities and informing policy. This call to action hopefully provides a blueprint, summarized in Figure 1, for areas in which clinical science can increase efforts, reprioritize, and invest to enhance training, interdisciplinary collaboration, and leadership development.

Our call for a public clinical psychological science is necessarily expansive, broadening our "lane" of scholarly inquiry relative to the current norm. That can be intimidating. The social problems we want to tackle are indeed massive and complex, so they require exposure to other literatures and approaches that are not common in our field, or that may stretch our current capabilities. Weick (1984) described the potential for psychological scientists to become overwhelmed by the scale of social problems, seemingly beyond the scope of our science and skill sets. Similar statements can be made about the mental health and psychopathology constructs that we try to study in the lab. In both cases, scalability of the problem is necessary. Weick (1984) promoted the idea of "small wins", wherein we can frame large social problems into more manageable parts and tackle those in turn. For example, rather than approaching firearm violence as a single undifferentiated problem, clinical psychological scientists can focus on a more specific and tractable pathway, such as suicide risk among individuals with access to firearms. A substantial body of work shows that access to firearms heightens the lethality of suicidal crises, making safe storage, temporary reduction of access during high-risk periods, and lethal means counseling important prevention targets (Studdert et al., 2020; Swanson et al., 2021). In response, many researchers and clinicians have worked to develop, evaluate, and implement interventions that promote safer firearm storage and incorporate lethal means counseling into health care and community settings (Bond et al., 2024; Stanley et al., 2020). These efforts do not solve firearm violence in its entirety, but they represent concrete, evidenceinformed points of intervention through which clinical psychological science can make meaningful and scalable contributions. Above all, building innovative training infrastructures and expanding the pipeline of new talent is critical. Without developing the expertise of the next generation of clinical scientists, efforts to advance social problems research within clinical psychology will be too slow. The effectiveness and/or feasibility of the training expansion model we articulated above will require iterative working through as well as formal evaluations of effectiveness. Drawing on implementation science frameworks, evaluations of new training models should measure the adoption, fidelity, and sustainability of training across research and community contexts (Proctor et al., 2011). These measures can be complemented by indicators of mentorship quality, trainee independence, equity in access to training resources, and postdoctoral placement in preventionrelated roles (Beidas et al., 2016). While not exhaustive, these metrics provide a structured and empirically supported means of determining whether psychology's training programs are preparing researchers capable of advancing both science and the public good. Comprehensive evaluation also requires ongoing feedback loops between trainees, mentors, and even the community partners involved in the work. This participatory approach in the evaluation of training initiatives in public clinical psycholological science not only strengthens accountability between mentors, trainees, and the communities they serve but also provides an early model of the collaborative structures necessary for sustaining community-engaged work over time.

The training plan must include exposure to the science of not only behavior change but also systems and organizational change (Beidas et al., 2025). As such, multidisciplinary training contexts are critical for clinical psychology students to gain the necessary exposure to methods and positionalities that will prepare them to advance the field. This requires more senior scientists in clinical psychological science developing collaborative partnerships across research fields and the community, working as a team to tackle the same problem in different and intersecting ways. Our own professional experiences serve to motivate this call. The second author (NT) is a clinical forensic research psychologist working within a department of surgery and running several large research grants in collaboration with violence prevention practitioners, community organizations and members, survivors of violence, and scholars in injury prevention, emergency medicine, surgery, counseling, engineering, game design, and social work, who all contribute to hospital-and community-based violence prevention efforts. The first author (EV) is a clinical psychologist who co-directs a multidisciplinary research center on the practices and policies of the criminal justice system, collaborating with scholars and practitioners across criminology and criminal justice, public health, sociology, social work, and public administration to apply research findings with the goals of reforming and improving the lives of those touched by multiple systems. There are many other examples of this type of team-science and multidisciplinary approach that we hope will become more common, creating many more opportunities for training in the science of social problems within clinical psychological science graduate or postdoctoral programs. Further efforts need to begin now, led by those well positioned to improve training infrastructure and practices in clinical psychological science.

infrastructure and mentored spaces. Graduate programs can encourage trainees interested in pursuing dedicated work in social problems or public scholarship to align their training with the National Institutes of Health's (NIH) structured funding mechanisms that support investigator development from graduate study through senior faculty. These mechanisms provide students more time and resources to pursue training experiences that are less commonly offered in clinical psychology graduate programs. At the predoctoral level, the F31 individual NRSA fellowship supports dissertation-stage research with more dedicated time for the crossdisciplinary knowledge acquisition that is critical for social problems research, including coursework in epidemiology, policy analysis, data science, and community-engaged methods. Postdoctoral researchers can compete for F32 fellowships or participate in institutional T32 programs, and early career investigators can pursue K-level career development funding mechanisms, that provide opportunities for multidisciplinary mentored research and formal didactics held by other disciplines like medical or public health departments. Clinical psychological science fellowship training plans can include the adept integration of research and applied experiences to help them answer the deep research questions that are needed to address gaps in knowledge. Across all stages, strong mentorship is the central feature of this developmental process. An emphasis on mentorship underscores why senior-level career-enhancement funding mechanisms, such as the NIH K18, are essential. Established investigators seeking to pivot into the study of social problems or to acquire new methodological or policy expertise can apply for the K18 mechanism to provide short-term, mentored career enhancement. Even this mechanism is not enough, as it helps fund retraining efforts for only few months at a time. Without opportunities for experienced investigators to retrain or pivot into these areas, the pipeline narrows, creating a bottleneck in the availability of qualified mentors. More opportunities like expanded forms of the K18 would help prevent this constraint by providing established faculty with the resources and time to acquire new methodological, policy, or community-based skills, thereby expanding institutional capacity to support pre-doctoral and post-doctoral level trainees. At this point in history, however, sustaining the training pipeline will require addressing the fragility of the national infrastructure and cuts in federal funding. The closure of national coordinating offices, reductions in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) capacity, and cancellations of NIH and National Science Foundation (NSF) awards have left research and mentorship networks vulnerable to disruption. Other areas in clinical science can take lessons from those engaging in the study and prevention of firearm violence, where the funding situation has been dire historically. The passage of the Dickey amendment in the 1996 appropriations bill (Omnibus Consolidated Appropriations Act of 1997, 1996) severely restricted federal funding related to firearm violence prevention for decades. This funding landscape likely discouraged whole generations of scholars from addressing firearm violence in their research, resulting in very few academic psychologists available to mentor new generations of scientists interested in tackling this issue. 1 Many investigators committed to the study and prevention of firearm violence often maintain their research programs through innovative collaborations, local institutional investments, and shoe-string budgets. The current administration's Dickey-like bans on diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives may likewise serve to discourage clinical scientists from pursuing research on a range of other social issues, for fear that these projects may be interpreted by funding agencies as advocating for DEI. These political impositions on research funding, unfortunately, serve as a meta-example of how political and societal structures emphasized by Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory can shape individual outcomes by restricting the research enterprise itself.
