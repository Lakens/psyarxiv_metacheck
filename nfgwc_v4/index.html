Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Abstract

Moral outrage spreads widely in online political discourse, but how do people understand others' outrage expressions? Across three studies (N = 1,855), including behavioral experiments and a field study, we discovered that partisan-biased motive inferences underlie the divergent effects of online outrage expression: While people typically inferred that their in-partisans' motives for expressing outrage were to raise awareness or inspire action, they tended to infer their political opponents' motives were to shame or troll.

These motive inferences were linked to greater political sectarianism and lower willingness to have a conversation about politics with the outrage expressor. In a fourth study (N = 720), we developed an intervention that changed people's motive inferences, which improved willingness to have a conversation about politics. These findings highlight the role of motive inferences in shaping contemporary online political discourse filled with strong emotions, and suggest key levers to foster civil interactions among cross-partisans.

Biased Motive Inferences of Online Outrage Undermine Cross-Partisan Conversations

Moral outrage-morally charged expression of anger and condemnation (henceforth: outrage)-spreads widely in online political discourse 1, 2 . Emerging research compellingly explains the viral spread of outrage as an interaction between human attention biases and the engagement-maximizing incentives of online platforms, whose algorithms amplify the visibility of outrage because of its attention-grabbing quality 3, 4.

Given that people increasingly rely on social media to learn about politics 5, 6 , it is important to understand not only why outrage spreads but also how people react to outrage.

On the one hand, outrage expression can raise awareness on important social issues and mobilize collective actions, particularly among in-partisans [7] [8] [9] . On the other hand, the same expression can lead to rising polarization across partisan lines 1, 3, 10 . Extending prior work on why outrage spreads, here we examine people's reactions to outrage with a focus on how people interpret others' outrage expression. Specifically, we investigate people's inferences of their in-and out-partisans' outrage expressions, whether they systematically differ, and whether these inferences differentially predict people's judgments of the outrage expressor.

We first develop a taxonomy of the motives for online outrage expression. Our taxonomy is grounded in a theoretical perspective that views emotion expression as a means to one or multiple ends and that organizes these motives along two dimensions: to-feel motives, including hedonic (to seek pleasant emotions) and contra-hedonic (to seek unpleasant emotions) motives, and to-do motives, such as to influence reputation and behavior 11 . Additionally, recent work has introduced a third, interpersonal dimension: for-whom. That is, motives can be directed toward the self (e.g., boosting one's own confidence) or toward others (e.g., changing others' behaviors) [12] [13] [14] [15] . research on online outrage expression to propose a taxonomy consisting of four key motives. These motives are (1) hedonic (to-feel pleasant, self-directed), (2) contra-hedonic (to-feel unpleasant, other-directed), (3) reputational (to influence reputation, self-directed), and (4) behavioral (to influence behavior, other-directed) motives. Importantly, these motives are not mutually exclusive, meaning that an instance of outrage expression can serve one or many motives. This four-motive taxonomy of online outrage expression describes common motives identified in previous research. Yet, it is possible that there are additional motives best captured by other dimensions not included in our taxonomy (e.g., to feel unpleasant, self-directed). We synthesized these findings and identified four core motives that underlie online outrage expression. Example responses were taken from Study 1, where participants reported their own motives (claimed motives) for expressing their outrage online and inferred the motives for others' online outrage expressions.

Using this taxonomy, we investigate the inferences people make about others' outrage expressions and the consequences of these inferences. When making social judgments of an action, people tend to infer the motives behind the action, and inferred motives can powerfully shape people's judgments about the actor and their responses to the action [28] [29] [30] [31] [32] . For example, an outrage expression could be inferred as trolling by one observer, leading them to dismiss the expressor, while inferred as an attempt to raise awareness by another observer, leading them to instead engage with the expressor 33.

Importantly, because the true motives of the actors are often, if not always, unavailable to observers, motives can only be inferred from observable environmental cues.

In this sense, motive inferences often matter more both conceptually and practically in predicting observers' subsequent judgments than the true motives of the actors 30, 33, 34 . In the context of online outrage expressions which is often characterized by clear political group boundaries 22, 35, 36 , political partisanship may be a key social identity cue that guides observers' inferences of the expressors' motives. Hence, people may infer different motives for the outrage expressions of their in-vs. out-partisans 31 , which in turn may contribute to their different reactions to and consequences of the same outrage expressions. We call these partisanship-driven differences in motive inferences "partisan-biased motive inferences".

We explored two important intergroup consequences of motive inferences: out-party hostility and willingness to have a conversation about politics. Out-party hostility is a group-level judgement linked to democratic backsliding 37 . We explored whether motive inferences of online outrage expressions are linked to out-party hostility given that motive inferences might shape people's judgement of whether outrage from the out-party is a threat 30, 31, 38 . Willingness to have a conversation about politics is an interpersonal judgement critical for constructive political discourse 39, 40 . Prior research in non-political contexts has shown that inferred motives of an online discussion thread can shape the civility of the conversations in the thread 34 . Here we extend this logic and explore whether people's motive inferences influence their willingness to have a conversation about politics, even when the messages come from out-partisans who are explicitly expressing outrage. Overall, by identifying whether and how different types of motive inferences of online outrage expressions impact these consequences, our research highlights potential points of intervention to foster greater empathy and support healthy democratic discourse in online social networks.

Validating the Four-Motive Taxonomy and Measuring Motives in Natural Language

Study 1 (N participants = 261; N responses = 638) was designed to validate our proposed motive taxonomy and to explore what claimed and inferred motives naturally emerged from the data.

Throughout, we use the term "claimed motives" to refer to participants' self-reported accounts of their own motives, in recognition that such accounts may be shaped by self-presentation concerns, limited introspective access, or post-hoc rationalization, and sometimes depart from "true" motivations 43 . We return to this point in the next section.

In the study, participants composed a hypothetical outrage message that they would be likely to post to their social media and reported what their motives would be for posting that message (i.e., claimed motives). They also reported what they thought Democrats and Republicans were typically trying to do with their online outrage expression in free response (i.e., inferred motives; see Method).

Validating the Four-Motive Taxonomy

We examined whether our predefined taxonomy sufficiently captured the naturally emerging motives in participants' free responses with zero-shot BERTopic modeling 44 . This technique allowed us to specify topics to look for in the corpus, while also providing the flexibility to discover other topics that emerged from the data, making it both theory-guided and data-driven. We used the definitions of each motive (see

Table 1) as the prespecified topics for the model. If our taxonomy was sufficient in capturing the motives in participants' responses, few or no additional topics should emerge, validating the coverage of our taxonomy. This was indeed what we found: the majority of participants' responses could be categorized by our taxonomy (558 responses; 87.46%), with no additional topics emerging, see Supplemental Information (SI), Sections 1.1.1 and 1.1.2.

Complementing the zero-shot BERTopic model, we conducted a fully data-driven content analysis using a large language model (LLM; model: GPT4o-mini 45 ). In this analysis, we simply provided our theory-driven definition of moral outrage, described our study task, and asked the model to conduct an inductive exploratory analysis based on participants' free responses. We found the freely generated themes by the model could be largely categorized by our taxonomy, supporting the conclusion from the zero-shot BERTopic. For further details and the specific themes, see SI, Section 1.1.3. Taken together, these results showed that our taxonomy covers the majority of motives that naturally emerged in people's free responses.

Measuring Motives in Natural Language with a Large Language Model

After validating our taxonomy, we developed and validated an LLM-based classifier (model: GPT-4o-mini 45 ) to measure motives in each participant's responses (see Method).

Taking insights from the recent discussions on using LLMs for classification tasks [46] [47] [48] , we developed a prompt with clear definitions of, and coding instructions for, each of the motives (see Table 1 for definitions and SI, Section 1.2.1 for detailed procedure and prompt). Given a text response from a participant, the model detected whether the response contained any of the motives in our taxonomy, allowing multiclass classification.

We evaluated the performance of the model by comparing model classifications with those from two human coders, computing intra-class coefficients (ICCs). We found that our model classified participants' claimed and inferred motives consistently with human coders (average ICC between the model and two human coders = .75, SD = .09), see SI, Section 1.2.2 for additional details. These results supported the validity of our prompt and we therefore applied this classifier to detect motives in participants' free responses across studies.

In response to concerns with reproducibility 49 and LLM-hacking 50 , we conducted a multiverse analysis using an additional prompt and two open-weight models. This analysis showed consistent conclusions with the ones presented below. Full analytic pipelines can be found in SI, Section 12, and we summarized the findings at the end of Results.

Claimed Motives and Partisan-Biased Motive Inferences for Online Outrage Expression

Claimed Motives

Before examining partisan-biased motive inferences, we first explored how people accounted for their online outrage expression in their own words. We emphasize that these data should be interpreted as claimed motives rather than "true" motives due to potential social desirability concerns and limitations in introspective access 43, 51 . They nevertheless provide useful information for us to test whether partisan-biased motive inferences are more than a self-other discrepancy in motive attribution.

Study 2 (pre-registered; N participants = 877; N responses = 2,366) employed the same design as Study 1 where we asked participants to imagine expressing outrage and to report their motives. Study 3 was a field study on Reddit where we directly messaged the authors (expressors) of outrage comments (N authors = 233) and asked about their motives, see Fig. 1 and Method.

We measured the prevalence of each motive by dividing the frequency that a motive was detected by our LLM-based classifier in a response by the total number of responses. For instance, if the hedonic motive was detected in 50 responses out of a total of 100 responses, then the prevalence was 50%.

Across Studies 1-3, we discovered that the behavioral motive was the most commonly claimed motive for online outrage expression (66.67%, 84.49%, 70.82%; e.g., "I would post to bring more attention to the issue and call for support and action to be taken"), followed by hedonic (29.44%, 31.50%, 33.05%; e.g., "I want to scream my rage into the void. I want to escape it, for it to escape. I want to get past the negative feelings."), contra-hedonic (16.67%, 10.62%, 14.59%; "to shut them up and make them angry"), and reputational motives (8.44%, 5.01%, 3.00%; e.g., "I was signaling I'm on the virtues side of history"). While our classifier allowed multiclassification, we found that most responses (76.11%; 71.14%; 80.26%) contained only one motive. We also did not find clear partisan differences in prevalence, suggesting that both Democrats and Republicans tend to claim similar motives for their online outrage expression, see SI, Section 5.1.

Overall, we found that when people are asked to articulate why they post outrage, they overwhelmingly describe themselves as driven by behavioral motives. Next, we examine whether people infer different motives for their in-vs. out-partisans' outrage expression, even though both Democrats and Republicans showed the same patterns for their claimed motives.

Moreover, if there were indeed partisan-biased motive inferences, we expected that they could not simply be explained by a self-other discrepancy in motive attribution.

Willingness to Have a Conversation with an Author about Politics

As a field study with real Reddit comments, Study 3 uniquely allowed us to examine the link between observers' motive inferences for a specific outrage comment and their willingness to have a conversation with the comment author about politics. This was measured by showing an author's outrage comment to observers and asking them to rate their willingness to have a conversation with the author about politics ("How willing are you to have a conversation with the author of the comment on a socio-political issue to learn more about their thoughts?" 1 = "Not willing at all" to7 = "Very much willing"; M = 3.35; SD = 2.12).

To test the relationship between motive inferences and willingness to have a conversation, we conducted a mixed-effects linear regression including the inference of each motive category as predictors, accounting for the partisanship of the comment and both the random intercepts of observers and of comments.

Unsurprisingly, we found that there was a significant relationship between partisanship and willingness to have a conversation, indicating that observers were less willing to have a conversation with an out-partisan author about politics (b = -1.29, SE = 0.12, 95% CI = [-1.51, -1.06], p < .001, β = -.45). Independent of the effect of partisanship, we found that inferring a contra-hedonic motive showed a negative association (b = -0.66, SE = 0.23, 95% CI = [-1.11, -0.20], p = .005, β = -.10) with willingness to have a conversation, whereas inferring a behavioral motive showed a positive association (b = 0.48, SE = 0.24, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.95], p = .042, β = .07). We did not find evidence of significant associations between inferring other motives and willingness to have a conversation, see Table 5.

The negative association between willingness to have a conversation and inferring a contra-hedonic motive was robust after we controlled for observers' age, gender, strength of partisan identity, political social media use, and authors' self-reported motives (SI, Section 8.1). By contrast, the positive association between willingness to have a conversation and inferring a behavioral motive slightly dropped and became non-significant (b = 0.45, SE = 0.24, 95% CI = [-0.02, 0.90], p = .060, β = .06) when accounting for the same covariates.

Finally, we found that the association between inferring a contra-hedonic motive and willingness to have a conversation was equally strong for both in-and out-partisan authors (SI, Section 8.2). By contrast, the association between inferring a behavioral motive and willingness to have a conversation was significant only when the author was an out-partisan (b = 1.14, SE = 0.23, 95% CI = [0.69, 1.59], p < .001, β = .50) instead of an in-partisan (b = 0.35, SE = 0.22, 95% CI = [-0.08, 0.79], p = .113, β = .16), possibly due to a ceiling effect.

Together, these findings demonstrate that inferences of contra-hedonic and behavioral motives, which vary by in-vs. out-partisanship, were linked to willingness to have a conversation about politics even when the counterpart is openly expressing outrage. These findings highlight how people's motive inferences of out-partisans' online outrage expression may play an important role in political discourse.

Changing Motive Inferences for Out-partisans' Online Outrage Expressions

Study 4 (N participants = 720, N responses = 1,198) was an intervention to test whether people's motive inferences could be changed and whether these changes had effects on intergroup consequences (see Method). The intervention specifically focused on people's inferences of contra-hedonic and behavioral motives for out-partisans' online outrage expression because of these motives' consistent patterns regarding partisan-biased motive inferences and associations with outcomes in Studies 2-3. The goal of the intervention was to change people's motive inferences such that they were more aligned with the claimed motives for expressing online outrage that people tended to report (i.e., much more behavioral than contra-hedonic). Regardless of their accuracy relative to authors' claimed motives, results from Studies 2-3 suggest that changing motive inferences in these directions can help improve intergroup consequences.

The intervention provided people with feedback on the prevalence of out-partisans' claimed motives from our prior studies and with a specific example of an out-partisan's comment and their claimed motives based on real comments from Reddit, see Method. We then assessed people's subsequent motive inferences made for other real outrage comments from Reddit as in Study 3. We examined whether our intervention changed people's motive inferences of online outrage expression, and whether changes in motive inferences translated to lowered out-party hostility and increased willingness to have a conversation with the outrage comment authors about politics.

First, we found that our intervention successfully changed people's motive inferences, and the intervention effect did not differ based on whether the outrage comment came from an in-partisan or an out-partisan, as shown by non-significant interactions between intervention and partisanship (contra-hedonic model: b = -0.02, SE = 0.07, 95% CI = [-0.15, 0.11], p = .794, OR = 0.98; behavioral model: b = -0.05, SE = 0.06, 95% CI = [-0.17, 0.08], p = .467, OR = 0.95), see Fig. 3.

Participants in the intervention group were significantly less likely to infer a contra-hedonic motive (b = -0.37, SE = 0.07, 95% CI = [-0.51, -0.23], p < .001, OR = 0.69) and more likely to infer a behavioral motive (b = 0.25, SE = 0.07, 95% CI = [0.12, 0.39], p < .001, OR = 1.29) compared to participants in the control group. In other words, our intervention made participants 1.45x less likely to infer a contra-hedonic motive and 1.29x more likely to infer a behavioral motive for any given comment they viewed after the intervention.

Yet, our intervention did not completely eliminate partisan-biased motive inferences.

We found that participants were still significantly more likely to infer a contra-hedonic motive (b = 0.29, SE = 0.07, 95% CI = [0.16, 0.43], p < .001, OR = 1.34) and less likely to infer a behavioral motive (b = -0.16, SE = 0.07, 95% CI = [-0.29, -0.04], p = .011, OR = 0.85) for an out-compared to in-partisan comment. These findings were robust after we controlled for participants' age, gender, strength of partisan identity, and political social media use (SI, Section 9.1), and the intervention was equally effective for both Democrats and Republicans (SI, Section 9.2). Finally, in SI, Section 9.3, we showed the interventions' precision, finding that it was successful in only changing the targeted two motives (i.e., contra-hedonic and behavioral) without changing the other motives (i.e., hedonic and reputational) in our taxonomy. Changing Motive Inferences Increases Willingness to Have A Conversation with Outrage

Comment Authors about Politics

Finally, we examined the effects of our intervention on willingness to have a conversation with an outrage comment author about politics. We found that our intervention increased participants' willingness to have a conversation with an author (b = 0.13, SE = 0.07, 95% CI = [0.00, 0.26], p = .045, β = .06), even though in general participants were still less willing to have a conversation with an out-partisan (b = -0.59, SE = 0.05, 95% CI = [-0.69, -0.49], p < .001, β = -.27). There was no significant interaction between intervention and partisanship (b = 0.01, SE = 0.05, 95% CI = [-0.09, 0.11], p = .233, β < .01), indicating that our intervention was equally effective in increasing the willingness to have a conversation across partisan lines.

Importantly, we found evidence that changes in motive inferences mediated the relationship between our intervention and willingness to have a conversation about politics with an outrage expressor, see Fig. 3. Using a Bayesian multiple mediation model, we found that our intervention made participants less likely to infer a contra-hedonic motive (posterior b = -0.83, 95% credible interval = [-1.19, -0.51]) and more likely to infer a behavioral motive (posterior b = 0.56, 95% credible interval = [0.26, 0.88]), which in turn translated to increased willingness to have a conversation (total indirect effect: posterior b = 0.85, 95% credible interval = [0.53, 1.24]). These findings were robust after we controlled for participants' age, gender, strength of partisan identity, and political social media use (SI, Section 11.1). We also found no evidence that the mediation effects differed across partisanship of the comment author (SI, Section 11.2). Together, these findings demonstrate how a one-shot intervention could change people's motive inferences, and further provide evidence for the mediating role of motive inferences in increasing people's willingness to have a conversation with an out-partisan about politics, even when they are actively expressing outrage.

Discussion

In this research, we proposed and validated a four-motive taxonomy-hedonic, contra-hedonic, reputational, and behavioral-to examine people's claimed motives for their own outrage expression, their inferred motives for their in-and out-partisans' outrage expression, and the intergroup consequences of these motive inferences. Across two behavioral experiments and a field study, we found that the behavioral motive (i.e., to raise awareness or inspire action) was the most commonly-claimed motive for online outrage expression. At the same time, we found that partisanship consistently shaped people's motive inferences: people were more likely to infer a contra-hedonic motive and less likely to infer a behavioral motive for their out-(vs. in-) partisans. These inference patterns were more than a mere self-other discrepancy and reflect broader intergroup dynamics, specifically the tendency to assign benign motivations to ingroup members and malicious ones to outgroup members 28, 30, 31 . Hence, these patterns may extend to other group contexts (e.g., race, gender) that future research can explore. These findings of partisan-biased motive inferences can help explain why online outrage expression can sometimes bring people together while at other times driving people apart, both within and across partisan lines. We found that when people inferred behavioral instead of contra-hedonic motives for online outrage expressors (even out-partisans), they were significantly more willing to have a conversation with them about politics. This suggests that people may still be receptive to civil dialogue when a political opponent expresses outrage, provided they infer that the opponent's goal is to raise awareness about a valued issue rather than to inflame partisan tensions. Our intervention therefore offers a strategy to foster constructive disagreement even in the face of strong negative emotions that naturally occur when discussing highly moralized political issues (also see ref. 54, 55 ). Given that partisans hold deep-rooted moral and political disagreements, our findings suggest that supporting democratic functioning may not require agreement on issues, but rather greater understanding of the motivations behind one another's strong emotions 30, 56.

Interestingly, we found that the group-level judgment of out-party hostility was not driven by individuals' general tendency to infer specific motives, but by partisan-biased motive inferences. This suggests that out-party hostility reflects a group-level distinction between "us" and "them," which can be detected in differences in motive inferences across groups. In our intervention, while we changed people's motive inferences in general, we did not fully eliminate partisan-biased motive inferences, which might be the reason why our intervention did not significantly impact out-party hostility. This work has several limitations. While self-report is common in research on motivations 57, 58 , it rests on two premises that we were unable to definitively confirm: people were (a) aware of and (b) honest about their true motives. Although analyses on the relationships between linguistic features of outrage comments and claimed motives showed some preliminary supportive evidence (SI, Section 5.2), these results do not definitively validate the two premises. For this reason, we treated these self-reports as claimed motives, rather than actual motives of outrage expressions. While this limitation does not invalidate our results on partisan-biased motive inferences, it remains important for future research to more comprehensively test how claimed motives for expressing outrage may be biased. For instance, future work can examine the changes in claimed motives under conditions in which known self-report biases are reduced, such as using a bogus pipeline to reduce social desirability concerns 59.

There are also limitations regarding the stimuli of our field study and intervention.

First, we only used comments from one social media platform-Reddit, and even though it is a popular platform with many strong-leaning partisans from both sides 60 , the generalizability of our results to other platforms should be tested in future research. There are also potential selection effects regarding the authors we included in the field study. However, additional analyses comparing the included vs. excluded authors showed that they did not differ systematically in terms of their online posting activity (SI, Section, 3.2). Yet, there may be other individual differences that we could not examine that impacted our results, such as self-reflection ability. There were also possible ideological differences between authors who responded and those who did not. Past research has indicated that left-wing and right-wing users interact with social media differently 61, 62 , and liberals' and conservatives' attitudes towards researchers have also been shown to differ 63 , both of which may have contributed to us receiving more responses from left-leaning than right-leaning authors. However, we were able to recruit an ideologically-balanced set of observers (those who inferred motives) which helps assuage concerns about how inferences were impacted by selection effects of comment authors (all observers viewed an equal number of in-and out-partisan comments). Additional sensitivity analyses based on balanced subsets of comments also showed that our conclusions were robust to the included pool of comments, see SI, Section 6.4.

We also note our intervention's limitations on its scope. Although we increased ecological validity by using real Reddit comments as the stimuli, our intervention was conducted in a tightly controlled setting. Generalization to real-world social media platforms and their users remains to be tested. For instance, future research might consider implementing an AI browser extension that helps users to be more lenient about motive inferences of others' outrage expressions and to engage in conversations more constructively. Moreover, our intervention did not directly reduce out-party hostility, and therefore interpretations about its effects on affective polarization should be cautious. One possibility is that out-party hostility is more stable and thus more difficult to change compared to willingness to have a conversation with an out-partisan. Still, given prior work showing the benefits of civil cross-partisan conversations 39, 40 , future research might treat willingness to have a conversation as an intermediate mechanism to reduce affective polarization.

Finally, due to our U.S.-based samples, we acknowledge that generalizability is a central limitation of this research. Future work should therefore directly examine whether partisan-biased motive inferences exist and function similarly in other cultural contexts (e.g., a culture with strong collectivistic values) and political systems (e.g., a multi-party democratic system). The U.S. political context is characterized by intense competition between two parties, which is also embedded in the broader cultural values of individualism.

We suspect that in other contexts, motive inferences might still be biased but less so by partisanship but more so by one's issue alignment with a party. As social scientists grapple with the effects of social media on democratic functioning around the world 64 , it is critical to consider how online outrage expressions are interpreted differently across these contexts and to examine when they might help unite versus divide.

Moral outrage is a defining feature of contemporary online political discourse. In this research we proposed a four-motive taxonomy of online outrage expression to examine how people understand others' outrage expressions. We found that people claim themselves, and infer in-partisans, as primarily driven by behavioral motives (i.e., to raise awareness or inspire action), but out-partisans as driven by contra-hedonic motives (i.e., to shame or troll). We also found that motive inferences were associated with out-party hostility and willingness to have a conversation with an outrage expressor about politics. Importantly, motive inferences appear to be malleable. In an intervention, we changed people's motive inferences and observed a causal effect on increased willingness to have a conversation about politics with outrage expressors. By uncovering the role of motive inferences for online outrage, our work opens new pathways for promoting cross-partisan conversations and fostering more constructive political discourse in the digital age.

Methods

Study 1

Study 1 was designed to validate our proposed motive taxonomy and to explore whether outrage inferences were biased by partisanship. We recruited 300 American, partisan social media users via CloudConnect Research. Participants first reported their partisan identity (Democrat or Republican), strength of partisan identity ("I identify with the [Democratic/Republican] Party"; ranging from1= "fully disagree" to7 = "fully agree"), gender, age, and frequency of political social media use ("How often do you use social media specifically to view or learn about political content?" ; ranging from1= "daily or more" to7 = "never"). Next, to ensure participants understood what outrage meant, they were shown four different statements and asked to select the one expressing outrage. All specific instructions and study materials are available online (https://osf.io/nmzk5/overview?view_only=84851d4575594b56a543e23145b4e052). This research received approval from the university ethics review board (ID: STU00218134).

For the main task, participants reported what they thought Democrats and Republicans were typically trying to achieve with their online outrage expression in their own words (i.e., they were asked to infer motives of online outrage via free response). Order was counter-balanced across participants. After inferring others' motives, participants composed a hypothetical outrage message that they would be likely to post to their social media and reported what their motives would be for posting that message.

We excluded 39 participants for failing to pass the comprehension check (i.e., did not correctly identify the outrage statement), failing to follow instructions (e.g., did not report any clear motives [e.g., "to display what their stance is", "Honestly no idea. It's always based on questionable morals"], or completing the study in less than five minutes), and we excluded 136 responses for intelligibility issues (e.g., "stolen car", "with thebdemocrates" ) or AI-generated text (both manually assessed by the first author and one research assistant), resulting in a final N of 261 (137 Democrats, 124 Republicans; 34.87% male, 63.60% female, and 1.15% other gender identity; mean [M] age = 37 years, standard deviation [SD] = 11.7) and 638 responses. Participants showed strong partisan identity (M = 5.77, SD = 1.07) and frequently used social media for political reasons (M = 3.13, SD = 1.91).

Validating the Four-Motive Taxonomy

We employed zero-shot BERTopic modeling 44 to examine whether our predefined taxonomy sufficiently captured the naturally emerging motives in people's free responses.

Unlike traditional topic model methods (e.g., Latent Dirichlet Allocation), BERTopic captures semantic context through high-dimensional embeddings and assigns a topic to documents by semantic similarity 65 . In zero-shot BERTopic, predefined topic descriptions were embedded alongside our corpus of free responses using a sentence transformer, see SI, Section 1.1.1 for additional details. Texts matching predefined topics above a similarity threshold received corresponding labels based on our taxonomy, while unmatched texts underwent unsupervised clustering to uncover additional topics. Hence, this technique allowed us to specify topics to look for in the corpus, while also providing the flexibility to discover other topics that naturally emerged from the data, making it both theory-guided and data-driven.

Here, we used the transformer gte-large 66 to embed predefined topics (i.e., the definitions of the four proposed motives in Table 1) and all participants' responses. The similarity threshold was set to .80 (see SI, Section 1.1.2 for results based on other similarity thresholds). We used the definitions of each motive (see Table 1) as the prespecified topics for the model. If our taxonomy was sufficient in capturing the motives in the participants' responses, few or no additional topics should emerge.

Because zero-shot BERTopic still imposed a structure to the data, there may have been other topics that were not uncovered by this technique. We therefore conducted a completely data-driven content analysis using an LLM (model: GPT-4o-mini). Following the recent guidelines on how to interact with LLM for inductive analysis [67] [68] [69] , we constructed a prompt specifically for the purpose of inductive exploratory analysis on our data 70 . In contrast to zero-shot BERTopic where we provided the model with our definition of motives, in this analysis we simply described the task and asked the model to discover the underlying themes in the data. Full analytic pipeline and results can be seen in SI, Section 1.1.3.

Measuring Motives in Natural Language with a Large Language Model

After validating our taxonomy, we developed and validated an LLM-based classifier (model: GPT-4o-mini) to measure motives in each participant's responses. Taking insights from the recent discussions on using LLMs for classification tasks 47, 48, 71 , we developed a prompt with clear definitions of, and coding instructions for, each of the motives (see Table 1 for definitions and see SI, Section 1.2.1 for detailed procedure and prompt). The model took the input of text response from a participant and provided the output of whether the response contained any of the motives in our taxonomy, allowing multiclass classification. Model performance was evaluated based on comparing model classifications with those from two independent human coders, computing intra-class coefficients (ICCs) and F1 scores with human codes as "ground truth".

To increase the reliability of the model response, we (a) set the temperature parameter to 0 and (b) ran the model five times and took the majority-voted code as the final code for each response. ICCs and F1 scores across the model and two human coders were used as evaluation criteria, with .70 as the threshold for a good performance.

Claimed Motives for Online Outrage Expression

To examine people's claimed motives for online outrage expression, we looked at participants' responses of their self-reported motives. Prevalence of each motive was operationalized as the number of times that a motive was detected in a response divided by the total number of responses. We then compared the prevalences of all the motives and determined their relative order.

Partisan-biased Motive Inference

We employed mixed-effects logistic regression to examine whether people tended to infer different motives for in-vs. out-partisans' online outrage expression. The outcome variable was the presence (coded as 1) or absence (coded as 0) of a motive in each inference response. In the model, we included partisanship as a predictor (dummy coded with1 = out-partisan,0 = in-partisan), accounting for the random intercepts of participants. A positive effect of partisanship indicated that participants were more likely to infer the motive for out-partisans compared to in-partisans. We conducted one model for each motive and adjusted p-values using the Bonferroni-Holm procedure.

Study 2

Study 2 was a larger, pre-registered, confirmatory study (https://osf.io/xtuqm/overview?view_only=c8f9f19fbfe743c7a5a51dd32e2c12b3; date registered: Oct 18, 2024). The study procedure mirrored Study 1, except that in Study 2, participants reported their motives prior to inferring others' motives, and they did so without writing a hypothetical social media message. They additionally reported their out-party hostility, measured by the three-item 'political sectarianism' scale 37 , at the end of the study ("No matter how hard I try, I can't see the world the way [Democrats/Republicans] do",0 = "I don't have any difficulty seeing the world the way [Democrats/Republicans] do" to6 = "It's impossible for me to see the world the way [Democrats/Republicans] do"; M = 3.36, SD = 1.46). This research received approval from the university ethics review board (ID: STU00218134).

Based on an a priori power analysis (see SI, Section 2.1), we recruited 1,000 participants via Prolific. We excluded 108 participants (failed to pass the comprehension check or follow instructions, or completed the study in less than five minutes) and 311 responses (no detectable motives, e.g., "I really do not have much to say about Democrats", "Create an echo chamber", "There is currently something wrong sith the economy. There is no way the inflation rate increase more than our wages."), leaving 877 valid participants (463 Democrats, 414 Republicans; 44.93% male, 53.71% female, and 1.37% other gender identity; M age = 40.6 years, SD = 13.5) and 2,366 responses. Participants showed strong partisan identity (M = 5.78, SD = 1.15) and frequently used social media for political reasons (M = 3.08, SD = 2.05).

Claimed Motives for Online Outrage Expression

Prevalence of claimed motives was computed in the same way as in Study 1.

Partisan-biased Motive Inference

Partisan-biased motive inference was analyzed in the same way as in Study 1. The analytic strategy deviated from our registration but both strategies arrived at the same conclusion, see SI, Section 2.2.

Associations Between Motive Inferences and Out-party Hostility

Because political sectarianism was measured as an individual difference score, we computed two composite individual-level variables to capture participants' general tendency to infer a motive category and to display partisan-biased motive inferences for each motive category. Individuals' general tendency to infer a motive category was operationalized as the total number of times a participant reported a given motive category for any inference target.

Higher values indicated that participants tended to infer the presence of a motive category more. Individuals' partisan-biased motive inference was operationalized as the discrepancy between the frequency of a motive inference category for an out-partisan compared to in-partisan. A positive value indicated that the participant inferred the motive category only for out-partisans but not in-partisans; a negative value indicated the opposite. These variables were not highly correlated and hence multicollinearity was not an issue (see SI, Section 7.1).

We conducted a linear regression with these variables as predictors and political sectarianism as the outcome. We report other exploratory analyses included in our preregistration in SI, Section 2.3.

Study 3

Study 3 was a field study on the social media platform Reddit, assessing the ecological validity of our findings. This study had two phases, involving authors of comments (outrage expressors) and observers of comments (people reading the authors' comments), see 2000026899). Procedure Author Phase. Using Reddit's API, we collected comments from political subreddits (e.g., r/politics; for the full list, see SI, Section 3.1; Waller & Anderson, 2021) from November 11 to December 2, 2024. We aimed to collect comments both expressing and not expressing outrage and comments both showing and not showing political ideology. Comments showing outrage and political ideology were used as the critical stimuli with which we conducted our analyses, and comments not showing outrage and political ideology were used as filler stimuli in the study. Moral outrage comments were identified using Google's Perspective API moral outrage endpoint 72 , with a threshold of .70. To ensure the reproducibility and robustness of our results and to facilitate future work on online outrage expression, we developed and validated a new outrage classifier based on an open-weight LLM, showing that our findings were robust to this alternative model. The development and validation process, including the prompt and the model used, are detailed in SI, Section 13.

We contacted the comment authors with a direct private message including the comment and its associated post, asking the authors to report their motives for posting the comment. In the private message, we explicitly disclosed our identity as researchers and our research purpose in learning about the motives behind people's online posts. Authors were aware that by replying to our message, they consented to participating and their responses would always be anonymous and would be used for subsequent analysis. No deception was used in the process and the participating authors were thanked for their responses and their questions about the research were addressed. We contacted 1,755 authors, and received 416 responses (a 23.70% response rate). See SI, Section 3.2 for how we addressed potential selection bias for this final sample of comments. We again used our LLM-based classifier to examine the motives in authors' responses.

To increase the ecological validity of the inference task in the observer phase (see below), we collected a screenshot of the post to which the comment replied (see Fig. 1). In the screenshots we hid the name of the subreddit and the username of the post author to ensure anonymity. Moreover, because some comments were nested within other comments (e.g., comments replying to another comment), we also collected the direct parent comment that these nested comments replied to. These served as important contextual information helping observers make sense of the target comment. Therefore, we excluded comments whose replying post was unavailable and the nested comments whose direct parent comment was unavailable. Including only the publicly available information ensured that we did not present data that the platform or other users did not want to share. After exclusions (undetectable motives, unavailable post or direct parent comment; 136 responses), two human coders who were familiar with American politics independently annotated each comment's ideology. Both coders annotated the ideology of the comment holistically, taking into account the stance of the comment and the post to which the comment replied. Importantly, coders annotated the comments without knowing which subreddit the comment appeared in and without knowing any explicit identity signaling cues of the authors (e.g., username, flair), mirroring what actual participants would see in the inference task. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion (ICC = .78). This process resulted in 184 left-leaning, 49 right-leaning outrage comments, and 47 non-political non-outrage filler comments. Our analysis was based on the ideology-leaning outrage comments (233 in total).

Observer Phase.

We recruited 800 American partisan, social media users via Prolific based on a priori power analysis (see SI, Section 3.3). These participants served as observers for the comments. Observers first provided demographic information as in previous studies, and then were given a standardized practice trial based on a non-political non-outrage comment. In the trial, they saw a screenshot of a Reddit post and a target comment, and they were asked to infer the motives of the comment's author, see Fig. 1. The name of the subreddit, the username of the post author and of the comment author were all removed to ensure the inference was purely based on the content. After this practice trial, observers completed five more trials based on randomly selected comments (2 left-leaning outrage, 2 right-leaning outrage, 1 non-political non-outrage filler). The target comment was accompanied by a parent comment if applicable. After inferring others' motives, observers were shown three previously-seen comments (1 left-leaning, 1 right-leaning, and 1 filler; in a random order) and asked to rate their willingness to have a conversation with the author about political issues ("How willing are you to have a conversation with the author of the comment on a socio-political issue to learn more about their thoughts?" 1 = "Not willing at all" to7 = "Very much willing"; M = 3.49; SD = 2.12). Finally, they reported their out-party hostility at the end of the study using the same scale as in Study 2 (M = 3.62, SD = 1.53).

We excluded 41 observers (failed to pass the comprehension checks or follow the instructions, or completed the survey in fewer than five minutes), and 1,123 responses with no detectable motives. Further inspection of the excluded comments showed that the high exclusion rate was mostly due to observers reporting a description of the comment (e.g., "Disagreement to the post", "to share his idea of fact", "Racial tension") instead of reporting their motive inferences. Final analyses included 717 observers (374 Democrats and 343 Republicans; 30.54% male, 69.46% female; mean age = 42.2 years, SD = 13.7) and 1,913 outrage motive inferences (957 left-leaning, 956 right-leaning). Additional sensitivity analyses with the complete data showed consistent results with the ones presented here, see SI, Section 3.4. Participants showed strong partisan identity (M = 5.76, SD = 1.16) and frequently used social media for political reasons (M = 2.87, SD = 2.03).

Claimed Motives for Online Outrage Expression

Prevalence of self-reported motives was computed with the authors' self-reports in the same way as in Studies 1-2.

Partisan-biased Motive Inference

Partisan-biased motive inference was analyzed in the same way as in Studies 1-2. In addition to the random intercepts of participants, models in Study 3 further accounted for the random intercepts of comments.

Associations Between Motive Inferences and Out-party Hostility

As in Study 2, we first computed the between-person variables of general tendency to infer each motive category and partisan-biased motive inference for each motive category. Again, these variables were not highly correlated (see SI, Section 7.1). We conducted a mixed-effects linear regression with these variables as predictors and political sectarianism as outcome, accounting for the random intercepts of comments.

Associations Between Motive Inferences and Willingness to Have A Conversation

To examine the association between observers' motive inferences and their willingness to have a conversation about politics with an author, we conducted a mixed-effects linear regression. In the model, we included observers' inferences of each motive category (1 = motive present,0 = motive absent in the inference text response) and behavioral motives. In the intervention group, after reporting their estimated percentages, participants received visual feedback (in a bar graph) on the percentages of each claimed motive (taken from the average prevalence of expressors' self-reported claimed motives across Studies 1-3). See SI, Section 4.1 for a visual illustration of the procedure.

In practice, this bar graph demonstrated that participants clearly overestimated the prevalence of a contra-hedonic motive and underestimated the prevalence of a behavioral motive for out-partisan online outrage expressions, with the claimed motives as the reference (see SI, Section 4.3). In the control group, no feedback was given.

Changing Specific Inferences.

Next, all participants were instructed to complete a standardized trial to infer the motives of a specific social media user. In the trial, all participants saw a real Reddit post, and then saw an out-partisan outrage comment based on real comments we observed on the post. Participants were asked to infer the motives of the comment's author (as in Study 3). In the intervention group, after inferring the motives, participants received feedback on the author's response stating their self-reported motives.

The response contained a behavioral motive and no other motives. Participants were told that the text came directly from the authors, although it was in fact written by the researchers (for the motive generation process, see SI, Section 4.4). In practice, this procedure provided participants with a reality check between what they inferred and what the authors claimed their motives to be. See SI, Section 4.1 for a visual illustration of the procedure.

Motive Inferences.

Next, all participants completed two more inference trials, with one trial containing an in-partisan outrage comment and the other an out-partisan outrage comment (order counter-balanced across participants). The comments were randomly drawn from a newly-collected (see below) pool of 10 left-leaning outrage comments and 10 right-leaning outrage comments (see below and also SI, Sections 4.5 and 4.6). No feedback was given in either group this time. These two were the critical trials with which we analyzed the intervention effect on motive inferences. After inferring authors' motives, participants read the two previously-seen comments again and rated their willingness to have a conversation with the author about politics using the same scale as in Study 3. Finally, they rated their out-party hostility using the same scale as in Studies 2 and 3 before being debriefed.

Comment Collection.

To increase the ecological validity, specifically the relevance of the comments, the comments in this study were collected from Reddit posts about the 2025 LA protest regarding ICE and immigration policy from political subreddits between June 16 and July 11, 2025, and the intervention was conducted on July 14, 2025. The comment collection procedure is described in more detail in SI, Section 4.5.

We excluded 196 participants (failed to pass the comprehension check or follow instructions, or completed the study in less than five minutes) and 410 responses (no detectable motives, e.g., "they wanted to show they support the post"), leaving 720 valid participants (388 Democrats, 332 Republicans; 47.08% male, M age = 43.3 years, SD = 16.2) and 1,198 responses. Participants showed strong partisan identity (M = 5.78, SD = 1.19) and frequently used social media for political reasons (M = 2.64, SD = 1.92).

Changing Motive Inferences for Out-partisans' Online Outrage Expressions

We employed mixed-effects logistic regression on motive inference to examine our intervention effect. In the model, we included intervention (effect coded with1 = intervention), partisanship (effect coded with1 = out-partisan), and the interaction between the two as predictors, motive presence (coded as 1) or absence (coded as 0) in a response as the outcome, accounting for the random intercepts of participants and of comments. A positive effect of intervention indicated that our intervention increased the probability of inferring a motive. A significant interaction between intervention and partisanship indicated that our intervention changed the probability of inferring a motive to a different extent for invs. out-partisans' comments.

Effects of Motive Inferences on Out-party Hostility

We first conducted a between-person t-test to examine the effect of intervention on political sectarianism. If there was a significant difference, we conducted a mediation analysis with intervention as predictor, motive inferences of contra-hedonic and behavioral motives as mediators, and political sectarianism as outcome. If there was no significant difference, we explored the associations between motive inferences and political sectarianism using a mixed-effects linear regression as in Study 3.

Effects of Motive Inferences on Willingness to Have A Conversation

We first conducted a mixed-effects linear regression with partisanship, intervention, and their interaction as predictors and willingness to have a conversation as outcome, accounting for the random intercepts of participants and comments. If there was a significant intervention effect, we conducted a mediation analysis with intervention as the predictor, motive inferences of contra-hedonic and behavioral motives as mediators, and willingness to have a conversation as the outcome. We included partisanship as a covariate and accounted for the random intercepts of participants and of comments. Because a frequentist modeling approach could not account for two categorical mediators and two random intercepts, we employed a Bayesian modeling approach with uninformative priors 73 . If there was no significant intervention effect, we explored the associations between motive inferences and willingness to have a conversation using a mixed-effects linear regression as in Study 3.

Pooling Effects of Partisanship

We conducted two random-effects meta-analyses to pool the effects of partisanship on motive inference across Studies 1-3, separately for contra-hedonic and behavioral motives.
