Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. The Unappreciated Helper: When an AI Facilitation Agent Improves Group Efficiency

Despite Poorer User Perceptions

Group work is ubiquitous in the twenty-first century, and a variety of digital technologies have been used to facilitate collaboration and develop teamwork skills (Cherbonnier et al., 2025). Over the past few decades, the focus has shifted away from the influence of digital technologies per se toward the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in various contexts, including groups and teams (Amiot et al., 2025; Bezrukova et al., 2023; Ontrup et al., 2025).

In recent years, the use of AI has expanded considerably, ranging from traditional intelligent tutoring systems and pedagogical agents to conversational agents, chatbots, virtual assistants, and AI facilitation agents powered by large language models¹ (Ganguly et al., 2026; Gunasekaran et al., 2026; Luo & Wen, 2025; Marquez-Carpintero et al., 2025; Nguyen, 2023). With the rapid development of AI based on large language models, growing attention has been devoted to AI agents capable of supporting collaborative work (Alsobay et al., 2025; Ba et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025). Unlike conventional AI assistants that respond to users' requests, AI facilitation agents proactively monitor group interactions and deliver adaptive, context-sensitive recommendations to regulate collaborative processes as they unfold. A recent review of AI agents in computer-supported collaborative learning by Ba et al. (2026) identified proactive facilitation as the most prevalent form of AI intervention, reported in 23 of the 46 studies reviewed. In this approach, AI agents continuously monitor group interactions and autonomously provide timely recommendations rather than waiting for users to request assistance. Nevertheless, the review also emphasized that most existing AI facilitation systems remain only partially autonomous, relying on instructor oversight or predesigned facilitation strategies alongside AI analytics (see also Naik et al., 2025).

Despite the growing interest in AI facilitation agents, important questions remain regarding their effectiveness. Most studies have primarily examined whether AI interventions improve collaborative processes or learning outcomes, whereas considerably less attention has been paid to how group members perceive the collaborative environments that integrate such agents. Yet, the success of AI facilitation is likely to depend not only on its ability to improve objective collaborative outcomes but also on users' acceptance of the platform in which it is embedded. A collaborative platform that integrates an AI agent may successfully regulate group interactions while simultaneously being perceived as controlling, unsupportive, or unnecessary. Examining both the objective and subjective consequences of AI facilitation therefore appears essential for understanding its role in collaborative work. Understanding whether integrating AI facilitation agents into collaborative platforms can improve collective performance without compromising users' subjective experience represents an important challenge for the design of future human-AI collaborative systems. The present study contributes to the literature on AI-supported collaboration by jointly examining the objective effects of an AI facilitation agent on group communication and performance, alongside group members' subjective perceptions of a collaborative platform with or without such an agent.

Effects of AI Facilitation Agents on Group Performance and User Perceptions

Some AI facilitation agents have been developed to support group discussions by monitoring interactions and adapting their interventions on the basis of linguistic cues that serve as indicators of members' behaviors (Barrios-Fleitas et al., 2026; Sottilare et al., 2018). Research has demonstrated that, under certain conditions, the deployment of AI facilitation agents can enhance the effectiveness of group discussions (Houde et al., 2025; Nguyen, 2023). However, although participants benefited from having an AI agent in the group, they disliked it when the agent seemed to dominate the conversation (Houde et al., 2025), suggesting that the effects of AI agents are not always positive. In the same vein, a growing body of research suggests that AI-based interventions may elicit negative subjective reactions from users. For example, Do and collaborators (2023) designed an AI agent that encouraged low-contributing members to participate in a collaborative decision-making task. Their findings showed that explaining to participants how the agent detected participation patterns increased users' trust and reduced feelings of embarrassment, relative to conditions in which no explanation about the agent's functioning was provided. A previous study using the same agent showed that the most effective and preferred way to increase the participation of under-contributing members was for the agent to send messages addressed to the individual through a private channel (Do et al., 2022).

Thus, the way AI agent interventions are designed and communicated to users may influence their acceptability. An AI facilitation agent that extracted discussion structures from online conversations and posted facilitation messages improved users' satisfaction, with stronger benefits observed when AI interventions were combined with human facilitation (Ito et al., 2022). More recently, Schlund and Zitek (2024) showed that individuals who believe they are being monitored and evaluated by an AI system rather than by a human report lower levels of autonomy, greater resistance, and more negative reactions. Similarly, users have been found to place less trust in AI facilitation agents than in human assistants (Cvetkovic et al., 2024), and recommendations provided by an AI facilitation agent were perceived as more useful when users believed that a human had contributed to its development (Zhang et al., 2024). In a study investigating how students react to AI-generated or human feedback in teamwork skills assessment, Kotlyar and Krasman (2025) also revealed a preference for human feedback over AI feedback. In such studies comparing reactions to human and AI feedback, the purpose of the AI agent is mainly evaluative rather than informational, which may amplify negative reactions (Schlund & Zitek, 2024).

Research has also shown that recommendations providing guidance on how to regulate group interactions and collaborate effectively can improve group performance when they are provided during the collaborative task (Hémon et al., 2024; Michinov et al., 2026). However, in these studies, the recommendations were delivered statically, before and/or during the collaborative task, without adapting to the ongoing interaction dynamics of the group. Recent advances in AI make it possible to go beyond this approach by integrating AI facilitation agents capable of monitoring group interactions and delivering adaptive, real-time recommendations tailored to the group's communication dynamics during the collaborative task.

The supporting role of AI facilitation agents may be particularly valuable when insufficient information is shared between group members (Stasser et al., 2000; Stasser & Titus, 1985). Research has consistently shown that group discussions are subject to information-sharing biases that negatively impact group performance (Dennis, 1996; Larson et al., 1994), and strategies aimed at reducing these biases are therefore considered a prerequisite for enhancing collective efficiency (see Lu et al., 2012; Schulz-Hardt & Mojzisch, 2012; Sohrab et al., 2015, for reviews). One such strategy involves guiding information exchange between group members, and AI facilitation agents may be particularly well suited to this objective by monitoring ongoing interactions and providing adaptive support whenever information sharing becomes insufficient. In line with this, Alsobay et al. (2025) recently found that an AI facilitation agent increased the amount of information shared during a collaborative task, although this improvement did not translate into better group performance. This finding suggests that AI facilitation agents may influence collaborative processes, but their effects on group performance remain unclear.

The present study aims to address this gap by examining whether an AI facilitation agent can improve both group communication and performance. Because most of the studies above assessed reactions to the agent itself, whereas control participants have no agent to evaluate, perceptions were assessed at the level of the collaborative platform, with or without such an agent, which allows both conditions to be compared on the same measures.

The present study

Taken together, these findings suggest that even when an AI agent is explicitly designed to facilitate collaboration, its effects on group communication and performance are not systematically observed, and group members may perceive its integration in the collaborative platform negatively, particularly if they interpret its interventions as an evaluation of their collaborative behaviors (Schlund & Zitek, 2024). Nonetheless, it is reasonable to hypothesize that an AI facilitation agent could help groups collaborate more effectively by prompting members to share the information needed to solve a collaborative problem efficiently.

H1.

Relative to groups working without the intervention of an AI facilitation agent, groups receiving recommendations from an AI facilitation agent will be more efficient on the collaborative task, solving it more quickly and with less communicative effort.

However, as suggested by recent findings (Cvetkovic et al., 2024; Houde et al., 2025; Schlund & Zitek, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), the benefits of AI intervention at the performance level may not necessarily translate into positive subjective experiences for group members, who may instead perceive the platform integrating such interventions negatively.

H2.

Relative to participants in groups working without the intervention of an AI facilitation agent, those in groups receiving recommendations from an AI facilitation agent will report more negative affective reactions and will perceive the platform as less acceptable, less supportive, and more intrusive.

Method

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Ethical approval was granted by the University Ethics Committee (approval no. 2026-057).

Participants

The initial sample comprised 217 second-year psychology students, who were randomly assigned to 57 groups of two to four members. Groups were then randomly assigned to one of two conditions: with or without the intervention of an AI agent during group discussions.

Thirty participants (14%) were excluded from the analyses. First, seven groups (25 participants) in the AI condition were excluded because the AI agent did not intervene (the discussion between members did not meet the criteria triggering an intervention). Second, five participants were excluded because they had already completed the task the previous year; their groups were retained when at least three members remained (five groups). The final sample therefore included 187 participants (165 women, 21 men, 1 unspecified; Mage = 20.24 years, SD = 3.85), distributed across 50 groups (25 per condition): 13 groups of three members and 37 groups of four members. Materials and Instruments

Digital Collaborative Platform

This study was conducted using the digital collaborative platform ECC'IPE (Enhancing Collaborative Competencies and Platform-Based Interventions), described in detail by Noël and collaborators (under review) and Michinov and collaborators (2026). The platform includes a synchronous text-based chat environment through which group members interacted to complete a collaborative collaborative task. This task was designed to simulate a disaster-survival scenario and to examine the benefits and challenges of collaborative work in a digital environment. Specifically, participants were asked to rank a set of objects according to their usefulness for survival following a hypothetical crash on the Moon (Hall & Watson, 1970).

Successful completion of the task required participants to share, discuss, and integrate distributed information in order to reach a collective decision. According to McGrath's (1984) circumplex model of group tasks, this activity can be classified as an intellective task, in which group members must collaboratively solve a problem that has a correct solution established by experts, although this solution is not readily identifiable to participants.

AI Agent Design and Training

An AI facilitation agent was designed to support groups in real time during ranking discussions conducted via a synchronous chat tool. The agent combined two components: an automatic message classifier and a decision policy that determined when to post a feedback message.

Message classifier. The classifier was a CamemBERT model (a language model pre-trained on French text) fine-tuned on a French-language dataset of ~10k messages extracted from prior sessions. Two independent coders annotated the messages into 15 behavioral categories organized along two dimensions of collaborative interaction: instrumental interaction (nine categories: providing information, requesting information, opinion on the ranking, coordination during the task, task planning, agreement, disagreement, time management, and off-topic discussion) and socio-affective interaction (six categories: encouragement, criticism, group socialization, apologies, negative emotions, and positive emotions). Inter-rater reliability was satisfactory, with a mean Cohen's kappa of .78 for instrumental categories and .88 for socio-affective categories. Because the rarest categories provided too few examples to be learned reliably, automatic classification focused on the six most frequent categories (providing information, requesting information, coordination, planning, opinion on the task, and agreement). The model reached a macro F1 of .79, an accuracy of .84, and a Cohen's kappa of .80 with the reference annotations. This agreement is comparable to the inter-rater reliability between the two human coders on the same six categories (κ = .81).

Decision policy.

At each new message, the policy determined whether the agent should post a fixed message encouraging members to share their ideas about the items to be ranked or remain silent. The policy was not defined in advance but learned through reinforcement learning. The agent was trained in a simulated environment modeling groups that differed in their responsiveness to feedback, with the objective of helping them reach and sustain sufficient information sharing. Information sharing was considered sufficient when the weighted proportion of information-providing messages in the recent context remained above 40% for several consecutive messages.

Procedure

The study was conducted as part of an undergraduate social psychology course on group dynamics. Data were collected during practical seminar sessions comprising approximately 30 students each. At the beginning of each session, a research assistant introduced the study and provided standardized instructions for completing the collaborative task. Each session lasted approximately two hours. During the first hour, participants completed the collaborative task on the platform and filled out an individual post-task questionnaire. The second hour was devoted to a debriefing session led by the regular teaching assistant, who discussed key theoretical concepts related to group dynamics related to the task experience.

Participants first watched a short-animated video presenting the survival scenario, followed by written instructions describing the task and the instructions. They initially completed the task individually by ranking the listed items from the most to the least important for survival after a hypothetical crash on the Moon. Participants were given up to 10 minutes to complete their individual ranking, although they could validate their responses before the time limit. Once a participant had submitted their ranking, a message appeared on the screen stating: "Please wait until all members of your group have completed the activity." After all group members had validated their individual rankings, participants accessed a shared collaborative interface. Depending on the experimental condition, groups completed the collaborative phase with or without the assistance of an AI facilitation agent. During this phase, group members discussed the task through the synchronous chat tool and were required to reach a consensus on a collective ranking of the items. They were given a maximum of 30 minutes to complete the collaborative task.

In the experimental condition (with AI facilitation agent), participants were informed that the platform integrated an AI-based chatbot that analyzed the group's interactions and proposed recommendations to assist group work. The agent monitored the messages exchanged in the text-based chat and, when the discussion met the criteria described below (see AI Agent Design and Training), posted a message encouraging members to share more information with one another ("Feel free to share your ideas about the items to be ranked"; originally in French). The agent thus intervened dynamically and in real time, and no recommendation was displayed outside of these interventions.

In the control condition (without AI facilitation agent), no mention was made of an AI agent, and no intervention was delivered during the discussion. To ensure comparability with the experimental condition, the same recommendation regarding information sharing was provided, but statically rather than adaptively: it was presented before participants began the collaborative task and remained permanently visible in a window above the chat throughout the collaboration phase (see also Hémon et al., 2024; Michinov et al., 2026). Thus, the platform was identical across conditions except for how the recommendation on information sharing was delivered: dynamically by an AI facilitation agent in the experimental condition, or statically in a window above the chat in the control condition without any AI agent.

Finally, participants individually completed an online questionnaire assessing their perception of the platform in terms of acceptability, perceived support, sense of intrusion, and affective reactions, as well as a manipulation check on the perceived involvement of an AI agent in the chat.

Measures

Perceived Involvement of an AI agent

A single-item manipulation check assessed the extent to which participants perceived an AI agent as involved in the chat: "To what extent did it seem to you that an AI agent was involved in the chat?" (1 = not at all,7 = completely).

Objective Outcomes

Communication. The number of messages sent in the chat by each participant was automatically counted by the platform.

Collaborative task completion time. The time each group spent to perform the collaborative task was automatically counted in minutes.

Collective efficiency.

It was assessed using a score ranging from 0 to 112, with higher scores indicating greater accuracy in the collective ranking. Because the AI agent intervention was designed to facilitate information sharing under time constraints, efficiency was considered a more appropriate outcome measure than performance alone. The collective efficiency index captures the extent to which groups were able to achieve accurate solutions while minimizing the time required to reach them. To capture both the accuracy of the solution and the speed with which it was reached, a normalized collective efficiency index was computed. Efficiency was defined as the ratio of group performance to task completion time (in minutes). To facilitate interpretation and comparison across conditions, the efficiency ratio was then standardized to a 100-point scale using the ratio between the maximum attainable task score (112) and the maximum allotted completion time (30 min) as a theoretical benchmark. The normalization constant corresponded to the maximum attainable performance divided by the maximum time allowed for task completion (112/30 = 3.73). Thus, the index was calculated as follows:

Normalized collective efficiency index = (Group Score/Completion Time ) 3.73 × 100

Higher scores indicate greater efficiency, reflecting the ability of groups to achieve higher performance in less time.

Subjective User Perceptions

Because participants in the control condition did not interact with an AI agent, items referred to the interventions provided on the platform rather than to the agent itself, allowing the same measures to be compared across conditions.

Perceived acceptability.

Acceptability was assessed using a four-item scale adapted from the French version of the UTAUT2-based questionnaire (Pagé et al., 2023; Venkatesh et al., 2012). (e.g., "This platform helps develop teamwork skills"; "This platform enabled me to work effectively as a team"). Responses were given on a 7-point Likert-type scale. Internal consistency was good (ω = .87).

Perceived support. Perceived support was assessed using an 8-item scale based on the literature and specifically developed for the present study. Their wording was iteratively refined with the assistance of ChatGPT (OpenAI) and reviewed by the research team prior to data collection. Items evaluated the extent to which participants perceived potential interventions on the platform as useful, reliable, and helpful for completing the collaborative task (e.g., "The support provided by the platform was useful for the group work"; "The support provided by the platform improved the quality of the group work"). Responses were given on a 7-point Likert-type scale. Internal consistency was excellent (ω = .92).

Perceived intrusiveness. Perceived intrusiveness was measured using a 3-item scale developed for the present study similarly to the perceived support measure. Items assessed the extent to which participants perceived the platform as disruptive or interfering with group interaction (e.g., "I felt that the platform was intrusive during the group work"; "During the group work, I felt that the platform disrupted our exchanges"). Responses were given on a 7point Likert-type scale. Internal consistency was acceptable (ω = .79).

Affect. Participants' affective experience during the collaborative task was assessed using a 16-item checklist adapted from the Multi-Affect Adjective Checklist (MAACL; Zuckerman, 1960). The checklist comprised eight positive affect adjectives (e.g., happy, relaxed, enthusiastic) and eight negative affect adjectives (e.g., anxious, frustrated, disappointed).

Participants selected the adjectives that best described how they felt while working in the group. Positive and negative affect scores were obtained by summing the number of selected adjectives within each category (range = 0-8).

Exploratory Analyses of AI Agent Interventions

To better understand how AI facilitation may have influenced group interactions, an exploratory analysis was conducted on the AI agent interventions that occurred in the experimental condition. Each intervention was examined by coding all interactions occurring during the 3-minute period preceding and following the intervention. A 3-minute observation window was selected to capture the interactional context surrounding each AI agent intervention. This interval was considered sufficiently long to allow group members to react to the recommendation and potentially adjust their communication, while remaining short enough to minimize the influence of subsequent discussion unrelated to the intervention.

Given the average collaborative task duration of approximately 16 minutes, a 3-minute window represented about one-fifth of the discussion, providing a reasonable compromise between capturing the short-term conversational consequences of an AI recommendation and preserving a localized analysis of the interaction sequence. A trained rater coded each interaction into one of six categories: Providing Information, Requesting Information, Coordination, Planning, Opinion on the Task, and Agreement. The reliability of this coding scheme had previously been established (see AI Agent Design and Training)).

Results

Analytic Strategy

Linear or generalized linear mixed models were conducted using Jamovi 2.6.44 to account for the nested structure of the data (individuals within groups). Experimental condition (AI facilitation agent vs. control) was entered as a fixed effect and group as a random effect.

Model specifications were adapted to the distribution of each dependent variable after checking distributional assumptions. Outcomes measured at the group level (task completion time and collective efficiency) were analyzed separately using parametric or non-parametric tests because only one observation was available per group.

Perceived Involvement of an AI agent

A linear mixed model was conducted to examine the effect of condition (with vs. without AI facilitation agent) on the perceived involvement of an AI agent in the chat, with group as a random effect. Condition had a significant effect,  = 1.07, SE = 0.23, t (47.4) = 4.66, p < .001: participants in the AI condition reported higher perceived involvement of an AI agent (M = 2.76, SD = 1.57) than those in the control condition (M = 1.69, SD = 1.10). Although absolute scores were low in both conditions, this difference indicates that the manipulation was detected, which is consistent with the brief and conditional nature of the agent's interventions.

Objective Outcomes Communication

A Poisson generalized linear mixed model with log link was conducted to examine the effect of condition on the number of messages sent by participants in the text-based chat, with group included as a random effect. The analysis revealed a significant effect of condition, β = -0.24, SE = 0.12, Z = -2.04, p = .042. In line with Hypothesis 1, it appeared that participants in the AI condition sent fewer messages, indicating reduced communicative effort (M = 19.62, SD = 11.68), compared to the control condition (M = 22.98, SD = 10.47). To examine whether the effect of condition on communication behavior was independent of individual task performance, we included individual performance as a covariate. The effect of condition remained significant, β = -0.23, SE = 0.11, Z = -2.09, p = .037, suggesting that reduced communicative effort in the AI condition cannot be explained by individual performance differences alone.

Collaborative Task Completion Time

As predicted in hypothesis 1, groups assisted by the AI facilitation agent completed the collaborative task significantly faster (M = 14.50 min, SD = 4.81) than groups in the control condition (M = 17.43 min, SD = 4.81), Welch's t(48.00) = 2.15, p = .037, d = 0.61, mean difference = 2.93 min, 95% CI [0.19, 5.66].

Collective Efficiency

Defined as normalized group performance relative to task completion time, it was analyzed at the group level. Because efficiency scores were not normally distributed, a Kruskal-Wallis's test was conducted. Exploratory Analyses of AI Agent Interventions

The AI facilitation agent generated a total of 39 interventions across the 25 groups (M = 1.56 and SD = 0.8). Specifically, the agent intervened once in 16 groups, twice in four groups, and three times in five groups.

As shown in

Table 1, the overall volume of interactions remained relatively stable (258 vs. 268 coded interactions), suggesting that AI interventions did not substantially alter the overall level of communication. However, noticeable changes emerged in the nature of the interactions. Following an AI agent intervention, requests for information increased from 20 to 28 occurrences (+28.6%), and task-related opinions increased from 15 to 22 (+31.8%).

Expressions of agreement also became more frequent, increasing from 71 to 85 occurrences (+16.5%). By contrast, the frequency of information provision decreased from 88 to 73 occurrences (-20.6%), although remaining at a high level, and planning-related interactions decreased from 17 to 12 (-41.7%). Coordination-related interactions remained virtually unchanged (47 vs. 48 occurrences, +2.1 %).

Taken together, these descriptive trends suggest that AI agent recommendations may have modified the thread of the discussion rather than increasing the overall amount of communication. Somewhat unexpectedly, although the agent was programmed to encourage information sharing when insufficient exchange of task-relevant information was detected, episodes of information provision became less frequent following its interventions even if it remained at a high level. At the same time, interaction episodes involving information requests, task-related opinions, and expressions of agreement increased. Although these observations are descriptive, they suggest that AI recommendations may have prompted groups to move beyond simply providing information toward evaluating the information available during collaborative problem solving. These findings are consistent with the higher collective efficiency observed in the AI condition. A possible explanation for the divergence between objective outcomes and subjective perceptions is that participants perceived the AI agent's interventions as externally imposed without providing any explanation about its functioning (Do et al., 2023). Although the agent helped groups complete the task more efficiently, this may have led participants to judge the platform as less supportive and less acceptable. This interpretation concerns participants' evaluation of the platform rather than the emotional experience it generated, since the platform was not perceived as more intrusive and did not elicit more negative affective reactions than the condition without an AI facilitation agent. It is consistent with research showing that AI agents designed to support collaboration can improve collective performance while reducing users' subjective experience, particularly when their interventions are perceived as dominating group interactions (Houde et al., 2025). However, the small number of interventions delivered by the agent in the present study (one to three per group) does not seem to reflect such dominating behavior, which is also consistent with the low perceived involvement of the agent. The unfavorable evaluations are thus more likely to stem from the perceived external or evaluative nature of the interventions, rather than from their frequency or intrusiveness, which is consistent with the absence of difference in perceived intrusion between conditions. Participants may also have interpreted the recommendations as a sign that their discussions were being monitored and evaluated (Schlund & Zitek, 2024), even though the interventions ultimately improved collective efficiency.

These findings contribute to the literature on AI-supported collaboration by showing that subjective evaluations of an AI-supported platform and the functional effectiveness of its facilitation agent do not necessarily converge. They contrast with those of Gunasekaran et al. (2026), who reported that participants rated an AI facilitation agent most favorably when it provided combined cognitive and affective feedback. One possible explanation is that the interventions in their study addressed both task performance and socio-emotional engagement, whereas the agent in the present study focused exclusively on information sharing, which participants may have perceived as evaluative rather than supportive.

A recent systematic review reported little evidence that AI facilitation agents change the overall volume of group communication (Ba et al., 2026; see also Hu et al., 2025). In the present study, although groups with the agent showed less communicative effort overall, the descriptive analyses did not reveal a change in interaction volume around the interventions.

The contribution of the agent may therefore lie less in altering the quantity of communication than in reshaping its functional organization, by redirecting discussions toward requesting and evaluating task-relevant information. This qualitative shift may account, at least in part, for the observed gain in collective efficiency.

Limitations and Perspectives

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample consisted exclusively of psychology students and was predominantly female (88%), which limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations and professional contexts. Second, the experimental manipulation combined two features, the announced presence of an AI agent and the adaptive, real-time delivery of the recommendation, whereas control groups received the same recommendation statically. The design therefore does not allow us to disentangle the effect of the AI nature of the agent from that of the timing and adaptivity of the recommendation.

Third, perceptions were assessed at the level of the platform rather than the agent itself, since control participants had no agent to evaluate. Fourth, the interactions analyzed before and after the interventions were descriptive and conducted at the group level, which precludes causal conclusions about mechanisms. Finally, the study relied on a single collaborative task completed in a single session, and we ignore whether the observed effects would persist over time or generalize to other tasks and settings.

Future research should address these limitations by using more diverse samples, validated measures, and designs that independently manipulate the label of the agent (AI vs.

human) and the adaptivity of its interventions. Such studies could also distinguish between an AI facilitation agent and an AI team member (Zercher et al., 2025). Whereas an AI facilitation agent primarily supports and structures group processes, by organizing exchanges, prompting participation, and formulating recommendations, an AI team member is conceived as a full participant in the collaboration, contributing directly to the group's work by engaging in discussion. Comparing these two roles would clarify which form of AI involvement best supports group collaboration while remaining acceptable to group members. Finally, combining automated conversation analysis with speaker identification could determine whether the interventions primarily influence low contributors, highly active members, or all group members.

Conclusion

The present findings have important implications for the design of human-AI collaborative systems. One of the main advantages of AI facilitation agents is their ability to provide adaptive, real-time guidance to multiple groups simultaneously, which is difficult for a human facilitator to achieve in authentic collaborative settings, although the present study did not compare the agent with a human facilitator. By continuously monitoring interaction patterns and intervening only when needed, AI facilitation agents may offer scalable support that enhances collective efficiency. At the same time, improving the objective effectiveness of AI facilitation is not sufficient: the mere presence of an AI agent in a group is not enough, and the way group members interact with it and perceive its interventions matters just as much.

Making the rationale behind recommendations more transparent and giving users greater control over the interventions may improve the acceptability of the agent while preserving its benefits. In closing, the challenge for future AI facilitation agents may not be to make groups work harder or communicate more, but to help them communicate more effectively while making this support acceptable to group members.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to examine whether introducing an AI facilitation agent into collaborative groups would influence objective group outcomes (i.e., efficiency and communication) and group members' subjective perceptions of the platform. The findings supported H1 and partially supported H2, revealing a clear dissociation between these two types of outcomes. Groups assisted by the AI facilitation agent completed the collaborative task more quickly and with less communicative effort, yet participants in this condition evaluated the platform less favorably, reporting lower acceptability and lower perceived support than those in the control condition. Importantly, the platform was not perceived as more intrusive, nor did it elicit more negative affective reactions. Together, these findings suggest that an AI facilitation agent may enhance collaborative efficiency without making the platform that integrates it appear more acceptable or supportive to group members.

The divergence between objective outcomes and subjective perceptions suggests that the impact of an AI facilitation agent on collective efficiency does not necessarily depend on users' evaluations of the platform. Exploratory descriptive analyses suggest that, rather than making groups communicate more, the agent may have made their communication more functional, helping groups identify and integrate relevant information more efficiently and thereby reducing the communicative effort required to solve the task. Compared with the three minutes preceding the agent's interventions, information requests, task-related opinions, and expressions of agreement became more frequent in the three minutes that followed, whereas information provision and planning episodes became less frequent. Although descriptive, these changes suggest that the interventions may have redirected the discussion toward the evaluation of information rather than the introduction of new information.
