Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is increasingly recognised as involving persistent difficulties in emotion regulation alongside well-established executive dysfunction. However, the cognitive mechanisms linking executive control impairments to emotional dysregulation remain insufficiently integrated within existing theoretical models. The present conceptual review proposes that attentional bias toward emotionally salient stimuli represents an intermediate cognitive-affective mechanism linking executive dysfunction to emotional dysregulation in ADHD. Drawing on evidence from attentional processing, executive control, affective neuroscience, and neurobiological models of ADHD, the review argues that impaired top-down prefrontal regulatory systems reduce the ability to suppress stimulus-driven attentional capture by emotionally salient information. Consequently, biased attentional allocation may amplify emotional reactivity, prolong engagement with negative affective stimuli, and interfere with adaptive emotion regulation processes. Neurobiologically, disrupted functional connectivity between prefrontal regulatory networks and salience-processing systems, including the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, and ventral striatum, may contribute to both attentional and emotional dysregulation in ADHD. The review further discusses theoretical and clinical implications of this framework, including the potential value of interventions targeting attentional control and emotional attentional processing. Limitations of the current evidence base and directions for future longitudinal and neurobiological research are also considered. Conceptualising attentional bias within a broader neurocognitive framework may provide a more integrated and mechanistically coherent account of emotional dysregulation in ADHD.

1. Introduction

Attentional bias refers to the preferential allocation of attention toward emotionally relevant stimuli, particularly threatening or negative information, over neutral inputs (Cisler & Koster, 2010; De Houwer & Koster, 2023). This bias is typically reflected in enhanced orienting, sustained attention, and difficulty disengaging from emotionally relevant stimuli, and has been widely observed across both experimental and clinical populations (Armstrong & Olatunji, 2012; Clauss et al., 2022). Attentional bias is implicated in the development and persistence of psychopathology, particularly anxiety and related disorders, with increasing evidence extending to depression and stress-related conditions (Shackman et al., 2016; Bar-Haim et al., 2007; Van Bockstaele et al., 2014). More broadly, attentional bias is increasingly recognised as a transdiagnostic cognitive-affective process implicated across multiple forms of psychopathology.

Importantly, contemporary perspectives conceptualise attentional bias not merely as a behavioural tendency, but as a dynamic cognitive mechanism emerging from interactions between attentional systems, executive control processes, and emotional reactivity. (Okon-Singer et al., 2015; Heeren et al., 2015).

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised not only by inattention and hyperactivity, but also by deficits in executive functioning and persistent emotion regulation difficulties (Shaw et al., 2014; Christiansen et al., 2019). Increasing evidence supports the view that emotional dysregulation represents a core feature of ADHD across the lifespan, contributing significantly to functional impairment and comorbid psychopathology (Bunford et al., 2015; Soler-Gutiérrez et al., 2023). From a cognitive perspective, these emotional difficulties have been closely linked to impairments in executive control processes, particularly inhibitory control, attentional shifting, and top-down regulatory processes supported by prefrontal systems (Rubia, 2018; Groves et al., 2022).

Notably, emerging evidence suggests that attentional abnormalities in ADHD may extend beyond general attentional deficits to include altered patterns of attentional allocation toward emotionally salient stimuli, suggesting a potential role for attentional bias in the disorder (Cremone et al., 2018; Mayer et al., 2022). Although growing evidence supports interactions among attentional, executive, and emotional dysfunction in ADHD, the mechanisms through which these processes influence one another remain insufficiently integrated within existing theoretical frameworks (Grossman & Avital, 2023; Carvalho, 2026). It is important to acknowledge at the outset that ADHD is a highly heterogeneous condition and that no single neurocognitive mechanism is likely to account for the full range of emotional difficulties observed across individuals. Contemporary accounts increasingly recognise that self-regulation involves multiple interacting top-down and bottom-up processes, suggesting that emotional difficulties in ADHD may arise through several distinct pathways rather than a single underlying mechanism (Faraone et al., 2021; Nigg, 2017). Against this backdrop of neuropsychological heterogeneity, the present review focuses on one specific and theoretically coherent pathway, proposing that attentional bias functions as an intermediate cognitive-affective mechanism linking executive dysfunction to emotional dysregulation in ADHD. While alternative mechanisms may also contribute to emotional difficulties, examining this pathway may help clarify how deficits in cognitive control influence the allocation of attention to emotionally salient information and, in turn, contribute to dysregulated emotional responding.

Despite well-established evidence linking executive dysfunction and emotional dysregulation in ADHD, the specific cognitive mechanisms underlying their interaction remain insufficiently integrated within existing theoretical frameworks (Faraone et al., 2021; Nigg, 2017). In particular, the role of attentional allocation toward emotionally salient stimuli has been comparatively underexplored within integrative models, despite growing evidence suggesting its potential to bridge cognitive control deficits and affective dysregulation (Mueller et al., 2017; Morales et al., 2016). Addressing this gap may provide a more mechanistic understanding of how cognitive and emotional dysfunction interact in ADHD.

Building on these considerations, the present article advances a conceptual integration of attentional bias within the broader neurocognitive framework of ADHD. Specifically, we propose that attentional bias toward emotionally salient stimuli functions as an intermediate cognitive-affective mechanism linking executive dysfunction to emotional dysregulation in ADHD. Drawing on advances in cognitive neuroscience and contemporary models of psychopathology, this review synthesises findings across attentional processing, executive control, and emotion regulation domains to advance an integrative explanatory framework (Pessoa, 2017; Cludius et al., 2020). In doing so, the article moves beyond descriptive accounts of isolated deficits and instead adopts a mechanism-based perspective, whereby impairments in top-down control increase susceptibility to stimulus-driven attentional capture, which may subsequently contribute to dysregulated emotional responding (Clauss et al., 2025; Faul et al., 2024). By integrating these previously fragmented lines of research, this paper aims to clarify underlying mechanisms and highlight implications for future research and intervention development in ADHD.

2. Attentional Bias as a Cognitive Mechanism

Attentional bias is increasingly conceptualised as a dynamic cognitive-affective process arising from interactions between bottom-up salience-driven systems and top-down attentional control mechanisms (Gaspelin & Luck, 2018; Anderson, 2019). Bottom-up salience-driven processes prioritise emotionally salient stimuli, particularly negative or threatening cues, due to their adaptive relevance for rapid detection of biologically significant information (Chaisilprungraung et al., 2024; Ngai et al., 2025). In contrast, top-down attentional control systems regulate attentional allocation by facilitating goaldirected behaviour and suppressing distracting or task-irrelevant information (Burgoyne & Engle, 2020; Favieri et al., 2024; Badre, 2025). The balance between top-down and bottom-up processes determines the extent to which emotionally salient stimuli capture and sustain attention (Trajkovic et al., 2025; Sawamura et al., 2025).

Disruptions in the balance between top-down and bottom-up processes increase the influence of stimulus-driven processing on attentional allocation, leading to attentional capture and biased allocation toward salient stimuli (Luck et al., 2021; Wolfe, 2021). Empirical research has identified attentional bias as a multi-component process encompassing facilitated orienting toward emotional stimuli, difficulty in attentional disengagement, and sustained attentional engagement (Zhang et al., 2024; Watson et al., 2025; Robles et al., 2024). These components reflect distinct yet interacting stages of attentional processing and have been investigated using diverse experimental paradigms, including dot-probe tasks, visual search tasks, and eye-tracking methodologies (Saalwirth et al., 2024; Suslow et al., 2025). Notably, eye-tracking studies have provided valuable insights into the temporal dynamics of attentional allocation, demonstrating that attentional bias is not a static tendency but a temporally dynamic process (Zvielli et al., 2014; Treviño, 2025).

From a cognitive control perspective, attentional bias may reflect impairments in top-down attentional regulation that reduce the capacity to regulate the influence of emotionally salient stimuli (Shi et al., 2019; MacLeod et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2020). Executive control systems play a critical role in maintaining goal-directed attention and inhibiting distraction; when these systems are compromised, attentional selection becomes increasingly driven by bottom-up salience processing (Walsh et al., 2019; Geng et al., 2017). As a result, attentional systems may become increasingly sensitive to emotionally salient cues and less flexible in disengaging attention from them (Samson et al., 2026; Fan et al., 2026; Adam et al., 2024). This framework suggests that attentional bias may emerge from reduced top-down regulation and may contribute to emotional dysregulation and maladaptive behavioural responses (Bellato et al., 2020).

3. Executive Dysfunction and Top-Down Dysregulation in ADHD

Executive dysfunction is widely recognised as a central feature of ADHD and a primary neurocognitive mechanism underlying attentional and behavioural dysregulation in the disorder (Rubia, 2018; Kofler et al., 2019). Individuals with ADHD frequently exhibit impairments across multiple executive domains, including inhibitory control, attentional shifting, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, all of which are critical for goal-directed regulation and adaptive behavioural control (Silverstein et al., 2020; Song et al., 2023). These impairments have been consistently linked to dysfunction within frontostriatal and frontoparietal neural networks, particularly within prefrontal systems involved in top-down attentional regulation (Isaac et al., 2024; Parlatini et al., 2024).

From a top-down control perspective, ADHD may be conceptualised as a disorder characterised by reduced capacity for top-down regulation of attentional allocation in accordance with internal goals and task demands (Cortese et al., 2012; Dziemian et al., 2025; Osborne et al., 2026). When executive control mechanisms are compromised, attentional processing becomes increasingly dominated by stimulusdriven salience signals, resulting in heightened distractibility and reduced attentional stability (Klein et al., 2025; Tseng et al., 2013). Importantly, these difficulties are not restricted to neutral attentional processes but appear to extend to emotionally salient information, suggesting that individuals with ADHD may experience greater vulnerability to emotional attentional capture (Karalunas et al., 2020; Graziano & Garcia, 2016; De Ronda et al., 2024).

Recent theoretical perspectives further propose that deficits in executive and self-regulatory processes may contribute directly to emotional dysregulation in ADHD by impairing the flexible regulation of emotional responses in emotionally salient contexts (Slobodin et al., 2025). Within this framework, deficits in executive control may contribute to heightened emotional reactivity and difficulties regulating emotional responses to emotionally salient information, thereby increasing vulnerability to persistent negative affective states in ADHD (Poznyak & Debbané, 2025). Consequently, attentional bias may represent an intermediate cognitive-affective mechanism through which executive dysfunction contributes to emotional dysregulation in ADHD (Pozo-Rodríguez et al., 2026).

4. Attentional Bias and Emotional Dysregulation in ADHD

Emotional dysregulation in ADHD is characterised by heightened emotional reactivity, impaired emotional control, and difficulties flexibly regulating emotional responses in accordance with contextual demands (Astenvald et al., 2025). Although emotional dysregulation was historically viewed as secondary to the core symptoms of ADHD, accumulating evidence supports its role as a clinically significant and functionally impairing dimension of ADHD (Cortese et al., 2025; Goh et al., 2024).

Individuals with ADHD frequently experience rapid emotional escalation, irritability, emotional impulsivity, and persistent negative affective states, all of which contribute substantially to interpersonal, academic, and occupational dysfunction (Callovini et al., 2024; Yue et al., 2022).

Within contemporary neurocognitive frameworks, attentional bias may represent an important cognitive-affective mechanism contributing to emotional dysregulation. Emotional stimuli that preferentially capture and sustain attention may amplify emotional reactivity by promoting prolonged processing of negative or threat-related information (Todd et al., 2012; Basanovic et al., 2024). In individuals with ADHD, impaired top-down executive regulation and attentional control may increase vulnerability to emotional attentional capture, thereby amplifying emotional reactivity and prolonging recovery from negative affective states (Petrovic & Castellanos, 2016; Callovini et al., 2024).

Consequently, attentional bias may contribute to the persistence and escalation of emotional symptoms in ADHD through sustained attentional allocation toward emotionally salient information (Everaert et al., 2020a; Hirsch et al., 2018).

Several theoretical perspectives further suggest that impairments in attentional disengagement may represent an important mechanism underlying emotional dysregulation in ADHD. Difficulties disengaging attention from negative affective stimuli may prolong emotional processing, thereby reducing cognitive flexibility and interfering with adaptive emotion regulation strategies (Marchewka et al., 2016; Materna et al., 2019). This process may be especially relevant in ADHD, where deficits in inhibitory control and attentional flexibility already compromise adaptive attentional regulation (Willcutt et al., 2005; Sjöwall et al., 2013). Under these conditions, emotionally salient stimuli may exert disproportionate influence over cognitive and emotional processing in individuals with ADHD, thereby contributing to irritability, frustration, emotional impulsivity, and persistent negative affective states (Marques et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022).

Emerging evidence suggests that attentional bias and emotional dysregulation may interact through reciprocal cognitive-affective processes, whereby heightened emotional reactivity increases vigilance toward emotionally salient or threat-related cues, while persistent attentional prioritisation of such stimuli may further reinforce maladaptive emotional reactivity over time (Segal & Gobin, 2022; Jenness et al., 2016). From this perspective, attentional bias may contribute to the maintenance of emotional dysregulation in ADHD by persistently directing attentional resources toward emotionally provocative cues and interfering with adaptive emotional disengagement. Such reciprocal cognitive-affective mechanisms may help explain the developmental persistence of emotional dysregulation in ADHD (Waters et al., 2010; Ursache et al., 2013).

Collectively, these findings support the view that attentional bias may represent one cognitive-affective mechanism linking executive dysfunction to emotional dysregulation in ADHD, reflecting interactions among attentional allocation, cognitive control, and emotional information processing (Everaert et al., 2023; Leung et al., 2022). Rather than reflecting isolated cognitive and emotional impairments, attentional control deficits, biased attentional allocation, and emotional dysregulation may represent interrelated processes operating within a broader self-regulatory system involving salience processing, executive-attentional regulation, and emotion-regulation mechanisms (Eysenck et al., 2007; Ince et al., 2023; Naim et al., 2022). Understanding these interactions may therefore help inform future intervention approaches targeting attentional regulation, emotional processing, and executive control in ADHD (Gabrieli et al., 2026; Kim & Jung, 2025).

5. Neurobiological Mechanisms Underlying Attentional Bias and Emotional Dysregulation in ADHD

Contemporary neurobiological models increasingly conceptualise ADHD as a disorder involving largescale dysregulation across distributed neural networks involved in attentional control, salience processing, and emotional regulation (Norman et al., 2022; Vaidya & Stollstorff, 2008). Rather than reflecting isolated executive impairments, ADHD appears to involve disrupted interactions between prefrontal regulatory systems and cortico-striatal-limbic networks involved in emotional and motivational salience detection (Posner et al., 2020; Hoogman et al., 2022). These abnormalities may contribute to altered attentional allocation toward emotionally salient information and impaired regulation of emotional responses, thereby providing a neurobiological framework linking executive dysfunction, attentional bias, and emotional dysregulation in ADHD (Pessoa, 2023; Mather & Sutherland, 2011).

At the neural level, top-down attentional regulation is primarily mediated by prefrontal and frontoparietal control networks, particularly the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and inferior frontal regions implicated in inhibitory control and goal-directed attentional regulation (Arnsten, 2020; Cai et al., 2014). Neuroimaging studies consistently demonstrate structural and functional abnormalities within these networks in individuals with ADHD, including reduced functional connectivity, delayed cortical maturation, and altered activation patterns during tasks requiring executive and attentional control (Castellanos & Aoki, 2016; Cai et al., 2021; Shaw et al., 2015). Such impairments may reduce the capacity to maintain goal-directed attentional control and suppress interference from emotionally salient distractors, thereby increasing susceptibility to stimulusdriven attentional processing (van Moorselaar & Slagter, 2020; Noonan et al., 2016).

In contrast, bottom-up salience processing is strongly associated with limbic and paralimbic regions, particularly the amygdala, ventral striatum, insula, and broader salience network regions involved in emotional evaluation and rapid detection of biologically relevant stimuli (Menon, 2023; McTeague et al., 2016). Emerging evidence suggests that individuals with ADHD exhibit altered neural responsivity within these systems, particularly during emotional processing tasks involving negative or rewardrelated stimuli (Herrmann et al., 2010; Plichta & Scheres, 2014). Increased amygdala reactivity and abnormal salience network functioning may heighten attentional capture by emotionally salient cues, especially under conditions of reduced prefrontal regulatory control (Iordan & Dolcos, 2017; Zhou et al., 2020). Consequently, emotionally provocative stimuli may exert disproportionate influence over attentional allocation and emotional responding in ADHD (Shushakova et al., 2018; Musser et al., 2013).

Importantly, recent network-based perspectives suggest that emotional dysregulation in ADHD may emerge not from isolated regional dysfunction, but from disrupted communication between executive control systems and emotion-processing networks (Cai et al., 2018; Francx et al., 2015). Inefficient functional connectivity between prefrontal regulatory regions and limbic structures may impair the ability to flexibly disengage from emotionally salient stimuli and regulate emotional responses following emotional activation (Ochsner et al., 2012; Li et al., 2025). Within this framework, attentional bias may represent a behavioural manifestation of broader neural dysregulation involving impaired topdown regulation of salience-driven emotional processing (Comte et al., 2016; Ince et al., 2025).

Neurophysiological evidence further supports this interpretation. Electrophysiological and eye-tracking studies indicate that individuals with ADHD display altered early-stage attentional responses to emotional stimuli, including abnormalities in attentional orienting, sustained attention, and inhibitory processing during emotional tasks (Raz & Dan, 2015; Manoli et al., 2024). These findings suggest that attentional bias in ADHD may emerge across multiple stages of information processing, from early automatic attentional capture to later stages of executive regulation. Such abnormalities may contribute to persistent emotional responding by increasing sustained attention toward emotionally salient information and reducing adaptive disengagement mechanisms (Comte et al., 2016; Manoli et al., 2024; Raz & Dan, 2015).

Collectively, these neurobiological findings support the view that attentional bias in ADHD may arise from dysregulated interactions between executive control systems and salience-processing networks.

Reduced prefrontal regulatory control, heightened salience responsivity, and impaired functional connectivity may collectively increase vulnerability to emotionally driven attentional capture and sustained emotional processing. Consequently, attentional bias may represent a neurocognitive mechanism through which executive dysfunction contributes to emotional dysregulation in ADHD, integrating attentional, emotional, and neural dysfunction within a unified self-regulatory framework (Shaw et al., 2014; Comte et al., 2016; Li et al., 2025). 6. Theoretical and Clinical Implications

The present review proposes that attentional bias may represent an important cognitive-affective mechanism linking executive dysfunction to emotional dysregulation in ADHD. By integrating findings across attentional processing, executive control, emotional regulation, and neurobiological research, the proposed framework extends beyond traditional models that conceptualise cognitive and emotional impairments in ADHD as relatively independent domains (Shaw et al., 2014; Posner et al., 2014). Rather than viewing emotional dysregulation as secondary to executive dysfunction, the current model suggests that impaired top-down executive regulation may increase vulnerability to emotionally salient information, thereby contributing to biased attentional allocation and persistent affective dysregulation (Shaw et al., 2014; Petrovic & Castellanos, 2016). From a theoretical perspective, this framework may help clarify inconsistencies within existing models of ADHD by providing a more integrative account of the interaction between attentional and emotional processes. Although executive dysfunction has long been considered central to ADHD, emotional dysregulation has often remained insufficiently incorporated within dominant neurocognitive theories of the disorder (Barkley, 2015; Petrovic & Castellanos, 2016). Conceptualising attentional bias as an intermediate mechanism may therefore help bridge cognitive-control deficits and emotional symptoms within a broader self-regulatory framework.

Such an approach aligns with emerging transdiagnostic perspectives emphasising dynamic interactions among attention, emotion, and cognitive control systems across psychopathology (Mareva & Holmes, 2019; Menon et al., 2023).

It is important to acknowledge, however, that attentional bias toward emotionally salient stimuli may not represent the sole pathway through which emotional dysregulation emerges in ADHD. Integrating attentional bias into broader neurocognitive models of ADHD may therefore help advance current understanding of the mechanisms underlying emotional dysregulation and support the development of more targeted and mechanistically informed intervention approaches (Shaw et al., 2014; Posner et al., 2020).

7. Limitations and Future Directions

Despite growing evidence linking executive dysfunction, attentional abnormalities, and emotional dysregulation in ADHD, several important limitations remain within the current literature. First, relatively few studies have directly examined attentional bias in ADHD within an integrated cognitiveaffective framework. Much of the existing evidence has been derived from broader transdiagnostic research or from studies primarily focused on anxiety, depression, or emotional processing more generally (Everaert et al., 2020a; McTeague et al., 2016). Consequently, the precise role of biased attentional processing in the development and maintenance of emotional dysregulation in ADHD remains insufficiently established.

A further limitation concerns methodological heterogeneity across attentional bias research. Existing studies employ diverse experimental paradigms, including dot-probe tasks, emotional Stroop paradigms, visual search tasks, and eye-tracking methodologies, each capturing different components and temporal stages of attentional processing (Armstrong & Olatunji, 2012; Zvielli et al., 2014). Variability in stimulus characteristics, attentional measures, and task designs may contribute to inconsistent findings and complicate direct comparisons across studies. In addition, attentional bias appears to be dynamic and context-dependent rather than stable across individuals and situations, further increasing conceptual and methodological complexity (Zvielli et al., 2014; Treviño, 2025).

Importantly, much of the current evidence is cross-sectional, limiting the ability to establish causal or developmental relationships between executive dysfunction, attentional bias, and emotional dysregulation in ADHD. Although the present framework proposes reciprocal interactions between attentional and emotional processes, longitudinal research remains necessary to clarify developmental trajectories and determine whether attentional bias contributes directly to the persistence of emotional dysregulation over time. Such approaches may help clarify how attentional control, emotional reactivity, and executive functioning interact across developmental stages in ADHD (Ursache et al., 2013; Shaw et al., 2014).

The heterogeneity of ADHD also represents an important challenge for future research. Emotional dysregulation and attentional abnormalities vary considerably across individuals with ADHD and may be influenced by developmental stage, symptom presentation, comorbid psychopathology, medication status, and environmental factors (Karalunas et al., 2014; Faraone et al., 2021). Future research should therefore examine whether attentional bias mechanisms differ across ADHD subtypes and developmental periods and whether distinct patterns of attentional processing are associated with specific emotional or behavioural profiles.

Further neurobiological research is also needed to better understand the neural mechanisms underlying attentional bias and emotional dysregulation in ADHD. Multimodal approaches integrating neuroimaging, electrophysiological measures, eye-tracking methodologies, and computational modelling may provide more comprehensive insight into the dynamic interactions among attentional control systems, salience-processing networks, and emotional regulation processes (Pessoa, 2017; Menon, 2023). Such approaches may help identify more precise neurocognitive markers associated with emotional dysregulation and attentional dysfunction in ADHD.

8. Conclusion

The present review proposed a mechanistic framework in which attentional bias toward emotionally salient stimuli functions as an intermediate cognitive-affective mechanism linking executive dysfunction to emotional dysregulation in ADHD. Drawing on evidence across attentional processing, executive control, emotion regulation, and neurobiological research, the review argued that impairments in top-down executive regulation may increase vulnerability to stimulus-driven attentional capture, thereby amplifying emotional reactivity and impairing adaptive emotional regulation processes. Rather than conceptualising executive dysfunction and emotional dysregulation as largely independent symptom dimensions, the proposed framework positions biased attentional processing as a cognitively meaningful bridge between these domains within a broader self-regulatory system.

Neurobiological findings reviewed in the paper further support this account by suggesting that dysregulated interactions between prefrontal executive-control systems and salience-processing networks may contribute to both attentional and emotional abnormalities in ADHD. Reduced prefrontal regulatory control, heightened salience responsivity, and impaired cortico-limbic connectivity may collectively increase vulnerability to emotionally driven attentional capture and sustained emotional processing. Within this framework, attentional bias may therefore represent not only a cognitivebehavioural phenomenon but also a neurocognitive manifestation of broader dysregulation across executive-emotional brain systems. Conceptually, this perspective extends existing neurocognitive models of ADHD by providing a more integrated and mechanism-based account of how cognitivecontrol deficits may contribute to affective dysregulation. Although emerging evidence from attentional bias modification and cognitive control training paradigms appears promising, further experimental and clinical research is needed to evaluate their long-term effectiveness and applicability within ADHD populations (MacLeod & Mathews, 2012; Diamond & Ling, 2016). From a clinical perspective, the proposed framework suggests that interventions targeting attentional allocation, emotional attentional processing, and executive regulatory control may represent valuable complementary approaches alongside existing behavioural and pharmacological treatments for ADHD. However, given the predominantly cross-sectional and indirect nature of the current evidence base, further longitudinal and experimental research remains necessary to clarify causal pathways, developmental trajectories, and individual variability. Overall, conceptualising attentional bias within a broader neurocognitive framework may help advance current understanding of emotional dysregulation in ADHD by highlighting the dynamic interplay among attentional control, salience processing, and emotional regulation. Continued research within this framework may contribute to the refinement of theoretical models, identification of clinically relevant neurocognitive targets, and development of more precise and mechanism-informed intervention strategies for ADHD across the lifespan.
