Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

A significant body of research highlights physical and mental health inequities faced by autistic adults. However, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with autistic people are often underpowered, with this population considered 'difficult to recruit'. This systematic review aimed to examine recruitment and retention to RCTs with autistic adults, as well as synthesising strategies used to facilitate recruitment and retention. MEDLINE, PsycINFO, EMBASE, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases were systematically searched for RCTs recruiting autistic adults published to June 2023, with a second search conducted in December 2025. ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry were also searched. We report details for N=47 trials, including type of intervention and control, planned and achieved recruitment and retention rates, and strategies to maximise these. Included studies focused on improving e.g., social skills, cognition, mental health, quality of life, employment, and driving skills.

Findings are reported in a narrative synthesis, showing a proliferation of trials recruiting small samples, wide ranging recruitment and retention rates, and a paucity of information regarding target sample sizes and/or power calculations. Facilitators of recruitment and retention included integrating Patient and Public Involvement and co-production, involving family/carers, public advertising and accepting self-referrals, remote participation, and reasonable adjustments.

Plain English Summary

Autistic adults experience significant health inequalities. Clinical trials are considered 'gold standard' for generating research evidence to support treatments, but it is often assumed that autistic people would be difficult to recruit to trials, e.g., because they are not being reached, or don't want to participate, and that when they do start in a trial, they may be more likely to drop out before the end. This systematic review looked at how autistic adults are recruited and retained in clinical trials, and identified the strategies trial teams used to try and improve participation rates. We searched several major databases and clinical trial registries and identified 47 relevant trials on a wide range of topics, including social skills, cognition, mental health, quality of life, employment, and driving skills. Most trials only recruited relatively small numbers of participants, and rates of actual recruitment compared to initial targets, as well as rates of participants retained (i.e., staying in the study) rates varied considerably. Many trials also provided little information about what their target sample sizes had been, and whether they had recruited enough participants to produce reliable results. The review identified several factors that may support recruitment and retention, such as involving autistic people in the design and delivery of trials through patient and public involvement and co-production, engaging family members or carers, using public advertising whereby people can refer themselves, offering remote participation options, and making reasonable adjustments to accommodate participants' communication and sensory needs. Overall, the findings suggest that many trials recruiting autistic people are small, and reporting of crucial information is often incomplete. The review highlights several practical approaches that may help researchers recruit and retain autistic adults more effectively in future trials.

Introduction

It is well established that, compared to the general population, autistic adults suffer with higher rates of physical and mental health inequities, as well as a greater incidence of premature mortality (Croen et al., 2015; Lai, 2023; Rai et al., 2018; Weir et al., 2022). However, treatment interventions to address such health inequities require a strong evidence base, for example from successful randomised controlled trials (RCTs). Despite this need, high-quality, well-powered studies of effective health interventions for autistic adults are lacking (Linden et al., 2023), and have tended to rely on small sample sizes (Green & Garg, 2018).

Poor recruitment and retention are leading causes of RCTs failing to obtain reliable results within the allocated time and budget (Jacques et al., 2022). Large systematic reviews indicate psychosocial factors in hesitation to participate in trials, including concerns about risks and uncertainty, lack of trust in science and research, and practical, logistical issues related to accessibility of participation (Houghton et al., 2020; Ross et al., 1999; Sheridan et al., 2020). Determinants of trial participation and successful delivery are also context-and population-specific (Houghton et al., 2020). It has long been assumed that conducting RCTs with autistic adults may be difficult due to a perceived intolerance of the uncertainty inherent within trial processes (i.e., randomised treatment allocation and blinding), but recent research shows that, with suitable support, this may not be the case (Beasant et al., 2024; Norris et al., 2026; Realpe et al., 2026). This systematic review aims to examine whether RCTs with autistic adults have been able to effectively recruit and retain participants, as well as reported barriers to and facilitators of recruitment and retention. The findings will be important to clinicians and researchers in understanding effective strategies for conducting RCTs with this population, facilitating improvements in trial design, and eventually contributing to improved health and wellbeing for autistic people. Therefore, the aims of this systematic review are to: i) compare planned vs. actual recruitment and retention of participants in RCTs involving autistic adults; ii) explore barriers to trial participation; and iii) identify strategies used by trial teams to facilitate recruitment and retention.

Method

Systematic Review

The protocol was registered with PROSPERO, the online International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration CRD42023437893). The inclusion criteria were later amended to exclude studies with N<40 due to a large number of small RCTs identified in the initial search; otherwise, the review proceeded as intended. The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines for conducting systematic reviews (Page et al., 2021).

Search strategy

A systematic search of 6 biomedical databases was conducted on August 4 th 2023, with searches updated on December 10 th 2025 (Medline, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science). We also searched for completed RCTs on the ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO International Clinical Trials Registry platforms. We conducted a search to include reports on recruitment or retention strategies used by clinical trial teams via ORRCA (Online Resource for Research in Clinical triAls (Kearney et al., 2018 (Kearney et al., , 2022))), which contains published and ongoing work in the field of recruitment and retention research.

We did not restrict searches by publication date or language. Core concepts, synonyms, and Boolean-style search strings can be found in Supplemental Materials -A.

Selection Criteria

The inclusion/exclusion criteria were as follows:

Study type:

Included: 1.

Any empirical articles that describe RCTs involving autistic adults (independent of clinical phase), defined as work reporting on a clinical trial that involves: a. at least one intervention treatment and one control treatment; b. concurrent enrolment; c. follow-up of the intervention-and control-treated groups; d. and to which participants are randomly allocated to a group 2. Empirical articles reporting on strategies to improve recruitment or retention rates associated with the included RCTs Excluded: 1.

Observational/case-control studies; 2. qualitative studies (not associated with an RCT); 3. non-randomised studies; 4. mixed-methods studies; 5. cluster randomised controlled trials; 6. reviews, editorials, commentaries, letters, books, or book reviews.

Condition/domain studied

Included: RCTs recruiting autistic adults (autism, autism spectrum disorder, autistic disorder, Asperger's syndrome/disorder, pervasive developmental disorder-not otherwise specified).

Excluded: RCTs recruiting people with other neurodevelopmental conditions; and samples including people with different diagnoses, in which autistic people are only a part of the sample recruited, as well as studies that examined neurobiological mechanisms, including comparisons to non-autistic brain functioning; genetic profile or natural history of autism; or which compared autistic with non-autistic people as a control group.

Participant demographics

Included: 1.

Adults (defined in this study as age =>16); 2. with a diagnosis of autism (including but not limited to autism, autism spectrum disorder, autism spectrum condition, Aspergers, etc).

Excluded: 1.

Studies including children (<16 years old); 2. Studies of carers of autistic people (e.g., interventions for parents or teachers); 3. or studies with both child and adult samples.

Context

We were interested in completed RCTs, as well as those that were closed before reaching their recruitment target. We were primarily interested in data on expected and actual recruitment and retention rates. Where necessary, we consulted the trial protocol or linked publications.

Data extraction (selection and coding)

Four independent reviewers conducted selection and coding of studies. Study designs were determined using (Hong et al.'s (2018) algorithm for classifying study designs (see Figure 1).

Screening and full-text review

Figure 2 reports the flow of study identification and selection. Duplicate references were initially removed using the automatic features of the screening software system Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016) and then completed manually by a reviewer. Two reviewers then screened the title and abstract of each study independently in Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016). Abstracts in other languages than English were excluded, as well as trials registered on Iranian and Indian clinical trial registers as these could not be accessed.

Reviewers were blinded to each other's screening decisions until all screening was complete.

Discrepancies were discussed with the other two reviewers for a final decision on inclusion. A reviewer obtained the full text of the articles and assessed them against the eligibility criteria. We found full texts of articles in French, Hebrew, and Japanese that were excluded because there were no English translations available. Other reasons for inclusion and exclusion were annotated for each paper, with decisions finalised in discussion with all authors. A reviewer paired the main study paper with the trial registration where available, and any other associated publications (e.g., pilot, economic evaluation, etc) to create a list of eligible studies. Many included trials were early exploratory/pilot or proof-of-concept trials which were not designed as definitive trials powered to measure effectiveness of interventions. Therefore, we decided to remove small trials (recruiting N<40) at the full text screening stage, which often did not state a target N nor provide a rationale for required sample size (i.e., power calculations), and where issues related to recruitment or retention were seldom discussed, as well as ongoing trials that had not yet finished recruitment as it was not possible to calculate attrition rates nor see final CONSORT information. Quality assessment

Our primary interest in this methodological review was whether the included RCTs achieved their stated recruitment and retention targets, rather than other potential biases within the trial design. Therefore, no risk of bias assessment was conducted.

Evidence synthesis

A narrative synthesis without meta-analysis was applied because our focus was on recruitment and retention outcomes rather than primary or secondary outcomes. Also, variability in reporting of recruitment and retention rates meant our results were not amenable to meta-analysis. We catalogued and clustered data from different sources (e.g., journal articles, trial registry searches, other reports) around RCTs testing an intervention with autistic adults. We report planned and achieved recruitment and retention, and describe challenges faced and strategies used to improve participation. We also describe the intervention type, geographical distribution of trials, and information used for power calculations for the primary outcome where provided.

Results

Results 47 studies were included in this review, consisting of RCTs with N≥40 as a minimum, completed with a total of 3,682 autistic adults across 15 countries.

Study characteristics

Included studies were primarily non-pharmacological interventions, both pilot and/or feasibility trials (N trials=20) and full-scale RCTs (N trials=27), which tended to target features of autism, e.g., social communication (N trials=9); co-occurring mental health difficulties (N trials=16), employment (N trials=8), cognitive functioning (N trials=4), or other factors including one study each on: driving ability, travel skills, quality of life and mental health, quality of life and self-esteem, quality of life and autism understanding, self-determination, disability pride, transition to adulthood, social and adaptive skills, and social participation.

i) Planned vs. actual recruitment and retention

Included trials recruited between 40 and 223 participants (median=64). Attrition rates varied, from 55% (recruited N=40; Andres-Garriz et al., 2025) to 0% (recruited N=40; e.g., Ameis et al., 2020). Overall, across studies, 87% completed the primary outcome (see Table 1). There was a high degree of variation in reporting of planned sample sizes and power calculations, and whether planned Ns were achieved (see Table 1). Study burden

Some trial teams reported difficulties for potential and enrolled participants in terms of the burden of participation. For example, some faced difficulties arranging face-to-face appointments, or completing the intervention due to travelling distances required (Gaigg et al., 2020; Langdon et al., 2016; Vindin et al., 2021; Quadt et al., 2021), or due to work commitments (Dvir et al., 2025). The COVID-19 pandemic also influenced recruitment and drop-out in some studies (e.g., Rodgers et al., 2024), due for example to concerns about attending in-person appointments (Corbett et al., 2023), or to logistical barriers imposed by the pandemic, e.g., social distancing preventing group-based interventions (Hirvikoski et al., 2025), or impacting study outcomes (Pfeiffer et al., 2024).

Expressing an interest in Wehman et al. (2017)'s employment study required a relatively high level of effort from potential participants, possibly leading to bias in the sample. The application process involved potential participants or a supporter completing a 'detailed, written application', after which they were interviewed to confirm eligibility and determine a 'match to the programme' (Wehman et al., 2017). Long study sessions due to numerous and lengthy questionnaires (Oswald et al., 2018), general time commitment difficulties (Quadt et al., 2021), or stress caused by not completing part of an intervention before the next part was due (Westerberg et al., 2023) were also cited as study burdens which may have increased attrition.

Treatment allocation and participant preferences

Participants sometimes reported poorer experiences depending on their treatment allocation. The relatively (un)appealing nature of the intervention may have led to poorer recruitment (an online psychoeducation intervention for transition aged youths; Backman et al., 2024) and/or retention for some studies (e.g., Spaargaren et al., 2025). Suggested reasons for higher attrition were the use of group interventions (Langdon et al., 2016), burden and/or anxiety caused by the intervention (online/remote mental health interventions; Li et al., 2025; Westerberg et al., 2023; or in a driving improvement trial; Vindin et al., 2021), or lack of support to complete the intervention (Spaargaren et al., 2025; Westerberg et al., 2023). Westerberg et al. (2023) also suggested that not experiencing a benefit, alongside high levels of required effort and engagement may explain higher attrition in their intervention group. These suggestions were supported by participants' reasons for drop-out, e.g., lack of time (n=2), and the intervention being too demanding (n=2; Westerberg et al., 2023). In their previous qualitative work (Westerberg et al., 2021), some participants became stressed when receiving new intervention modules before they had completed a previous one. Relatedly, in Gaigg et al (2020), retention was higher (88%) for the mindfulness-based therapy (MBT) intervention, which included weekly email reminders, compared to the CBT intervention (64%), which was entirely self-guided. In addition, participants in the intervention group for Li et al. (2025) were more likely to experience emotional discomfort (moderate (3%) -significant (16%)), although no participants reported stopping the intervention as a result. Some trial teams also speculated that poor retention in the intervention group may be exacerbated when interventions were not sufficiently adapted, or when participants perceived some aspects as not personally relevant to them (Westerberg et al., 2023).

In some studies, allocation to the intervention was more desirable than the control arm (e.g., Russell et al., 2020; Wijker et al., 2020). For example, Wijker et al. (2020) aimed to test the feasibility of animal assisted therapy (AAT) in stress reduction, social responsiveness, and depression and anxiety symptoms in autistic adults. The authors speculated that, alongside potential direct benefits of the intervention itself in terms of stress reduction, AAT may create a relaxing context for participants, leading to enjoyment of the intervention and potentially lower attrition (Wijker et al., 2020). Russell et al. (2020) Other studies also cited poorer experience of those allocated to the control arm, including those utilising a waitlist control, for example due to a lack of researcher interaction, feeling that their participation was complete, or that further interaction with the study would not benefit them (Andrés-Gárriz et al., 2025; Quadt et al., 2021; Russell et al., 2020; Westerberg et al., 2023). In addition, some studies reported participant preference for allocation to the intervention, and were therefore more likely to drop out when allocated to TAU (e.g., Russell et al., 2020). Several studies experienced greater attrition rates in control compared to intervention groups e.g., with long wait times for TAU (Russell et al., 2020 , Wehman et al., 2017; Wehman et al., 2020 , Quadt et al., 2021). Finally, some studies indicated individual differences which may account for some instances of poorer retention across groups. For example, Gaigg et al. (2020) found that those dropping out tended to have lower Verbal IQ scores, as well as greater ADOS-measured difficulties in communication than those completing participation (see also Wehman et al., 2020).

Slow recruitment

Some authors discussed difficulties working with multiple institutions for recruitment. For example, the UK the National Health Service (NHS) has different Trusts representing a number of geographical areas. Each Trust has its own processes and rules, which can cause delays and sometimes restrict numbers of potential participants who can be contacted with information about a study (e.g., Russell et al., 2020). Finally, some studies noted barriers early in their recruitment stages due to higher than expected proportions of potential participants meeting exclusion criteria (Quadt et al., 2021), or difficulties reaching and/or attracting the target population (Backman et al., 2024). For example, Quadt et al. (2021) initially excluded those with co-occurring mental health diagnoses, but later broadened the inclusion criteria to include more individuals with co-occurring conditions. In addition, Backman et al. (2024) reported slower than expected recruitment, with suggested barriers including the intervention being perceived as too timeconsuming or unappealing.

iii) Strategies used to improve recruitment and retention

Many authors discussed financial compensation in terms of their retention improvement strategies (Eack et al., 2018; Holländare, 2025; Li et al., 2025; Lunsky et al., 2025; Park, 2025; Smith et al., 2021), and some provided further information on strategies they used to maximise recruitment and retention:

Advertising

Most studies reporting recruitment methods used established clinical, research, charity, and other networks, as well as using media, press releases, and social media (Andrés-Gárriz et al., 2025; Backman et al., 2024; Bolognani et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2025; Corbett et al., 2025; Duncan et al., 2023; Gaigg et al., 2020; Hesselmark et al., 2014; Hirvikoski et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Lunsky et al., 2025; McVey et al., 2016; Oswald et al., 2018; Park, 2025; Pfeiffer et al., 2024; Rodgers et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2021; Spaargaren et al., 2025; Stecher et al., 2024; Vindin et al., 2021; White et al., 2021; Yamasue et al., 2022).

Some of the more recent studies explicitly discussed facilitating potential participants to self-refer, i.e., routes to express interest which were not clinician-dependent (Gaigg et al., 2020; Hirvikoski et al., 2025; Lunsky et al., 2025; Rodgers et al., 2024; Yamasue et al., 2022). Such methods, including advertising via social media, have the benefit of circumventing issues faced by trials when relying on medical referrals for recruitment (e.g., Russell et al., 2020), and have helped to produce excellent recruitment rates in more recent large-scale RCTs with autistic people (Rai et al., Accepted; Russell et al., 2026).

Protocol -reasonable adjustments

Most studies did not explicitly report strategies to improve retention. Where reported, some used reasonable adjustments to the trial protocol and delivery. For example, Wehman et al. (2017) were flexible in how they included participants who were unable to engage as per protocol, e.g., where participant communication abilities precluded full engagement in an interview, a classroom observation was used in its place (see also Wehman et al., 2020). In addition, Hagner et al. (2012) facilitated participants to feedback throughout the intervention such that it could be tailored to their needs, including e.g., informal meetings between the facilitator and participant to build rapport (see also Vindin et al., 2021); preparatory meetings to discuss the agenda and participation strategies; flexible break-taking, enabling alternative methods of participation by e.g., listening-in and interjecting from an adjacent room/the other end of the room; allowing written participation, use of assistive communication devices, or a "thumbs up/thumbs down" system; and remote video-conferencing participation (Hagner et al., 2012). In addition, Chen et al. (2025) offered flexibility in terms of attending intervention training sessions based on participant availability.

Some studies discussed the importance of a person-centred communicative approach with participants for retention. In particular, researchers focused on individualised and flexible engagement, including facilitating remote access (Duncan et al., 2023), respecting contact preferences (Rodgers et al., 2024; Russell et al., 2020), and integrating home visits (Hagner et al., 2012).

Russell et al. (2020) gathered preferred methods of communication (e.g., email, text message, phone call), a specific person to communicate with (e.g., self or specified other), as well as the number of follow-up attempts that should be made. Similarly, Vindin et al. (2021) asked participants to complete a personal profile with learning style preferences before driving lessons, providing instructors with background information to aid rapport-building and lesson planning. Hagner et al. (2012)also ensured the accessibility of surveys, which were administered in-person in family homes, allowed for breaks or multiple sessions as required, with survey questions read aloud for clarity and comprehension as needed, and with unfamiliar terms explained. Other studies utilised technology to support participation for those with higher verbal needs (Wehman et al., 2020), or otherwise followed more standardised communicative approaches, such as regular phone calls to facilitate retention (Gaigg et al., 2020), and providing easy-read study information (Langdon et al., 2016). Chen et al. (2025) commented that their retention may have been affected by differences in prompting and reminders, with the intervention group receiving weekly reminders to attend training sessions.

Patient and Public Involvement (PPI)/stakeholder engagement

Particularly in more recent studies, PPI and co-production feature as longer-term investment strategies to improve recruitment and retention (e.g., Rodgers et al., 2024; Russell et al., 2020). In Russell et al. (2020), two autistic adults were involved in designing and formatting the materials for the Guided Self-Help intervention for depression, and a consultation group of autistic adults gave their feedback on the study information sheets and initial intervention session. The study also included an autistic researcher as a member of the Trial Steering Committee (Russell et al., 2020), and these coproduction methods have been built upon in the authors' more recent trials (Rai et al., Accepted; Russell et al., 2026). In their study aiming to improve the transition to adulthood, Oswald et al. (2018) implemented an integrative therapy aimed at increasing adaptive, social, and self-determination skills, as well as coping self-efficacy. The authors invited participants from a prior, similar intervention to focus groups, including autistic people, their parents/caregivers, and professionals delivering the intervention to maximise acceptability of the trial, e.g., less emphasis on learning about using a new app/technology and more on the content, such as social functioning in the workplace, developing a social support network and self-determination skills, and more interactivity (Oswald et al., 2018). The authors concluded that PPI may have contributed to participant satisfaction with the intervention (Oswald et al., 2018). PPI was also implemented 'live', such as in Rodgers et al. (2024), who co-designed participant newsletters with an advisory group of autistic adults, summarising study progress and sending thank-you cards to participants to facilitate retention.

Russell et al. (2020) highlighted important considerations for integrating PPI in autism RCTs, including an understanding that traditional formats (e.g., group discussions) may not work for all autistic people. They also emphasise the need to provide written materials in advance of meetings to allow people time to prepare, and ensuring alternative methods for input (e.g., written and remote options; Russell et al., 2020; see also (Rai et al., Accepted; Russell et al., 2026).

Family involvement

Particularly for studies including younger autistic people, researchers involved family to improve retention. In a study of transition planning for employment readiness, Hagner et al. (2012) recruited both students and their parents as participants. Parents were present when the students completed the study surveys and could assist with the student's understanding of the questions. The trial team also integrated flexibility into parental involvement, such that when illness etc meant that a parent missed a group intervention session, the information was presented to them in their homes instead. Wehman et al. (2020) also integrated parents into their youth internship employment intervention, involving parents in discussions about their vision of their child's future employment, and in practical planning around the impact employment would have on family life, transportation, supplemental income, etc. Corbett et al. (2023) recognised the utility of parental involvement for retention in youth studies, in contrast to the realities of RCTs with adults, whereby many participants may not have 'invested' family members or friends to encourage adherence and retention. Other studies also used a participant-approved 'trusted person' as a back-up contact in case of non-responding by a participant (Rodgers et al., 2024).

Discussion

Included studies were a mix of pilot and feasibility studies and full-scale RCTs focused on social communication skills and participation, cognitive functioning, mental health, quality of life, employment, driving and travel skills, self-determination, and the transition to adulthood. Many did not report a-priori power calculations to determine planned sample sizes. Where provided, studies tended to meet recruitment targets, but attrition varied due to a range of factors, including study burden, accessibility, and timing of interventions and outcome measures. Treatment allocation also affected retention (i.e., poorer retention for intervention or control groups depending on study type and group desirability). Trial teams sometimes reported strategies used to improve recruitment and retention, including e.g., integrating PPI and involving family and carers, facilitating public advertising of studies and accepting self-referrals, and enabling individual reasonable adjustments, adapted communication, and remote participation. Findings are discussed in turn below.

Our review presents a complex picture. On one hand, recruitment and retention to primary outcome appear to have proceeded relatively well in the included studies. However, we note significant gaps and inconsistencies in the reporting of a-priori target sample sizes, power calculations, and planned recruitment periods. Furthermore, a large number of RCTs were excluded from this review due to small recruited samples (Ns <40). Many early studies also failed to develop into definitive trials because of a lack of significant results and/or insurmountable recruitment or retention barriers (LaPoint et al., 2025). Newer trials tended to include more meaningful involvement of and co-production with members of the autistic community, focused on topics and interventions addressing community priorities such as co-occurring conditions and health inequalities, and also show improved quality of reporting. However, such trials needed significant additional delivery time, as well as investment in multi-faceted recruitment and retention efforts to achieve their target samples. The level of effort required to deliver large trials with autistic people may be related to documented barriers to recruiting a participant population often perceived as vulnerable, particularly in mental health research, including clinician gatekeeping and paternalistic risk-management judgements (Borschmann et al., 2014; McCann et al., 2026). Our findings highlight and expand upon common recruitment issues by discussing autism-specific aspects of adapting trial delivery, and how researchers in larger trials managed these.

Several studies highlighted a variety of barriers to participation. Similarly to findings from Houghton et al. (2020) in the general population, our results show that key components of trials were perceived as burdensome to participants, disincentivising participation and making recruitment and retention challenging.

Study burdens included physical or structural barriers (needing to travel to appointments, requiring face-toface participation), which due to intervention type may be difficult to avoid (e.g., driving improvement, employment interventions; Gaigg et al., 2020; Langdon et al., 2016; Quadt et al., 2021; Vindin et al., 2021).

In-person participation during the Covid-19 pandemic was also a specific barrier in some studies due to concerns about exposure to the virus, or logistical issues such as social distancing rendering group interventions impossible. Study burden was also reflected in the time required to take part, often competing with other commitments such as work. Alongside generally high levels of required effort and time (Quadt et al., 2021), some specific study procedures were reported as particularly burdensome, including long expression of interest processes (Wehman et al., 2017), lengthy and numerous questionnaires (Oswald et al., 2018), and interventions causing emotional discomfort (Li et al., 2025; Westerberg et al., 2023).

Treatment allocation also affected recruitment and retention. Group interventions were often less desirable, with issues also noted around a lack of support for their completion (Spaargaren et al., 2025; Westerberg et al., 2023). Relatedly, some authors discussed insufficient person-centred intervention adaptation (Westerberg et al., 2023). Interventions may also have been too demanding, not sufficiently personally relevant, or even emotionally distressing (Backman et al., 2024; Gaigg et al., 2020; Langdon et al., 2016; Li et al., 2025; Spaargaren et al., 2025; Vindin et al., 2021; Westerberg et al., 2023). For both intervention and control groups, perceiving no benefit from participation also affected retention. Retention was also poorer in waitlist/TAU control groups, especially with a lack of researcher interaction and long waits for treatment as usual, or feeling that participation was complete/further interaction with the study would not benefit them (Andrés-Gárriz et al., 2025; Quadt et al., 2021; Russell et al., 2020; Wehman et al., 2017 Wehman et al., , 2020;; Westerberg et al., 2023). Desirability of an intervention also meant that participants often had a clear allocation preference (i.e., problem of equipoise; Russell et al., 2020; Wijker et al., 2020).

Other barriers to recruitment included those at the system or organisational level, such as a limited pool of potential participants, often not due to lack of interest (but see Backman et al., 2024), but e.g., higher than expected rates of meeting exclusion criteria (particularly in mental health trials; Quadt et al., 2021). In addition, when recruiting via clinical lists, varying processes and rules in different regions can slow or limit the number of potential participants reached (e.g., Russell et al., 2020). Although not directly reported by trial teams in the included studies, clinician-level barriers to recruitment are also well established, e.g., gatekeeping (Borschmann et al., 2014; Donovan et al., 2014; McCann et al., 2026).

Alongside standard forms of compensation for time taking part (i.e., monetary payment), establishing relationships across sectors (e.g., educational, charitable, public, private, and third sectors), and using direct public advertising via traditional and social media were found to facilitate recruitment as they enabled potential participants to self-refer, avoiding reliance on busy clinicians to refer (Gaigg et al., 2020; Hirvikoski et al., 2025; Lunsky et al., 2025; Rodgers et al., 2024; Yamasue et al., 2022).

In some cases, trial teams were able to implement adjustments to alleviate study burden. For example, offering remote participation (Duncan et al., 2023), taking a person-centred communicative approach, e.g., using preferred communication methods and having a trusted family member/supporter as a contact in the event of non-response (Rodgers et al., 2024; Russell et al., 2020; Wehman et al., 2017) and to help with treatment concordance, implementing individualised rapport-building and communication plans (Hagner et al., 2012; Vindin et al., 2021), offering flexible assessment methods (Wehman et al., 2017 (Wehman et al., , 2020) ) and varied methods of participation (Hagner et al., 2012), as well as preparatory meetings, and being flexible in terms of intervention participation timing (e.g., Chen et al., 2025), and tailoring the study based on individual feedback (Hagner et al., 2012). Vindin et al.'s (2021) driver training trial integrated thoughtful communicative plans and participant learning style preferences to aid instructors with lesson delivery. Other studies integrated home visits to ensure outcome completion (Hagner et al., 2012), a strategy thought to be particularly effective with younger participants (Corbett et al., 2025; Hagner et al., 2012; Wehman et al., 2020). Such approaches are supported by evidence that tailoring communication and ensuring flexibility where possible improve the participant-researcher therapeutic alliance (Houghton et al., 2020; McCann et al., 2026; Norris et al., 2026; Westerberg et al., 2023), ultimately improving retention (see also Murphy et al., 2026).

Strategies reported in the included studies to improve recruitment and retention of autistic people to RCTs broadly reflect those in recent reviews of general population trials (e.g., McCann et al., 2026; Treweek et al., 2018). However, the extent to which any one of such strategies directly impacts upon recruitment and retention successes is unclear. In their systematic review, Treweek et al. (2018) found that the only high-certainty evidence available (according to Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)) for the factors involved in improving recruitment found a 10% improvement when using open trials compared to blinded/placebo trials; 6% improved recruitment (to trials with lower underlying recruitment) when using telephone reminders for potential participants who did not respond to initial postal invitations; and finally a ~1% improvement when using a 'particular, bespoke, usertesting approach' to develop participant information leaflets, including PPI and co-production methods.

In a recent review, McCann et al. (2026) synthesised the qualitative evidence around experiences of trial staff recruiting participants to mental health trials. Most of the available evidence related to recruitment, with several findings of relevance here, e.g., reluctance of staff to introduce a trial after often long clinical appointments (i.e., several hours of autism assessment and diagnostic outcome; Realpe et al., 2025), as well as other gatekeeper-related factors, such as problems of equipoise (McCann et al., 2026). The authors concluded that staff tended to consider prioritising perceived clinical risk management and preserving therapeutic relationships over introducing potential trials, due to concerns that participation may disrupt patient care (McCann et al., 2026). The extent to which such findings may extend to the autistic population is currently not well understood. However, current findings may indicate a converse effect for this population, whereby a relative lack of other support may at least in part drive autistic adults' motivation to participate in RCTs (Norris et al., 2026; Realpe et al., 2026). Newer, large mental health in autism trials have shown promise in implementing PPI and co-production to develop attractive trials, ultimately achieving higher recruited and retained samples (Rai et al., Accepted; Russell et al., 2026). However, the effectiveness of individual recruitment and retention strategies is not currently well understood.

Especially for more recent studies, PPI and co-production are also beginning to be integrated, enabling further refinement of interventions and outcome measures for autistic people at the earliest stages (e.g., Oswald et al., 2018; Rodgers et al., 2024; Russell et al., 2020). This was felt to improve acceptability of the intervention and outcome measures, ultimately facilitating participation. Recent trials published after this review have also observed excellent recruitment and retention rates after thorough integration of coproduction into intervention design and delivery, participant-facing study information, appointment frequency, medication dosing, etc (Norris et al., 2026; Rai et al., Accepted; Russell et al., 2026), as well as retention activities such as participant newsletters (Rodgers et al., 2024). Accessibility is a crucial consideration for implementing PPI and co-production, e.g., flexible ways for community members to provide input and not solely relying on traditional methods (e.g., focus groups; Russell et al., 2020). Some studies also discussed benefits of involving participants' family members and/or supporters (e.g., Hagner et al., 2012; Wehman et al., 2020), who often encouraged continuation in and engagement with studies, although this may be more straightforward for trials with young people compared with adults (Corbett et al., 2025; but see Murphy et al., 2026). Finally, it is noted that most studies did not report reimbursing expenses e.g., travel to study appointments, which may be a barrier to participation.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this review include the application of broad inclusion criteria, including all intervention types from any time point, as well as only including individually-randomised RCTs, not including autistic vs.

non-autistic comparison studies, the use of multiple raters, and considering evidence quality by including only trials recruiting N>40. In terms of limitations, we were only able to include studies published or available in English, and were unable to retrieve all studies in some cases (i.e., clinical trial registers in some countries). In terms of reported barriers to participation, due to their nature, clinician-level barriers are likely under-reported, i.e., gatekeeping via not referring a potentially eligible participant to a trial due to one's own feelings around perceived vulnerability (Borschmann et al., 2014; Donovan et al., 2014; McCann et al., 2026).

In addition, it is likely that difficulties recruiting and retaining autistic participants to RCTs is underestimated by this review, due for example to the limited information provided by most of the included studies regarding target sample sizes and retention. In addition, most only recruited small samples, often in feasibility or pilot RCT designs. Planned recruitment periods were often unreported or unclear, making it difficult in some cases to determine whether the relatively small sample sizes were pre-planned, or due to recruitment problems (especially when studies did not report recruitment barriers and facilitators). Many studies did not explicitly state target sample sizes, final recruited sample size (in total and across arms). In addition, power calculations and/or justifications for sample sizes were often absent. In many cases, the primary outcome of interest was either not defined or not sufficiently clear. Many studies did not fully adhere to CONSORT guidelines for the reporting of clinical trials, making it difficult to extract basic information (e.g., planned sample size, defined primary outcome and timepoint, retention rates at each follow-up timepoint, etc). Trials should ensure robust strategies for recruiting sufficient sample sizes such that important research funding and participant time are used appropriately.

Future research

Findings have implications for the conceptualisation, design, and conduct of RCTs with autistic adults, namely: i) collaborate early & often -trial teams should aim to engage researchers, clinicians, and the autistic community from initial trial planning stages onwards, including meaningful PPI and coproduction; ii) prioritise individualised communication & flexibility -participation rates may be improved by asking about and respecting participant contact preferences, adapting communication with a patient-centred approach; iii) going beyond clinical settings -improved recruitment may follow the widening of channels for recruiting potential participants, e.g., via charities and community organisations, and general advertisements with straightforward self-referral; iv) make participation straightforward -recruitment and retention may be improved when remote participation is offered (where possible), considering the potential support needs of participants regardless of group (e.g., offering support to complete intervention tasks and outcome measures), and when ensuring flexibility and understanding (e.g., when appointments may be missed).

Recruitment to trials may reasonably be expected to be more successful when interventions and outcomes are important to the autism community (see e.g., James Lind Alliance research priorities; James Lind Alliance, 2016). Trial teams should also consider the impact of their choice of intervention and control group mode on participation (e.g., benefits and limitations of TAU and waitlist control options). In addition, researchers should be mindful of the impact a desirable intervention may have on participation and attrition.

The context of a trial is likely to influence this, for example in research areas where there are limited opportunities for care (e.g., mental health, or post-diagnostic support; Norris et al., 2024) the opportunity to take part in a trial may be particularly appealing (e.g., Realpe et al., 2026), and allocation to a control group, particularly TAU, may be unmotivating (Russell et al., 2020). Recent research indicates that autistic people can understand and appreciate the need for placebo-controlled designs (Beasant et al., 2024; Norris et al., 2026; Realpe et al., 2026). Participants should therefore be informed about equipoise in plain language, and about the value of their participation in providing sufficient data to answer the research question regardless of individual group allocation. It is also important to emphasise that individuals may also benefit from trial participation more broadly, beyond direct effects of the intervention itself (e.g., Norris et al., 2026).

To improve the provision of transparent trial information in publications, trial teams should explicitly state their target sample size and the power calculations justifying these, as well as the final recruited sample, and total numbers completing participation in all arms and at all timepoints. Trial teams should also ensure CONSORT reporting guidelines are followed, and report all methods used for recruitment and retention in trial publications (Murphy et al., 2024 (Murphy et al., , 2025)).

Finally, although many of the included trials discussed issues around a lack of sample diversity, in particular in terms of gender and ethnicity (McVey et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2021), few described efforts to address this, or how future research could do so. Better representation of non-white ethnic groups is of particular importance in mental health trials considering higher risk of mental health difficulties faced by many such groups (McCann et al., 2026). Efforts should be combined to make trials more inclusive to enhance sample diversity, considering neurodevelopmental conditions as well other characteristics, e.g., ethnicity, currently under-addressed in autism research (Dawson et al., 2025; Morgan et al., 2026; NIHR, 2020).

Conclusions

RCTs with autistic adults may appear to report good recruitment and retention rates, but this is unclear due to missing information and inconsistencies in reporting power calculations and/or sample size justifications, as well as target sample sizes and other important trial information. Some studies reported barriers to recruitment and/or retention, as well as strategies implemented to try and address these. Trial teams emphasised the utility of wide-reaching advertisement, enabling self-referral by potential participants, as well as taking a person-centred approach to communication and participation. Early engagement with communities via PPI and co-production are also valuable in ensuring acceptability of trial processes at the earliest stage, ultimately improving participation rates.
