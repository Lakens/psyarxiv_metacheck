Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Existential psychotherapy addresses suffering from a position that resists reduction to pathology: confrontations with death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness that manualized frameworks tend to bracket. Despite its prominent influence on the history and development of psychotherapies, the existential tradition has lost ground in a field characterized by symptom reduction and reimbursable outcomes, in part due to a thin empirical foundation and resistance to structured approaches. This thematic review traces existential therapy from its philosophical foundations in phenomenology and existentialism, through core models of Frankl, Yalom, and May, to more contemporary integrative and meaning-centered approaches. Across process, qualitative, and outcome research, three commitments recur: meaning as a mechanism of recovery, therapeutic relationship as the site of change, and clients' authority over their experience. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that meaning-centered interventions increase meaning and reduce distress. Therapeutic realness is generally associated with stronger client alliances, while existential-humanistic principles play an integral role in effective psychotherapy broadly. Existential therapies are not irrelevant, but lack infrastructure: measurement of proposed mechanisms, methods to identify target populations, and training guidelines that prepare therapists to meet existential encounters. Precision-based methods offer a promising path toward an existential therapy that is empirically legible while true to its core commitments.

Existential Psychotherapy and the Human Condition: Philosophical Foundations, Clinical Practice, and the Challenge of Evidence

Psychotherapeutic modalities are typically defined by their techniques and by theoretical assumptions about what causes distress and which principles effect change (Anderson et al., 2009). Yet outcomes depend substantially on how those techniques are delivered (Hill, 2005), and factors such as therapeutic alliance and client-therapist fit contribute to outcomes, often beyond the modality itself (Norcross & Wampold, 2011). Fidelity to the modality, client expectations, relational patterns between client and therapist, and various external factors all shape the therapeutic process (Sousa, 2015). If so much of what heals lies outside technique, in the persons and the relationship between them, then a tradition that places both at its center deserves renewed attention.

Existential psychotherapy occupies a strange position across modality, philosophy, and technique. Loosely defined, it is a modality that emphasizes the confrontation of existential givens (e.g., death, anxiety, dread) through a shared journey with the therapist using genuine and non-judgmental guidance. However, existentialism can also complement rather than replace other modalities (Leontiev, 2015), or stand wholly unattached to modality and technique as a philosophical facilitation of the therapeutic approach. Overall, existentialism attempts to come to terms with the absurdity of life's suffering without pathologizing the strain that takes place (Berra, 2021). While the questions of existence (death, freedom, isolation) are necessarily unanswerable, restitution is found in a direction, purpose, or meaning (Berra, 2021). Attempts have been made to reintroduce existential initiatives into contemporary psychotherapy, but its role is best demonstrated by what the field lacks.

A common practice of psychotherapy is the pathologizing of psychological processes.

Impaired insight (e.g., psychosis), for instance, is not necessarily psychological in its origin (Ghaemi, 2007). The separation of abnormal from normal psychology, an "us" and "them," was considered dismantled when phenomenology had prominent influence over psychotherapy (Wharne, 2021), yet contemporary psychotherapy still adheres to it. The humanness surrounding the temporal experience of depression and anxiety is not fully met by addressing psychopathology alone (Rayner & Vitali, 2016), which can leave psychotherapy vulnerable to medical reductionism.

In an age of manualized reimbursement and coverage, a clinic's ability to function is contingent on demonstrating symptom reduction (Bland, 2025). When demonstrated progress is required, it can be difficult to incorporate a client's meaning-making or self-motivation (Wharne, 2021). This reflects what Leontiev (2005) summarizes as the assumption of classical psychology: that human behavior can be determined in a predictable fashion, provided all relevant factors are considered. The same assumption underlies the widespread reliance on the GAD and PHQ for substantiating a modality's progress and effectiveness. Moreover, the clinical efficacy of a therapeutic research trial is often not reflected in the clinical effectiveness of routine therapy (Rainer & Gay, 2013).

While contemporary psychotherapy is equipped to take on certain levels of psychological distress and maladjustment, true crises present a roadblock. Personal crises, which may be understood as existential, do not respond well to classical approaches because the situation does not allow for a productive way to achieve therapeutic goals (Leontiev, 2005). Clinicians, especially those who work with young persons, also hesitate to adopt existential approaches because of the relative lack of empirical evidence and concrete treatment guidelines (Keshen, 2006; Schneider, 2008). When 26 trained psychotherapists watched a recorded session of a client overwhelmed by meaninglessness and were asked to imagine themselves as the client's therapist, the majority described withdrawing from the therapeutic encounter (Vanhees, 2021, as cited in Vanhooren, 2022). Even so, writing on existential training focuses on the relational process of supervision rather than extending existential philosophy into practice (Smith et al., 2023).

Despite its loss of influence in contemporary psychotherapy, existential practice still holds potential and importance. This paper is a thematic review that traces the philosophical and clinical foundations of existentialism into the meaning-centered approaches now gaining empirical support. It seeks to contextualize what existential approaches have inherited, what they risk leaving behind, and what an empirically engaged existentialism would need to retain to remain both faithful to its commitments and mindful of its limits.

Historical and Philosophical Foundations

Existential Phenomenology

Existential psychotherapy builds upon depth psychology and existential philosophy, a mode of inquiry into the human confrontation with existential givens (death, anxiety, grief, etc.).

For Heidegger, the state of Being is inherently tied to the being in which it is experienced (Heidegger, 1927 (Heidegger, /2001, p. 33), p. 33). For Camus, to understand the world is to reduce it to human terms, stamping it with a human seal; yet the world resists this demand for clarity, and the absurd arises from their confrontation (Camus, 1942 (Camus, /1991, pp. 9-10), pp. 9-10). Truth, for Heidegger, is only secondarily the agreement of a statement with its object; primarily, it is the disclosedness of Being itself (Heidegger, 1927 (Heidegger, /2001, p. 62), p. 62). Existential phenomenology thus holds that Being and truth cannot be understood outside the existing individual through whom they are disclosed.

Meaning and Nihilism

For Kierkegaard, the deepest commitments cannot be reached by reason alone; in Fear and Trembling, Abraham's faith is a leap made by the individual, held by virtue of the absurd rather than secured by argument (Kierkegaard, 1843 (Kierkegaard, /2000)). Existentialism inherits this: a meaningful life is pursued without guarantee, in the face of uncertainty and death. For Tillich, courage is the self-affirmation of being in spite of nonbeing, holding these ultimate threats together in a meaningful present rather than escaping them (Tillich, 1952 (Tillich, /2000, pp. 152-153), pp. 152-153).

Nihilism presses the opposite case. Schopenhauer positions vanity squarely: that which wastes away cannot be worth any straining effort (Schopenhauer, 1851 (Schopenhauer, /2004, p. 52), p. 52), and nihilism arises when the self has become disillusioned with all meaning (Nietzsche, 1901 (Nietzsche, /2017, p. 18), p. 18).

Existentialism does not seek to answer whether ultimate purpose exists, but how to strive forward while death, anxiety, and adversity linger on.

Existentialism

The self has the freedom to choose their course of action, and with that freedom comes the responsibility to act in a manner that reflects all humanity (Sartre, 1946 (Sartre, /2007, p. 29), p. 29). For Sartre, because existence precedes essence, individuals are not bound by predetermination; rather, they exert themselves through their choices and actions. Existential inquiry consequently begins with the subjective experience of being in such a world. Levinas extends this as a responsibility beyond the self: no other can assume one's responsibility for the Other, and ethical existence requires seeking the same freedom for others as one does the self (Levinas, 1974 (Levinas, /1981, p. 141), p. 141). Existentialism positions itself as an acknowledgement of the fundamental and harsh givens of life and the responsibility to recognize the freedom and subjectivity of others.

Core Models of Existential Therapy Frankl and Logotherapy

Viktor Frankl's existential approach arises from the confrontation with adversity.

Suffering is a given in this world; perhaps it was not a chosen life, but it is life nonetheless.

Frankl holds to this even in an event as catastrophic as the Holocaust, reaffirming that a life in suffering remains a meaningful pursuit: it is the realization of meaning that allows suffering to cease being suffering (Frankl, 1963, p. 122). Responsibility for finding that meaning is, in part, other-sparing. With the death of a loved one, the suffering felt in grief is also the opportunity to have spared that loved one the suffering of one's own death (Frankl, 1963, p. 172). For the therapist, trust in being not only allows fortitude in challenges, but allows the session to be void of ideological stance (Frankl, 1967).

Yalom and the Four Ultimate Concerns

Irvin Yalom, who laid the groundwork for group therapy and other modern existential approaches, proposes that the essence of being is the confrontation of four existential themes: death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness (Yalom, 1980, pp. 481-482). The goal of existential psychotherapy is not to eliminate it, but to balance the fear of death with the essence of living; as Tillich writes, the threat of nonbeing can never be erased, only met with courage (Tillich, 1952 (Tillich, /2000, p. 39), p. 39). Grounding, for Yalom, is the mandate to live truthfully, balancing Schopenhauer's awareness of oblivion with Nietzsche's gay science. Importantly, Yalom does not contend that the absence of meaning causes psychopathology: diminished meaning in life is a common co-occurrence (Yalom, 1980, p. 460). He extends existentialism to a person-centered perspective, where the relationship of therapist and client is what heals, not the instruction or manualization of a particular orientation (Yalom, 1980).

May and Existential-Humanistic Therapy

Rollo May calls on the daimonic, the self's confrontation with the universe: not an individual or entity, but the fundamental that underlies all experience (May, 1969, p. 123).

Anxiety is not inherently learned; learning modulates it, amplifying its quantity and forms (May, 1977, p. 218). Existentialism therefore does not fit neatly into a categorical psychotherapy; it lives as an underlying premise (May, 1967, p. 156). Any therapist's practice is inherently existential in that, whatever technical training one undergoes, authentic therapeutic relationships still hinge on the ability to coexist with the other (May, 1983, p. 158). One might go from session to session setting up the problem, lamenting it, and interrogating it to a pulp, but change comes only when one engages truthfully and wholly (May, 1969, p. 174).

Autonomy and Being

A central premise of existential psychotherapy is that the client's autonomy and being should be respected. The client-therapist relationship hinges on the ability to sit on level ground, to walk a path together for some time, and to recognize when the relationship would not be beneficial (Perls, 1969, p. 4). What further separates existential psychotherapy from psychoanalysis is the acknowledgment of the creative will, a life-affirming forward direction that Otto Rank held to be better understood through phenomenology and a perspective-taking of the other (Rank, 1936 (Rank, /1978)). It also asks that the therapist be able to take on the pain, grief, and suffering of another person without losing one's self (Rogers, 1980, pp. 142-143).

Integrative Approaches Exploration of Meaning and Values

Across the decades, existential psychotherapy has preserved its core goal: reorienting an otherwise meaningless, limited being toward a purposeful way forward. Changing one's orientation to meaning and ordering of values requires the balancing of two truths, a yin and yang (Rayner & Vitali, 2018): humans seek a meaningful life in an otherwise meaningless and cruel reality. This encounter with meaning is inevitable. Clients and clinicians alike may not put a name to it, but the exploration of thoughts and feelings to gain clarity and coherence and to deepen one's sense of self is an encounter with meaning in life (Hill, 2017). To this degree, existential meaning is present in all therapeutic encounters.

Phenomenological Dialogue

Where empiricism often treats behavior and cognition in terms of their divergence from a normative threshold, phenomenology treats subjective experience as a fundamental cue into the patient's state and being, contextualized by personal variance and history. Both are useful: one asks how far a person departs from the norm, the other whether that departure is understandable given the circumstances. Just as a mild depressive state was once filtered solely through psychoanalysis, modern protocols may unnecessarily filter it through CBT (Ghaemi, 2007). With Dostoevsky's underground man, an existential approach would not medicalize the internal rage, but contextualize it as unrelinquished existential distress and an attempt at meaning-making (Aho, 2021). Likewise, depressed and nondepressed persons alike misconstrue the world in a biased way some of the time (Ghaemi, 2007), so such distortions are not self-evidently unique to depression. Phenomenology may even delocalize an emotional disorder from the individual psyche to an inability to fulfill the demands of one's environment or to incorporate fully into the world (Fuchs, 2019), a nuance not well addressed in contemporary psychotherapies.

Authentic Therapist-Client Encounter

Existential psychotherapy was unique for its time in the emphasis it placed on the therapist-client coupling. The APA task force on evidence-based therapy relationships concluded that the relationship contributes to outcome consistently and independently of treatment type, and that tailoring it to what each client brings (e.g., culture, preferences, history) enhances effectiveness (Norcross & Wampold, 2011). One component is realness: the willingness of the therapist to reveal their personal thoughts and feelings despite some form of risk (Hopwood et al., 2024), which can catalyze the patient's ability to reciprocate in an unaltered manner.

Phenomenology commits the therapist to a sympathetic and real immersion in the lived, subjective experience the patient expresses (Georganda, 2022; Rayner & Vitali, 2016). Here the therapist's own lived experience can intertwine with the patient's phenomena, uncovering insights otherwise lost (Schneider, 2008, pp. 50-51).

Therapeutic Mentoring

For most existential psychotherapists, the encounter with existential themes predated the attribution of such processes to existentialism; the inadequacy of a prior modality prompted them to answer patients' questions of meaning head-on (Adams et al., 2025). Existential ideals need not be a sole commitment: blending existentialism with an integrative stance is not only possible, but allows a broader learning experience that can be used outside the context of existentialism (Sotskova & Dossett, 2017). A therapist's orientation toward their own meaning in life is, in turn, reflected in the ability to converse deeply with others on such issues (Hill, 2017).

Training, often described as "passing on the baton" (Adams et al., 2025), places less emphasis on implementing a cognitive model of existentialism than on cultivating the ability to embody existential philosophical challenges in practice (Smith et al., 2023). Existential professionals hold that the key to competency is applying the theory to oneself and one's own life (Adams et al., 2025): the same struggles in the other are present within the self, a "beingtogether" (Demilkhanova, 2024; Frediani et al., 2023). This path is demanding. Attending equally and deeply to every dimension of a client's experience would likely overwhelm most novice therapists (Sotskova & Dossett, 2017), and the experiential nature of the training entails a period of profound anxiety and uncertainty (Smith et al., 2023). The same stance has a practical benefit: because it separates existential responsibility from calls to action and indirect moral directives, the approach can be used cross-culturally and when therapist and client values do not align (Salamon, 2019).

What Makes an Existential Psychotherapy?

The practice of existential psychotherapy does not stand detached from the context in which it arises (Adams et al., 2025). The therapist accompanies the patient like a former pathtaker; taking on existential givens and tuning into those processes allows some dread, angst, and pathology to remedy itself in the realization of being (Ratner, 2019). Human behavior cannot be simply biological and mechanistic; these lenses would not capture the human capacity for pointless cruelty, outbursts of rage, and overtly self-destructive behavior (Aho, 2021). The purpose of an existential approach is therefore not to change or rebuild the inner world of the client, much less to get rid of that which humans are in essence (Kociunas, 2007), but to help the individual find their own way through the world, drawing on the creative will and intention (Ratner, 2019). To deny our capacity for harm and destruction is also to deny our ability to connect, relate, and be tender toward others (Aho, 2021).

Existential therapy is also integrative in nature. It emphasizes a holistic understanding of the client, seeking to simultaneously address cognition, affect, behavior, and relational facets while holding close the idea of a deeply meaningful being (Sotskova et al., 2016). In a controlled merging of life-worlds, the joint interaction with an ambivalent Other allows for the transformation of the inner self (Krasilnikov, 2022). The therapist, too, must be in tune with themselves, their desires and feelings (Demilkhanova, 2024); as May states simply, "a life is at stake," and the call of a psychotherapist is serious (Ratner, 2019).

Adaptability

Existential phenomenology seeks to treat not symptoms in isolation, but the whole of every unique situation in which we are found, affectively and relationally (Aho, 2021). This demands psychological flexibility, the ability to shift with one's experiences rather than rigidly adhere to beliefs or rules (Rainer & Gay, 2013), from therapists no less than patients. Clinically, the therapist pushes the boundary of the patient's comfort little by little, allowing ample time to determine whether the patient is amenable, and only then continues with questions or selfreflective prompts (Silva & Sousa, 2022). While this has historically been hard to characterize, adaptability can be taught and applied to the individual using generalities identified in research (Norcross & Wampold, 2011).

Necessary Components

Existential psychotherapy differentiates its approach from other modalities in that gathering information begins from the old Socratic saying, "I know that I know nothing" (Rayner & Vitali, 2018). The position of knowing, describing, or changing is consequently in the hands of the patient: change is effected by facilitating discovery, not by preconceived notions and general training (Berra, 2021; Rayner & Vitali, 2018). Therapy does not presuppose that mental suffering must be classified as a pathology or that outcomes are the only measure of interest (Berra, 2021; Bland, 2025), and it encourages epistemological pluralism and eclectic training while cautioning against scientism's exclusivity (DeRobertis & Bland, 2025). Lastly, the existential framework is characterized by the relationship of patient and therapist, in which themes of freedom, responsibility, and finite limitation allow authentic self-disclosure and genuine concern (Krasilnikov, 2022; Sotskova & Dossett, 2017).

Clinical Relevance

Types of Distress

Existential therapy is particularly well equipped to address suffering not easily situated in standard diagnostic categories. Berra (2021) describes an existential depression rooted not in cognitive distortion but in an overtly accurate, and therefore unbearable, confrontation with the tragic facets of existence. Current classifications tend to fold this state into the same diagnostic criteria and treatment plan as other depressive categories, heightening the risk of an inappropriate protocol.

Existential work also offers an alternative to the struggle-against model of distress. As Rainer and Gay (2013) note, efforts to overpower unwanted internal experience tend to intensify its negative consequences. Existential and acceptance-based approaches instead give the client a platform to stop fighting and reorient toward what they perceive as mattering. Clients who have received existential therapy describe a greater capacity to cope with specific areas of life and their relationships, and a newly sparked sense of what their lives could be (Sousa & Vaz, 2020).

Meaninglessness, Despair, Anxiety, and Isolation

The distress existential therapy addresses most directly takes the form of Yalom's (1980) ultimate concerns as they appear in lived experience. Berra (2017) characterizes it as a diffuse anguish that follows the loss of reference points, the sense that the frame by which one had oriented one's life no longer holds. Its defining feature is hopelessness in a specific sense: the existential depressive can no longer deny the tragedy of life that others have kept at a distance (Berra, 2021). Such concerns often go unspoken; clients may privately wrestle with meaning without raising it in therapy, sometimes because they do not realize it is a legitimate focus of treatment (Golovchanova et al., 2021).

The therapeutic response is not resignation. Acceptance, better understood as willingness, is an active openness to one's situation and being (Rainer & Gay, 2013). When suffering centers on the collapse of meaning and identity, philosophical reflection itself becomes a therapeutic tool (Berra, 2016), and the existential and phenomenological traditions converge on care for the client's being-in-the-world and on truthfulness as a guide for the work (Silva & Sousa, 2022).

Meaning-centered suffering is therefore addressed not by supplying answers but by accompanying the client as they reconstruct a livable orientation to their own life.

Evidence and Outcomes

Qualitative and Case-Based Support While existentialism has historically been hesitant to take on empirical backing, recent research has begun classifying and measuring the outcomes of existential programs. Qualitative research offers perhaps the most direct view of what clients and therapists experience as effective. Across two sets of Belgian person-centered case studies (43 and 42 cases, each spanning 15 or more sessions), all four of Yalom's concerns appeared in every case (De Decker, 2020, and Helsen, 2020, as cited in Vanhooren, 2022). However, the weight given to existential material differs considerably. Group therapists in correctional settings ranked existential factors tenth of Yalom's eleven therapeutic factors (Morgan et al., 1999), whereas a sample of offenders ranked them second (MacDevitt & Sanislow, 1987). A similar mismatch appears at intake: clinicians often spend the time gathering diagnostic information, yet patients rate the working alliance more favorably when the clinician allows them to tell their whole story and responds to their personal history and sociocultural background (Nakash et al., 2015).

Clients' own accounts emphasize both relational and personal change. They attribute significant moments partly to the therapist's skill, but also to their own reflective effort and to being recognized and validated in their sense of being by another; they describe recovering a sense of purpose, a clearer identity, greater acceptance of previously rejected parts of themselves, and a maturation that sits side-by-side with a better quality of life (Sousa & Vaz, 2020). Therapists describe a parallel process: once clients connect with what genuinely matters to them and what holds them back, they begin to adjust the course of their life, often by themselves (Schneider, 2016).

Existential concerns are also developmentally broader than often assumed. Adolescents think about existential questions and can process them with at least some sophistication (Berman et al., 2006 (Berman et al., , 2009)). In a high school sample, identity problem symptoms predicted psychological symptom scores (β = .55, p < .001) even when accounting for Marcia's identity statuses (Berman et al., 2009). Case reports of existential-integrative treatment with anxious and depressed adolescents likewise describe clients who, once fear and uncertainty were acknowledged as stemming from life itself, resumed valued activity and adopted a present-and future-oriented mindset: "I know I'm going to be okay either way" (Shumaker, 2012, p. 393). This undercuts the hesitation, noted earlier, to raise existential themes with adolescents.

Quantitative Outcome Studies

Quantitative outcome research, albeit more recent, has begun to support these accounts.

Across hundreds of studies, the presence of meaning and meaning-oriented coping have been moderately to strongly associated with higher quality of life, lower depression and anxiety, and better physical health (Vos, 2023). The presence of meaning also matters more than the search for it: in a profile-based analysis, clients low on presence of meaning reported the most psychological distress (Golovchanova et al., 2021).

At the level of interventions, an early meta-analysis found that meaning therapies (k = 6) produced substantial gains in positive meaning in life at post-treatment (d = 0.65) and follow-up (d = 0.57), along with a moderate reduction in psychopathology (d = 0.47) and gains in selfefficacy (d = 0.48), though there was no effect on self-reported well-being in the one study that measured it (Vos et al., 2015). A later meta-analysis extends these findings. From pre-treatment, clients improved substantially in quality of life (g = 1.13 post-treatment; g = 0.99 at follow-up) and psychological stress (g = 1.21; g = 0.67). In controlled trials, large advantages over control conditions survived for both quality of life (g = 1.02; g = 1.06) and psychological stress (g = 0.94; g = 0.84), and did not differ meaningfully by sample type or method of study selection.

Most interestingly, increases in meaning predicted decreases in psychological stress (β = -0.56; & Vitali, 2018). Because this is a study-level meta-regression, it is an ecological association between studies rather than evidence that a given client's distress falls as their meaning rises, a distinction taken up in the methods proposed below.

Evidence from routine care is also encouraging. Rayner and Vitali (2016) delivered a sixsession intervention in UK primary care to clients referred for mild-to-moderate depression or anxiety, with large pre-post reductions in depressive symptoms (PHQ-9, d_z = 1.38), anxiety (GAD-7, d_z = 1.09), and general distress (CORE-OM, d_z = 0.89). These effects come from a non-controlled design, which leaves them prone to regression to the mean and non-specific effects; they speak less to efficacy than to feasibility and acceptability in a service context.

Role of Alliance, Presence, and Authenticity

Existential theory holds that the relationship itself is the vehicle of change, and process research lends partial support. Therapeutic realness, the therapist's willingness to share genuine thoughts and feelings at some personal risk, is more frequent in process-oriented than in cognitive-behavioral treatments and is associated with stronger real relationships, working alliances, and therapeutic presence (Hopwood et al., 2024). Existential therapists describe this authenticity as a way to keep patients oriented to the process of their experience rather than piling up content (Rayner & Vitali, 2018). Authenticity here has a precise meaning, a sense of authorship: an action is authentic when chosen and held with full awareness of the situation and its consequences (Van Deurzen & Adams, 2011). As they succinctly describe, "authentic living is aware living" (p. 92).

If existential therapy asks therapists to apply its ideas to their own lives, it also changes them. Therapists who regularly engage with clients' existential concerns report becoming more aware of their own existential reality, adjusting their lives, tolerating more presence with clients' concerns, and developing more nuanced views of life's central questions; those with existential training more often picked up implicit and complex existential dynamics than those without it (Frediani et al., 2023).

Parallels to Contemporary Approaches

Existential therapy shares more with contemporary approaches than its reputation may suggest. Beck and colleagues explicitly credit the phenomenological movement, rooted in Kant, Heidegger, and Husserl, and its clinical application by Jaspers, Binswanger, and Straus, as influences on cognitive therapy (Beck et al., 1979, p. 9). This is apparent in the focus on what is directly accessible to the therapist, rather than the therapist acting as interpreter, and in the cognitive triad of self, world, and future (Menzies et al., 2023). Rainer and Gay (2013) likewise map the bridges between existential therapy and ACT onto three levels: content, where the client's problems are met with nonjudgmental observation; processing, where attention shifts to the strategies the client uses to meet a problem's demands; and relationship, treated throughout as a catalyst of both action and analysis.

Both traditions also share an affirmative vision of health. Ratner (2019) describes a joy that comes not from symptom relief, but from becoming fully alive, creatively confronting one's life, and openness to others. Both ACT and existential phenomenology are contextualist: ACT rests on functional contextualism, aiming at prediction and influence, whereas existential phenomenology aligns more closely with descriptive contextualism, which seeks to understand the whole event (Menzies et al., 2023). Despite a reputation for resisting structure, existential therapy's core commitments appear to be widely shared, and outcome research suggests that existential-humanistic principles are integral to the effectiveness of bona fide psychotherapies (Angus et al., 2015; Elkins, 2009; Schneider & Längle, 2012).

Limits of the Evidence Base

The evidence reviewed here should be read with its limitations in mind. Some are philosophical: existential therapists have historically doubted that there is a shared, objective reality that research instruments can measure, holding that experience can only be described phenomenologically (Vos, 2023), a stance that has no doubt contributed to a thinner evidence base than that of manualized approaches. Moreover, the measures most often used, such as the PHQ-9 and GAD-7, capture exactly the outcomes existential therapy has regarded as secondary.

The approach is therefore evaluated on terms it does not fully accept.

Discussion

Strengths of Existential Therapy

Across this literature, existential therapy's strengths lie in treating dimensions of suffering that other approaches tend to bracket. It takes seriously the distress that is not reducible to pathology, places the relationship at the center of care, and offers a framework of meaning that clients consistently describe as deeper than symptom relief. Its commitments also appear to be clinically active: realness, presence, and meaning are related to alliance and outcome. A further strength concerns empathy itself. Vanhooren (2022) distinguishes existential empathy from ontic empathy, which concerns the particulars of a client's daily struggles and in which the difference between self and other is easier to maintain. With existential struggles, the therapist must face the same givens as the client. This makes existential empathy more demanding, but it also makes possible the "being-together" that existential therapists describe as central to their work.

Notes and Limitations

Several limitations follow from this review's scope and method. The review is narrative rather than systematic; sources were selected for theoretical and clinical relevance rather than through a structured search, so the synthesis is vulnerable to selection bias. Some favorable findings may also reflect a survivorship effect: experienced existential therapists describe an early phase of discomfort with existential material that later gave way to growth and peace (Frediani et al., 2023), so studies that sample seasoned practitioners may understate how difficult the path is.

Other limitations are methodological. The trials pooled by Vos and Vitali (2018) drew largely on patients with serious or life-threatening physical illness, which limits generalizability to broader outpatient settings, and the primary care data are uncontrolled. Vos and colleagues (2015) found weak or nonsignificant effects for non-meaning existential approaches (experiential-existential, supportive-expressive), consistent with meaning as an active ingredient, though structure, directiveness, and population are confounded with it. Much of the support for existential processes is qualitative, drawn from small samples of clients and therapists who selfselected into existential work. There is also a risk of partisanship: participants in existential training, even when not asked to compare, tend to judge other approaches as more superficial (Smith et al., 2023), which argues for an integrative approach to the philosophy of psychotherapy.

Existential Therapy and CBT

The comparison with CBT is instructive because the two approaches answer different questions. CBT and other manualized approaches have become dominant partly because their structure makes them easy to measure, implement, and reimburse (Rayner & Vitali, 2016); that same structure is what existential therapy resists. The difference is sharpest around distortion.

Where CBT treats biased cognition as a target for correction, a phenomenological stance first asks whether a given perception is understandable in light of the person's circumstances (Ghaemi, 2007). Existential therapy offers what CBT tends to set aside: the meaning of a symptom. CBT offers what existential therapy has historically lacked: a transparent, testable account of what the therapist does and why. The two are better seen as complements than as rivals. Third-wave CBT therapists increasingly help clients live meaningful lives without fully adopting an existential framework (Vos, 2023), and the evidence reviewed here suggests that the relational and meaning-centered ingredients of existential therapy may strengthen structured treatments rather than compete with them.

Future Directions for Research

The central challenge facing existential therapy is to become empirically legible without abandoning what makes it existential. A recent scoping review of existential group psychotherapies notes that such groups have been applied across diagnoses and that standardized outcome measurement would strengthen the evidence base (Frøkedal et al., 2026). Three directions follow from the present review: measuring existential therapy's proposed mechanisms, identifying for whom it works best, and training therapists to carry it out.

Process

Existential therapy's proposed mechanisms (meaning, realness, presence, and existential empathy) can be measured (Geller et al., 2010; Hopwood et al., 2024; Steger et al., 2006; Vanhooren et al., 2026). Research should track them as mechanisms of change rather than relying on pre-post symptom scales alone. Even session-level questions, such as whether the client feels understood, accepted, and validated (Wharne, 2021), could anchor a measurement approach consistent with existential values.

Training

The desire to pick up this branch of therapeutic work is widespread, but the infrastructure is not well adapted to it. Clinicians who work with meaning in life describe being held back by a lack of supervision, the perception that the approach is not evidence-based, training that ends at the undergraduate level, and the absence of guidance on bringing it into practice (Hill, 2017).

Existential empathy, moreover, depends on therapists' self-awareness of their own existential challenges, not only on knowledge of the ultimate concerns (Frediani et al., 2026; Vanhooren, 2022). Training research should therefore examine how reflective practice and supervision can be structured so that trainees are not left alone with the profound uncertainty of existential training (Smith et al., 2023). Training should also draw on integrative models that combine existential principles with techniques from other modalities (Adams et al., 2025; Sotskova & Dossett, 2017), encouraging trainees to situate existential principles within empirical models rather than treat the two as opposing.

Precision

Rather than asking whether existential therapy works on average, research should ask for whom and under what conditions it works. The finding that clients low on presence of meaning carry the greatest distress (Golovchanova et al., 2021) points to a subgroup for whom meaningcentered work may be especially indicated. Idiographic designs, including intensive longitudinal and single-case methods, suit an approach that insists each client's situation is unique, because they model change within the person rather than averaging it away (Fisher et al., 2018; Molenaar, 2004). Profile-or moderator-based matching could also give empirical form to existential therapy's longstanding emphasis on fit between client and therapist (Norcross & Wampold, 2011). Treatment-selection models may appear to conflict with the commitment to clients as the authority over their experience. The conflict dissolves when prediction is understood as informing rather than dictating: a model's estimate that a client is likely to benefit from meaning-centered work becomes one input to a decision the client and therapist make together. Idiographic models go further, since they are built from the client's own session-bysession reports; while not a replacement of their account, it takes it seriously enough to measure.

Even the field's own meta-analysts have concluded that the pressing question is which existential intervention works best for which client (Vos et al., 2015).

Methods for Testing Claims

Longitudinal Methods. The most pressing open question is temporal: does change in meaning precede change in distress, follow it, or move alongside it? Vos and Vitali's (2018) finding that increases in meaning predicted decreases in distress is consistent with meaning as a mechanism of change, but associations between pre-post scores cannot establish which change comes first. Answering this requires repeated measurement across the course of treatment, which existential psychotherapy's empirical foundation currently lacks (Frøkedal et al., 2026). A feasible design would pair a brief client-reported measure of meaning, such as the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (Steger et al., 2006), and measures of the relational processes described above with standardized symptom measures (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7) administered each session.

Clinician ratings of client progress, collected alongside, would allow a direct comparison of how therapist and client each understand the unfolding work, a discrepancy with clinical consequences (Nakash et al., 2015). In such a design, symptom scales are not the sole arbiter of success; they serve as a shared benchmark that allows comparison with other treatments.

Analytic Approach.

Session-level data nested within clients call for multilevel modeling. Mixed-effects models, linear or nonlinear, allow within-person effects to be separated from between-person effects (Curran & Bauer, 2011). That clients with generally higher meaning report less distress is a between-person claim; that a given client's distress falls after their meaning rises is a within-person claim, and only the latter speaks to mechanism. Lagged withinperson effects, in which meaning at one session predicts distress at the next while controlling for prior distress, supply the necessary temporal precedence that a mediation claim requires (Kazdin, 2007). Baseline meaning should be kept continuous rather than dichotomized (MacCallum et al., 2002), which also permits testing whether its moderating effect is nonlinear, for instance, whether benefit is concentrated below some threshold of meaning, as has been modeled for intervention dosage in nonlinear mixed-effects frameworks (Cudeck & Harring, 2007; Henry et al., 2026). This directly tests whether clients low in meaning differ in the shape and magnitude of their benefit, and it moves toward treatment-selection models that estimate, for an individual, the expected advantage of one treatment over another.

Conclusion

Existential therapy's unique contribution is its insistence that suffering is not always a malfunction to be corrected. Often it is a response to the conditions of existence, and it deserves to be understood. Across the approach's philosophical foundations, core models, and emerging evidence base, the same commitments recur: meaning as a mechanism of recovery, the relationship as the site of change, and the client as the authority on their own experience. These commitments are not relics. Meaning-centered interventions promote quality of life while reducing distress, therapeutic realness tracks the alliance, and existential-humanistic principles appear integral to effective psychotherapy broadly. What existential therapy lacks is not relevance but infrastructure: measurement that captures its mechanisms, methods that identify whom it serves best, and training that prepares therapists to meet existential encounters rather than withdraw from them. Wharne (2021) suggests the scientist-practitioner might take an agnostic stance toward the client's world, not knowing whether the room is bugged or the client unjustly paranoid until evidence settles the question. Existential therapy asks for more than agnosticism: that the therapist genuinely entertain the possibility that the client's way of seeing is justified, and in doing so, learn a perspective that formality would otherwise miss. Existentialism's place in psychotherapy is not a lost art to be recovered from history. It is a set of commitments the field already depends on. The end goal is to make them visible, precise, and teachable.
