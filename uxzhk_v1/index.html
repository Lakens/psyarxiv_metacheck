Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. Systems Factorial Technology (SFT; Townsend & Nozawa, 1995; Little et al., 2017) was developed as a non-parametric framework for inferring the properties of an informationprocessing system directly from response-time (RT) distributions. Rather than specifying a particular generative mechanism, SFT derives diagnostic predictions from factorial manipulations of experimental conditions and distribution-level contrasts, including the mean interaction contrast (MIC), survivor interaction contrast (SIC), and capacity coefficient (C(t)).

These measures provide converging evidence about processing architecture, stopping rules, and workload capacity. Because SFT does not require researchers to assume a particular distribution of observed outcomes or a specific response-generating process, it is well suited for distinguishing between theories that share similar parametric implementations or produce overlapping observable patterns in conventional analysis (Townsend, 1971; Khodadadi & Townsend, 2015; Wilken & Ma, 2004). Its relatively minimal assumptions have supported applications across a broad range of domains, including visual search (Fifić et al., 2008), categorization (Blunden et al., 2015; Fifić & Townsend, 2010; Little et al., 2011 Little et al., , 2013;; Moneer et al., 2016), face perception (Cheng et al., 2018; Fifić & Townsend, 2010; Cheng & Little, 2025), numerical processing (Garrett et al., 2019), and memory-based change detection (Blunden et al., 2022; Fong et al., 2024).

Empirical application of SFT is commonly based on the double factorial paradigm (DFP). In this design, the quality of two information sources is manipulated independently at high and low levels, producing four factorial conditions: high-high (HH), high-low (HL), lowhigh (LH), and low-low (LL). Under the assumption of selective influence, manipulating the quality of one source changes the processing of that source without directly changing the processing of the other. Lower information quality is expected to slow processing, producing an ordered relationship among the RT distributions across four factorial conditions. The HH condition is generally associated with the fastest responses, the LL condition with the slowest responses, and the two mixed conditions with intermediate response times. This stochastic ordering provides the basis for combining the four survivor functions into the SIC. Different processing architectures and stopping rules predict distinct SIC shapes, allowing combinations of architecture and stopping rule to be distinguished without specifying a detailed parametric model of the underlying decision process.

These diagnostic predictions are derived from the completion-time survivor functions generated by four factorial manipulations. In empirical applications, however, the relevant survivors are generally constructed from correct-response RTs. SFT studies therefore often seek to limit error rates by using relatively easy or highly discriminable stimuli, training and repeatedly testing a small number of observers across multiple sessions (Fifić et al., 2008; Fox & Houpt, 2016), or calibrating stimulus salience levels to each observer's performance (Glvan et al., 2019). These strategies reduce the amount of excluded data but do not eliminate the theoretical question of how error exclusion affects the SIC. Recent studies have extended SFT to incorporate error responses through several approaches (Little et al., 2022; Gondan & Legner, 2026), including modifications of workload-capacity measures (Eidels et al., 2010) and assessment functions that jointly characterize response time and accuracy (Townsend & Altieri, 2012). Nevertheless, the consequences of excluding errors from analysis, at a theoretical level, remain insufficiently understood.

This question is important because the same factorial manipulation used to generate RT differences will likely also generate differences in response accuracy. Lower-quality information is not only processed more slowly but is also more likely to produce an incorrect response. Consequently, the four DFP conditions may differ in both their RT distributions and their error rates, with errors typically occurring more frequently in the lower-quality conditions. This is not an exceptional feature of the task or evidence of a failed manipulation; rather, it is a natural consequence of varying the quality of the information available to the observer.

Maintaining high overall accuracy can reduce the magnitude of these differences, but it does not resolve the theoretical question of whether the excluded error responses contain part of the effect produced by the factorial manipulation.

The present study therefore aims to determine whether SIC-based diagnoses of processing architecture remain valid when the DFP produces substantial errors. Specifically, we examine whether differences in error rates across the HH, HL, LH, and LL conditions alter the correct-response RT distributions and whether excluding these errors distorts the SIC despite the underlying processing architecture remaining unchanged.

A well-established finding in human performance on speeded cognitive tasks is that errors are often systematically faster or slower than correct responses, depending on task conditions (Luce, 1986; Ratcliff, 1978; Ratcliff et al., 2016; Ratcliff & McKoon, 2008; Smith & Ratcliff, 2004; Smith & Vickers, 1988). This pattern-commonly referred to as fast and slow errors-has been observed across a wide range of paradigms. For example, in two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) perceptual decision tasks, errors are often faster than correct responses when stimuli are easy to discriminate perceptually or when instructions emphasize speed, but slower than correct responses when stimuli are difficult or when instructions emphasize accuracy (Donkin et al., 2014; Ratcliff et al., 2003; Ratcliff & Rouder, 1998 , 2000; Ratcliff et al., 1999; Van Zandt et al., 2000). Similar patterns have been reported in lexical decision tasks, where the relative speed of errors depends on speed-accuracy instructions and stimulus frequency (Ratcliff et al., 2004a; Tillman et al., 2017; Wagenmakers et al., 2004 Wagenmakers et al., , 2008)), and in recognition memory tasks, where errors can be slower than correct responses when evidence is weak (Dubé et al., 2012; Ratcliff et al., 2004b; Starns et al., 2018; Voormann et al., 2025).

Recent studies of perception and visual working memory have commonly employed continuous-outcome paradigms (Zhang & Luck, 2008; Smith et al., 2020; Oberauer, 2023), which yield joint distributions of RT and continuously valued response error. In these paradigms, slow errors are commonly observed and visualized as dish-shaped patterns in joint quantile-probability plots of RT and response error (Smith et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2025). These findings directly challenge early models that assume identical temporal dynamics for correct and error responses (Laming, 1968; Stone, 1960; but see Laming, 1979; Luce, 1986, for extended discussion) and motivate models that allow heterogeneous temporal dynamics (Ashby, 1983; Blurton et al., 2020; Brown & Heathcote, 2008; Link & Heath, 1975; Ratcliff, 1978; Smith, 2023; Smith & Vickers, 1988).

Most theories that predict fast or slow errors formalize their predictions using structured combinations of selected sequential sampling processes, such as Poisson counting process, random walk, and diffusion process (see Audley & Pike, 1965; Ratcliff & Smith, 2004, for historical and recent reviews). The sequential sampling models are widely studied because they provide a unified prediction of all major outcomes in speeded cognitive tasks: (a) the proportion of responses to each alternative, (b) the full RT distribution for each alternative, and (c) the relationship between accuracy and RT within and across alternatives. Across all models, the diffusion decision model proposed by Ratcliff (1978) has received sustained attention because it successfully captures behavioral patterns across a wide range of paradigms (Ratcliff et al., 2016). In this model, evidence accumulation is described as a diffusion process (or equivalently, Wiener process) that evolves over time toward one of two decision boundaries. The drift rate specifies the average rate at which evidence accumulates toward a boundary, the starting point specifies the initial position of the accumulation process relative to two boundaries, and the boundary separation specifies the total amount of evidence required before a response is executed. Smith (2016) extended this framework to continuously valued responses through the circular diffusion model (CDM), which successfully predicts the joint RT and error distributions, the marginal RT and response-error distribution, and the relationship between response error and RT, including patterns of fast and slow errors.

In the diffusion model literature, fast and slow errors arise from distinct sources of variability that operate across trials. Variability in starting point, which reflects trial-to-trial fluctuations in pre-decisional bias toward one response alternative, increases the probability of rapid boundary crossings at the incorrect boundary and therefore produces fast errors. Similarly, variability in boundary separation, which reflects trial-to-trial changes in the amount of evidence required before responding, can also contribute to relatively fast errors when the effective boundary is reduced on some trials. Variability in drift rate, which reflects fluctuations in the quality or strength of evidence extracted from the stimulus, leads to slow errors because incorrect responses tend to arise on trials with weak or misleading evidence, resulting in longer accumulation times before reaching the incorrect boundary.

The inclusion of cross-trial parameter variability provides a systematic account of how different sources of variability generate distinct temporal patterns of errors at the level of response time distributions, thereby allowing sequential sampling models to accommodate a wide range of behavioral patterns. However, the same assumption of cross-trial parameter variability has also been challenged for introducing excessive model flexibility (Jones & Dzhafarov, 2014; but see Smith et al., 2014 and Heathcote et al., 2014 for further discussions). Ratcliff et al. (2018) provided the first behavioural evidence for trial-to-trial variability in drift rate, using double-pass procedures across five perceptual decision tasks to distinguish systematic, stimulus-dependent variability from internal noise (see also Burgess & Colborne, 1988; Cabrera et al., 2015; Lu & Dosher, 2008 , 2014). Smith (2023) subsequently compared the standard diffusion model and three dual-diffusion racing models against data from a numerosity-discrimination study (Ratcliff, 2008) and a spatial-cuing study (Smith et al., 2004), concluding that cross-trial drift-rate variability was required to account satisfactorily for the fine-grained RT and choice patterns, even though the racing architecture assumed in several models could generate slow errors without it.

The presence of cross-trial parameter variability is especially meaningful for current study, as they may influence the SFT double factorial conditions to different degrees. For example, variability in evidence quality may exert a greater influence when mean evidence strength is low, producing more pronounced changes in the RT and accuracy distributions of the LL and mixed conditions than in the HH condition. Because distribution-level contrasts such as the SIC depend on specific relationships among all four factorial conditions, any source of variability that reshapes these distributions non-uniformly may change the observed architectural signature.

The present study uses simulations to investigate whether an SIC calculated from correct-response RTs remains diagnostic in the presence of errors and, specifically, to disentangle the effects of slow and fast errors. We hold processing architecture constant while introducing the same degree of parameter variability across the HH, HL, LH, and LL conditions, allowing the resulting error rates and error-time relationships to differ naturally with stimulus quality. We then examine whether excluding these errors reshapes the four correct-response distributions sufficiently to alter the SIC, and whether this effect depends on errors being concentrated among relatively slow or fast responses. Our results show that SIC distortion emerges primarily when the prevalence and timing of errors differ across factorial conditions, resulting in unequal selection of the correct-response distributions. Under these circumstances, the SIC can become substantially attenuated and may resemble the prediction of a different architecture, even when stochastic ordering remains intact.

We develop this investigation primarily around coactive processing and then test whether the central effect generalizes to parallel self-terminating architectures. We first introduce SFT, the DFP, and the SIC prediction for coactive processing. We then present the partitioned Circular Diffusion Model as our implementation of a coactive architecture (Smith, 2019), describe its multidimensional diffusion properties, and map its predictions onto AND and OR task rules. Using this model, we examine slow errors generated by cross-trial drift-rate variability and fast errors generated by decision-boundary variability under both task rules, using conditional accuracy functions to visualize the error temporal properties before evaluating the corresponding survivor functions and SICs. Finally, we conduct a narrower extension to parallel self-terminating processing using a racing diffusion model, the linear-drift, linear-infinitesimal-variance model (Smith, 2023). This extension focuses specifically on slow errors because racing can generate them intrinsically even when model parameters remain fixed across trials (Tillman et al., 2020). This extension therefore provides a benchmark for separating the mere presence of slow errors from the effects of increasingly conditiondependent slow errors. We first establish whether race-generated slow errors preserve the canonical parallel SIC and then introduce drift-rate variability to determine whether disproportionate error selection attenuates that signature. This additional simulation also tests whether error-related SIC attenuation reflects a general consequence of conditioning on correct responses, rather than a property specific to coactivation or circular diffusion geometry.

Systems Factorial Technology

Systems Factorial Technology (SFT) is a meta-theoretical framework designed to diagnose the structure of multiple-source information processing (Townsend et al., 2018; Townsend & Nozawa, 1995). SFT seeks to draw qualitative inferences about how multiple sources of information are combined to guide a single response. A central advantage of SFT is that its inferences are non-parametric and model-free. That is, SFT does not assume a particular distributional form for RTs, nor does it rely on parameter estimation within a specified generative model. Instead, conclusions about processing structure are derived directly from order relationships and contrasts among empirical RT distributions obtained from carefully constrained experimental conditions. SFT characterizes a processing system based on three primary properties: architecture, stopping rule, and workload capacity.

Architecture refers to how multiple sources of information are temporally and functionally organized during processing. SFT distinguishes three processing architectures: serial, parallel, and coactive. In a serial architecture, sources are processed one at a time following a sequence. The order of processing may be fixed or flexible across trials, and whether processing proceeds to subsequent sources depends on the stopping rule, which is introduced in the next paragraph. In a parallel architecture, multiple sources are processed simultaneously in separate channels. Processing across channels may be independent or may involve interactions that produce facilitatory or inhibitory effects (Eidels et al., 2011). Despite such interactions, information remains distributed across distinct processing channels. In a coactive architecture, multiple sources are processed simultaneously but pooled into a single processing stream. Rather than being processed in separate channels, information from all sources jointly contributes to a unified decision process (Houpt & Townsend, 2011; Townsend & Houpt, 2025).

The stopping rule specifies the condition under which processing terminates. SFT distinguishes between self-terminating and exhaustive stopping rules. Under a self-terminating stopping rule, processing ends as soon as sufficient information is obtained to support a response, and the remaining sources need not complete processing. Under an exhaustive stopping rule, processing exhausts all sources, regardless of whether sufficient information for a response has been obtained earlier. Stopping rules are conceptually independent of architecture, and different combinations of architecture and stopping rule yield distinct predictions for processing time. For example, a serial exhaustive system predicts that total processing time equals the sum of processing times across all sequentially processed sources, whereas a parallel self-terminating system predicts that total processing time corresponds to the minimum processing time among the sources sufficient to trigger a response.

Workload capacity describes how efficiently a processing system handles increases in processing load, such as the addition of information source that requires processing. SFT distinguishes among three capacity regimes. A system exhibits unlimited capacity when processing efficiency remains unchanged as workload increases, indicating that additional sources do not impair performance. A system exhibits limited capacity when processing efficiency decreases with increasing workload, consistent with constraints on total processing resources or inhibitory interactions among sources. A system exhibits super capacity when processing efficiency increases with workload, potentially suggesting facilitative interactions among sources.

SFT identifies above three properties commonly through the Double Factorial Paradigm (DFP), an experimental framework specifically designed to isolate properties of information processing structure through selective manipulation of multiple (typically two) information sources. In typical DFP experiments, an observer is presented with either two stimuli that occupy distinct locations (Fifić et al., 2008; Fifić et al., 2010) or a single stimulus that comprises two physically distinct features (Fifić et al., 2010; Little et al., 2011; Little et al., 2013; Moneer et al., 2016; Little & Moneer, 2026). The observer's task is to make a single response based on the joint status of these two sources.

Two task variants are most commonly used. In an OR task, the observer responds affirmatively if either of two sources is present, whereas in an AND task, an affirmative response requires both sources to be present. Importantly, two tasks naturally impose different optimal stopping rules (Fifić et al., 2008). In an AND task, correct responses require exhaustive processing of all sources, whereas in an OR task, processing can self-terminate as soon as a target is identified, yielding correct responses with reduced processing demands. Although nonoptimal stopping rules are possible, their use may lead to reduced accuracy or increased response times (Little et al., 2022).

Within each task, the quality or salience of each source is manipulated independently, typically at two levels (e.g., high vs. low salience). Salience is irrelevant to the response itself-both high and low salience targets are treated as present-but it systematically influences the speed with which information is processed. By independently varying the salience of each source, the DFP creates a full factorial set of conditions that selectively influence processing time at each source without altering response instructions.

The inferential logic of the DFP depends on selective influence: each factorial manipulation must affect only the processing of its corresponding information source.

Increasing the salience of a source is also assumed to selectively accelerate its processing, producing an expected stochastic ordering among the observed RT distributions. This ordering provides an observable check on the effects required by selective influence, although it does not by itself establish that the assumption holds. When these requirements are satisfied, contrasts among the four RT distributions can be interpreted in terms of processing architecture, stopping rule, and workload capacity. In current study, we focus on the inference for architecture and stopping rule by computing the survivor interaction contrast.

The cardinal and polar representations are mathematically equivalent, while emphasizing different aspects of the accumulation dynamics. The cardinal formulation highlights the contribution of each source dimension separately. Changes in 𝜇 % and 𝜇 & can be directly interpreted as changes in the quality or strength of evidence associated with the corresponding source, making this representation particularly useful when stimulus manipulations are assumed to cause independent effects along each dimension. Although the two components retain source-specific interpretations, they do not drive independent decision processes. The decision boundary parameter, 𝑎, specifies the radius of the circular absorbing boundary and therefore the amount of accumulated evidence required to terminate the decision process. In later simulations, we assume the boundary varies across trials according to a uniform distribution. A smaller value of 𝑎 places the boundary closer to the starting point, producing shorter RTs but increasing the probability that moment-to-moment noise drives the process into an incorrect response region. A larger value requires the process to travel farther before termination, generally producing longer but more accurate responses. Psychologically, cross-trial variability in 𝑎 represents fluctuations in response caution, such that the amount of evidence required before responding varies across trials. This variability produces a mixture of relatively fast, error-prone decisions arising from lower boundaries and slower, more accurate decisions arising from higher boundaries.

As with standard diffusion models, pCDM also includes parameters governing withintrial diffusion noise and non-decision time. The within-trial diffusion noise governs momentto-moment stochasticity in the accumulation process, and non-decision time captures peripheral processes such as stimulus encoding and motor execution. In the present work, these two parameters are held constant across all simulations. This design choice ensures that any observed changes in RT distributions, response accuracy, or SFT diagnostics can be attributed to differences in drift structure, decision boundary, or their respective cross-trial variability.

The structure of pCDM places it conceptually close to several influential models of multidimensional decision making that combine probabilistic continuous evidence representations with stochastic decision processes (Ashby, 2000; Nosofsky & Stanton, 2005; Fifić et al., 2010; Blunden et al., 2022). One particularly relevant example is the stochastic General Recognition Theory (sGRT) model (Ashby, 2000). As discussed below, although the pCDM and sGRT differ in their formal assumptions and intended applications, they share important structural features and therefore provide useful points of comparison.

The sGRT extends the classic General Recognition Theory (Ashby & Townsend, 1986) by introducing moment-to-moment fluctuation in stimulus representation, which in turn drive a decision process modelled using a one-dimensional Wiener process. In sGRT, perceptual processing is described within a multidimensional stimulus space, where the momentary outputs of perceptual channels evolve stochastically over time. At each moment, channel outputs are combined through a linear discriminant function to compute a scalar discriminant value, which then drives a univariate diffusion process with two absorbing barriers that predicts accuracy and response time. In this way, sGRT implements a two-stage coactive architecture, in which perceptual representation and evidence accumulation are formally distinct but theoretically linked through the discriminant function (Ashby 2000, Figure 3).

The pCDM and sGRT represent two different ways of obtaining RT predictions from the classic GRT and share similar conceptual foundations. In both models, probabilistic stimulus representations are modelled using bivariate normal distribution, and decision are made by setting boundaries that directly partition the stimulus representation space. In this sense, both models preserve the core representational commitments of GRT while embedding them within stochastic decision dynamics.

The two models differ, however, in how stimulus representation and evidence accumulation are organized, and in the range of decision tasks they naturally accommodate. In sGRT, stimulus representation unfolds in a multidimensional perceptual space, whereas evidence accumulation occurs in a separate, unidimensional space derived from that representation. Decision boundaries operate in the representation space, generate scalar input for the accumulation stage. By contrast, in pCDM the stimulus-related evidence space and the accumulation space are unified: evidence accumulates directly as a two-dimensional diffusion process toward a circular absorbing barriers defined in the same space. Decision boundaries directly cut the absorbing barriers to generate RT and choice prediction. As a consequence of these structural differences, sGRT applies most naturally to tasks involving binary classification judgements based on two-feature stimuli, while pCDM accommodates multialternative classification of such stimuli, including four-way identification as illustrated in Figure 2, and binary judgment by appropriately grouping the partitioned response regions, as we later demonstrate in simulation.

Linking pCDM and SFT

Before turning to formal simulations and diagnostics, it is helpful to discuss how the structure of pCDM bears on key concepts in SFT, particularly the notion of coactive processing.

At a conceptual level, SFT characterizes coactive processing as a system in which information from multiple sources is pooled into a single processing channel before a decision is made (Townsend & Nozawa, 1995). The pooling could take various form, though the most welldeveloped schemes assume evidence strength from two source are directly summed to be compared against a single decision criterion (Schwarz, 1989; Schwarz, 1994; Houpt & Townsend, 2011; Townsend & Houpt, 2025).

Compared with coactive simulations in much of the existing literature, pCDM exhibits several noteworthy properties. First, pCDM combines information from multiple stimuli in a theoretically tighter manner. Previous studies have often simulated coactive processing by summing drift rates from two independent one-dimensional diffusion or deterministic accumulating processes that terminate at a shared decision boundary. While such formulations allow information from multiple sources to influence response time, they effectively reduce coactivation to additive contributions at the decision stage. As a result, potential interactions between stimulus dimensions, and the consequential joint influence on response time and accuracy, are not explicitly represented within the accumulation process itself. In contrast, pCDM implements coactivation at a more fundamental level by modeling evidence accumulation as a single two-dimensional diffusion process with jointly distributed drift rates, such that information from multiple sources jointly determines both the direction and speed of accumulation, which in turn generates joint prediction of RT and accuracy.

Second, pCDM provides a unified theoretical framework for comparing performance across different task instructions. SFT has been applied primarily to OR tasks, AND tasks, and has recently been extended to full identification tasks, in which observers report the state of each stimulus by pressing uniquely labeled responses (Howard et al., 2021; Howard et al., 2026). SFT has developed distinct architectural and capacity predictions for each instruction type, treating them as analytically related but formally separate cases. When these instruction types are applied to the same stimulus configuration, pCDM provides a theoretical account across all three tasks using a common set of diffusion parameters. Differences in observed distributions arise not from changes in the accumulation process itself, but from differences in how decision outcomes are mapped onto responses. Within pCDM, this mapping is implemented by partitioning boundary-hitting locations differently across decision bounds. As a result, pCDM offers a unified process-level account in which task instructions modulate response categorization rather than the underlying evidence accumulation dynamics.

Finally, pCDM provides a principled way to examine SFT predictions in the presence of mixed data arising from multiple latent subprocesses, a situation that is likely to occur in empirical experiments but is difficult to disentangle directly from observed responses. For example, consider an OR task trial in which both stimuli are present, the correct response may come from either correctly identifying stimuli at both locations or from only identifying a stimulus at a single location while failing to identify the other. The latter case, while still generate correct response, includes incorrect stimulus identification. Within pCDM, such mixtures arise naturally from the stochastic properties of the accumulation process. Changes in tangential cross-trial drift-rate variability, 𝜂 tan , alter the relative proportions of diffusion trajectories terminating in different boundary regions, thereby changing the composition of latent subprocesses contributing to correct responses. By explicitly monitoring simulated mixture proportions, pCDM enables systematic investigation of how such latent variability interacts with SFT diagnostics, without requiring empirical separation of subprocesses that are observationally indistinguishable.

Taken together, these properties illustrate how pCDM provides a coherent process-level framework for examining SFT predictions across task instructions, mixture structures, and sources of variability. The resulting loss of the negative SIC component demonstrates that preserved stochastic ordering and high overall accuracy do not, by themselves, guarantee preservation of the architectural signature.

Interim Summary

Across the preceding simulations, the same general relationship emerged in both AND and OR tasks. Cross-trial variability in drift magnitude produced slow errors and progressively attenuated the coactive SIC, whereas variability in drift direction had comparatively little effect on either the relationship between accuracy and RT or the resulting architectural diagnosis.

Variability in decision criteria produced fast errors and likewise altered the SIC by selectively removing early responses from the correct-response RT distributions. Although the magnitude of these effects depended on the task rule, both fast and slow errors distorted the SIC when their prevalence and temporal distribution differed across the four factorial conditions. These results demonstrate that an unchanged coactive architecture can produce qualitatively different SIC patterns once error responses are excluded.

Generalization: Slow errors in the Parallel Self-terminating structure

In the preceding section, we show that conditioning on correct responses altered the SIC when fast or slow errors were distributed disproportionately across the factorial conditions.

However, because these demonstrations used a coactive architecture with a circular decision geometry, above results leave open whether the effect reflects a general consequence of errorrelated selection or a property specific to the pCDM.

In this section, we extend the analysis to a racing-diffusion implementation of parallel self-terminating processing, a benchmark system in SFT. In racing diffusion models, each stimulus channel is modeled using two single-boundary diffusion processes, each represents an alternative stimulus-identification and races to determine the response and completion time.

With two stimuli, four diffusion processes evolve in parallel, and the system-level response and RT are determined according to the self-terminating stopping rule. Alongside the standard style diffusion model (Ratcliff, 1978; Ratcliff et al., 2016; Smith & Ratcliff, 2022), racing diffusion models have also been extensively used in modeling discrete-outcome decision (Pike, 1966; Logan, 1996; Townsend & Ashby, 1983; Usher & McClelland, 2001; Smith & Van Zandt, 2000; Hawkins & Heathcote, 2021; Smith, 2023). Ratcliff (2018) extended the racing diffusion structure to a model of continuous-outcome decision, the spatially continuous diffusion model (SCDM, see also Fennell & Ratcliff, 2023). Smith et al. (2026) compared the CDM and SCDM on three perceptual experiments that showed quantitatively different patterns of RT and errors, and found both model structures provided good accounts of performance.

One special property of racing diffusion models is that this racing structure can generate slow errors through competition among accumulators even when drift rates remain fixed across trials. Tillman et al. (2020) used this property to argue that between-trial drift-rate variability is not required to explain the existence of slow errors. Subsequent work, however, has suggested that race-based mechanisms alone may not always reproduce the magnitude of slow errors observed empirically (Smith, 2023). In present simulations, we first show the SFT diagnostics under cases where the slow errors come only from the race. We then introduce progressively greater cross-trial drift-rate variability into the model. This manipulation allows us to distinguish between slow errors generated intrinsically by the race and the additional slow-error effects produced by variation in evidence quality across trials.

Linear-Drift, Linear-Infinitesimal-Variance model

For the following simulation, we implemented pairs of racing accumulators using the linear-drift, linear-infinitesimal-variance (LDLIV) model introduced by Smith (2023). The LDLIV is a dual-diffusion model that was developed to embody three properties commonly associated with neural evidence representations: activation remains nonnegative, approaches saturation at high levels, and is represented separately for each response alternative. These properties arise from the dynamics of the diffusion process itself rather than from an externally imposed reflecting boundary. In addition, its moment-to-moment variability increases with its current activation state, capturing the general neural property that firing-rate variability tends to increase with mean activity.

Smith (2023) compared the LDLIV with the standard diffusion model and two alternative dual-diffusion models: a race between Wiener processes with lower reflecting boundaries and a race between unconstrained single-boundary Wiener processes. Across benchmark numerosity-discrimination and spatial-cuing datasets, the LDLIV and standard diffusion model provided similarly successful accounts of choice probabilities and correct and error RT distributions, although the standard diffusion model was more parsimonious. The LDLIV performed somewhat better for the numerosity data, whereas the standard diffusion model was favoured for the spatial-cuing data, particularly after penalizing model complexity.

Both models nevertheless performed appreciably better than the other racing-diffusion models.

In particular, the unconstrained racing model could generate slow errors without drift-rate variability but did not adequately reproduce their magnitude and distribution across the full range of empirical conditions. These comparisons support the LDLIV as a tractable and neurally-motivated implementation of racing evidence accumulation.

Discussion

The present study examined whether SIC-based diagnoses remain robust when correctresponse RT distributions are selected from data containing temporally heterogeneous and condition-dependent errors. Using the pCDM, we held a coactive architecture constant while generating slow errors through cross-trial variability in drift magnitude and fast errors through variability in the decision boundary, under both AND and OR task rules. In each case, the expected stochastic ordering was generally preserved, yet increasingly differentiated CAFs were accompanied by attenuation of the characteristic coactive SIC, such that the resulting pattern could resemble parallel self-terminating processing. Variability in drift direction, by contrast, produced little systematic relationship between accuracy and RT and largely preserved the coactive signature. The LDLIV simulations extended this result to parallel self-terminating processing: slow errors generated by racing alone did not compromise its positive SIC, whereas added drift-rate variability differentiated the CAFs across conditions and progressively weakened that signature. Collectively, these findings show that errors do not necessarily undermine architectural inference; the greater threat arises when their timing and prevalence differ across factorial conditions, causing correct-only analysis to reshape the contributing RT distributions unequally. We next discuss the pCDM as a distinctive realization of coactive processing before considering the broader implications of error-related SIC attenuation.

The pCDM as a model of Coactive Processing

The present simulations use the pCDM as a specific realization of coactive processing.

Generally, coactivation refers to architectures in which information from multiple sources is pooled before the decision is made (Townsend & Nozawa, 1995; Houpt & Townsend, 2011; Townsend & Houpt, 2025). The pCDM satisfies this functional definition because evidence from the two stimulus dimensions jointly determines the evolution and termination of a single diffusion process. Unlike conventional coactive models, which mostly reduce evidence from multiple sources to a scalar sum, the pCDM retains the two-dimensional structure of the evidence throughout accumulation. It therefore provides a useful case for examining whether established SIC predictions generalize to a form of coactivation in which the relative strength and variability of the component sources remain explicitly represented.

A distinctive feature of the pCDM, as a multidimensional stochastic decision process, is its relationship to General Recognition Theory (GRT; Ashby & Townsend, 1986). GRT represents multidimensional stimuli as probability distributions within a perceptual space and partitions that space using decision bounds. The stochastic GRT model extends this framework to RT by allowing momentary perceptual representations to fluctuate over time and projecting them through a discriminant function onto a separate, unidimensional diffusion process (Ashby, 2000). It therefore implements a two-stage form of coactivation: multidimensional perceptual information is first combined into a scalar discriminant value, which then drives evidence accumulation.

The pCDM provides a different stochastic extension of the same representational framework. Rather than separating the stimulus representation from the accumulation process, it unifies them: evidence accumulates directly as a two-dimensional diffusion process within the stimulus-related evidence space and terminates at a circular absorbing boundary, defined in that same space. Decision bounds partition the absorbing boundary into response regions, and the joint distributions of choice and RT are obtained from the locations and times at which trajectories first reach those regions. This construction makes the effects of relationships between stimulus dimensions directly expressible in the model's predictions. Differences in their relative evidence strength, variances, and covariance can influence both the direction in which the process travels and the time required to reach the boundary, thereby jointly determining response probabilities and RT distributions. The pCDM consequently provides a natural framework for studying how multidimensional evidence interactions affect SFT diagnostics.

The same latent representation also supports different task instructions without requiring a different accumulation process. In the present simulations, the AND and OR tasks were generated from the same partitioned evidence space and differed only in which boundaryhitting regions were grouped to define a correct response. In the AND task, a correct affirmative response required termination in the region representing successful identification of both stimuli. In the OR task, termination in any region representing identification of at least one stimulus was sufficient. The OR-task correct-response distribution therefore contained a mixture of trajectories corresponding to accurate identification of both stimuli and trajectories corresponding to accurate identification of only one. This mixture is ordinarily latent in behavioral data but can be identified directly within the pCDM through the boundary region in which each trajectory terminates.

We consider this common representation under different tasks as a modeling constraint rather than a claim that observers necessarily use identical representations under AND and OR instructions. Nevertheless, holding the latent process constant provides a useful theoretical benchmark: it isolates the consequences of the stopping rule and response mapping from changes in the underlying representation. Systematic empirical departures from this benchmark would be theoretically informative because they would suggest that task instructions alter how stimulus information is represented or accumulated, rather than merely how a common evidence state is categorized at response.

The decomposition of cross-trial drift-rate variability further illustrates the value of preserving the multidimensional evidence space. Radial variability, 𝜂 )+, , changes the magnitude of the joint drift and therefore directly affects the effective rate of accumulation.

Tangential variability, 𝜂 -+. , changes the direction of the drift while leaving its overall magnitude comparatively stable. It consequently alters the relative balance of evidence across stimulus dimensions and the proportions of trajectories terminating in different response regions but has much less influence on boundary-crossing time. This form of variability has been theoretically associated with the division of a limited cognitive resource across stimulus representations, including sample-size model accounts of memory capacity (Lilburn et al., 2019; Lilburn & Smith, 2020; Sewell et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2025).

Across both AND and OR tasks, increasing 𝜂 -+. altered response accuracy and, particularly in the OR task, the mixture of latent outcomes contributing to correct responses.

Nevertheless, it produced little change in the relationship between accuracy and RT and largely preserved the characteristic coactive SIC. By contrast, radial variability generated conditiondependent slow errors and progressively attenuated the SIC. Because the processing architecture was held fixed by construction, this dissociation shows that representational variability does not inevitably compromise architectural diagnosis. When variability changes the relative balance of evidence without systematically coupling accuracy to boundary-crossing time, the SIC continues to recover the unchanged coactive architecture. This result is consistent with recent empirical application of SFT (Fong et al., 2024), which suggested that the processing architecture remains invariant with increasing stimulus set size.

Townsend and Nozawa (1995) defined coactivation as an architecture in which activation from two or more parallel channels is pooled into a common channel and compared with a single decision boundary. For a coactive Poisson-counter model, they derived the canonical SIC signature as an early negative component followed by a larger positive component. Unlike their distribution-general predictions for standard serial and parallel architectures, however, this coactive prediction was initially established only for a particular class of counting processes. Houpt and Townsend (2011) subsequently extended the result to coactive Wiener diffusion processes, showing that the canonical signature was not restricted to discrete-state Poisson accumulation. Together, these studies suggested that the SIC pattern might reflect a broader property of coactive evidence pooling, although its generality across different stochastic implementations remained uncertain.

Townsend and Houpt (2025) substantially expanded the analytic foundation of this prediction, proved that the canonical coactive SIC is generated by a coactive linear ballistic accumulator (Theorem 2.1), the broader class of inhomogeneous Poisson processes (Theorem 2.2), and the newly introduced bare-bones accrual model (Theorem 3.1). They also derived sufficient conditions under which more general monotonic activation processes produce an early negative SIC followed by a larger positive component (Theorem 2.3). These results considerably broaden the range of coactive models known to produce the canonical signature.

The pCDM implements coactivation through a different state-space structure. In the models considered by Townsend and Houpt (2025), two independent, nondecreasing activation processes are collapsed into a scalar sum, 𝑋 7 (𝑡) + 𝑋 8 (𝑡), and only this pooled quantity is compared with a single boundary. The pCDM instead preserves the two evidence components as coordinates of a common vector throughout accumulation. Their joint magnitude determines when the circular boundary is reached, while their relative values determine where it is reached and, consequently, which response is produced. Thus, evidence is combined without discarding its dimensional configuration. Moreover, the component states can fluctuate in both directions, unlike the monotonic accrual assumed in Townsend and Houpt's general proof. The pCDM therefore satisfies the functional principle that multiple evidence sources jointly drive a common decision process, but it is not a scalar summed-activation model and falls outside the class covered by their analytic results.

Despite these differences, the pCDM recovered the canonical coactive SIC in our baseline simulations, and this signature remained largely stable when variability altered the direction rather than the magnitude of the joint drift. This provides constructive simulation evidence that the canonical pattern can extend beyond scalar summed-activation models to a multidimensional diffusion in which the magnitude and direction of evidence jointly determine RT and choice. This conclusion is narrower than Townsend and Houpt (2025), as we demonstrate the result through simulation rather than proving it for the entire class of pCDMs or multidimensional diffusion processes. Our simulations also identify an additional boundary condition. When error prevalence and timing substantially differed across factorial conditions, conditioning on correct responses attenuated the canonical SIC even though the coactive architecture remained fixed. Thus, the present findings both broaden and constrain the emerging generality of the coactive signature: a geometrically distinct coactive process can produce the predicted SIC, but the empirical recoverability of that signature depends on how response accuracy selects the distributions entering its calculation, as we now discuss in more details.

Error-related selection and interpretation of SICs

The central result of the present study is that excluding error responses can alter the SIC even when the underlying processing architecture remains unchanged. This distortion does not arise merely because errors are present. It arises when the probability and timing of errors differ across the HH, HL, LH, and LL conditions, causing the correct-response RT distributions to represent differently selected subsets of trials. Because the SIC is computed from a contrast among these four distributions, condition-dependent selection need not cancel and may instead change the magnitude or shape of the resulting function.

The direction of this effect depends on where errors occur within the RT distributions.

Slow errors disproportionately remove observations from the later portions of the affected distributions. When this selection is strongest in LL and weaker in the mixed and HH conditions, the retained correct-response distributions become more similar, progressively attenuating the SIC. Fast errors instead remove observations from the leading edges of the correct-response distributions, again with the largest effect generally occurring in the lower-quality conditions.

This changes the early separation among the survivor functions and can alter the corresponding SIC components in a different direction. Fast and slow errors therefore do not distort diagnosticity in the same way, they reshape different portions of the distributions entering the SIC and consequently produce distinct forms of distortion.

Historically, this issue may have had limited practical impact because DFP experiments have generally been applied to tasks that produce highly accurate performance (Houpt et al., 2014; Little et al., 2017). Carefully calibrated stimulus setup and the general application of small-N design (Smith & Little, 2018; Little & Smith, 2018) in typical SFT tasks has limited the potential effect of error exclusion. Likewise, our results here do not imply that conclusions from previous literature are invalid. Nevertheless, our results identify a vulnerability that becomes increasingly relevant as SFT has recently trended to account for more difficult tasks, in which substantial error rates are theoretically informative. Little et al. (2022) provided the first systematic extension of SFT to error responses by deriving SIC predictions separately for RT distributions conditioned on correct and incorrect responses.

Their analysis showed that canonical architectural signatures can be recovered from either response class, provided that the required ordering relations between factorial conditions are preserved and that exhaustive processing is necessary and sufficient for the relevant response. Importantly, their model analysis demonstrated that these conditions hold for broad classes of Wiener diffusion models, including models with cross-trial parameter variability, but may fail for some Poisson-counter models.

Our results provide an important qualification to this conclusion. Consistent with the diffusion-model proofs in Appendix A of Little et al. (2022), the expected ordering of the correct-response survivor functions was generally preserved under cross-trial parameter variability. We extended this observation to a coactive architecture using the pCDM, which accumulates evidence jointly in a two-dimensional space and therefore complements the serial and parallel structures considered in their principal derivations. However, our result further suggested that preserved stochastic ordering did not ensure that the diagnostic components of the SIC remained sufficiently pronounced to distinguish competing architectures. This does not contradict Little et al.'s formal results, which concern the qualitative sign and shape of the population SIC under specified assumptions. Instead, our result identifies a practical limitation: a theoretically consistent SIC component may become too small to detect reliably.

A closely related result appears in the parallel self-terminating OR simulation reported in Figure 10 of Little et al. (2022). Under Poisson Race Model I, when accuracy became low and strongly disproportionate across factorial conditions, the correct-response survivor functions converged despite retaining their ordering, and the SIC approached zero. This convergence occurred alongside pronounced differences in accuracy across the factorial conditions: Their table 6 reports correct-response probabilities of .75, .57, .58, and .26 for HH, HL, LH, and LL, respectively. Our racing-diffusion simulations reproduce the broader phenomenon using LDLIV as a different implementation of the same parallel self-terminating processing. In our result, slow errors were present even without cross-trial drift-rate variability, but the SIC remained diagnostic while their effects were relatively balanced across conditions.

Attenuation emerged as increasing variability concentrated errors disproportionately in the lower-quality conditions. Together, the two studies indicate that the presence of errors alone is not sufficient to compromise an SIC; the more consequential factor is how conditioning on accuracy differentially selects responses across the four factorial conditions. Gondan and Legner (2026) provide a complementary response to the limitations of accuracy-conditioned SICs. They first observe that the original SFT results can, in principle, be applied to an accuracy-agnostic RT distribution formed from pooling all responses, provided that selective influence and stochastic dominance hold for the component-processing times, regardless of the processing outcome being correct or not. However, this pooled distribution does not distinguish between correct and incorrect outcomes. They therefore further derive interaction contrasts separately for the unconditional correct and error subdistributions, 𝐹 / (𝑡) = 𝑃(𝑇 ≤ 𝑡, 𝑅 = +) and 𝐹 0 (𝑡) = 𝑃(𝑇 ≤ 𝑡, 𝑅 = -) . Unlike conditional RT distributions, these subdistributions are not normalized separately to one: their asymptotes equal the probabilities of correct and error responses. They consequently preserve information about both when a response occurs and how frequently that response is produced. The analysis proposed in Gondan and Legner (2026) is tightly related to the selection mechanism examined in the present study. Our simulations show that conditioning on correct responses can remove different amounts and temporal regions of probability mass from the HH, HL, LH, and LL conditions.

Dividing each retained subdistribution by its condition-specific accuracy then rescales it to a complete distribution, concealing how much probability mass was removed. Gondan and Legner's unconditional correct subdistribution retains this information: a loss of correct responses in LL reduces 𝐹 !! / (𝑡) instead of being normalized away. Their framework therefore provides a formal alternative to the conventional correct-only SIC by avoiding the information lost from discarding error responses, which distorts the correct-SIC as demonstrated in our study. Our findings also relate to the architectural mimicry identified by Gondan and Legner's inference. Consequently, although these contrasts jointly characterize how RT and accuracy interact across factorial conditions, none distinguishes parallel from serial self-terminating processing in this setting. Gondan and Legner's framework therefore extends SFT to tasks with appreciable errors but does not guarantee unique architectural identification. Instead, it reveals that incorporating response accuracy can introduce new model mimicries, requiring additional task conditions or theoretical constraints to distinguish the competing architectures.

This model mimicry is consistent with the broader conclusion of the present simulations.

Researchers should consider examining accuracy separately in the HH, HL, LH, and LL conditions, because a moderate aggregate error rate may conceal a pronounced concentration of errors in LL. This examination is especially relevant, as the LL is usually the condition most susceptible to behavioral fluctuation. Large discrepancies between LL and the remaining conditions should consequently be treated as a warning that the correct-response survivor functions may have been differentially selected. This is not an argument for a universal accuracy cutoff, because the consequences of a given error rate depend on when the errors occur and how they are distributed across conditions. Condition-specific accuracy and the temporal distribution of errors are more informative than overall accuracy alone. The CAFs provide a useful companion to the SIC for this purpose. Plotting the CAFs for the factorial conditions together reveals both whether accuracy changes systematically with RT and whether that relationship differs across four factorial conditions.

In addition to the condition-specific error rate, the empirical task instructions also require attention. Strong emphasis on speed can increase early, noise-driven errors, whereas strong emphasis on accuracy can increase the prevalence of slow errors under weak or variable evidence. Instructions should therefore be standardized carefully across participants and conditions, and pilot testing should aim to avoid extreme speed-accuracy regimes unless that trade-off is itself the object of study (Donkin et al., 2014). Taken together, current results support a broader empirical workflow in which condition-specific error pattern, stochastic ordering, and SICs are evaluated jointly. An SIC should be regarded as most secure when the required survivor ordering is preserved, error rates remain reasonably balanced across the factorial conditions, and the CAFs provide little evidence that correct-response selection differs systematically among those conditions. When these criteria are not met, the observed SIC is still informative, but it should be treated with caution as a signature of the underlying architecture.

In conclusion, our study tested the validity of SIC-based architectural diagnoses under different error patterns across four conditions of the double-factorial design. Across coactive and parallel self-terminating models, disproportionately removing slow or fast errors from the survivor functions systematically altered the SIC, despite the underlying processing architecture remaining unchanged. These findings identify error-related selection as an important boundary condition for SFT. Examining CAFs alongside SICs provides a direct, nonparametric means of detecting this risk and should strengthen the interpretation of SFT in tasks that produce appreciable errors.

Psychologically, the mean drift rates 𝜇 % and 𝜇 & index the average quality of information obtained from each source, which may vary with physical properties such as stimulus clarity or discriminability. The standard deviations 𝜂 % and 𝜂 & quantify cross-trial variability of the representational quality, and the correlation parameter 𝜌 indexes the degree to which these fluctuations tend to occur together across the two source dimensions. Empirically, the variance terms tend to capture trial-by-trial fluctuations in task engagement level or task-relevant cognitive resources. At the level of observed outcomes, the cross-trial drift rate variability produces a mixture of relatively slow, error-prone decisions arising from lower drift rates and faster, more accurate decisions arising from higher drift rates.In the preceding sections, drift rate components in pCDM are expressed in cardinal coordinates as a two-dimensional vector (𝑉 % , 𝑉 & ) , where each component corresponds to evidence accumulation along one source-related axis.
