How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Up until now, the role of dispositions in the psychosocial effects of adolescent instant messaging (IM) remains inconclusive. While the straightforward rich-get-richer and poor-get-richer hypotheses do not allow for nuances about the individual-specific (dis)advantages of offline and online interactions, the literature on the dispositional tendencies for interpersonal emotion regulation shows a more complex picture. In this ecological momentary study (one week, 8 assessments/day), we adopted the rich-get-richer and poor-get-richer framework to test the social and psychological effects of IM with regard to the dispositional tendencies for co-rumination, co-dampening, and co-enhancing in 181 Belgian adolescents (n = 181; Mage = 15.04; 63% women). Adolescents with a higher tendency for co-dampening reported lower mean levels of momentary positive affect. Individuals with a clear tendency for co-enhancing experienced, on average, higher momentary IM emotional support.

Regarding the tendency for co-rumination, no significant differences were found. Notably, (dis)advantageous social effects did not go together with similar psychological effects. Unexpectedly, IM behaviors (e.g., IM negative social exchange) were, regardless of the studied outcome and the level of analysis, systematically significant. Overall, this study endorses the importance of the abovementioned dispositional tendencies. Yet, their value was more limited than the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypotheses predicted.

Introduction

In 2002, Kraut et al. introduced the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypotheses to explain the inconsistent effects of individuals' online engagement. Building on the -at that moment emerging -options to sustain or expand one's social network, either persons who easily cultivated offline social support (i.e., "rich") or individuals who insufficiently secured offline social support (i.e., "poor") were anticipated to profit psychosocially from online activities (e.g., more social resources; less loneliness). About fifteen years later, the two hypotheses became mainstream to clarify the frequently found inconsistencies in adolescent social media research (e.g., Cheng et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2020).

For several years, studies based on these hypotheses have tried to identify dispositional traits (e.g., extraversion, social anxiety) that robustly moderate the association between the time adolescents spent on social media and the associated social (e.g., perceived social support; e.g., Desjarlais et al., 2022) or psychological outcomes (e.g., depressive symptoms; e.g., Lee et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2016).

Stimulated by new research methods (e.g., ecological momentary assessment (EMA) research) that acknowledge and confirm effect heterogeneity (Parry et al., 2022; e.g., van der Wal, 2025), the richget-richer and the poor-get-richer hypotheses receded recent years progressively into the background with some of the most recent studies even merely focusing on ideographic effects (Johannes et al., 2024; e.g., Shawcroft et al., 2025).

Nevertheless, scholars emphasize, besides the necessary shift from vague duration-based measures towards the more informative inquiry of specific social media domains (e.g., instant messaging: correspondence through messages, either one-on-one or in small groups; IM) and its content involved (e.g., how the messages are perceived; e.g., Valkenburg, 2022), the importance of moderators to identify adolescent groups at risk (e.g., Johannes et al., 2024; Parry et al., 2022). For example, Sequeira et al. (2025) attribute the sudden insignificance of the within-person association between affect and the encountered (un)pleasant social media stimuli (i.e., binary variable) after taking (among others) the autoregressive effects into account, to the underlying presence of stable moderating dispositions.

TRAIT IER, IM EMOTIONAL SUPPORT & MENTAL HEALTH IN DAILY LIFE 4

Consistent with the aforementioned recommendations, the study of how adolescents generally tend to regulate their emotions in interaction with peers (i.e., interpersonal emotion regulation; IER; e.g., chatting with one's best friend about being chosen last for a group assignment) shows potential in the investigation of the social and psychological effects of -by definition interactive -instant messaging conversations with peers. Indeed, the most well-known type of interpersonal emotion regulation, co-rumination (i.e., rehashing problems with a confidant), does not uniformly fit the "rich vs poor" typology. Although the tendency to co-ruminate is associated with smaller support networks and fewer social skills (Tompkins et al., 2011), co-ruminating adolescents report, both concurrently and prospectively, close dyadic friendship bonds (e.g., Rose et al., 2007). Co-rumination partners take on a range of supportive roles, going from emotional to instrumental social support (Jones & Heerey, 2022), which might be related to the high mutual responsiveness individuals perceive during co-rumination (but not regular) conversations (Tudder et al., 2023). Felton et al. (2019) even found a bidirectional association between co-rumination conversations and friendship strength over a period of four months. Yet, despite the frequently replicated protective effect of social support on mental health (e.g., Harahap et al., 2024), co-rumination also shows a well-established link with contemporaneous and future internalizing symptoms (e.g., Cernik et al., 2025; Hankin et al., 2010).

On the whole, an intricate balance between social advantages and psychological disadvantages emerges. In a study of van der Mey-Baijens et al. (2025), co-rumination altered the association between perceived best friend social support and the experienced level of internalizing symptoms. While best friend social support was associated with little internalizing symptoms (i.e., stress and depressive symptoms) in case of low dyadic co-rumination and high internalizing symptoms (i.e., stress) in case of high co-rumination, no significant association could be found when moderate co-rumination was present (van der Mey-Baijens et al., 2025). In addition, adolescents are aware of the ambivalent consequences of co-rumination as they reported both feeling cared for and distressed immediately after a dyadic co-rumination conversation (Borowski & Rose, 2024). However, the results of the latter study also point to the developmental pursuit of immediate reward: only the aforementioned immediate positive (and not the more distal negative) perceptions were associated with the prospective assumption that sharing emotions will cause comfort, which was linked to more concurrent co-rumination conversations.

Given that adolescents get confronted with both positive and negative feelings though instant messaging (e.g., Tsai et al., 2019; Verbeij et al., 2024), positive interpersonal emotion regulation should not be overlooked. However, research on co-dampening, in which adolescents collaboratively attempt to push positive feelings away (e.g., After receiving uplifting news, two friends dwell on why this positive episode might not last), and co-enhancing, in which adolescents reinforce and thus nurture each other's positive feelings (e.g., After receiving uplifting news, the friends rave about how special this news is), has generated so far limited research interest with, to the best of our knowledge, only two studies explicitly focusing on these IER-strategies (Bastin et al., 2018; Wong & Zhao, 2025). Again, individuals who tend to use co-dampening and co-enhancing cannot be classified easily as "rich" or "poor". Regarding its social effects, a discrepancy exists between its relationship with concurrent (i.e., lower in case of co-dampening and higher in case of co-enhancing) and future (i.e., not significant) friendship quality after one year (Bastin et al., 2018). Its psychological effects, on the other hand, remain ambivalent as well. Despite concurrent associations, both IER-strategies could not be linked to mental health in a one-year longitudinal study (after controlling for co-rumination; Bastin et al., 2018).

By contrast, in a daily diary study, a positive association with psychological adjustment was found for co-enhancing but not for co-dampening (Wong & Zhao, 2025), thereby giving additional indications of the presumable oversimplifications of the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypotheses.

The Present Study

This EMA study challenges the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypothesis from an IERperspective. By focusing on the dispositional tendency for co-rumination, co-dampening and coenhancing, we address the hypotheses' conceptual gap that the effects of instant messaging uniformly depend on the level of the dispositional traits. We will tackle two complementary research questions series, focusing on the social and psychological effects of instant messaging in everyday life respectively.

For this purpose, we study associations with regard to IM conversations with friends or close friends.

Striving to approximate reality as close as possible, we incorporate both time-varying positive (i.e., IM emotional support) and negative aspects of instant messaging (i.e., IM negative social exchange; e.g., the perception of being distracted by the messages) in the analyses.

Regarding the social effects of IM, we will first explore whether the dispositional tendency for co-rumination, co-dampening and co-enhancing predicts differences in the mean level of momentary IM emotional support (i.e., RQ 1A). That is, do adolescents who tend to co-ruminate, co-dampen and co-enhance with their best friend feel generally more connected during IM conversations? Furthermore, we will exploratively determine if the dispositional tendency for co-rumination, codampening and co-enhancing alters the strength of the within-person association between momentary IM negative social exchange and momentary IM emotional support (i.e., RQ 1B). For example, if we find that "when the IM conversation proceeds with less social friction than usual, adolescents feel more emotionally supported in the chat", does this association becomes stronger or weaker for individuals with higher scores on the dispositional tendencies? For the psychological effects of IM, the outcomes (a) depressive feelings and (b) positive affect will be studied. Based on the philosophy of the rich-getricher and the poor-get-richer hypothesis, we will investigate if the dispositional tendency for corumination, co-dampening, and co-enhancing predicts differences in the mean level of momentary mental health (i.e., RQ 2A). Secondly, we intend to discover whether the dispositional tendency for corumination, co-dampening and co-enhancing alters the strength of the within-person association between momentary IM emotional support and momentary mental health (i.e., RQ 2B).

In line with the abovementioned literature, we expect an advantageous social effect and partially (dis)advantageous psychological effects (i.c., increased positive affect, increased depressive feelings) for individuals with a higher tendency for co-rumination. Moreover, for adolescents with a higher tendency for co-dampening, an unfavorable social effect and insignificant psychological effects are anticipated. With regard to the tendency for co-enhancing, we expect beneficial social and psychological effects. Regarding the explorative questions, no hypotheses were formulated.

Methods

This study is part of a larger research project with a main focus on the psychosocial effects related to IM. Overlapping, not study-specific elements (e.g., sample) are discussed in outline. More details can be found in De Raeymaecker et al. (under review).

Procedure and Participants

The data collection of this project was directed at the general adolescent population.

Adolescents could take part as long as they attended courses in secondary school. No other selection criteria were applied. Interested individuals and their parents were invited to an online information session in which the procedure and the EMA questions were addressed in detail. In line with institutional ethical approval (1432023000129), parents and adolescents signed an informed consent respectively informed assent. After the session, adolescents were requested to fill in a baseline questionnaire with trait measures. The EMA surveys, for which the app m-Path (Mestdagh et al., 2023) was used, consistently began on a Monday in the week following the briefing, lasted for seven days, and always took place during the holidays. Adolescents received eight semi-random survey notifications a day between 10:00 in the morning and 10:00 in the evening. After the initial notification, each two-minute questionnaire was accessible for 15 minutes. Participants received extra survey notifications after 5 and 10 minutes in case of initial non-response. Questions with regard to instant messaging always concerned, in case a new IM interaction took place, the last IM conversation with peers. Given that this study focusses on the tendency for IER, which manifests during intimate close conversations (Zaki & Williams, 2013), only IM conversations with friends and close friends were considered. Accordingly, IM conversations with acquaintances and strangers were excluded for this study. Completion was tracked daily by the research team and followed up upon with encouraging text messages. Adolescents were offered an incremental but capped reward of 45€. The post-registration of this study can be consulted on the OSF website (https://osf.io/cnw9v/).

After three data collection periods (July, August, and October 2024), our recruitment goal (power analysis with R package "EMAtools"; Kleiman, 2021) was reached with 181 Belgian Dutch-speaking adolescents (Mage = 15.04; SD = 1.74) between 11 and 20 years old engaging in the project.

The majority of the participants were females (63%) in the second stage of secondary school (41.99%) who attended general education (81.22%). Individuals mostly engaged in instant messaging through Snapchat (64.64% "a lot"; 17.68% "pretty often"), WhatsApp (38.67% "a lot"; 38.12% "pretty often"), Instagram (17.68% "a lot"; 22.65% "pretty often"), and TikTok (17.13% "a lot"; 20.44% "pretty often").

For more information on the sample, see De Raeymaecker et al. (under review).

Measures

State Measures

IM Emotional Support.

The item "I felt connected to the person(s) I was chatting with.", rated on a 7-point Likert scale ("I completely disagree" (0) -"I totally agree" ( 6)), was used to measure the momentary perception of emotional support during IM conversations. The construction of this item was based on the Online Social Support Questionnaire (OSSS; Nick et al., 2018), a broader trait questionnaire addressing different aspects of social support individuals perceive when navigating online. The chosen type of perceived IM social support closely aligns with IER.

IM Negative Social Exchange.

Negative aspects related to the last IM conversation adolescents had with (a) (close) friend(s) were evaluated with an hostility ("I perceived the messages as being hurtful."), interference ("The messages distracted me from what I was doing."), insensitivity ("I had to wait a long time before I received a response from the person(s) I was chatting with."), and ridicule item ("The person(s) I was chatting with made fun of me."). The development of the items was led by the IMTENSE questionnaire (Tsai et al., 2019), an adaptation of the TENSE questionnaire (Test of Negative Social Exchange; Ruehlman & Karoly, 1991) to quantify negative experiences in an IM environment. The four items were answered on a 7-point scale, varying between "I completely disagree" (0) and "I totally agree" (6). For the purpose of this study, a momentary person-specific average was created by averaging the four answers of each completed survey. In the dataset of this study, the between-person reliability was adequate (ꙍ between = .65). The within-person reliability was poor (ꙍ within = .27). In line with our postregistration, this overarching indicator was maintained.

Depressive Feelings.

The extent to which adolescents felt depressed at a particular moment, was measured with the item "Since the last beep, I have felt down.". Again, a 7-point Likert scale was used with higher scores indicating more intense depressive feelings. The item was inspired by the Experience Sampling Methodology Item Repository (Kirtley et al., 2024) and further refined through consultation of youth support websites.

Positive Affect.

Based on the ESM Repository (Kirtley et al., 2024), the item "Since the last beep, I have felt happy." was used as an indicator of positive affect. The same scoring approach applied as for the other EMA items.

Trait Measures

Co-Rumination.

The tendency for co-rumination was measured with the Co-Rumination Questionnaire (CRQ; Rose et al., 2002; Dutch translation: Dam et al., 2014). At the beginning of this traditional crosssectional 27-item survey, individuals are invited to think of their best same-sex friend and to consider this person while answering the questions. Rose (2002) takes nine co-rumination facets into account, which are each measured with three items: (a) rehashing problems, (b) giving priority to repeated problem discussions in favor of other endeavors, (c) probing information of your best friend, (d) being probed for information by your best friend, (e) persistently engaging in the same problem discussions, (f) pondering about the source of the problem, (g) pondering about the aftereffects of the problem, (h) pondering about incomprehensible problem facets, and (i) fixating on feelings of emotional distress.

All statements are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ("not at all true" -"really true"). The overarching dyadic co-rumination score is computed as the mean of all statements. In previous studies, the psychometric qualities of the questionnaire were established (Rose et al., 2007; Davidson et al., 2014).

In this study, the internal consistency was .92.

Co-Dampening and Co-Enhancing.

The tendency for co-dampening and co-enhancing was gauged with the Co-Dampening and Co-Enhancing Questionnaire (CoDEQ; Bastin et al., 2018). This 18-statement survey with four-point scale format ("almost never" -"always") requires to be responded by taking one's best-same-sex friend into account. Regarding co-dampening, the questionnaire zooms in on (a) the fleetingness of positive emotions, (b) deliberately moving attention towards worries, (c) focusing on the pitfalls of positive experiences, (d) measuring oneself with more successful others, (e) using external causality to explain the event, and (f) musing on past negative events. Conversely, the following aspects of co-enhancing are inquired: (a) behavioral manifestations of positivity (e.g., cheering), (b) emphasizing the experienced positive feelings, (c) reminiscing past positive experiences and envisioning future ones, (d) measuring oneself with less successful others, (e) focusing on personal strengths. Total co-dampening and co-enhancing scores are calculated by summing the nine relevant items per subscale. In research of Bastin et al. (2018), the validity and the reliability of the questionnaire was confirmed. In the present study, the internal consistencies were excellent for co-dampening (α = .81) and adequate for coenhancing (α = .77).

Data Analysis Plan

To accomplish the study objectives, two series of multilevel vector autoregressive (MVAR)

Residual Dynamic Structural Equation Models (RDSEM) with linear Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) estimation (Asparouhov et al., 2018; McNeish & Hamaker, 2020), directed at the social and psychological outcomes respectively, were run. Given the time framing of the involved EMA items (i.e., right now vs. since the last beep), we only concentrated on contemporaneous associations.

Accordingly, the autoregressive effects were estimated at the level of the residuals (i.e., RDSEM; Asparouhov & Muthén, 2019), meaning that the time-related aspect of these associations is cancelled out (McNeish & Hamaker, 2020).

After identifying violations of the assumption of stationarity (McNeish & Hamaker, 2020) in two of the three study outcomes (i.c., negative association in case of IM emotional support, positive association in case of depressive feelings), time since the start of the study (expressed in days) was incorporated in the corresponding models. Relatedly, the TINTERVAL statement ensured the temporal sequence of (non)consecutive data was correctly taken into account. Consistent with the postregistration of this study, individuals with constant time-series for the relevant time-varying X and Y MVAR-variables were excluded. All models were executed with 2500 MCMC iterations and a thinning factor 20 (i.e., each 20 th iteration of the algorithm was maintained for parameter estimation). The RDSEM models were only interpreted in case of Gelman-Rubin Potential Scale Reduction (PSR) values around 1 (Hamaker et al., 2018), indicating model convergence (Gelman & Rubin, 1992). Bayesian 95% credible intervals that did not include zero pointed to significant effects.

To answer our research questions focusing on the social effects of IM, three RDSEM models were examined. Each model addressed one of the three time-invariant standardized between-person dispositional tendencies (i.e., tendency for co-rumination, co-dampening and co-enhancing). In all models, the covariates age (standardized) and sex (men-women) were taken into account.

Furthermore, the time-varying constructs IM emotional support and IM negative social exchange were systematically included. At the within-person level, the models encompassed IM emotional support, its autoregressive effect, the covariate IM negative social exchange, its autoregressive effect, and the random unique variances of both variables. All variables were latent person-mean centered, which implies that they were centered after an underlying person-specific mean was defined (Asparouhov & Muthen, 2019). At the between-person level, the models contained (a) the latent person-mean of momentary IM emotional support, (b) the latent person-mean of momentary IM negative social exchange, (c) the latent predictive association between momentary IM negative social exchange and momentary IM emotional support, (d) the association between the overall mean of momentary IM negative social exchange and IM emotional support, (e) the latent autoregressive effect of IM emotional support, (f) the latent autoregressive effect of IM negative social exchange, and (g) the residual variances of IM emotional support and IM negative social exchange, which were able to covary.

Note that, although the included associations imply directionality from IM negative social exchange to IM emotional support, models with a second latent within-person crossed-effect were not able to achieve convergence. This aligns with the SEM literature, showing that models with reciprocal contemporaneous associations are, despite theoretically plausible, often not practically identifiable (Nagase & Kano, 2017). In the context of our research questions, our primary focus was on (a) the effects of the dispositional tendencies on the person-mean of IM emotional support and (b) the effects of the depositional tendencies on the relationship between IM negative social exchange and IM emotional support. In case the effects of several dispositional tendencies were significant, we intended to add these dispositional tendencies in a joint model, thus acknowledging their co-occurrence within adolescents.

Regarding the psychological outcomes of IM conversations with (close) friends, a series of six RDSEM models were studied: three models with depressive feelings and three models with positive affect as main outcome. Each of these models focused on one dispositional tendency. In all models, IM emotional support was included as time-varying covariate. The model decompositions were completely analogous to the ones outlined above.

Discussion

Confronted with inconsistencies surrounding online interactions in adolescence, this study applied the rich-get-richer and poor-get-richer frameworks (Kraut et al., 2002) in an IM context while incorporating alternately the trait-like tendencies for co-rumination, co-dampening, and co-enhancing.

Although none of the three IER-strategies could be clearly labeled as "rich" or "poor" according to the scientific literature about offline interactions, this study showed a more straightforward picture in which adolescents with a tendency for co-enhancing became unequivocally richer through IM conversations and individuals with a tendency for co-dampening became poorer. Yet, contrasting with the predictions of the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypotheses, the social and psychological effects of the dispositional tendencies for interpersonal emotion regulation did not go hand-in-hand.

Below, the detailed social and psychological outcomes for each IER-tendency are explored.

The Tendency for Co-Rumination

Apart from the effects related to sex, all co-rumination models showed no relevant significant results. Hence, adolescents with a higher tendency for co-rumination did not report better or worse social and psychological average momentary effects after accounting for positive or negative timevarying aspects of IM and the time-dynamics of the models. Relatedly, the dispositional tendency for co-rumination did not alter the strength of the studied associations.

Building on the literature about co-rumination as a depositional tendency and a behavior, these findings are surprising. Individuals who co-ruminate frequently with a friend traditionally report strong and intense friendship bonds, in which different types of support (e.g., informational support, companionship) are reciprocally provided (Jones & Heerey, 2022). Some scholars even hypothesize that co-rumination is used as a friendship selection filter, meaning that friendships would dissolve in case of scant co-rumination (Schwartz-Mette & Rose, 2012). Interestingly, this harmony inside friendships is nourished by interpersonal tension outside the friendship, which is used as rehashing material (e.g., Spendelow et al., 2017). Although co-rumination helps to reduce the level of ambiguity that interpersonal situations entail (Rose, 2021), adolescents with a high dispositional tendency for corumination feel less comforted by these conversations compared to individuals with such a lower tendency (Nolen-Hoeksema & Davis, 1999). Another explanation for the absence of significant social effects might be the timing of the data collection. In order to make sure that participants were able to answer several EMA questionnaires a day, all data were collected during a holiday period. Given the absence or reduced physical proximity of peers during this period, we can assume that adolescents experienced a lower or equal amount of interpersonal stressors, thereby also reducing the "need" for co-rumination. Conversely, this context might also have increased the number of IM interactions with friends, which is, after face-to-face communication, the second most used co-rumination modality (Battaglini et al., 2021; Battaglini et al., 2024). Indeed, co-rumination can also be generated by future challenging events or setbacks from the past (Verkuil et al., 2007), making it plausible that individuals with a high tendency for co-rumination will be more susceptible for co-rumination, even in the absence of direct stressors.

Regarding the psychological effects of IM, a similar line of thought can be followed. Research of Hruska et al. (2017) showed that co-rumination was not associated with negative affect in case participants did not report stress during that day. Reversibly, co-rumination did show the typical disadvantages on days with life stress. Similar conclusions were formulated by Bastin et al. (2015) and White and Shih (2012) who found unfavorable depressogenic effects for girls with a high trait corumination profile in case of high interpersonal stress respectively a moderating effect of the tendency for co-rumination on the distressing experiences -depression association. Given that distress triggers emotional sharing (Brans et al., 2014) and that perceiving problems as impassible is known to initiate rumination (Lyubomirsky et al., 1999), this explanation is logical. However, we could not find an indication for the reverse reasoning (Hruska et al., 2017): even if we assume that stress was absent during this time period, the tendency for co-rumination could not be linked to positive outcomes. Indeed, contrasting with research about co-rumination behavior (Borowski & Rose, 2024), the tendency for co-rumination was not associated with a higher average level of momentary positive affect. Secondly, friendships in adolescence might not always feel completely safe. Friends might fulfill both the role of ally and audience, making disclosing intimate information more dangerous compared to sharing the same story with a parent (Miller-Slough & Dunsmore, 2020; Waller & Rose, 2013). Finally, we note that the study of van der Mey-Baijens et al. (2025), which resembles our study, did not find a significant effect of moderate and high co-rumination on the association between best friend support and depressive symptoms either. In their explanation, the authors only focus on the significant effect of support on adolescent depressive symptoms in case of low co-rumination, which they attribute to its positive reflective aspects (i.e., co-reflection). For both studies, one can also wonder whether the protective effect of social support is, in accordance with the main effects hypothesis of Cohen & Wills (1985), not generally advantageous, regardless of the dispositional tendency for co-rumination. For example, Nick et al. (2018) showed that both individuals with a high overall amount of perceived offline and online social support experienced less depressive thoughts in dealing with cyberbullying compared to a low social support group. The fact that perceived IM emotional support was quite high in our sample, may provide initial evidence for this hypothesis.

The Tendency for Co-Dampening

Considering the tendency for co-dampening, the findings were the opposite of our expectations (e.g., Wong et al., 2025). A higher tendency for co-dampening could not be linked to lower average levels of momentary IM emotional support. For the psychological effects, a higher tendency for co-dampening was related to a lower mean level of momentary positive affect across situations.

Only the outcome depressive feelings met our expectations: the mean level of momentary depressive feelings was not significantly predicted by a lower tendency for co-dampening.

These interpersonal findings can be partially framed based on existing trends in the domain of intrapersonal emotion regulation. Indeed, several studies suggest that state emotions influence more trait-like emotion regulation strategies and not the other way around. For example, Nelis et al. (2016) found that negative affect predicted more dampening one year later, but did not detect the reverse association. Similarly, Hudson et al. (2015) discovered that only positive affect predicted later increased dampening in young adults. However, other scholars claim the opposite: trait-like tendencies impact our emotions. In cross-sectional research of Bogaert et al. (2026), dampening was linked to a reduced preference for positive affect, which they interpreted as evidence for the Contrast Avoidance Model (Newman & Llera, 2011). Analogously, co-dampening might be used with the ultimate purpose of diminishing rough transitions between negative emotional states and incoming positive stimuli.

However, according to the Contrast Avoidance Model, intrapersonal dampening could also be linked to increased negative affect, which was not replicated at the interpersonal level in this study.

Furthermore, in the study of Hamilton et al. (2017), intrapersonal dampening predicted less future pleasant interpersonal experiences. Again, the additional significant reduction of negative interpersonal experiences they found, could not be traced in our data.

Overall, our results point in the direction of the dispositional tendency for co-dampening as broad psychological vulnerability factor. Even though the positive association between IM emotional support and positive affect was, both at the within-and the between-person level, significant in the corresponding RDSEM model, IM emotional support could not compensate for the unfavorable effect of this tendency. Hence, the presence of high levels of this dispositional tendency seems to affect both the daily online and offline life of these participants, which stresses its possible widespread character.

Following this line of thought, future studies should additionally clarify whether offline emotional support and the combination of offline and online emotional support does not offset its effect on affect.

The Tendency for Co-Enhancing

For the tendency for co-enhancing, only a significant social effect of IM could be detected: adolescents with higher levels of this dispositional tendency experienced on average more momentary IM emotional support. While this finding matched our tentative hypothesis stemming from two previous studies on this topic, more research that sheds light on the social impact of co-enhancing is necessary. Regarding its psychological effects, it is remarkable that no "significant" effects, and especially no "significant" differences related to positive affect, could be found. Indeed, co-enhancing has been linked to increased positive affect and life satisfaction in a daily diary study (Wong & Zhao, 2025). Relatedly, De Raeymaecker et al. (2025) explained the finding that co-enhancing transferred longitudinally across offline and online modalities by referring to the resulting rise of positive emotions. This is also endorsed by studies showing transactional associations between intrapersonal enhancing and positive affect throughout time (e.g., Nelis et al., 2016). Furthermore, consistent with the crucial role of positive affect in establishing and continuing relationships (Campos et al., 1994), co-enhancing predicted (among others) heightened positive interpersonal experiences six months later (Hamilton et al., 2017).

Dispositional Traits vs. (IM) Behavioral States vs. Interplay Between the Two

Regardless of the IER tendency, outcome or level of analysis (i.e., within vs. between) involved, IM behavior (i.c., IM negative social exchange, IM emotional support) was always significantly related to the studied social and psychological outcomes. For instance, individuals who experienced more IM emotional support than usual or compared to others, reported less (momentary) depressive feelings.

These results provide support for the interpersonal-connection-behaviors framework, which claims that online (dis)connection behaviors are the main driver of psychosocial (dis)advantages (Clark et al., 2018; Taylor et al., 2022).

On a critical note, these between-person spillover effects were consistently large, outperforming other ("significant") effects. This contrast sharply with the occasionally "significant" associations between the IER-tendencies and the outcomes (e.g., individuals with a higher tendency for co-rumination did not show a higher average level of momentary depressive feelings). Although not testable with a RDSEM model, a clear conceptual link exists between IM behavior and the IERtendencies (e.g., IM emotional support and the tendency for co-rumination). For example, we know that providing support is part of the definition of co-rumination (Rose et al., 2016). Hence, it becomes plausible that, for instance, the variance in depressive feelings associated with IM emotional support overlaps with the variance associated with the dispositional tendency for co-rumination. In other words, once IM emotional support is incorporated in the model, the tendency for co-rumination might not explain additional unique between-person variance of depressive feelings anymore.

On a theoretical level, we add that acting in line with or against one's dispositional tendency, will presumably alter the social and psychological outcomes. Although none of the here-studied crosslevel interactions were significant, we know for example that informational self-disclosure (and not the "expected" sharing of emotionally-tinted information) predicts future co-rumination (Stevic et al., 2025). Therefore, studying (IM) behaviors that might alter the outcomes of adolescents' dispositional tendencies remains a valuable road for exploration.

Limitations

This study examined the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypotheses. That is, we focused in essence on the effects of stable dispositional traits which are assumed to predict adolescents' outcomes across situations. However, research with regard to intrapersonal rumination shows that the predictive effects of state, trait and average state rumination differ (Hann et al., 2026). Moreover, the same study established that not trait rumination, which is traditionally considered to be a person's "average level" of rumination across situations and offline/online modalities, but average state rumination was the best predictor of negative affect. Hence, we can wonder whether an average within-subject measure of the dispositional tendencies would have provided different results than our current trait measure, which was only assessed at baseline. Nevertheless, in the EMA study of Sequeira et al. (2025), certain disposition-like tendencies were identified despite the sole focus on momentary affect measures. In addition, Wei et al. (2024) concluded that the neural circuitries involved in intrapersonal state and trait rumination are largely the same, which might also hold for co-rumination.

Relatedly, some studies seem to suggest that personality traits interact with (the tendency for) interpersonal emotion regulation. For example, Kovacs et al. (2025) reported that emerging adults with trait worry, which shows similarities with dispositional anxiety, tend to co-ruminate more on a daily basis. Furthermore, co-rumination was shown to mediate the association between the tendency to seek approval from others, which resembles the deposition social comparison orientation, and depressive symptoms (Weinstock & Whisman, 2007). Third, neurotic individuals are concurrently more susceptible to co-ruminative behavior and the associated internalizing symptoms though their use of intrapersonal brooding (Luo & Luo, 2025).

Closely related to the point raised by the latter study, this study did not include intrapersonal assessments. Rumination has been shown to affect co-rumination, certainly in combination with life stress. Kovacs et al. (2025) found that individuals relied more on co-rumination in case they used higher-than-usual rumination. Moreover, on days with reported negative experiences, the association between the two got stronger. Likewise, Barreira et al. (2025) found that face-to-face co-rumination mediated the association between rumination and internalizing outcomes. Adding an extra layer, intraand interpersonal trait and state information do not always coincide. In the abovementioned study of Kovacs et al. (2025), trait rumination was not significantly related to daily co-rumination.

Adolescents with a tendency for co-dampening might benefit from "guidelines" how they could deal with positive experiences more neutrally, without actively diminishing positive emotions. Conclusion

In this study, we explored the rich-get-richer and the poor-get-richer hypotheses from the nontraditional angle of the tendency for interpersonal emotion regulation. Both social and psychological outcomes were studied while taking adolescents' instant messaging conversations with friends and close friends into account. Although all three tendencies for interpersonal emotion regulation could not be easily classified as "rich" or "poor" based on the literature about their offline life, our study showed a clear-cut view. Individuals with a higher tendency for co-dampening had a lower mean level of momentary positive affect, which points to an unambiguous "get poorer"-effect. In addition, the tendency for co-enhancing predicted differences in the mean level of IM emotional support (i.e., higher), a clear "get richer"-effect. For the tendency for co-rumination, we unexpectedly did not find significant results. Contrary to the rich-get-richer and poor-get-richer framework, the dispositional tendencies did not significantly affect both the social and psychological outcomes at the same time.

Additionally, all explorative cross-level interactions were insignificant. Third, although not in line with the rich-get-richer and poor-get-richer hypotheses, IM behavior consistently proved to be a predictor of all studied outcomes. Overall, this study endorses the socially beneficial value of a tendency for coenhancing and the psychological disadvantage related to a tendency for co-dampening. Moreover, more attention should be devoted to possible (IM) behavioral determinants of psychosocial outcomes that fluctuate throughout time.

Furthermore, we measured the tendency for co-rumination, co-dampening and co-enhancing in a very specific way. Firstly, all three tendencies were measured in relation to one's best same-sex friend. This means that these tendencies are in essence interactive. Yet, these tendencies were only assessed from a subjective self-report perspective. Reciprocal assessments that merge information provided by both friends and therefore allow to calculate more objective (latent) trait scores, should be further explored in the future. Secondly, the time-varying constructs of this study (e.g., IM emotional support) were measured while referring to the last IM conversation with one's close friends and friends.

Consequently, these interactions do not necessary involve one-on-one contacts with one's best friend.

Relevantly, IM conversations with regular friends or group chats in which several people are involved might be less intimate (Cooney et al., 2020) and might thus engender less interpersonal emotion regulation. Thirdly, we focused, in line with the research tradition on this topic, on the tendency for corumination, co-dampening and co-enhancing in interaction with same-sex friends. The importance of mixed-sex relationships in adolescence was already discussed more than 25 years ago by Bukowski et al. (1999): while same-sex and mixed-sex friendships may function independently from one another (i.e., parallel systems), (additional) mixed-sex relationships become especially meaningful for adolescents who miss out on emotionally intimate same-sex friendships (i.e., back-up system). Boys reportedly find it easier to disclose their inner world towards individuals of the other sex (Calmes & Roberts, 2008; Borowski & Rose, 2022). As such, a relevant group of interaction partners might be left out in this study, thereby providing scope for future investigation.

Implications

In this study, the social and psychological effects of the dispositional tendencies for interpersonal emotion regulation did not go hand-in-hand. Although the tendency for co-dampening was associated with more widespread outcomes (i.e., positive affect in general, not just linked to IM) than the tendency for co-enhancing, these equivocal social and psychological effects emphasize that we should be cautious about overgeneralizing the impact of the considered dispositional tendencies.
