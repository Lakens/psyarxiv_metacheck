How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Abstract

For many, flavour is the quintessential multisensory experience. It emerges from synthesizing information across sensory modalities and temporal scales. Disparities between these cues are inevitable, and regulation is necessary to synthesize a unitary flavour percept, triggering hedonic and attentional responses, and ultimately liking. Although several frameworks address specific consequences of flavour incongruity, an integrative account unifying perceptual, attentional, hedonic, and adaptive responses remains poorly specified. Here, we explore whether the principles of conflict monitoring, originally developed to explain sensorimotor competition, can be extended to explain the dynamics of competition between flavour-related perceptual representations. We argue that brain mechanisms that monitor for, signal, and regulate discrepancies in information processing can help explain how incongruent flavour cues are resolved, how attention and hedonic responses are modulated, and how flavour representations are updated through experience. This framework is complementary with existing accounts based on predictive processing, causal inference, associative learning, and assimilation-contrast, by specifying when unresolved competition between flavour representations recruits attentional control and adaptive learning. Considering the role of conflict mechanisms in flavour can provide some unification across existing accounts and suggest avenues for research both in the laboratory as well as in the consumer industry.

1. Introduction

Flavour refers to the experience associated with consuming food and drink, and it contributes to the biological imperative of nutrient selection and avoiding harmful substances (Breslin, 2013). Its perceptual, cognitive, and emotional correlates are deeply entangled with multisensory processes involving most, if not all our senses (Prescott, 2015; Spence, 2013).

However, one often neglected aspect is that incongruities between the multiplicity of flavour cues are bound to occur due to input variability, the imprecision of our sensory systems, and the uncertainty of expectations. Whereas small discrepancies may be resolved seamlessly through multisensory integration, larger or behaviourally salient discrepancies may recruit additional monitoring and control mechanisms. Here, we propose the tentative hypothesis that conflict monitoring and control processes, whose bases are well established in other domains of human cognition (e.g., Botvinick et al., 2001 Botvinick et al., , 2004;; Botvinick & Cohen, 2014; Shenhav et al., 2013 Shenhav et al., , 2016)), can help explain how these ubiquitous disparities in flavour are dealt with.

2. What makes up flavour?

The subjective phenomenon of flavour emerges from the combination of cues in different sensory modalities experienced concurrently as we eat or drink, as well as a range of contextual factors unfolding over a more extended time frame (see Figure 1). Although there is some disagreement about how to classify flavour cues (see, Skrzypulec, 2023), here we adopt a widely accepted categorisation distinguishing between interoceptive (or in-mouth, proximal) and exteroceptive (or distal) inputs (Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2015; Small et al., 2008).

Interoceptive cues (gustatory, olfactory, auditory and oral-somatosensory cues) are constitutive of flavour while it is being experienced in the mouth: the core flavour experience. Several wellknown crossmodal interactions occur amongst these interoceptive cues, some leading to commonplace illusions such as oral capture and odour referral (whereby retronasal odours are experienced as originating in the mouth rather than in the nose, Spence, 2016; Lim & Johnson, 2012; Stevenson et al., 2011). Exteroceptive inputs are distal cues from vision (colour, shape, and texture), orthonasal olfaction (smell experienced through the nose), haptic properties of the food object or its container, and external sounds like the fizz of a carbonated drink (Zampini & Spence, 2010). Their crossmodal influence on flavour acts by anticipating core sensory properties prior to (and sometimes during) consumption. In addition to exteroceptive inputs, one could also include product-extrinsic cues that surround the flavour object but are not physically part of it, such as labelling, visual characteristics of packaging or simply the context where the food is being consumed. These may exert a critical influence on the flavour experience, also due to the powerful expectations they generate (see, Okamoto & Dan, 2013; Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2015). Irrespective of the classification adopted, an important consequence is that the multiplicity of cues and time scales involved in everyday life flavour experience must constantly create mismatches; the motivating question here is how our brains resolve them. 3. What has conflict got to do with flavour?

Perception is an inferential process in which the brain combines sensory evidence with prior expectations to estimate the most likely causes of input, and hence the presence of objects or the occurrence of events (Clark, 2013; Friston, 2005; Vetter et al., 2024). This inferential process inevitably generates mismatches or incongruities that must be smoothed out to form coherent stable percepts. How these incongruities are resolved is central in current theories of perception, for example minimising prediction errors in predictive processing theory (Clark, 2013; Sprevak & Smith, 2023) or reconciling crossmodal cue disparity in causal inference theories of multisensory perception (Cao et al., 2019; Körding et al., 2007; Rohe & Noppeney, 2015; Shams & Beierholm, 2010). Flavour has been described under similar inferential terms, where multiple noisy cues are synthesized into a unitary flavour object (Hong, 2025; Lyons & Gottfried, 2025; Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2015). We argue that this synthesis ultimately involves the resolution of representational competition and eventually engages conflict processing.

It is worth here distinguishing between several concepts. Representational competition is a core concept of this proposal: it refers to a computational or neural state where alternative and incompatible flavour interpretations remain simultaneously active. Representational competition is an intermediate stage and links to crossmodal causal inference, a model for crossmodal integration developed in other contexts where integrated vs. segregated interpretations of the input cues compete. One claim of this proposal is that strong representational competition engages conflict resolution mechanisms to arrive at a univocal perceptual interpretation. Cue disparity refers to differences in the value of input cues with respect to their common (learned) association for an individual. Cue disparity is diagnostic but not sufficient for representational competition to engage conflict resolution, in the sense that small or unimportant cue disparities may well be resolved without engagement of conflict resolution. Cue disparities can occur as statistical, semantic, or affective mismatch between concurrent sensory input cues, or as prediction error if mismatch unfolds as a breach in expectation or a deviation in one input cue predicted from another input cue.

3.1. The conflict monitoring and cognitive control theory

The conflict monitoring theory explains how humans dynamically allocate resources adaptively in response to processing demands. According to its seminal formulation, conflict monitoring and control operate through three core mechanisms. An ongoing background mechanism monitors for conflict throughout various levels of information processing. Its goal is to signal conflict, when it happens. Here, conflict refers to the concurrent activation of incompatible neural representations, for instance alternative and mutually exclusive action plans.

A paradigm-case of conflict is the Stroop task (Stroop, 1935): when tasked with naming the ink colour of written words such as RED (e.g., correct response 'blue'), the automatic but incorrect urge to read aloud (e.g., 'red') must be inhibited (for examples of flavour Stroop, see White & Prescott, 2007; Yang et al., 2024). Second, a reactive control mechanism is put into action when conflict is detected. Reactive control deploys immediate short-term adjustments to stifle conflict, so that a univocal outcome allows the appropriate representation to dominate (in the earlier Stroop example, to carry out the correct action, e.g., saying 'blue'). Behaviourally, reactive control leads to slower responses and increased mistakes. The engagement in reactive adjustments can trigger a switch from an automatic (fast, low demand) processing mode typical of low conflict situations, to a controlled (slow, high demand) one. Third, a proactive control mechanism implements strategic longer-term adjustments after conflict to exert more efficient conflict regulation in future reoccurrences (Botvinick et al., 2004; Braem et al., 2019; Gratton et al., 2018). These proactive adjustments lead to conflict adaptation or congruence sequence effects (CSE), and can be characterised via associative learning mechanisms (Abrahamse et al., 2016).

The broader neural network associated with conflict monitoring and control is relatively well characterized. Although its precise computations remain debated, the dorsal aspect of the anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) plays a central role in conflict monitoring and detection (Clairis & Lopez-Persem, 2023; Vassena et al., 2017). This conflict signal leads to subsequent reactive adjustments involving the central executive brain network typically including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) (Botvinick et al., 2001 (Botvinick et al., , 2004;; Shenhav et al., 2016). The dACC does not simply signal presence of conflict, but anticipates costs-benefits from control (Shenhav et al., 2013 (Shenhav et al., , 2016)), as well as associated affective value (Dignath et al., 2020; Dreisbach & Fischer, 2015; Spunt et al., 2012). These computations are thought to be implemented via connectivity of the ACC with the anterior insular cortex (AI), the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and midbrain reward circuitry including dopaminergic pathways (e.g., the ventral tegmental area, VTA). Based on a trade-off between these sources of information, conflict signals call for a reorientation in the current distribution of resources and scale such adjustments to meet task requirements. Dopaminergic gating from the reward circuit is key in the proactive adaptation process to minimise the costs of potential conflicts in the future, that is, conflict adaptation (Botvinick & Cohen, 2014).

3.2. The role of conflict monitoring and control in flavour

Applying this framework to flavour requires two assumptions. First, that the classic conflict framework developed on the basis of competition between actions or responses, extends to competition between perceptual representations before any explicit response is selected. This extension has been considered within conflict monitoring accounts and has received growing empirical support (Botvinick et al., 2001; Drew et al., 2022; Drew & Soto-Faraco, 2023; Morís Fernández et al., 2017 , 2018), but it remains tentative (see, Ruzzoli & Soto-Faraco, 2025, for an extended discussion). Second, we assume that flavour perception is inferential, with flavour priors corresponding to constellations of strongly associated cues (Figure 2). This inferential view of flavour perception has been advocated independently of this proposal elsewhere (Lyons & Gottfried, 2025; Molnar-Szakacs & Uddin, 2022; Zelano et al., 2011).

Under the two assumptions described above, the conflict monitoring system can signal representational competition between incompatible candidate flavour representations, along with their reward and salience value, and regulate the ensuing perceptual, attentional, hedonic, and adaptive responses. When eating or drinking, core interoceptive cues in the mouth and anticipatory exteroceptive cues if present, provide diagnostic evidence supporting some prior flavour representation/s. The conflict monitoring system, ongoing and mostly unconscious, may signal competition between supported but incompatible candidate flavour representations, along with their reward and salience. When all inputs give enough support to one single representation competition is weak, and the inference process will settle into a univocal dominant percept (Figure 2A). In these cases, we experience a familiar flavour, and reactive conflict regulation plays a subtle role by reconciling small disparities without triggering a call for attention, leading to the characteristic wholistic appreciation of flavour (e.g., Millar, 2021; Smith, 2013; Stevenson, 2023). In other cases, however, concurrent cues provide poorer support to a prior (Figure 2B) or even support equally well two or more alternative priors (Figure 2C). These cases may not be readily resolved into a univocal dominant flavour representation. This triggers a high-conflict signal, modulo salience and cost-benefit, leading to attention orienting, analytical processing, and possibly a negative hedonic reaction. In addition to reactive adjustments, competition may also trigger proactive adjustments. If reward is positive, proactive adjustments will be implemented associating the current cues, fostering the formation of new representations or shaping existing ones to prevent future competition (Figure 2D). 4. Flavour phenomena through the lens of conflict

>Flavour phenomena through the lens of conflict

4.1. Sensory-discriminative phenomena: congruity leads to unitary perception

When flavour cues are mostly congruent, that is disparity is small, cues are integrated and incongruities tend to be reconciled leading to the perception of a unitary flavour object.

Generally, congruency is associated with larger multisensory effects between interoceptive cuesfoot_0 (Fondberg et al., 2018; Lim & Johnson, 2011; Sundqvist et al., 2006; Zampini et al., 2007; Zampini & Spence, 2010 , 2012). For example, congruent olfactory-taste cues (e.g., the smell of soy sauce paired with the taste of salt), compared to incongruent pairs (e.g., the smell of cherry tree leaf paired with the taste of salt), lead to better detection and identification (Dalton et al., 2000; Shepard et al., 2015; White & Prescott, 2007), to a wider temporal binding window (Gotow & Kobayakawa, 2023). The stronger influence of congruent exteroceptive cues can be also considered under this light. For example, semantically congruent visual-odour pairings (e.g., photo of a bus with the smell of diesel) are detected faster than incongruent pairs (e.g., image of cheese with the smell of fish), just like the pattern in classic sensorimotor interference effects (Gottfried & Dolan, 2003). The flavours of drinks coloured congruently (i.e., a lime flavoured green drink) are more likely to be identified correctly than those coloured incongruently (i.e., a lime flavoured orange drink) (Zampini et al., 2007; for recent reviews on visual influence on taste/flavour perception, see Motoki et al., 2023; Spence & Levitan, 2022). These phenomena tend to weaken or break down altogether when high representational conflict leads to cue segregation.

4.2. Attentional effects: incongruity leads to orienting

Conflict in flavour may trigger attention orienting, and a switch to an analytical (as opposed to synthetic) mode of processing, particularly when the discrepancy is salient, taskrelevant, or difficult to resolve. Given the vital implications of food recognition, it is not surprising that the attentional consequences of flavour incongruities are accompanied by emotional signals that impregnate the experience with (sometimes strong) hedonic reaction . As pointed out by Stevenson (2016), binding between flavour cues seems to respond to the imperative of detecting (and reacting to) deviations from expectations established by exteroceptive cues (Köster et al., 2014). Attention allocation and control mechanisms from the conflict monitoring theory can help explain how familiar flavour experiences undergo wholistic mode of processing whereas unfamiliar combinations call for the investment of resources into an analytical mode of processing (often associated with negative affect). The computation of costs vs. expected rewards associated to conflict resolution serves to scale subsequent attentional demand and resource deployment (Egner & Hirsch, 2005; Shenhav et al., 2013 Shenhav et al., , 2016;; Silvetti et al., 2013). Flavour representational conflicts that are large and/or high in expected costs or rewards (such as a sour taste found in milk) automatically recruit additional resources aiding to reconcile the incompatible representations receiving support from the cues. These tend to trigger a switch to an analytical processing mode, react to the new information, and prepare for (or eventually avoid) future occurrences. Cue disparities and/or breaches of expectation in flavour have been shown to trigger neural markers of attention orienting, such as the P300 event related potential (Hörberg et al., 2020), a point we come back to later.

4.3. Hedonic effects: preference for familiarity and context-dependency

Broadly speaking, flavour literature reveals that humans prefer to consume food and drinks that are familiar, as they are perceived as safe, and that familiarity is based on repeated exposure to paired cues (Amsellem & Ohla, 2016; Prescott et al., 2008; White et al., 2020). Although the strength of this preference varies substantially across individuals, contexts, and cultures, association has been shown to enhance reports of liking (Stevenson & Yeomans, 1995). In line with this, congruent pairings of cues (e.g., olfactory-gustatory, and olfactory-auditory) are rated as more palatable than incongruent mixtures (Amsellem & Ohla, 2016; Fondberg et al., 2018; Khorisantono et al., 2024; Seo & Hummel, 2011), although congruence is not always manipulated independently of familiarity and pleasantness in these studies. Also, food that when consumed fails to meet our expectations tends to be disliked, sometimes rather strongly (Schifferstein, 2001; Schifferstein & Verlegh, 1996; Yeomans et al., 2008). Expectations arising from product-extrinsic cues (such as marketing, labels, or context) have been given particular attention, as they can strongly alter consumer evaluations and drive future purchasing decisions (Spence, 2015, for a review). For instance, consumers enjoy pure taste solutions more when they are presented along with semantically informative congruent labels, rather than arbitrary ones (Okamoto et al., 2009). Nevertheless, we must note two exceptions to the preference for congruence. First, there are some hardwired, biologically determined aversive and pleasurable gustatory attributes (e.g., Prescott, 2015). For example, bitter taste is generally associated with aversion, consistent with its potential role in signalling harmful chemicals (Wooding et al., 2021). However, note that even these biologically determined hedonic associations can be escaped via adequate pairing with crossmodal cues (e.g., a bitter solution will be experienced by many as an enjoyable drink when having a certain texture, warmth, and aroma of Arabica coffee). Second, chefs have long used breach of expectation as a creative device in gastronomy (Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2012), showing that incongruity can induce surprise, enjoyment, and capture attention in consumers (Spence & Piqueras-Fiszman, 2014). This suggests that incongruity effects on hedonics are context dependent; although some tastes or cue incongruities may trigger an alert response (as we are experiencing an unfamiliar cue combination), this automatic reaction can eventually be stifled (Hong, 2025).

Collectively, this phenomenology sits well with conflict theories. A crucial element of the conflict monitoring theory is that conflict signals carry relevant contextual information, such as anticipation of the costs and rewards expected from deploying adjustments (Shenhav et al., 2013 (Shenhav et al., , 2016)), as well as the affective value of the conflict (Dignath et al., 2020; Dreisbach & Fischer, 2015). Hence, the size as well as significance (salience) of conflict determines whether disparities are stifled and kept unconscious, or else require engagement of control. This flexible system, which confers value to control as a function of complex, contextual, information, offers a viable interpretation of the context-dependent nature of hedonic reactions in flavour described above. Cues that have high biological salience, such as bitter tastes, may trigger a strong conflict if unexpected, or a rewarding seamless experience if anticipated by other modality cues.

4.4. Food and consumer product industry: congruity guides consumers, incongruity grabs their attention

Exteroceptive and extrinsic cues are used to create anticipation at or prior to purchase or consumption. Research shows that consumers generally prefer that these cues are coherent, and their expectations met. This pattern is true of marketing in general (Hultén, 2011), and certainly of food & drink marketing (van Bergen et al., 2022). In supermarkets and grocery stores consumer choices are largely driven by product extrinsic cues (semantics, branding, packaging, etc.) and their associations (Figure 3A). For example, semantically coherent associations between colour and package label facilitate consumers' search (Huang, Zhao, et al., 2021; Velasco et al., 2015); food advertisements use visual and acoustic cues to evoke associated olfactory and gustatory attributes of the product (Koubaa & Eleuch, 2021); and, marketing research suggests that colour drives up to 62-90% of the consumer's purchase decision (Singh, 2006). On the other hand, however, marketing has shown that incongruity can be used to grab the consumer's attention. Besides the use of incongruities in the context of gastronomy and fine dining (mentioned above), food packages coloured incongruently can occasionally be introduced to capture the attention of consumers and, according to some authors, promote purchasing decisions (Huang et al., 2019; Huang, Peng, et al., 2021; Spence & Piqueras-Fiszman, 2014). In line with this, some marketing campaigns have resorted to counterintuitive food imagery that breaches expectations to grab people's curiosity, such as Burger King's mouldy Whopper (Figure 3B), discussed in Gao (2026).

5. Relationship of the conflict proposal with existing flavour frameworks

So far, we have sketched how a conflict monitoring perspective can be introduced in the context of flavour processing, and how it can integrate some key behavioural and subjective phenomena. In this section we present some current flavour accounts how the conflict monitoring perspective can help provide some theoretical unification.

5.1. Associative learning: what makes congruent congruent?

Congruency amongst flavour cues is a core element in our proposal. However, up to this point, we have assumed congruency relations without explicitly defining what constitutes congruency. What makes the smell of cherry or almond congruent with a sweet taste, but incongruent with a salty, sour, bitter or savoury taste (Dalton et al., 2000)? Why is a sweet drink more congruent with a red solution than with a lime-green one (Zampini et al., 2007)? The currently dominant view is that these links between flavour-related cues, which we experience as cue congruity, are acquired through experience via associative learning (Hong, 2025; Prescott, 2012 Prescott, , 2015;; Stevenson, 2014). The acquired nature of cue congruence in flavour has important implications. First, cue associations are created and can be modified through exposure, consciously or unconsciously, via contingency and perceptual learning. For example, contingent exposure to odour-taste pairs can make smells acquire gustatory qualities (Spence & Piqueras-Fiszman, 2014; Stevenson et al., 1999; Stevenson & Yeomans, 1995; and, Stevenson & Boakes, 2004, for a review). Because odours also can acquire the hedonic qualities of the tastes they become associated with (Havermans & Heuvel, 2023; Prescott, 2015; Zellner et al., 1983), these associations do not only foster cue integration, but also liking (Hong, 2025). A second, related implication is that different individuals (and collectives) can differ in their flavour congruency associations (Spence & Levitan, 2021; Yeomans, 2010). These experience-dependent congruency mappings have been suggested to serve as cultural motifs that help humans negotiate the 'omnivore's dilemma': the trade-off between neophobia and adaptation to new environments (Rozin, & Rozin, 1981).

According to the conflict framework, proactive adjustments following conflict are based on a learning process aimed at establishing new associations or modifying existing ones (Shenhav et al., 2013 (Shenhav et al., , 2016)). Many researchers in cognitive control have highlighted the key role of associative learning mechanisms in proactive adjustments (Abrahamse et al., 2016; Botvinick & Cohen, 2014). This, we argue, extends a natural bridge between conflict monitoring theory and the flavour accounts based on associative learning (Hong, 2025; Prescott, 2012 Prescott, , 2015)).

Incongruities between flavour cues trigger an implicit 'valuation' process assessing the novelty of the stimulus (Stevenson & Yeomans, 1995), the potential hedonic impact (Stevenson et al., 2000), as well as the degree of perceptual mismatch (Frank et al., 1993). If consistently paired with a positive outcome, learning may strengthen the association; for instance, a novel odour paired with sweetness might lead to a liking a previously neutral food (Case et al., 2004).

Existing expectations may be modified if a new experience deviates significantly from prior experience; learning might occur to update these expectations, thus reducing future conflict.

5.3. Perceptual inference and predictive processing in flavour

Predictive processing (PP) -or predictive coding -theories account for perceptual experience in the face of prior expectation and noisy sensory inputs (Clark, 2013; Pezzulo, 2012). According to PP, the brain is continuously engaged in an inference cycle: generative internal models cast top-down predictions throughout the brain hierarchies, and error signals resulting from comparing these predictions with actual sensory signals are passed bottom-up and used to refine the internal models (Clark, 2013; Friston, 2005 Friston, , 2012; see, Frascaroli et al., 2023 for a compendium). This ongoing cycle of error minimization optimises the internal models, which constitute the perceptual representation (the 'best guess' or hypothesis about the cause and nature of incoming sensory signals). This framework has been adapted to the case of olfaction and the associated crossmodal interactions during flavour perception (Lyons & Gottfried, 2025; Molnar-Szakacs & Uddin, 2022; Zelano et al., 2011). According to olfactory PP, the neural architecture of the odour system implements the prediction-error cycle in its bidirectional hierarchy (see Figure 5). The relatively shallow-processing odour hierarchy and its low precision renders olfactory perception strongly reliant on crossmodal top-down predictions. This explains the substantial influence that other sensory modalities (such as taste and vision) and even semantic linguistic cues, exert on the perception of odour. The anterior insula (AI), along with its connections with the ACC, has been proposed to regulate the error signal in this process (Molnar-Szakacs & Uddin, 2022). Predictive processing theory has also been contemplated as an explanation for the influence of product-extrinsic cues on flavour (Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2015; Spence, 2022), and is compatible with an associative learning framework, where flavour priors are conglomerates of strongly associated cues established through experience (Hong, 2025). The conflict framework is compatible with these perceptual inference accounts and complements them by suggesting specific mechanisms whereby discrepancies are detected and regulated.

6.1. The flavour network

Here, we briefly highlight the cortical areas composing the 'flavour network' (see Figure 5). (For comprehensive reviews, see Rolls, 2019; Verhagen & Engelen, 2006). The dorsal anterior and middle insula (containing the primary taste) cortex and the frontal operculum are believed to represent the identity and support the recognition of taste inputs (Iannilli et al., 2014; Nakamura et al., 2013; Veldhuizen, Albrecht, et al., 2011), while olfactory inputs are represented in the piriform (primary olfactory) cortex. In both, strength of activity is positively correlated with perceived intensity (Rolls, 2019). Evidence suggests that some flavour integration (gustation, olfaction, somatosensory) starts already in the ventral AI (Huerta et al., 2014; Khorisantono et al., 2025; Molnar-Szakacs & Uddin, 2022) and continues throughout several levels of the neuroaxis (Small, 2012). The AI, piriform cortex, as well as other flavour-related brain areas project to the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), which supports the formation of a flavour object representation, and encodes hedonic and reward value of food (Araujo et al., 2003; Kringelbach, 2005; Rolls, 2019; Small & Prescott, 2005). It is at the level of OFC that additional multisensory integration, via exteroceptive cues, occurs (Lyons & Gottfried, 2025). The OFC and the ACC (which the OFC projects to) serve the broader objective of encoding long-term reward value and guiding food choice and behaviour and are considered flavour integration areas (Araujo et al., 2003; Small et al., 2004). The commonly proposed 'core' cortical flavour thus includes the AI, piriform cortex, OFC and ACC.

6.2. Overlap between the flavour brain network and conflict processing areas

Many of the key areas in the flavour network are connected, or even integral, to the conflict processing machinery (see, Figure 5). To start with, the OFC, often highlighted as the secondary taste cortex, is strongly interconnected with the ACC. These connections act as a gateway between the reward circuitry (emotion) and executive control (cognition). Recent models of ACC function explicitly bring together its known role in signalling conflict, prediction error, novelty and surprise, with affective signalling, in an attempt toward a unitary computational description (for reviews, see Clairis & Lopez-Persem, 2023; Vassena et al., 2017).

In turn, both the piriform cortex (primary olfaction) and the OFC are considered part of the reward circuitry, connected to midbrain structures important in emotion regulation and dopaminergic responses. In particular, the OFC plays a central role in providing affective signalling (related to reactive conflict adaptation, and to flavour hedonics), as well as in enabling associative learning (important in proactive conflict adaptation, and in forming new flavour associations). The AI, containing the primary gustatory cortex, has a complex pattern of connectivity, enabling a multiplicity of roles. For example, its role in integration of various interoceptive flavour cues (visceral, olfactory, gustatory, somatosensory inputs including trigeminal) is increasingly supported by evidence (Khorisantono et al., 2025). Different subdivisions of the AI display responses related to emotion (ventral AI) and to cognitive control (dorsal AI). The dAI, richly connected to the dACC, has been described as a hub orchestrating the switching between brain networks related to control, including conflict processing (Dosenbach et al., 2008; Molnar-Szakacs & Uddin, 2022). Finally, there is some evidence that the dlPFC is involved in processing aversive foods (Hasegawa et al., 2026), in line with the regulatory effort predicted by the conflict monitoring framework when the brain must negotiate high-salience, negative-valence inputs that cannot be assimilated into a positive prior.

This remarkable anatomical overlap establishes the plausibility but not direct demonstration of a link between flavour processing and conflict mechanisms. Specifically, three limitations must be noted. First, ACC, insular, and OFC responses often involved in many fMRI flavour studies (Araujo et al., 2003; Faridi Esfanjani & Mohebbi, 2023; Grabenhorst et al., 2008; Pierzchajlo et al., 2024; Rolls, 2019; Small et al., 2004; Small & Prescott, 2005; Verhagen, 2007; Verhagen & Engelen, 2006) may not be specific to conflict as they may also reflect surprise, uncertainty, salience, valuation, or task difficulty. Second, it will be important to disentangle the extent to which these overlaps survive when finer subdivisions of the involved neural structures are investigated. More direct evidence would require experimental designs that dissociate these processes and test for shared activity patterns, connectivity, temporal dynamics, or causal involvement.

6.3. Responses of the conflict network to incongruent flavour cues

Exploring the question of specificity a bit further, we have explored evidence for increased neural responses in conflict-relevant areas along with behavioural interference, when flavour incongruity is directly manipulated. Some protocols have manipulated expectations using successive protocols. For example, Veldhuizen et al., (2011) presented participants with drinks whose taste was incongruent with a previously heard taste-related word. They found slower RTs and greater activation in the dACC (conflict monitoring), IFG and the IPS (control network), and the OFC (reward). Similarly, Pierzchajlo et al., (2024) also found slower RTs to odour related images and to olfactory targets which were incongruent with a prior verbal cue, together with increased ACC responses to incongruity in the case of odour targets. In another expectation study, Schienle and Wabnegger (2021) found ACC responses when pictures of sweets were shown while the observers experienced a bitter aftertaste (though only in a group selected for high propensity to disgust). These results typically trace the neural effects of incongruency to the dACC and are interpreted in terms of prediction error (Corlett et al., 2022), hence providing supporting evidence for the current proposal. Other studies have used concurrent flavour cues (that is, cues presented at approximately the same time), instead of successive cue-target protocols. These have typically found the opposite pattern, that is stronger responses to congruent over incongruent flavour cue combinations in the ACC. For example, Small et al. (2004), found a superadditive response pattern in the ACC for congruent (but not for incongruent) taste-odour combinations. Similarly, de Araujo et al. (2003) found increased ACC and OFC responses for congruent, but not incongruent taste-odour combinations, a finding also in line with Seo et al. (2013). These studies highlight the role of the ACC as a higher-order multisensory integration area for flavour. 6.4. Attention networks engagement by incongruent flavour cues

Breaches of expectation and disparity between flavour cues should engage attentional orienting processes, to determine an 'appropriate response'. The degree of conflict should scale with the extent of attentional reorienting. Experimental evidence from neuroimaging is so far consistent, but still insufficient. The activity arising from gustatory breach of expectation in Veldhuizen et al.'s (2011) study included attention-related areas in the control executive network such as the IFG and the intraparietal sulcus in the PPC. In Lundström et al.'s (2019) fMRI study, evidence for the recruitment of attention networks (to visual/odour incongruity, and/or to flavour ambiguity) is limited. However, in this study the recruitment of the control network as stimulus ambiguity increased, consistent with the predicted attentional deployment in situations where cues are ambiguous with respect to the flavour object. In line with this interpretation, an ERP study by Hörberg et al., (2020) found that incongruent visual-olfactory pairs produced an attention-related fronto-central P300 component, whose magnitude was in turn correlated with faster visual categorization RTs. This P300 component is related to the mobilization of resources necessary to resolve interference and hence aligned with the proposal here (Polich, 2007).

6.5. Hedonic responses to flavour incongruity

The positive hedonic properties of taste, smell and flavour are known to reflect in OFC activity (Rolls, 2019), which has been found to correlate with liking and reported pleasantness (Plassmann et al., 2008). The question in the context of this proposal is, do negative hedonic reactions derived from flavour incongruities correlate with OFC activity? There is not much direct evidence, but one potentially important finding was reported by Veldhuizen et al. (2011) in their study using breaches of taste expectation (e.g., expecting sweet, vs. receiving tasteless), as they reported decreased pleasantness ratings and increased activity in reward related areas including the OFC. They interpreted these OFC responses 'to reflect sensory, reward, or attentional processing, or perhaps an integration of the three processes.' In another relevant These apparently opposite patterns of OFC activity in response to hedonic value are puzzling, but one should bear in mind two important aspects. First, the OFC is a complex structure with functionally separate subdivisions; whereas more medial parts are activated by positive reward, lateral aspects are thought to track prediction error and punishment reinforcersfoot_2 (see, Kringelbach, 2005). Second, there are remarkable methodological differences between studies, notably that one addressed incongruity between concurrently presented interoceptive cues (tasteodour), whereas the other used breach of expectations between a conceptual (verbal) cue and a successively presented taste. During perceptual inference, input consisting of a multisensory combination of diagnostic sensory cues renders support to existing prior representations in memory. These prior representations are created and updated as events are experienced, via associative learning. Competition between viable but alternative representations, which is experienced as cue misalignments, is detected by a conflict monitor (dACC) and regulated via reactive adjustments by frontal lateral brain areas (dlPFC, IFG). This process is modulated not only by the magnitude of conflict, but by its salience as a function of potential positive or negative reward (salience and reward networks, including the OFC). The resulting outcome of this process is a flavour experience. If there is sufficient univocal support for one flavour representation, this process doesn't need to emerge consciously or call for attention, unless voluntary attention is directed to the analysis of the flavour experience. Instead, if the cues are far from fully supporting any one representation, that is disparities are large, strong ACC activity signals for conflict, dlPFC adjustments are not sufficient to integrate the cues into a coherent flavour object. This disparity may be of sufficient importance (depending on the reward signal) as to trigger an attention shift and strongly negative hedonic experience. These outcomes, in turn, help update internal representations leading to building new flavour objects or modifying existing ones, via associative learning.

7. Discussion

Incongruities between flavour cues and breaches of expectation are not just casual nuisances, or curiosities to be sought for when we go to fancy restaurants. Rather, they are pervasive in everyday life eating and drinking because of the multisensory makeup of flavour and the inferential nature of perception. However, the phenomenology and neural mechanisms governing the regulation flavour incongruities remain poorly understood. Here, we have proposed that the theoretical framework of conflict monitoring, an account of how incompatibilities are dealt with in human cognition and perception, can be useful in the flavour domain.

7.1. The basic elements of the proposal

First, internal flavour representations are constellations of strongly associated flavourrelated cues, which can be considered priors. These priors are built and updated through experience via mechanisms of associative learning. Second, when consuming or anticipating foods or drinks, multisensory inputs provide diagnostic evidence for prior flavour representation/s. If there is a good match between cues and just one prior, we experience familiar, coherent flavours. Third, an ongoing conflict monitoring process will signal competition between incompatible candidate flavour representations, along with reward value and salience. Fourth, the conflict signal triggers reactive adjustments. In the case of small lowsalience signal, these adjustments will resolve into a unitary dominant percept, stifling cue disparities, rendering wholistic flavour appreciation, and potentially a positive hedonic experience. Instead, when conflict is large and high salience, cues cannot resolve into a unitary flavour, leading to attention orienting, analytical processing or segregated inputs, and possibly a negative hedonic reaction. Fifth, conflict also trigger proactive adjustments via associative learning, which foster the formation or updating of the prior representations referred to in the first point. Figure 7 summarises a functional architecture that incorporates these elements.

7.2. Behavioural expression and phenomenology

The conflict framework provides a unified interpretation of a variety of flavour-related phenomena. First, crossmodal integration effects can be naturally incorporated under the perceptual inference plus experience updating elements, whereby small disparities supporting a single dominant prior fosters integration across modalities. Crossmodal illusions such as oral capture, odour referral, and other expectation-based classification and identification crossmodal effects are examples of how small disparities are resolved in favour of a coherent, univocal percept. Second, incongruities and breaches of expectation in flavour may trigger attention orienting, a phenomenon that corresponds well with what is predicted and observed under the conflict monitoring framework when large/salient conflict is detected (e.g., Shenhav et al., 2013 Shenhav et al., , 2016)). Little cue incongruity, leading to weak representational competition, is less likely to trigger involuntary attentional orienting, although it may still receive attention because of intensity, hedonic value, motivational relevance, or deliberate evaluation. Third, at least some of the hedonic and affective reactions to flavour reported in the literature can be related to the conflict monitoring framework directly. Cue incongruity including breaches of expectation often leads to lower hedonic evaluation and negative affect in flavour (Amsellem & Ohla, 2016; Fondberg et al., 2018; Khorisantono et al., 2024; Schifferstein & Verlegh, 1996 , 1996; Seo & Hummel, 2011; Yeomans et al., 2008). The link between conflict and negative affect has been often highlighted in connection to the experience of conflicts in the conflict theory (e.g., the affective-signalling hypothesis Dignath et al., 2020). As we have noted earlier, biologically driven aversive or appetitive associations of certain gustatory stimuli such as bitter tastes are not necessarily linked to in/congruency, although they can be incorporated in this framework via salience signals. Fourth, consumer experience and preference are strongly driven by congruency relations, with coherent cue combinations typically supporting findability and acceptance, while incongruity being used to increase salience, novelty, and attention. These two contrasting trends raise important questions on the use of cue congruity and incongruity in product searching, choice, and subsequent enjoyment. At the same time, they overlap with two of the principles already highlighted within the framework of cognitive conflict and control: congruency leads to more efficient processing, and incongruency triggers attention orienting. In real world consumer behaviour crossmodal congruency correspondences can take on rather complex forms based on symbolism (S. Anderson, 2025; Spence & Ngo, 2012), and even the weather (Islam, 2026). In this context, the conflict monitoring and cognitive control theories might prove relevant to understand the psychological and neural consequences of flavour congruency and crucially, to estimate whether incongruity is likely to deliver the desired results.

7.3. Theoretical integration with current accounts

The current framework is complementary to various current accounts explaining separate aspects of flavour processing. In flavour, associative learning is the accepted account that explains how sensory cues are bound through experience to form familiar flavours, including the very definition of what is congruent (Prescott, 2012 (Prescott, , 2015)). The conflict monitoring perspective provides a more detailed mechanistic description of how associative learning operates during flavour processing, via proactive adaptation (Abrahamse et al., 2016). In addition, inferential accounts, increasingly adopted in the domain of flavour (Lyons & Gottfried, 2025; Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2015), propose a central role of prediction error. Conflict monitoring offers a plausible mechanism that explains how sensory evidence is evaluated in the light of flavour expectations and prior representations, potential mismatches detected and resolved, and flavour representations updated if necessary (Molnar-Szakacs & Uddin, 2022). Finally, assimilationcontrast patterns explain subjective reactions to cue incongruity in flavour (Davidenko et al., 2015; Yeomans et al., 2008). A conflict framework helps relate these behavioural reactions to concrete neural mechanisms that lead to the minimisation of small disparities, that is assimilation into a dominant coherent flavour object with possibly positive hedonic experience, and to the magnification of large disparities often triggering attention orienting toward segregated cues and possibly a negative hedonic experience. As flavour research has shown repeatedly, many of these behavioural phenomena are context dependent (Okamoto & Dan, 2013; Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2015). Context dependency, in the form of proactive adaptation, is naturally accounted for in conflict theories (Botvinick & Cohen, 2014).

7.4. Neural and mechanistic implications

Neuroimaging research in human flavour processing is still scarce, and therefore it is at present difficult to draw general conclusions. Nevertheless, we have attempted to use available neuroimaging findings to contrast some predictions of this proposal. First, we note the remarkable overlap between flavour and conflict processing brain areas, something that has not been highlighted before in either field of research. However, the significance of this overlap is limited by lack of specificity (conflict responses may be conflated with ambiguity, surprise, or difficulty) and the complexity of functional subdivisions of the areas involved. Another neural expression is that cue incongruity in flavour should engage responses in the dACC, a key area associated with conflict detection. This is only partially confirmed, but a tentative pattern emerges when cue timing and task structure are considered. In successive cue-target paradigms, breach of expectation is often associated with activity in dorsal ACC regions, as the conflict framework would predict. In contrast, studies presenting concurrent olfactory and gustatory cues have more often reported enhanced responses to congruent combinations, originating from ventral or perigenual ACC regions. One speculative possibility is that expectation violations preferentially engage prediction-error and control-related processes, whereas concurrent proximal cues primarily engage multisensory integration and hedonic responses. The connection between the attention orienting network and flavour incongruity has received some support from fMRI and ERP findings, but the number of studies is simply too small to make a call. Finally, the links between brain networks supporting hedonic valuation and flavour processing are well established, but the specific prediction that incongruities will engage negative valuation and decreased activity in reward-related brain areas, such as the OFC, has so far been confirmed by one study and disconfirmed by another. Large methodological differences, and the complexity of OFC responses once one examines its subdivisions more closely, limits the interpretation of these findings for now.

7.5. Further theoretical integration

Besides integration with other flavour accounts, the present proposal must bridge some relatively fundamental gaps. We summarise them briefly. First, conflict monitoring and perception: although conflict theories are compatible with perceptual inference theories (e.g., Clairis & Lopez-Persem, 2023; Drew et al., 2022; Drew & Soto-Faraco, 2023; Safavi & Dayan, 2022 , 2025), whether and how they connect with each other is by no means agreed upon. Classic conflict theory and the paradigms used to validate it involve sensorimotor competition, but the notion of conflict in the present proposal entails conflict between perceptual representations.

Although still an unconventional perspective, the role of conflict mechanisms in perception has been considered from the origins of the theory (Botvinick et al., 2001), and it is increasingly supported (Becker et al., 2024; Drew et al., 2022; Morís Fernández et al., 2017 , 2018). Here, for convenience, we have just used conflict signalling as providing a sort of prediction error, which is one of the accepted interpretations in the field (Clairis & Lopez-Persem, 2023; Corlett et al., 2022; Modirrousta & Fellows, 2008; Vassena et al., 2017). Second, consciousness: whilst the outcomes of perception can be (and often are) conscious, the processes that generate them are typically not (Frankish, 2016; Morsella, 2005). Because the traditional view is that conflict detection and its regulation are associated to conscious control (Dehaene et al., 2003; Kunde et al., 2003), this raises a contradiction of one of the assumptions here; that flavour conflict is detected and regulated by a continuous ongoing process, and that small disparities do not emerge as a conscious event. Although the debate is still on, there is accumulating evidence from perceptual and sensorimotor research that conflict signals emerge, and have an impact on brain processing and behaviour, even when they occur unconsciously (Abrahamse et al., 2016; Desender et al., 2013; Drew et al., 2022 Drew et al., , 2026;; Morís Fernández et al., 2017; Van Gaal et al., 2012). Finally, conflict and affect: although conflict is often associated with negative affect (Dignath et al., 2020) our proposal assumes that this relationship is context dependent. However, we note that current proposals have revealed incongruity as opportunities for information gain and believe updating (La Pietra et al., 2025) and propose that successful conflict resolution may itself be rewarding (Kiverstein et al., 2019; Van de Cruys, 2017; Van de Cruys & Wagemans, 2011). Along these lines, we propose that the hedonic outcome of flavour incongruity may depend partly on whether the incongruity is resolvable. Resolvable incongruity may promote curiosity, information gain, and pleasure, whereas incongruity that remains unresolved may sustain uncertainty and promote aversion or avoidance. Flavour perception provides a particularly suitable domain in which to test this hypothesis, given the use of unexpected but ultimately interpretable sensory combinations in culinary practice (Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence, 2012; Velasco et al., 2016).

7.6. Limitations, some potential tests, and a caveat

The present framework is consistent with findings concerning flavour perception, attention, affect, and neural processing, but it remains insufficiently constrained. Flexible reference to context, salience, and expected reward can retrospectively explain integration or segregation, positive or negative hedonic responses, and the presence or absence of attentional orienting.

Ideally, these moderators need to be defined and measured independently of the outcomes. For example, the proposal could be tested more strictly if incongruity produced behavioural or neural correlates of control once surprise, uncertainty, valence, stimulus frequency, and feature repetition had been controlled. Also, one should seek computational evidence that predictive or causal inference models improve by incorporating a conflict signal. In terms of hedonic reaction, disparity magnitude and expected reward should be manipulated independently, with the prediction that the same incongruent flavour associated with low or high reward should lead to opposite outcomes (avoidance, liking).

We have not addressed in depth the variability in the consequences of incongruities between different kinds of cues. We suspect, however, that there may be important differences if incongruities happen between pairs of interoceptive cues (often presented concurrently or near concurrently, at consumption stage) than if incongruities happen between exteroceptive and interoceptive cues (often successive and leading to expectation breaches). The contrasting results of neuroimaging experiments using concurrent vs. successive cue protocols, which we have highlighted in the neural correlates section, seem to point in this direction (e.g., Araujo et al., 2003; Veldhuizen, Douglas, et al., 2011; see Table 1).

Testing conflicts and breaches of expectation involving interoceptive and exteroceptive cues can bring more clarity. In addition, we have hinted that different flavour-related cues may be weighted differently, depending on the salience they carry, for example based on their biological significance (Breslin, 2013).

Experiments measuring the consequences of flavour conflict as a function of expected value may provide a stronger test of the role of cognitive control mechanisms. Another potential validation of the role of conflict mechanisms in flavour processing is to address conflict adaptation effects, either in trial-by-trial designs or in blocked designs, such as Congruence Sequence Effects (Gratton et al., 2018).

Finally, because the present proposal is largely compatible with existing accounts of flavour, it is important to specify what conflict monitoring adds beyond theoretical unification.

We see three potential contributions. First, the framework proposes that reactive adjustments to conflict provide a mechanism through which unresolved competition between flavour representations may recruit and scale attentional control. Second, that proactive adjustments provide an adaptation and learning mechanism across successive flavour encounters: processing an incongruent event should alter the response to subsequent incongruities, even when simple stimulus repetition and contingency learning are controlled. This provides a wider scope to study and interpret the role of associative learning in flavour processing. Third, by incorporating expected control value, the framework links the estimated costs and benefits of resolving an incongruity to whether the individual continues to explore the stimulus, rejects it, or reinterprets it in light of a different flavour representation (Botvinick & Cohen, 2014; Shenhav et al., 2013 Shenhav et al., , 2016)). These proposals generate tests that are not straightforward consequences of associative learning or perceptual inference alone.

7.7. Conclusions

Flavour perception presents a multisensory inference problem in which sensory evidence, prior expectations, and contextual information must be combined to form a coherent percept. We have proposed that conflict monitoring principles may help explain what happens when this process leaves salient competition between incompatible flavour representations unresolved.

Within this account, minor discrepancies may be resolved through ordinary multisensory integration, whereas larger or behaviourally relevant discrepancies may recruit attention, control, and adaptive learning. This proposal provides a potentially useful connection between flavour research and theories of cognitive control, but the available evidence remains preliminary and largely indirect. Establishing its contribution beyond predictive processing, causal inference, and associative learning will require experiments that distinguish representational conflict from surprise, uncertainty, valence, familiarity, and decision difficulty. The framework should therefore be regarded as a testable basis for future theoretical and experimental development rather than as a complete account of flavour incongruity.
