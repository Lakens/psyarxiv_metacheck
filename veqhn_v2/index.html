Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. Do Scales Semantically Resemble the Sources They Cite? Embedding Analysis of Construct Proliferation in Five Anomy Instruments

Construct proliferation challenges the cumulative character of psychological science, and this is visible in its measurement instruments (Eronen & Bringmann, 2021). A recent computational survey identified over 43,000 unique measures tied to more than 38,000 distinct constructs across the behavioral sciences (Wulff & Mata, 2025a), while empirical audits suggest that between 40% and 93% of published studies employ measures with no validity evidence beyond their original development sample (Anvari et al., 2025). The resulting landscape has been likened to a toothbrush problem, in which instruments are developed ad hoc and rarely reused across studies. A systematic review of aggression questionnaires found that 43% were used only once and 13% only twice across three decades of research (Elson et al., 2023). Under these conditions, what have come to be known as jingle and jangle fallacies (Kelley, 1927), whereby the same name is applied to different constructs (jingle) or different names designate the same construct (jangle), can become systemic rather than incidental, with 81 documented cases spanning emotion, personality, mindfulness, and organizational psychology (Altgassen et al., 2024; Casper et al., 2018; Hanfstingl et al., 2025; Weidman et al., 2017).

The problem is partly conceptual. Construct proliferation has been characterized as a symptom of insufficient clarification at the pre-measurement stage, where imprecise definitions propagate into overlapping operationalizations that subsequent empirical work inherits rather than corrects (Bringmann et al., 2022; Flake & Fried, 2020). The empirical methods available for construct validation (AERA et al., 2014) address this problem from the side of response covariation of participants, yet the semantic content of items, that is, the declarative meaning that respondents evaluate and endorse (Likert, 1932; Osgood et al., 1957), relates only indirectly to the co-variation patterns these methods capture. Factor-analytic solutions are underdetermined by the covariance matrices they fit (Guttman, 1955; Steiger, 1979), and items can co-vary for reasons orthogonal to shared meaning, including method variance, acquiescence, or positional effects (Maraun, 1996). These methods illuminate how items function in the hands of respondents without directly evaluating what the items say.

More fundamentally, jingle and jangle fallacies are claims about names, and in psychology a construct's name is a reference to the theoretical formulation it invokes. The definitions that propagate across operationalizations are texts, yet, to our knowledge, no validation method currently reads them against the theoretical sources they cite. A complementary line of evidence lies in the semantic content of the instruments themselves and the theoretical texts that motivated them. If the text of items and their cited theoretical sources carry information about what a scale intends to measure and where it comes from, then approaches that can evaluate this content computationally, without requiring participant responses, would address the need for conceptual clarification prior to and alongside empirical validation (Borsboom et al., 2021; Bringmann et al., 2022).

Semantic embeddings offer a computational approach to filling this gap. These models represent each piece of text as a point in a high-dimensional space, positioned so that texts with similar meaning occupy nearby regions; the distance between any two points thus provides a measure of their semantic similarity. The approach is grounded in the distributional hypothesis that words occurring in similar linguistic contexts tend to share aspects of meaning (Firth, 1957; Harris, 1954), and operationalized through neural architectures that learn dense vector representations from large text corpora (Mikolov et al., 2013; Reimers & Gurevych, 2019).

When applied to psychometric items, the resulting vectors capture the declarative content of each item in a form amenable to geometric analysis. Wulff and Mata (2025a) illustrated this potential at taxonomic scale, embedding 4,452 items and 277 constructs from the International Personality Item Pool with a fine-tuned model that predicted empirical inter-scale correlations at r ≈ .70 and detected hundreds of candidate jingle-jangle fallacies invisible to traditional methods alone. Subsequent work indicated that language models infer inter-item correlations, scale reliabilities, and inter-scale associations from text with substantial accuracy (Hommel & Arslan, 2025), and embedding-based approaches have been applied to detect semantic redundancy across psychological scales more broadly (Huang et al., 2025). In a conceptual companion to their taxonomic analysis, Wulff and Mata (2025b) argued that embedding-based methods can serve not only as diagnostic tools but as instruments for increasing conceptual clarity and refining psychological taxonomies, while Ravenda et al. (2025) provided empirical evidence that semantic similarity among items correlates strongly with empirical score correlations, suggesting that item semantics impose their own structure on collected data. These developments suggest that the textual content of items constitutes an informative source of evidence about construct structure, one that operates independently of response variance and sample characteristics.

However, these applications detect jingle-jangle fallacies between scales without examining the fallacy at the level where it originates. The within-level approach compares item content across instruments and can identify pairs of scales that share semantic space despite different names or that diverge despite similar names. What it is not designed to evaluate is whether any individual scale semantically reflects the theoretical sources it cites, because the foundational formulations of the construct are absent from the embedding space. A taxonomic survey can flag that two scales share semantic space, but it is not designed to determine whether their overlap traces to a common theoretical ancestor, to convergent operationalization from independent traditions, or to gradual displacement of meaning across decades of use. Along the path from foundational formulation to operational item, the conceptual commitments embedded in definitions may be preserved, transformed, or gradually displaced, a process that can be read through Lakatos's (1978) distinction between the hard core of a research programme and the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that successive operationalizations modify. This transmission constitutes what may be termed the theoretical lineage of a construct (Borsboom et al., 2021), and examining it requires bringing the foundational texts into the same space as the items. If the foundational texts of the construct's tradition are embedded alongside the items, the analysis can evaluate whether items and articles lie semantically closest to the sources their authors cite or to a different foundational text altogether.

The construct anomy illustrates both candidate fallacies. Five sociopsychological scales have been developed across seven decades (Bjarnason, 2009; Hartwich & Becker, 2019; McClosky & Schaar, 1965; Srole, 1956; Teymoori et al., 2016) under three different terms, the French anomie (Durkheim, 1893 (Durkheim, /1984)), the Greek-derived anomia (Srole, 1956), and anomy (MacIver, 1950; McClosky & Schaar, 1965), for what may or may not be the same construct, a candidate jangle. Histories of the concept distinguish two classical sources with different conceptual commitments, Durkheim's deregulation of collective life and Merton's disjunction between cultural goals and institutional means (Besnard, 1988; Deflem, 2015; Orrú, 1987), and Merton (1968) himself identified a further formulation, the psychological conception of anomie advanced by MacIver (1950) and Riesman (1950), as a counterpart to the sociological concept rather than a substitute for it. The secondary literature distinguishes an individual-level anomia, measured by Srole and by McClosky and Schaar, from anomie as a state of society (Deflem, 1989; Featherstone & Deflem, 2003), and the three later instruments present themselves as measures of the perceived state of society (Bjarnason, 2009; Hartwich & Becker, 2019; Teymoori et al., 2016). The shared label is therefore a candidate jingle at the level of attribution, because the same name designates instruments that the literature attaches to different theoretical formulations. Whether any of the five instruments semantically resembles the source it cites remains, to our knowledge, untested.

What the five articles themselves say about their sources is less tidy than this sorting suggests. Bjarnason (2009) and Teymoori et al. (2016 ) adopt Durkheim's Suicide (1897 /1951) as the definitional source of their instruments, the former through Hilbert's (1986) reading of exteriority and constraint, the latter by deriving the two dimensions of the Perception of Anomie Scale from Durkheim's integration and regulation. Srole (1956) proposes his own definition of anomia as self-to-others alienation and afterwards names MacIver (1950) and Lasswell (1952) as the formulations most closely approximating it. McClosky and Schaar (1965) define anomy as a state of mind on their own terms, cite MacIver once, in passing, without adopting his formulation, and treat Durkheim, Merton, and Srole as positions to be revised. Hartwich and Becker (2019) report creating their seven items to measure a perceived lack of support and solidarity from society, name Durkheim as the origin of the term, and cite Merton (1938) only to explain why neoliberal conditions should raise anomie. No article adopts Merton's formulation as the basis of its instrument, although all five cite Merton in some capacity.

The corpus can thus be arranged in a three-level structure. The foundational texts that introduced the concept (Durkheim, 1893 (Durkheim, /1984 (Durkheim, , 1897 (Durkheim, /1951;; MacIver, 1950; Merton, 1938) constitute the deepest level, the scale articles that operationalized it represent the intermediary level, and the psychometric items they produced form the operational surface. The present study explores the semantic relationships within and across these three analytical levels, extending the embedding framework of Wulff and Mata (2025a) from within-level comparison of scales to cross-level comparison with foundational texts. The items from the five scales are embedded alongside propositions extracted from the nine source texts in a shared vector space, using the psychometrically fine-tuned model of Wulff and Mata (2025a) as the primary analysis and five additional models from three architectural families for cross-model replication.

Eight exploratory research questions guide the analysis, organized in two groups. Within each level, the study asks how the anomy items are semantically organized in the embedding space (RQ1), how the five scale articles are organized relative to each other (RQ2), and how the four foundational texts are organized relative to each other (RQ3). Across levels, it asks whether items are semantically nearest to the content of the article that introduced them (RQ4), how items are distributed across the four foundational texts (RQ5), which foundational text each scale article is nearest to and how this compares with the sources the articles cite (RQ6), how the propositions from scale articles and foundational texts are organized when pooled across levels (RQ7), and what integrative architecture emerges when all three levels are analyzed together (RQ8). This extension combines semantic proximity with a documentary audit of citation acts, enabling an evaluation of whether the sources an article cites are also the ones its text most resembles. Because the method detects semantic affinity rather than causal influence, the resulting patterns are consistent with, but not demonstrative of, direct theoretical transmission.

The corpus comprised the 43 psychometric items, five scale articles, and four foundational texts organized into a three-level structure (foundational texts, scale articles, and items).

Source Texts

Nine source texts were organized into two upper levels (Figure 1). At the intermediary level, the five scale articles correspond to the papers that introduced each instrument (Srole, 1956; McClosky & Schaar, 1965; Bjarnason, 2009; Teymoori et al., 2016; Hartwich & Becker, 2019).

At the foundational level, four texts represent the classical formulations of the concept as identified in histories of anomie rather than as declared by any single scale author. Durkheim's The Division of Labor in Society (1893 Society ( /1984) ) and Suicide (1897/1951) together with Merton's (1938) article constitute the two classical sources of the concept (Besnard, 1988; Deflem, 2015; Orrú, 1987), and MacIver's (1950) chapter is the formulation that Merton (1968) identified, alongside Riesman (1950), as the psychological conception of anomie, and the text Srole (1956) named as closest to his own definition. For the Durkheim texts and MacIver's book, only the chapters directly relevant to anomy were processed (Book III, Chapter 1 for The Division of Labor; Book II, Chapter 5 for Suicide; Chapter 4 of The Ramparts We Guard). No a priori assignment of articles to foundational texts was made; the foundational texts enter the analysis as reference points in the shared space, and the relation between each article and these texts is examined empirically.

The theoretical sources each article cites were documented through a full reading of the five scale articles. Every passage relating a theoretical source to the definition of the construct or to the construction of the scale was classified as either a direct adoption or an indirect citation.

Direct adoption means that the article declares its definition, dimensions, or items to derive from that source, whether by quoting a prior formulation as the definition used, stating that the instrument follows or operationalizes a named framework, or deriving dimensions or items from a named theoretical model. All other forms of citation, including naming a source as the historical origin of the term, citing for causal mechanisms only, claiming convergence after proposing an own definition, and citing to contrast or reject, were classified as indirect. Embedding Models

The primary embedding model was dwulff/mpnet-personality (Wulff & Mata, 2025a), hereafter the psychometric model, an MPNet architecture fine-tuned on 200,000 personality item pairs from the IPIP, producing 768-dimensional embeddings in English. To assess model dependency, all analyses were replicated with five additional open-weight models spanning three architectural families and dimensionalities from 384 to 1,024 (all-mpnet-base-v2 (MPNet without domain tuning, 768d), paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019; 384d), multilingual-e5-large-instruct (Wang et al., 2024; 1,024d), nomic-embed-text-v1.5 (Nussbaum et al., 2024; 768d), and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Yang et al., 2025; 1,024d)). All models used frozen weights hosted on HuggingFace; proprietary API-based models were excluded for reproducibility. All embeddings were L2-normalized prior to analysis, making cosine similarity equivalent to the dot product.

Procedure

Phase 1: Item Embedding

Each of the 43 items was embedded in English using all six models. Embedding was performed via the Sentence-Transformers library (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019).

Phase 2: Proposition Extraction

Propositions were extracted from the nine source texts through a deterministic pipeline. PDF texts were converted to plain text using PyMuPDF (Artifex Software, 2025), line breaks were rejoined to reconstruct prose from column-formatted layouts, and cross-page paragraph splits were merged. The resulting text was segmented into sentences using pysbd (Sadvilkar & Neumann, 2020), a rule-based sentence boundary detector, and filtered through ten exclusion rules that removed questions, meta-discourse, statistical data, table and figure headers, sentence fragments shorter than five words, equations, boilerplate, running headers, bibliographic entries, and metadata (Supplementary Table S2). Each retained sentence constitutes one proposition in the corpus. This pipeline yielded 3,762 declarative propositions across the nine texts (88,368 words; M = 23.5 words per proposition, Mdn = 21, range = 5-416) without any language model involvement.

Phase 3: Proposition Embedding and Centroid Computation

The 3,762 propositions were embedded using all six models. For each model, nine text centroids were computed as the mean embedding of all propositions belonging to each source text. The number of propositions per text (Supplementary Table S3) ranged from 199 (Teymoori et al., 2016) to 728 (Durkheim, 1893 (Durkheim, /1984)); the bootstrap described below, which resamples propositions within each text, addresses the uncertainty this imbalance introduces in the centroidlevel results.

Analyses

Eight exploratory research questions were addressed, adopting the structural fidelity framework of Wulff and Mata (2025a) and extending it from within-level analysis of scale overlap to crosslevel comparison with foundational texts. All analyses are descriptive of the geometry of the shared space; the single inferential test concerns the one cross-level relation that is a matter of publication record, which article introduced each item. All analyses were performed in Python 3.12 using NumPy (Harris et al., 2020), scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), and SciPy (Virtanen et al., 2020). Results are reported for the psychometric model as the primary analysis, with crossmodel replication across all six models reported inline for each question.

RQ1 examined the thematic organization that emerges among the 43 items when freed from their scale labels. A 43 × 43 cosine similarity matrix was computed from item embeddings to quantify pairwise semantic proximity. To determine whether the five scale groupings are preserved in the embedding space, structural fidelity was assessed via z-scores comparing within-scale to between-scale similarity (Wulff & Mata, 2025a), where z > 2 indicates full recovery and z > 1 partial recovery. Predicted internal consistency was derived from the Spearman-Brown formula (Wulff & Mata, 2025a , 2025b).

where S ̄ is the mean within-scale cosine similarity and N is the number of items. This metric allowed a direct comparison between semantic similarity and the empirical reliability reported by each scale's authors. K-Means clustering with k ranging from 3 to 15 (extended to 30 as a check), evaluated via the silhouette coefficient (Rousseeuw, 1987), identified emergent thematic groupings independent of scale labels, and agreement with the five scale groupings was quantified via Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) and the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI).

HDBSCAN complemented K-Means by identifying density-based item groupings without a fixed k, with the minimum cluster size set to 3 given the small corpus.

Semantic structure within the two upper levels of the corpus was addressed by RQ2 and RQ3. At the article level (RQ2), a 5 × 5 cosine similarity matrix between article centroids quantified inter-article proximity and Ward hierarchical clustering produced a dendrogram of article groupings, whose three-cluster partition is reported descriptively and compared with the documentary audit. At the foundational level (RQ3), a 4 × 4 matrix between foundational centroids described how the four reference texts relate to one another.

Whether items are semantically close to their upper-level sources was the concern of RQ4 and RQ5. For item-article alignment (RQ4), cosine similarity between each of the 43 item embeddings and the five article centroids yielded a 43 × 5 proximity matrix. Nearest-article fidelity, defined as the proportion of items whose nearest centroid matched the article that introduced them, was tested via binomial test against chance (1/5); this is the only cross-level relation fixed by the publication record, and therefore the only one for which a fidelity statistic is meaningful. For items and foundations (RQ5), the 43 × 4 proximity matrix was summarized as the distribution of items across their nearest foundational text, per scale and overall, without a fidelity statistic.

At the article-foundation level (RQ6), cosine similarity between each of the five article centroids and the four foundational centroids produced a 5 × 4 proximity matrix. For each article, the nearest foundational text and the margin between the mean proximity to Merton (1938) and to the remaining foundations were recorded. These geometric results were then set against the documentary audit by asking, for the two articles that adopt a foundational text, whether the adopted text is also the nearest one, and, for all five, which act of citation links them to the text that is.

The semantic landscape formed by the 3,762 propositions was the focus of RQ7. K-Means clustering with k ranging from 5 to 25 served as the primary partitioning method, and NMI against paper of origin assessed the extent to which propositions organize by source text.

HDBSCAN (Campello et al., 2013) complemented this approach by identifying dense regions without imposing a fixed partition, requiring a minimum of 50 propositions to form a cluster and classifying the remainder as noise. Intra-paper semantic cohesion, computed as the mean pairwise cosine similarity among each text's propositions, provided a measure of the internal thematic consistency of individual source texts.

The integrative architecture (RQ8) was assessed by subjecting all nine text centroids to Ward hierarchical clustering. Because Ward's method assumes Euclidean distances, linkage was computed on Euclidean distances between L2-normalized centroids, and the same procedure was used for RQ2. The partitions obtained under the cosine-distance variant were identical (NMI = 1.0 in every model).

The stability of the nearest-foundation assignments was assessed through two resampling procedures. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) drew propositions with replacement within each text, recomputed centroids, and evaluated the stability of the nearest-foundation assignment for each article. A permutation test (10,000 iterations) shuffled foundation-text proposition labels to estimate the probability of observing convergence of all five articles on a single foundation under a null model that preserves the distributional properties of the proposition space.

A genre control analysis assessed whether the convergence on Merton reflects anomiespecific content or textual genre proximity. Three control texts were embedded using the same pipeline. Merton's (1936) article on the unanticipated consequences of purposive social action, published in American Sociological Review two years before his anomie text, controlled for authorial style (188 propositions). Davis and Moore's (1945) article on social stratification, also in American Sociological Review, controlled for the genre and temporal register of mid-century American sociological prose (694 propositions). Hilbert's (1986) review of the Durkheimian tradition in Sociological Theory, a later text on anomy that discusses Merton's reading in order to reassert Durkheim's and is the reading through which Bjarnason (2009) adopts Durkheim, was embedded as a test of whether semantic proximity to Merton distinguishes direct adoption from critical engagement (581 propositions). The analysis compared the proximity of the five scale articles to Merton (1938) against their proximity to the two genre controls and reported the proximity of the articles and of the foundations to Hilbert. A hubness analysis assessed whether Merton's centroid functions as a disproportionate nearest neighbor due to geometric properties rather than content alignment. A sensitivity analysis tested whether the convergence survives when article centroids are restricted to propositions from the pages preceding each article's first methods or results heading, with page ranges reported in Supplementary Table S9.

Predicted internal consistency (Wulff & Mata, 2025a), computed via the Spearman-Brown formula from mean within-scale cosine similarity, was compared against the reliability reported in the original scale publications (Table 1). Leaving aside Srole (1956), whose published coefficient is not comparable, PAS showed the smallest discrepancy (α̂ = .74 vs. α = .77, Δ = -.03), while the Anomie Scale (H) showed the largest (α̂ = .70 vs. α = .87, Δ = -.17), despite having the highest structural fidelity (z = 6.68). Predicted α underestimated reported α for every comparable scale, in line with the expectation that empirical co-variation captures variance components not present in the declarative content of items alone.

Scales retain measurable within-scale cohesion, and the finer partition that the clustering favors cuts across scales along thematic lines. Cross-Level Proximity

RQ4: Are Items Semantically Nearest to the Article That Introduced Them?

The proportion of items whose nearest article centroid was the article that introduced them was 14% (6/43, binomial p = .885 against the 20% expected by chance). McClosky and Schaar (1965) items showed the highest self-alignment (33%, three of nine), while Srole and Bjarnason items showed none. The ASEC result is notable in light of RQ1, where ASEC items achieved the second-highest structural fidelity among themselves (z = 4.55). A scale can be internally cohesive while being semantically distant from the article that introduced it, compatible with the items occupying a region distinct from the broader discourse of their source. Across the sixmodel panel, item-article fidelity ranged from 14% to 33% and reached significance in one of six models, so that the proportion remained near chance in five of six models.

RQ5: How Are Items Distributed Across the Four Foundational Texts?

Each item was assigned to its nearest foundational centroid (Figure 3, Panel B). Overall, 21 of the 43 items were nearest to MacIver, 12 to Merton, seven to Suicide, and three to the Division of Labor. The item level therefore did not reproduce the article-level convergence on Merton reported below; the foundational text nearest to most items was MacIver. Most PAS items (8 of 12) and five of nine S and MS items were nearest to MacIver, ASEC items spread across all four foundations (two to the Division of Labor, two to Merton, one to Suicide, one to MacIver), and H items were nearest to Merton in four of seven cases. No fidelity statistic is attached to this distribution, because the documentary audit shows that only two scales adopt a foundational text and because item wording in several scales was borrowed from earlier instruments rather than derived from a source (see Method). Across the six-model panel, the proportion of items nearest to Merton ranged from 7% to 51%, marking this as the level at which model choice matters most (Supplementary Table S4). Genre Control Analysis

To evaluate whether the Merton convergence reflects anomie-specific content or textual genre proximity, two control texts from the same journal (American Sociological Review) were embedded alongside the corpus using the same deterministic pipeline and psychometric model. Merton's (1936) article on the unanticipated consequences of purposive social action, published two years before his anomie text, controlled for authorial style; Davis and Moore's (1945) article on social stratification controlled for the genre of mid-century American sociological prose.

Mean cosine proximity of the five scale articles to Merton's 1938 anomie text (.801) exceeded their mean proximity to Merton's 1936 non-anomie text (.673, Δ = .128) and to Davis and Moore (.656, Δ = .145). Merton's anomie text was the nearest target for all five articles, whereas neither control text was nearest for any article. The proximity to Merton therefore exceeds what Merton's authorial style or mid-century American sociological prose alone produce, and it is specific to the 1938 text. The three foundational texts other than Merton (1938) occupied the same proximity band as the two genre controls (range .652-.686 vs. .656-.673), so that the articles were about equidistant from all non-Merton texts whether those texts address anomy, stratification, or other topics. A complementary hubness analysis indicated that Merton's centroid does not function as a disproportionate nearest neighbor in the 9-text space (koccurrence = 3 out of 8 possible), which argues against a geometric artifact as the source of the convergence. A sensitivity analysis restricting article centroids to propositions from the pages preceding each article's methods section yielded a mean proximity to Merton of .812 (vs. .801 for full centroids), with Merton nearest for all five articles, so that the convergence does not depend on shared empirical reporting vocabulary (Supplementary Table S9).

To assess whether the convergence is specific to Merton or extends to a later text on anomie, a third control text was embedded using the same pipeline. Hilbert (1986), a review of the Durkheimian tradition published in Sociological Theory and the reading through which Bjarnason (2009) adopts Durkheim (Table S8), yielded 581 propositions. The five scale articles showed mean proximity to Hilbert (.778) that exceeded the genre control band but fell below their mean proximity to Merton (.801). Two articles (McClosky & Schaar; Bjarnason) were marginally closer to Hilbert than to Merton, while the remaining three were nearest to Merton.

Hilbert's own centroid was nearest to Merton (.855) rather than to either Durkheim text (.794 and .663). A text that engages the Mertonian reading in order to reassert the Durkheimian one is thus placed by the embedding space next to the text it criticizes, which marks a limit of what semantic proximity can distinguish. Discussion

This study asked how the items, scale articles, and foundational texts of five anomy scales are organized in a shared semantic space, and whether the foundational texts the articles cite are also the ones their content most resembles. Two results answer these questions together. They make visible a layer of construct development that traditional validation methods leave unexamined.

What a theoretical tradition formulated, what scale developers say they adopted, and what their instruments' texts most resemble can diverge in ways that response co-variation does not capture.

Geometrically, all five scale articles were semantically nearest to Merton (1938) regardless of which source they cite, a pattern identical across the six embedding models tested.

Documentarily, none of the five articles adopts Merton as the definitional basis of its instrument; the two that adopt a foundational text, Bjarnason (2009) and Teymoori et al. (2016), both adopt Durkheim's Suicide, and neither is nearest to it.

Items, for their part, formed thematic clusters that crossed scale boundaries while retaining within-scale cohesion, and were more often nearest to MacIver (1950) than to any other foundational text.

The within-level and cross-level analyses of the item space point in the same direction.

Within the item space (RQ1), each scale retained a measurable degree of internal cohesion, and at the same time free clustering produced emergent themes that cut across scale boundaries, with items from different instruments co-clustering around government legitimacy, existential uncertainty, normative relativism, and moral erosion. The two observations coexist rather than compete. Items were rarely nearest to the article that introduced them (RQ4), and their distribution across the four foundational texts (RQ5) did not follow the sources the articles cite.

The ASEC (Bjarnason, 2009) illustrates the disconnection across levels. None of its items was nearest to its own article, and they spread across foundations rather than concentrating on Merton as the scale articles did, which suggests that items and articles can occupy different regions of the construct space independently of each other.

Scale articles sorted into three groups by content (RQ2), Srole with McClosky and Schaar, Bjarnason with Teymoori et al., and Hartwich and Becker apart, a partition identical across all six models. This partition mirrors the documentary record. The first pair comprises the two articles that define an individual-level anomia on their own terms, the second pair comprises the two articles that adopt Durkheim's Suicide, and the isolated article is the one that reports creating its items without a stated source. The foundational texts themselves (RQ3) did not form distinct regions, as MacIver (1950) and Merton (1938) were more similar to each other than the two Durkheim texts were to each other. When propositions from all texts were pooled (RQ7), the space was predominantly continuous, with the vast majority of propositions resisting densitybased grouping. The integrative dendrogram (RQ8) placed four of the five contemporary articles together, while Hartwich and Becker (2019) grouped with the foundational texts, in line with its high proximity to MacIver.

The dissociation between semantic proximity and citation practice emerged most clearly at the article-foundation level (RQ6). Merton's (1938) text served as a semantic convergence point in the anomy construct space, with all five scale articles being semantically nearest to Merton, and with their mean proximity to Merton exceeding their mean proximity to the other three foundations by a consistent margin. Set against the documentary audit, this geometry can be interpreted. The foundational text every article most resembles is the one no article adopts.

Merton enters the five articles as a historical reference, as an explanation of the conditions under which anomie should arise, as a position to be contrasted or empirically rejected, or through item wording borrowed from later Mertonian studies, but never as the definition an instrument sets out to operationalize. Conversely, the text that two articles adopt, Durkheim's Suicide, was the nearest foundation for neither. For Teymoori et al. it was the farthest of the four foundations; for Bjarnason, whose adoption runs through Hilbert's (1986) exegesis, it was not the farthest but still not the nearest.

At least three processes could account for the convergence of all five scale articles on Merton. Merton's 1938 reconceptualization may have introduced the default vocabulary through which subsequent authors discussed anomy, a vocabulary of culturally prescribed goals, legitimate institutional means, and structural strain that later scale developers used even when they defined the construct on other terms or adopted Durkheim explicitly. Alternatively, the convergence could reflect textual genre proximity rather than intellectual influence, as Merton (1938) is an article in American sociological prose, the textual genre closest to the five scale articles, whereas the Durkheim texts are 19th-century French treatises read in English translation and MacIver (1950) is a political essay. The scale articles may also operationalize Mertonian concepts more than the sources they cite, consistent with Orrú's (1987) historical observation that Merton's reconceptualization progressively absorbed earlier formulations of anomy across American social science. In Lakatosian terms (Lakatos, 1978), the successive operationalizations that constitute the protective belt of anomy research may have gradually displaced the hard core of each cited formulation, converging on a shared Mertonian vocabulary while maintaining distinct citation practices.

Three control analyses provide empirical leverage on these competing accounts. To test whether the convergence on Merton reflects textual genre proximity rather than content, two control texts were embedded alongside the corpus, both written by sociologists active in the same period as Merton but addressing topics unrelated to anomy. Merton's own 1936 article from the same journal and Davis and Moore's (1945) stratification article served as control texts. The five scale articles were substantially more proximate to Merton's 1938 text than to either control text, indicating that the convergence is not fully explained by shared register or disciplinary vocabulary.

The same logic was extended to a text that engages Merton's reading critically in order to reassert Durkheim's. Hilbert's (1986) exegesis of the Durkheimian tradition, which is also the reading through which Bjarnason (2009) adopts Durkheim, was embedded as a test of the method's limits. Its centroid was nearest to Merton rather than to either Durkheim text, and the five scale articles were, on average, closer to Hilbert than to the two control texts, though less close than to Merton. This result is best read as a test of what the method can and cannot separate. A text that discusses the Mertonian reading in order to reassert the Durkheimian one shares vocabulary with what it criticizes, and the embedding space registers the shared vocabulary without registering the stance. Semantic proximity to a foundational text is therefore consistent with adoption, with critical engagement, and with inherited vocabulary alike; the documentary audit, not the geometry, is what distinguishes these cases.

To evaluate whether shared empirical reporting conventions rather than theoretical content produced the article-level convergence, a sensitivity analysis restricted each article's centroid to the pages preceding its methods section. The proximity to Merton did not decrease, indicating that the convergence is more compatible with theoretical vocabulary than with shared methodological language. These three controls, together with the invariance of the central findings across six architecturally diverse embedding models (two MPNet variants, MiniLM, E5large, Nomic, and Qwen3) and the bootstrap stability of the nearest-foundation assignment, provide convergent evidence that the proximity to Merton is a robust property of the textual record, compatible with content-driven proximity. The pattern identified by this analysis remains an analytical construction that quantifies textual proximity across levels, identifying where the sources an article cites diverge from the sources its text resembles, without adjudicating whether the divergence reflects vocabulary drift, textual genre effects, or genuine conceptual displacement.

A further observation emerges from setting the geometric and the documentary evidence side by side. The five scales are psychometric instruments, each designed to measure a construct or its constituent dimensions. Yet at the partition examined (k = 15, the upper end of the grid), items grouped around themes such as government legitimacy, existential uncertainty, normative relativism, moral erosion, and interpersonal indifference, rather than the one or two dimensions each scale's theoretical model would predict. Although all five scale articles gravitate toward Merton's semantic region (RQ6), the item clusters that emerged in RQ1 organized around experiential themes that do not map directly onto the structural mechanism of strain, that is, the disjunction between cultural goals and institutional means that constitutes Merton's central theoretical contribution. The documentary audit adds a third layer. All five articles are semantically nearest to Merton, all five cite Merton in some capacity, and none adopts Merton's definition. The definitions that are adopted belong to a text that neither adopting article most resembles. This pattern is consistent with a selective form of transmission in which semantic proximity to a foundational text persists across operationalizations while the text's core argument does not. The present method cannot adjudicate whether the experiential themes of the items constitute fine-grained expressions of strain or conceptually independent content. This dissociation between semantic proximity and adopted definition complements the jingle-jangle framework at a level that previous approaches have not addressed. The framework has moved from a label-based insight (Kelley, 1927; Weidman et al., 2017) to automated withinlevel detection via embeddings (Wulff & Mata, 2025a). The present cross-level analysis extends this trajectory by bringing the foundational texts of a construct into the same embedding space and documenting how each instrument cites them, making it possible to identify when the source an article most resembles and the source it adopts come apart. Content-level analyses have documented comparable discrepancies at the scale level; Fried (2017) found that seven common depression instruments collectively assess 52 distinct symptoms with limited overlap, a finding that the present approach could recover computationally and extend to the foundational level. In a field with over 43,000 unique measures developed under conditions of limited conceptual clarification (Anvari et al., 2025; Flake & Fried, 2020), scalable approaches for evaluating how instruments relate to the sources they cite could complement the within-level tools already available. For anomy, the same name is attached by adoption to Durkheim in two instruments and by semantic proximity to Merton in all five, a candidate jingle at the level of attribution identified through documented citation acts.

These findings speak to the broader enterprise of theory construction in psychology. Borsboom et al. (2021) proposed a systematic methodology for building theories that requires, among other steps, evaluating how theoretical concepts travel from their original formulation to their operationalization in measurement instruments. The cross-level embedding approach, combined with a documentary audit of citation acts, provides a concrete tool for this evaluation.

That semantic similarity among items correlates with empirical score patterns in the absence of participant data (Ravenda et al., 2025) suggests that the textual relationships the approach examines are not merely linguistic but carry implications for the empirical structure of the instruments. By embedding foundational texts alongside the instruments developed in their name and reading the resulting geometry against documented citation acts, the cross-level approach makes the textual relationship between theoretical formulation and operationalization empirically examinable, including cases in which semantic proximity diverges from declared theoretical adoption. Its inferential structure is triangulation rather than hypothesis testing against a coded ground truth. Geometric patterns, documented citation acts, and control-text comparisons provide complementary sources of evidence whose convergence or divergence informs the interpretation. The anomy demonstration illustrates both the analytical capacity of the approach and the type of finding it can yield, namely, that semantic proximity between instruments and a foundational text may coexist with the absence of any adoption of that text, a pattern that traditional psychometric methods are not designed to detect because they do not incorporate the theoretical texts that generated the constructs under evaluation.

Scope Conditions

The cross-level embedding approach presupposes four conditions that delimit its applicability.

First, the approach applies to standardized measurement instruments whose construction involved construct validation procedures, whether these take the form of self-report scales, performance tasks, or other formats with documented psychometric properties. The analysis evaluates the semantic relationship between such instruments and the theoretical texts of their construct's tradition, and is therefore not designed for informal assessments or ad hoc measures lacking a documented validation history.

Second, the construct must have foundational texts that histories of the concept identify as its classical sources, so that the reference points placed in the space are not chosen by the analyst to fit any single instrument. Third, the scale articles must be available in full, because the documentary component of the analysis depends on reading every passage in which an article relates a source to its definition or to its items. Explicit adoption of a foundational text is not a precondition. The present case shows why. Only two of five articles adopt a foundational text, and the frequency of direct adoption and indirect citation is itself a result the analysis is designed to document rather than a requirement it must assume.

Fourth, the foundational texts must be available as embeddable prose documents. When the textual genre of the foundational texts differs from that of the scale articles (as when comparing 19th-century treatises in translation with mid-20th-century journal articles), the resulting register differences constitute a confound that should be managed through genre control analyses rather than treated as evidence of semantic distance.

The present case illustrates this heterogeneity within a single construct. Of the five articles analyzed, two adopt a foundational text, one names convergent formulations, one defines its construct against the tradition, and one creates its items without a stated source. The approach documents each of these relations rather than requiring any particular one as a precondition.

Limitations

Semantic embeddings capture the propositional content of items but not the response processes through which participants interpret and answer them. Their insensitivity to negation and polarity means that some thematic clusters may group items that share vocabulary but differ in valence.

More broadly, the semantic structure identified by embeddings reflects one operationalization of meaning, grounded in the distributional hypothesis (Firth, 1957; Harris, 1954), not meaning itself. The invariance of the central findings across six architecturally diverse models mitigates but does not eliminate this concern. The Hilbert control shows a specific form of the limitation. Proximity in the space does not distinguish a text that adopts a formulation from one that discusses it in order to reject it.

The documentary audit is itself interpretive. Citation acts were coded by a single reader against an explicit scheme, with every passage and page number reported so that the coding can be checked, but without an independent second coder or an estimate of inter-rater agreement.

One source that Srole (1956) names alongside MacIver, Lasswell (1952), is not in the corpus because it is a short essay in an edited volume rather than a text on anomy. Four sentences of Srole (1956) that the PDF text layer omitted were transcribed from the page image; the remaining texts were not checked page by page for similar omissions.

Foundational texts written in other languages and historical periods, such as Durkheim's 19th-century French treatises read in English translation, may appear semantically distant from contemporary scale articles for reasons of register and translation rather than conceptual content, a confound that the genre control analyses address but cannot fully eliminate. Finally, the study analyzed five scales measuring a single construct, and the generalizability of the approach to other constructs remains to be established.

Future Directions

The most immediate extension of the cross-level embedding approach involves applying it to constructs whose instruments relate to their sources in different ways, from those with adopted classical texts to those built on consensus statements or on self-founding instruments, which would show whether the dissociation between semantic proximity and adopted definition observed for anomy is specific to this construct or reflects a broader pattern. Such comparisons would benefit from two independent coders of citation acts and from integrating semantic evidence with item response data, combining what items say with how respondents answer them into joint models of construct structure. The foundational layer itself may prove useful as a preregistration tool, enabling researchers to derive predictions about convergent and discriminant validity from text alone before collecting data, particularly as recent tutorials have made embedding methods increasingly accessible to behavioral researchers (Debelak et al., 2025; Kjell et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Semantic embeddings of psychometric items and the foundational texts of a construct's tradition, read against a documentary audit of how instruments cite those texts, can identify patterns of semantic proximity consistent with lexical transmission across the levels of construct development. For anomy, the cross-level analysis identified a construct space in which items retained within-scale cohesion while forming thematic clusters across scale boundaries, in which all five scale articles are nearest to Merton's text, and in which the only foundational text that any article adopts is not the one those articles most resemble. The extension of Wulff and Mata's (2025a) jingle-jangle framework to the relation between instruments and their sources, and the observation that semantic convergence on a text may coexist with the absence of its adoption, offer an approach for evaluating whether instruments semantically reflect the sources they cite, one that can be applied to constructs with traceable intellectual histories.
