Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

 ACEs shape how well trauma-exposed people tolerate and benefit from meditation  Meditation adverse effects and dropout vary with trauma burden and context  A staged, risk-stratified model guides trauma-informed meditation delivery  Challenging experiences may aid growth when practice is staged and supervised  Guidance for screening, pacing and monitoring in high-adversity rehabilitation

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and other forms of trauma (including singleincident trauma, complex relational trauma, and presentations that do not meet full PTSD diagnostic criteria; Bhutta et al., 2023; Cruz et al., 2022) have been identified as significant factors affecting rehabilitation (defined here as psychological and social intervention across clinical, correctional, and community settings) since they shape emotional regulation, health behavior, and long-term psychosocial outcomes across the life course (Bhutta et al., 2023; Claypool & Moore de Peralta, 2021). Trauma burden is especially pronounced in criminal justice system-involved and other high-adversity populations, where ACE exposure is associated with poorer mental health, substance use, self-harm, and more difficult reintegration (Barnert et al., 2024; Berring et al., 2024; Coelho et al., 2025; Ford et al., 2020; Gaber et al., 2025; Han et al., 2021; Mazher & Arai, 2025).

These patterns highlight the need for rehabilitation models that do not treat trauma as a background issue, but as a central determinant of recovery, functioning, and treatment engagement. Traumainformed care (TIC) has, in turn, emerged as a significant paradigm in health, behavioral, and justice systems. However, recent reviews indicate that TIC remains conceptually broad, inconsistently defined, and unevenly operationalized across settings (Berring et al., 2024; Han et al., 2021). This is particularly relevant in correctional and rehabilitative contexts, where compounding stressors including institutional pressure, psychiatric comorbidity, and social instability can undermine recovery (Barnert et al., 2024; Coelho et al., 2025; Gaber et al., 2025; Mazher & Arai, 2025). Consequently, there is increased awareness that trauma-informed principles must be translated into more concrete, practical, and context-specific strategies.

In this context, meditation-based and mindfulness-based interventions have become increasingly popular as potentially useful aids in trauma recovery and rehabilitation. Recent data indicate that mindfulness-based programs can alleviate post-traumatic stress symptoms, enhance emotional regulation, and overall psychological functioning, yet the results are inconsistent across the population and intervention types (Badola et al., 2025; Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024; Scafuto et al., 2025). The work is especially timely since contemplative interventions are being investigated not only in general wellness contexts, but also in populations exposed to trauma, as well as in rehabilitation-relevant populations, such as violence survivors, individuals with substance use disorders, and criminal justice system-involved populations (Badola et al., 2025; Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024).

Interest in meditation has grown not only because of its potential symptom-relieving effects, but also because it may affect mechanisms directly relevant to rehabilitation. Recent studies suggest that mindfulness-related practices may improve interoception, emotion regulation, self-compassion, and autonomic function (Arslan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Fox et al., 2024; Molteni et al., 2024; Sezgin, 2025; Shatrova et al., 2024; Sparby & Sacchet, 2022 , 2025a , 2025b; Treves et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). The specific benefits and risks, however, depend on both trauma history and the individual clinical profile, including diagnosis and current symptom severity. These processes are closely associated with those that trauma disrupts: embodied awareness, affect tolerance, and self-regulation. Meanwhile, there is growing recognition that meditation is not always benign. Trauma-exposed populations may show heterogeneity of response, early disengagement, adverse effects, or reduced benefit when meditation is delivered without sufficient attention to trauma history, symptom profile, and tolerability (Bautista et al., 2024; Canby et al., 2025; Garfin et al., 2024 Garfin et al., , 2025;; Scafuto et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Thus, the central question is no longer simply whether meditation can help trauma-exposed individuals, but how it should be adapted, paced, and monitored within rehabilitation settings, including according to other co-morbid illnesses and disorders.

However, this framing benefits from being situated within the emerging study of advanced meditation, which suggests meditation can itself be understood as a developmental process in which practitioners confront and grow through challenging experiences, including the reactivation of trauma-related material (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). Based on this view, meditation-related psychological adverse effects, challenging, unpleasant, and negative experiences (MRPAEs) within practice may not be merely side effects to be minimized, but potentially essential components of meditative growth catalysts through which the practitioner gains insight into the nature of suffering and learns to decondition unhelpful reactive patterns (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). From this perspective, the relationship between trauma and meditation is not merely one of risk management, but also one of potential resolution and transformation (Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a).

Nonetheless, trauma reactivation is not a prerequisite for benefit. Many people benefit from stabilization and improved self-regulation without deliberately engaging with traumatic memory.

Where deeper engagement occurs, qualified clinical supervision is essential, so this should not be delivered by untrained facilitators. It is also worth noting that trauma presentations vary considerably. Some individuals carry trauma from experiences during unconscious states, such as sexual abuse during drug-induced incapacitation, where standard screening tools may not capture the full history. Facilitators should be aware that reported ACE scores may underestimate actual burden in such cases. The implication for rehabilitation is a productive tension: practices must be adapted to individual trauma burden, while also, over time, being capable of supporting productive engagement with difficult experience as a component of meditative development itself (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b). This is of particular interest to an ACE-sensitive view. Recent data indicate that contemplative interventions can be influenced by processes that are closely related to developmental adversity, such as self-critical rumination, shame, guilt, resilience, self-compassion, and psychological flexibility (Arslan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Fox et al., 2024; Joss et al., 2025; Sezgin, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). In this regard, ACE burden shapes not only who may benefit from trauma-informed meditation but also the routes through which these interventions can be effective, challenging, or destabilizing, making it a critical variable to assess prior to delivery.

But the literature is still disjointed. Trauma-informed care reviews do not commonly describe how meditation is operationalized in trauma-exposed groups, and meditation reviews tend to focus on efficacy without adequately incorporating ACE science, safety surveillance, rehabilitation contexts, or developmental processes (Badola et al., 2025; Berring et al., 2024; Han et al., 2021; Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024; Scafuto et al., 2025). Likewise, rehabilitation-focused studies often report feasibility or symptom outcomes without offering a unifying framework that links ACE burden, trauma-informed adaptation, contemplative mechanisms, and functional recovery (Barnert et al., 2024; Bautista et al., 2024; Canby et al., 2025; Coelho et al., 2025; Gaber et al., 2025; Garfin et al., 2024 Garfin et al., , 2025;; Joss et al., 2025; Mazher & Arai, 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022).

The integration of mindfulness into structured clinical treatments for trauma-related conditions is not new. Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), which is widely used with patients who have borderline personality disorder, a condition characterized by emotion dysregulation strongly associated with early trauma, incorporates mindfulness as a core skill (Treleaven, 2018). This narrative review aims to fill that gap by synthesizing the recent literature on ACEs, traumainformed care, contemplative interventions, and rehabilitation. It argues that ACE burden should be considered a developmental moderator of meditative response and that trauma-informed meditation requires explicit attention to safety, choice, pacing, embodiment, and MRPAE monitoring. The review also suggests that rehabilitation-based contemplative practice must be measured not only based on symptom change, but also based on other outcomes, including interoception, self-compassion, emotional flexibility, treatment engagement, and functional recovery. Crucially, the review draws on an emerging framework of meditative development in which practitioners may confront and work through challenging experiences, including traumarelated reactivation, as part of the path toward greater self-regulation, psychological integration, and, in advanced stages, a more peaceful and resilient mode of existence (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Galante et al., 2023; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b). This framework aims to accommodate the individualized, dynamic, and multidimensional nature of meditative development, wherein practitioner-, practice-, relationship-, and environmental-level factors influence whether the meditative practice effectively supports or hinders long-term flourishing (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Galante et al., 2023; Hugemark et al., 2026). In doing so, the review aims to move the field from broad calls for trauma sensitivity toward a more clinically actionable and rehabilitation-relevant framework for traumainformed meditation.

Review Approach and Scope

This paper is a narrative review that synthesizes and interprets recent literature at the crossroads of ACEs, trauma-informed care, contemplative practice, and rehabilitation. Unlike a systematic review, the aim was not to produce a pooled estimate of effect or a formal risk-of-bias assessment, but to provide an interpretive and integrative account of the field while identifying conceptual and practice-related gaps. PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, and Web of Science were used as literature sources because they offer a wide range of biomedical, psychological, rehabilitation, and interdisciplinary literature on trauma and meditation. The primary review window included publications from 2020 to 2026 to reflect recent changes in trauma-informed care, mindfulnessand meditation-based interventions, ACEs research, and rehabilitation-based implementation.

Limited pre-2020 landmark sources were also retained where necessary for foundational definitions and conceptual grounding. Approximately 70 sources were reviewed in total, including empirical studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and key conceptual papers. The 2020 start date was chosen to capture the most recent growth period in trauma-informed contemplative research, which accelerated substantially following increased interest in community-based and scalable mental health support. Earlier foundational work including original ACE studies and early trauma-informed care frameworks incorporated where it underpins key concepts.

Priority was given to peer-reviewed empirical studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and high-level conceptual papers that contributed to one or more of the following areas: trauma exposure and ACEs, mindfulness-or meditation-related interventions, trauma-informed adaptation, contemplative mechanisms, and rehabilitation-relevant outcomes. The review focused on clinical, correctional, substance use, and community rehabilitation settings, where trauma exposure, institutional constraints, and difficulties maintaining treatment engagement may influence recovery (Barnert et al., 2024; Coelho et al., 2025; Dobischok et al., 2026; Gaber et al., 2025). This focus allows examination of functional outcomes, treatment continuity, and community reintegration alongside symptom improvement (Gawande et al., 2025; Georgiadis et al., 2026; Melillo et al., 2025).

The synthesis was guided by four broad questions: 1) how childhood adversity shapes processes relevant to meditation and rehabilitation; 2) what recent evidence shows about the benefits, limitations, and risks of contemplative interventions in trauma-exposed populations; 3) how trauma-informed principles have been translated into meditation-related practice; and 4) how these strands can be integrated into a practical rehabilitation-oriented framework. By answering these four questions, this review also aims to offer mechanisms and strategies by which traumasensitive adaptation is not just a coping strategy but also a prerequisite for working through difficult psychological material in a supported, titrated way, so that deeper transformation can be accessed and actualized within meditative development (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022 , 2025a).

Literature was read in a comparative and thematic manner, with greater weight given to works that provided conceptual clarity, methodological soundness, relevance to rehabilitation, and helpful information on safety, mechanisms, or implementation. As a narrative review, this paper does not claim exhaustive retrieval of all eligible studies and does not apply protocol registration, duplicate screening, formal risk-of-bias scoring, or meta-analysis. Instead, it aims to offer a narrow, conceptually consistent synthesis to inform framework development and subsequent research on trauma-informed meditation for rehabilitation. Terms such as women and men are used as reported in the source studies; sex and gender were not defined or analyzed separately in this review, which limits generalizability across sexes and genders.

Conceptual Foundations and Definitional Boundaries

This review uses several related yet distinct concepts that cannot be interchanged (see Table 1). The types of adversity encompassed within this framework include abuse (physical, emotional, sexual), neglect, and household dysfunction (e.g., domestic violence, parental substance use, mental illness, incarceration, or loss; Bhutta et al., 2023). ACEs are conceptualized here as cumulative adversity in childhood, whereas childhood trauma refers to harmful developmental experiences with enduring emotional, relational, or regulatory outcomes (Bhutta et al., 2023; Claypool & Moore de Peralta, 2021; Cruz et al., 2022). Although closely related, these two constructs are not the same, as not all ACEs result in clinically significant trauma, and the presence of protective factors such as caregiver support can moderate whether adversity translates into lasting harm. For individuals with high ACE burden in criminal justice-system-involved or rehabilitation settings, however, ACE exposure and childhood trauma substantially overlap in their developmental and regulatory consequences. In addition, TIC is not viewed here as a treatment model but as a service framework focused on safety, trust, choice, collaboration, and empowerment (Berring et al., 2024; Melillo et al., 2025). In this review, trauma-informed meditation is used as the primary working concept, denoting contemplative practices delivered with explicit attention to trauma history, pacing, safety, titration, and ongoing monitoring (Melillo et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). This subsumes narrower distinctions (e.g., trauma-sensitive mindfulness) under a single, clinically actionable umbrella that is more tractable for rehabilitation contexts.

Meditation is also not treated as a single uniform practice. It is a general term for a variety of contemplative practices, including focused attention, open monitoring, compassion-based practice, body scan, breath-based practice, and movement-based practices (Bashir et al., 2025; Calderone et al., 2024; Ooishi et al., 2021; Sparby et al., 2026; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

These differences are important since trauma-exposed people might react differently to attentional requirements, emotional arousal, and bodily salience. For a comprehensive taxonomy of meditation practices, this review draws on the Activity-Based Phenomenological Classification System proposed by Sparby and Sacchet (2022), which classifies meditation techniques according to core activities, including focusing, releasing, imagining, and moving in relation to an object of meditation (e.g., the breath), as well as fields of experience. This framework enables researchers and clinicians to distinguish techniques by the mental activity and associated phenomenological content they elicit, reflecting different attentional, embodied, imaginative, or relational demands and allowing more precise reasoning about which types of practice are most and least suitable for trauma-exposed populations (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022). Specifically, breath-based and body-based practices can be regulatory for some participants and activating or destabilizing for others (Molteni et al., 2024; Shatrova et al., 2024; Treves et al., 2025). That is why this review mentions certain contemplative modes when needed, rather than presuming that all meditation practices work the same way.

In this context, meditative development is defined as the non-linear, multidimensional process through which practitioners progressively acquire and deepen capacities for selfregulation, including attentional stability, interoceptive tolerance, emotion regulation, nonreactivity, and self-compassion (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Galante et al., 2023). Crucially, this developmental process is not solely a function of practice duration, but rather likely follows principles of skill acquisition and learning (Ehmann, Sezer, Keller, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sezer, Treves, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Galante et al., 2023; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b). When meditation is practiced under increased trauma load, these meditative skills may specifically include learning to titrate and work with difficult emotions, loss of stability, and challenging inner material (Galante et al., 2023; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b). However, for such progressive engagement with increasingly challenging experience to occur within traumaexposed rehabilitation populations, building skills for tolerating, managing, and adapting to current trauma burden appears essential. These two sets of skills, mutually supportive as they may seem, may therefore be better understood as parallel tracks. In addition, despite mindful balancing of these skills, 1) titrating exposure and 2) stabilizing amidst instability are particularly important for trauma-exposed individuals; they likely apply to each practitioner independent of experience and constitute a central part of the meditative journey (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Galante et al., 2023; Hugemark et al., 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b).

Implications of Adverse Childhood Experiences for Trauma-Informed Meditation ACEs as a Potential Moderator of Contemplative Response

ACEs should not be treated as background characteristics when meditation is introduced in rehabilitation. Developmental trauma influences stress responsivity, attachment, emotion regulation, and self-processing. Indeed, contemplative practice always begins at a non-neutral point, shaped by each practitioner's prior experience, neurobiological state, and relational history, and this is particularly consequential for those with significant trauma burden (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Hugemark et al., 2026; Pasteuning et al., 2025; Rossi et al., 2025; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). ICD-11 research on PTSD and complex trauma further underscores how the specific symptom profile of trauma-exposed individuals, including re-experiencing, avoidance, hyperarousal, and negative self-cognitions, shapes both their contemplative tolerability and the type of meditation-related adaptation that is most clinically warranted (Thoma et al., 2025). As a result, the same practice may be grounding for one person but dysregulating for another. ACE burden may therefore shape whether meditation is experienced as tolerable, beneficial, or destabilizing, particularly when practices rely heavily on stillness, breath tracking, body scanning, or sustained inward attention (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). These observations support treating ACE exposure as an important moderator of contemplative response rather than as a peripheral clinical detail (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a) (see Figure 1 for the full conceptual model of these pathways). Systematic ACE assessment before intervention could also support personalized matching informing both practice selection and predictions about likely tolerability and treatment response.

Mechanisms Relevant to Adaptation

Several mechanisms explain why trauma history matters in the delivery of meditation.

Developmental trauma is associated with autonomic dysregulation, leaving some individuals in states of hyperarousal or shutdown, and may alter interoception such that body-based practices are destabilizing rather than grounding (Leech et al., 2024; Molteni et al., 2024; Pasteuning et al., 2025; Shatrova et al., 2024; Treves et al., 2025). Turning attention inward can also be disruptive for individuals whose inner experience feels fragmented or unsafe (Chan et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Self-critical rumination, shame, and reduced resilience further shape how practitioners respond to contemplative demands (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Together, these mechanisms mean that trauma-informed meditation must account for the full impact of trauma on bodily, emotional, and cognitive tolerability, not simply provide a calming activity.

Toward Solving the Apparent Clinical Paradox

The literature presents a significant paradox. Meditation can regulate mechanisms frequently impaired by trauma, such as interoceptive awareness, emotional regulation, and selfcritical rumination (Molteni et al., 2024; Treves et al., 2025). Yet for trauma-exposed individuals, turning attention inward toward precisely these mechanisms can trigger or intensify distress (Canby et al., 2025; Chan et al., 2024). This is the core of the paradox that the therapeutic mechanism and the risk mechanism are the same. Research has shown that childhood trauma and subclinical PTSD symptoms predict poorer outcomes, more negative effects, and higher attrition in mindfulness-based programs (Canby et al., 2025).

Recent work on MRPAEs helps clarify this (Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Hugemark et al., 2026). Evidence also suggests that individual traits such as cognitive fusion may moderate how people respond to mindfulness practice (Pux et al., 2022). Hugemark et al. (2026) distinguish between experiences that are genuinely harmful and those that are uncomfortable but remain within a productive range and may serve longer-term growth. Whether a difficult experience falls into the first or second category depends on practitioner-level variables such as current symptom load and ACE burden, practice-level variables such as meditation type and intensity, and relational and environmental factors such as facilitator support and delivery setting (Hugemark et al., 2026). Adaptation is therefore not simply about making practice gentler.

It means configuring these conditions so difficulty remains manageable and potentially resolutionoriented.

This implies that trauma-informed meditation cannot be reduced to providing mindfulness to trauma-exposed people. It requires differential matching, pacing, attention to dissociation and bodily tolerance, and systematic monitoring of negative experiences (Canby et al., 2025; Hugemark et al., 2026; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). The goal is to keep practice within a productive window, not to eliminate difficulty entirely, but to prevent escalation into sustained distress or functional deterioration (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Hugemark et al., 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). Success, therefore, becomes not the absence of difficulty, but its appropriate management.

Current Evidence on Trauma-Informed Meditation in Rehabilitation Evidence of Benefit

A 2024 umbrella review reported a pooled reduction in PTSD symptoms following mindfulness-based interventions (adjusted SMD = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.30-0.52), although findings varied across study designs and intervention types (Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024). Reviews published in 2024 and 2025 report overall small-to-moderate improvements across trauma symptom measures, with effect sizes typically in the range of g = 0.3-0.6 (Badola et al., 2025; Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024). The strongest positive evidence comes from studies that modified delivery to highadversity groups. Adapted mindfulness interventions were feasible and subsequently improved depression, anxiety, and quality-of-life-related outcomes in trauma-exposed unhoused women with substance use disorder (Garfin et al., 2024 (Garfin et al., , 2025)). The same tendencies can be observed in women with comorbid PTSD and substance use disorder, where the integration of mindfulness-based relapse prevention with trauma was identified as a viable and acceptable option (Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Studies of trauma-informed yoga and blended mind-body interventions in incarcerated, forensic, and other vulnerable populations also report reductions in stress and mood disturbance, suggesting that embodied contemplative practices may be especially useful where direct inward attention is difficult to tolerate (Richard et al., 2024).

Evidence of Risk and MRPAEs

These results must be balanced against non-response, dropout, and MRPAEs. Reporting of harm is not consistent throughout the wider meditation literature, although recent research indicates that trauma history can have a significant impact on tolerability. More MRPAEs, worse outcomes, and higher attrition in mindfulness-based programs have been linked to childhood trauma and subclinical PTSD symptoms (Britton et al., 2021; Canby et al., 2025). In practice, negative effects do not necessarily appear as a dramatic worsening; they can show up as attentional overload, disengagement, or an inability to stay with the intervention long enough to be useful.

These results underscore the importance of trauma-sensitive pacing, adaptation, and monitoring rather than universal suitability.

Evidence of Non-Response and Dropout

Non-response is related to but distinct from MRPAEs. Where MRPAEs reflect active harm, non-response reflects a failure of the practice to produce benefit, often because the demands exceed what the participant can currently tolerate. Appropriate pacing may involve both shortening session length and softening instruction; or, in different words, titrating both training volume (practice length) and training intensity (the degree to which a practitioner engages with their chosen object of attention; Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025). In the context of trauma-informed meditation, this may include gradual, careful progression toward more challenging attentional objects so that selfregulatory capacity is not overwhelmed and functional well-being is maintained. When practice is not adapted in this way, non-response and dropout are more likely, particularly in individuals with higher ACE burden (Canby et al., 2025; Hugemark et al., 2026).

Evidence Across High-Adversity Trauma-Exposed Settings

The settings highlighted in this review-correctional, substance use, women's services, and community re-entry-were selected on the basis of documented high ACE burden, the availability of recent relevant literature, and the practical urgency of adapting contemplative delivery for highrisk contexts. The case for trauma-informed adaptation is strongest in settings where ACE burden is especially high and where standard contemplative delivery is most likely to be inadequate (Berring et al., 2024; Coelho et al., 2025; Gaber et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). In prison and correctional contexts, reviews suggest that trauma-informed interventions may improve mental health and possibly support outcomes related to behavioral self-regulation relevant to recidivism reduction, though the causal link to recidivism itself remains unclear (Ford et al., 2020; Gaber et al., 2025). The evidence remains methodologically uneven and is still limited in male populations and non-US settings (Georgiadis et al., 2026). Correctional interventions based on yoga appear feasible and have been linked to reductions in distress, anger, and trauma-related symptoms, yet long-term reintegration outcomes are under-researched (Georgiadis et al., 2026). A key implementation challenge in correctional contexts is that institutional conditions, including coercive environments, limited privacy, and staff distrust, can undermine the safety and choice principles that trauma-informed meditation depends on (Coelho et al., 2025; Gaber et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). These contextual factors must be addressed at the delivery level, not only through practice adaptation.

Community and re-entry settings also show promise, particularly when interventions are culturally responsive, peer-supported, and embedded within broader support structures rather than delivered as isolated self-regulation techniques (Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025). The transition point from incarceration to community is a period of heightened vulnerability in which the loss of structured support can rapidly erode any gains made during institutional programs (Gawande et al., 2025). Contemplative skills developed during incarceration are more likely to transfer when they are embedded in ongoing peer or community support after release (Dobischok et al., 2026).

Across both contexts, the common thread is that trauma-informed meditation is not simply a clinical add-on. It is most effective when it is responsive to the specific trauma histories, institutional conditions, and social ecologies of the people it serves (Berring et al., 2024; Coelho et al., 2025; Hugemark et al., 2026; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Remaining Gaps in the Literature

The evidence base remains inconclusive, despite evident growth in the field. Pilot studies, feasibility trials, mixed-methods work, and uncontrolled designs continue to dominate much of the literature (Garfin et al., 2024; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Most interventions are multicomponent, and it is challenging to determine which aspects of trauma-informed meditation are contributing to the benefit. Most studies classify participants by diagnosis or service setting rather than ACE burden, so ACE-stratified contemplative research remains limited. MRPAEs monitoring is often inconsistent, and relatively few studies systematically assess dissociation, interoceptive tolerance, shame, self-criticism, or functional reintegration (Leech et al., 2024).

Overall, the evidence supports developing overarching frameworks, but it lacks the specificity needed to determine which contemplative practices, in what form, and for which subgroups have the strongest rehabilitative effects.

Translating Trauma-Informed Care Into Meditation Practice

Trauma-informed meditation can be described as the application of TIC principles to the practice of contemplation (Berring et al., 2024; Melillo et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). David Treleaven's foundational work on trauma-sensitive mindfulness has been particularly influential in translating these principles into practical guidance for facilitators (Treleaven, 2018).

TIC is not an intervention, but a model that focuses on safety, trust, choice, collaboration, empowerment, and cultural humility in service delivery (Berring et al., 2024; Davies et al., 2025).

Applied to meditation, these principles shift contemplative practice from a one-size-fits-all model toward a relational, context-sensitive process that takes trauma history and current vulnerability seriously (Berring et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Trauma-informed meditation is thus not synonymous with trauma-specific psychotherapy.

Instead, it modifies contemplative practices to minimize the risk of retraumatization and improve tolerability in rehabilitation environments (Melillo et al., 2025). Notably, trauma-informed meditation should only be delivered by facilitators trained in trauma-informed care who have access to clinical supervision or a clear referral pathway (To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024; Treleaven, 2018). In practice, safety means offering predictable settings, posture flexibility, and alternatives to highly activating anchors such as prolonged breath fixation (Richard et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Trust and transparency require explaining what the practice involves and how participants can modify or stop it (Berring et al., 2024; Davies et al., 2025). Choice and collaboration mean providing options and welcoming feedback rather than requiring stillness or silence (Berring et al., 2024; Seitanidou et al., 2024). Empowerment shifts the focus toward agency and skill-building rather than passive compliance (Keesler et al., 2025; Melillo et al., 2025).

Cultural humility requires contextualizing practice to participants' linguistic, cultural, and community backgrounds, not offering it as a universal or neutral activity (Davies et al., 2025; Harerimana et al., 2025).

Operationalizing Trauma-Informed Meditation: The Proposed Framework

Clinical Considerations for Delivery

Trauma-informed meditation ought to begin with a concise clinical evaluation rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. The importance of early screening is that the trauma burden may affect tolerability, MRPAEs, and dropout (Britton et al., 2021; Canby et al., 2025). PTSD-related hyperarousal, intrusive symptoms, and dissociative vulnerability may affect meditation tolerability and require shorter sessions, external attentional anchors, or greater flexibility in practice delivery (Canby et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Co-occurring substance use disorders may further influence engagement and treatment response (Joss et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Dissociative vulnerability is particularly significant because internal focus can be destabilizing rather than regulating for some individuals (To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Interoceptive tolerance should be evaluated, as body-and breath-oriented practices may be supportive or distressing to the person (Leech et al., 2024; Treves et al., 2025). Context also shapes feasibility and safety (see Figure 3 for the risk-stratified entry framework that integrates these variables). Housing instability can decrease continuity and engagement (Garfin et al., 2024 (Garfin et al., , 2025)).

The use of coercive or highly controlled treatment environments can also impact perceived safety and choice (Gaber et al., 2025; Seitanidou et al., 2024). Future studies could use a brief, standardized intake battery to strengthen risk stratification. For example, a validated ACE screen, the PCL-5 for trauma symptoms, the DES-II for dissociation, and the MAIA-2 for interoceptive awareness (Binda et al., 2022; Britton et al., 2021; Leech et al., 2024).

Additional intake considerations include current psychotic symptoms, manic agitation, suicidal ideation, significant sleep disturbance, and acute intoxication or withdrawal from substances (Britton et al., 2021; Matko & Van Dam, 2026). The decision to proceed, adapt, or pause practice should be based on current symptom profile and clinical context rather than diagnosis alone. Before sessions begin, it is worth establishing in advance who will monitor for symptom escalation, under what conditions practice will be adapted or discontinued, and how psychiatric support can be accessed if needed.

Once practice begins, delivery is adjusted through a small number of core variables. The issue of modality is important since contemplative forms cannot be substituted in populations exposed to trauma (Richard et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Some participants might find movement-based approaches more accessible (Richard et al., 2024). In comparison, others might find prolonged breath fixation or body scanning too stimulating (Leech et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). The length of the session must start with the minimal dose that can be tolerated (minimum effective dose) and not on the assumption that longer practice is always good (Bowles et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Galante et al., 2023). Emerging evidence on dose-response relationships in mindfulness-based interventions suggests that clinical gains may plateau or even diminish with excessive practice volume, particularly in vulnerable populations (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Galante et al., 2023). Pacing must remain gradual because early overload reduces engagement and retention (Bautista et al., 2024; Garfin et al., 2024). Participants at greater risk might require additional guidance, greater flexibility, and more frequent check-ins in the initial stages of practice (for a summary, see Figure 3; Garfin et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Taken together, these considerations support differential matching rather than uniform delivery (Richard et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024) (for a summary of entry-level risk stratification that informs these delivery decisions, see Figure 3).

Staged Sequencing of Practice

The literature indicates that trauma-informed meditation is best introduced in a gradual five-stage progression as opposed to direct exposure to conventional meditation (Richard et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). This staged framework is a theoretical synthesis based on available clinical and empirical literature, not yet a formally validated protocol. Empirical testing of the staged approach remains a priority for future research (Ooishi et al., 2021). This approach is consistent with trauma-informed care because it prioritizes safety, titration, and functional restoration (Berring et al., 2024; Galante et al., 2023; Melillo et al., 2025). It also aligns with systematic training principles (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025), which propose that effective training often requires calibrated, dynamically structured alterations to both training volume and intensity across stages of meditative development, so training variables match current life circumstances and overarching goals. This longer-term structuring of training is called periodization. In traumaexposed populations, periodization may involve increasing volume and intensity only as the practitioner adapts to their current regimen and experiential intensity by having developed sufficient self-regulatory capacities and skills (adaptation). This sequencing over time will allow for greater stability and a better chance to engage more productively with more demanding objects of attention (intensity), and for longer periods of time (volume). This staged logic is essential for severe trauma-exposed rehabilitation populations, where regulatory capacity is more variable, and the consequences of premature intensity are more severe (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a).

Stage 1: Stabilization and Safety

The first phase must focus on orientation, predictability, and choice. Short-term grounding exercises are often most suitable at this point (To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Within the Activity-Based Phenomenological Classification System framework of Sparby and Sacchet (2022), Stage 1 corresponds to the most basic focusing activity, stabilizing attention on an object (breath, sounds, body periphery) with minimal releasing (letting go or allowing) demands. In different words, the practitioner is given permission to shift focus when distress arises, rather than being asked to sustain and allow contact with difficulty. This reduces both attentional and embodied demands while still building the foundational regulatory scaffold that later stages will require (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022). To increase tolerability, it is strongly recommended to anchor attention in external objects, either by opening the eyes or through specific externally anchored exercises, such as tracking exterior sounds (To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). The goal is not deep meditation, but basic safety, engagement, and stability (To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Stage 2: Tolerable Embodiment

Once a basic sense of safety and orientation is established in Stage 1, practice may progress to a cautious engagement with embodied experience. In Activity-Based Phenomenological Classification System terms (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022), Stage 2 involves a gradual extension of the focusing activity from external objects toward internal objects, such as bodily signals beginning with peripheral, low-threat sensations (e.g., the sensation of the feet on the floor, the weight of the body in a chair) before approaching more central or emotionally laden areas such as the chest or abdomen. Some participants find gentle breath awareness accessible at this stage (Treves et al., 2025); others with significant trauma to the torso, heightened hypervigilance, or severe interoceptive dysregulation may find breath focus destabilizing, in which case movement-based contemplative approaches such as mindful walking or gentle yoga offer an alternative entry point to embodied awareness (Leech et al., 2024; Richard et al., 2024). The aim at this stage is to build interoceptive tolerance gradually, without creating overwhelm or forcing internal contact before the regulatory capacity to hold it is in place (Leech et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024; Treves et al., 2025).

Stage 3: Emotional Regulation and Nonreactivity

At this stage, more explicit mindfulness practices can be introduced in brief and titrated forms. Within the Activity-Based Phenomenological Classification System (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022), Stage 3 introduces a combination of focusing and releasing, the practitioner learns to sustain attention on an object, either internally or externally, while also practicing releasing by gently acknowledging and letting go of arising thoughts, sensations, or emotions rather than suppressing or avoiding them. Sparby and Sacchet (2022) describe this as the core of mindfulness practices, which both depend on an oscillation between sustained focus and receptive observation. In traumaexposed populations, the releasing dimension requires particular care, as releasing that activates dissociation or overwhelm should be temporarily substituted with refocusing on a stable external anchor (Calderone et al., 2024; Ooishi et al., 2021; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Noticing-andreturning exercises can help with distress tolerance (Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024; Scafuto et al., 2025). They should focus on control, not stamina (Price et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Stage 4: Compassion and Meaning-Making

Compassion-and loving-kindness-based practices can be progressively introduced to the participants who are stable enough. These activities can be used to address shame and harsh selfrelation (Arslan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). They may also support relational repair in some cases (Arslan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). In the Activity-Based Phenomenological Classification System framework (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022), Stage 4 primarily involves the imagining activity, which includes deliberately cultivating specific mental states of warmth, compassion, and equanimity in relation to an object that may include oneself, others, or difficult experience. Sparby and Sacchet (2022) characterize this stage as generative and productive, representing a qualitative shift in the practitioner's relationship to difficult experience, where intense distress is no longer primarily something to be managed or avoided, but something that can be turned toward and transformed through compassionate awareness. However, for trauma-exposed populations where shame, self-criticism, and relational mistrust are prominent (Chen et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), directing compassion toward oneself can be activating rather than soothing, particularly in early rehabilitation. Readiness for Stage 4 must therefore be assessed individually, and the generative activity should be introduced gradually, potentially beginning with neutral or external objects before turning toward the self (Chen et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Stage 5: Rehabilitation Transfer

In Stage 5, meditative skills developed across earlier stages are explicitly connected to rehabilitation objectives and daily functioning. This transfer involves applying skills from earlier stages: urge surfing for relapse prevention, emotion regulation in response to cravings or conflict, and body awareness as an early warning system for dysregulation (Gawande et al., 2025; Georgiadis et al., 2026; Joss et al., 2025; Leech et al., 2024; Somohano & Bowen, 2022; Treves et al., 2025). Preliminary evidence suggests that cognitive and attentional skills developed through meditation may carry over into daily life contexts (Ehmann, Sezer, Keller, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sezer, Treves, et al., 2025), though more research is needed on how this transfer occurs in complex, real-world situations (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022). At this stage, meditation ceases to be a discrete clinical exercise and becomes a self-management resource embedded in the participant's recovery ecology (Dobischok et al., 2026; Melillo et al., 2025). In correctional and community re-entry settings, this transfer is particularly important, as the loss of structured support at point of release can otherwise lead to rapid discontinuation of practice (Davies et al., 2025; Gaber et al., 2025; Gawande et al., 2025).

Safety Monitoring and Adaptation

A trauma-informed meditation framework also requires explicit safety monitoring. Recent research on the phenomenology of challenging meditation experiences demonstrates that such experiences are sufficiently common and consequential to warrant systematic attention (Britton et al., 2021; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Hugemark et al., 2026; Matko & Van Dam, 2026; Pauly et al., 2022). Critically, however, not all difficult experiences signal harm, and this distinction is central to the delivery of trauma-informed meditation in rehabilitation. Recent work on challenging meditation experiences suggests a useful distinction (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Galante et al., 2023; Hugemark et al., 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). Some difficulty reflects productive engagement with difficult inner material within a person's current regulatory capacity, a possible catalyst for growth, while other experiences exceed that capacity, leading to symptom escalation, dissociation, or functional deterioration. Safety monitoring should distinguish manageable discomfort from clinically significant distress, since challenging meditation experiences do not necessarily indicate therapeutic progress and may require practice modification or referral (Britton et al., 2021; Pauly et al., 2022; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). Session-level warning signs include escalating panic or inability to stay present (Britton et al., 2021; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Over time, signs of harm include sustained symptom elevation, increasing avoidance of practice, and functional deterioration outside of sessions (Britton et al., 2021; Pauly et al., 2022). For a practical MRPAEs classification, facilitators and researchers may draw on the taxonomy proposed by Binda et al. (2022), which distinguishes mild, moderate, and severe adverse meditation events applicable in both clinical and research contexts. Where either set of indicators is present, the appropriate response is practice pause, adaptation, or, where necessary, referral rather than encouragement to persist. Facilitators must be trained to hold this nuanced balance, and institutional settings must provide the supervisory structures to support such real-time clinical judgment (Canby et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Outcome Domains

Trauma-informed meditation in rehabilitation must be evaluated across multiple outcome domains rather than symptom reduction alone. Four domains are especially relevant: 1) Clinical outcomes, including PTSD symptoms, anxiety, depression, and substance-related distress, which remain the most commonly reported targets in the existing literature (Garfin et al., 2025; Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024; Orme-Johnson et al., 2024; Somohano & Bowen, 2022); 2) Mechanistic outcomes, which explain how change occurs-these include interoceptive awareness (Leech et al., 2024; Molteni et al., 2024; Treves et al., 2025), self-critical rumination (Fox et al., 2024; Joss et al., 2025), shame and guilt (Chen et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), and dissociation and emotion regulation, all of which have direct implications for tolerability and safety (Canby et al., 2025; Leech et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024); 3) Functional and rehabilitation outcomes, such as sleep quality, behavioral self-regulation, coping capacity, self-management, treatment retention, relapse prevention, and social reintegration (Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025; Georgiadis et al., 2026; Joss et al., 2025; Melillo et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022); and 4) Implementation outcomes, including acceptability, feasibility, adherence, and safety monitoring data (MRPAEs, distress-related dropout, and symptom exacerbation; Bowles et al., 2025; Britton et al., 2021; Canby et al., 2025; Matko & Van Dam, 2026; Pauly et al., 2022). These four domains are not exhaustive, but represent the minimum measurement architecture required to track clinical progress, understand mechanisms, monitor safety, and evaluate real-world implementation fidelity.

The selection of these four domains is grounded in the existing trauma-informed meditation literature (Canby et al., 2025; Garfin et al., 2025; Joss et al., 2025; Jovanovic & Garfin, 2024; Matko & Van Dam, 2026; Melillo et al., 2025; Orme-Johnson et al., 2024; Pauly et al., 2022; Somohano & Bowen, 2022; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024), and reflects the priority of moving beyond single-outcome designs toward multi-domain assessment that can capture the full complexity of trauma-informed contemplative rehabilitation.

ACEs-Influenced Meditative Development Development as a Functional Continuum

Trauma-exposed individuals do not begin contemplative practice from a neutral baseline.

ACE burden shapes regulatory starting conditions through altered stress reactivity, interoceptive disruption, and shame-proneness, so early progress often looks different from what general mindfulness research describes (Leech et al., 2024; Pasteuning et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Small gains matter here: learning to tolerate brief grounded attention, noticing slightly reduced distress during a session, or completing a practice without dissociating are all clinically meaningful achievements that make further stages of practice accessible. It is also worth noting that some frameworks of meditative development (Galante et al., 2023; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b) suggest that, over time and as regulatory capacity grows, practice can become a space for engaging with difficult material rather than simply managing it. This is an important broadening.

However, the evidence reviewed here is mostly concentrated on the earlier part of that process, making practice safe, tolerable, and accessible for trauma-exposed populations in rehabilitation.

Greater self-regulatory capacity may allow some practitioners to engage with challenging experiences more safely, although direct trauma reactivation is not required for meditative development (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Galante et al., 2023; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a).

Nonlinearity and Differential Pathways

Progress is rarely linear. A participant may make gains in grounding and orientation while continuing to struggle with shame activation or interoceptive discomfort. In some cases, distress increases temporarily before stabilizing, which can reflect initial engagement with previously avoided experience rather than harm (Britton et al., 2021; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). The practical implication is that facilitators need to distinguish productive difficulty from genuine harm, rather than either discontinuing practice at the first sign of discomfort or encouraging persistence without monitoring. A central clinical implication is the concept of the window of tolerance (Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Hugemark et al., 2026). Each person works most effectively when practice keeps them within a range of activation that is manageable not so low as to be disengaging, and not so high as to be overwhelming. This window varies by individual, session, and life circumstance. Well-being improvements through meditation are also not uniformly linear (Galante et al., 2023); even in the absence of harm, practitioners may experience periods of plateau or temporary difficulty that reflect natural variation in practice, not treatment failure. Ongoing assessment of both progress and distress markers is therefore essential throughout the rehabilitation course (Britton et al., 2021; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Galante et al., 2023; Hugemark et al., 2026; Matko & Van Dam, 2026; Pauly et al., 2022).

Moderators and Mediators of Response

Response to trauma-informed meditation varies across individuals based on several moderating factors, including ACE severity, PTSD symptom burden, dissociative vulnerability, substance use severity, housing instability, and the quality of facilitation and adaptation built into delivery (Arslan et al., 2025; Roberts et al., 2026; Rossi et al., 2025; Seitanidou et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). The most likely mediating mechanisms are interoceptive awareness, shame, self-critical rumination, and emotion regulation (Chen et al., 2025; Fox et al., 2024; Joss et al., 2025; Leech et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). These mechanisms explain why matching practice type, pacing, and facilitation to individual need matters clinically, and why generic delivery is unlikely to produce consistent results in high-ACE populations. Future studies should examine these pathways longitudinally in rehabilitation samples (Canby et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Pasteuning et al., 2025; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Rehabilitation Applications Correctional Rehabilitation

One of the most obvious contexts where trauma-informed meditation can potentially be useful is correctional settings. Prison populations are highly burdened with exposure to trauma (Coelho et al., 2025; Ford et al., 2020; Gaber et al., 2025). They are also often characterized by distrust, hypervigilance, shame, and difficulties with behavioral regulation (Gaber et al., 2025; Mazher & Arai, 2025). The evidence base for contemplative interventions in this specific subgroup remains sparse, and both the mechanisms of benefit and the risks of harm may differ meaningfully from those in trauma-exposed populations without such features. Future research should address this gap explicitly. In this respect, contemplative practice can be considered a trauma-informed emotional regulation and rehabilitative activity rather than a generic calming practice (Coelho et al., 2025; Gaber et al., 2025; Georgiadis et al., 2026).

Substance Use Rehabilitation

Substance use rehabilitation is another setting in which trauma-informed meditation may be especially relevant. Most people in addiction treatment do so in part to cope with distress, trauma triggers, hyperarousal, or severe self-criticism (Joss et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). This makes contemplative practice potentially useful yet challenging in early recovery (Garfin et al., 2024 (Garfin et al., , 2025;; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Meditation can augment contact with desire, physical discomfort, or emotional distress prior to regulatory advantage being achieved (Canby et al., 2025; Joss et al., 2025). That is why trauma-integrated mindfulness-based relapse prevention is particularly significant, as it introduces trauma responsiveness and relapse prevention into the same logic of intervention (Somohano & Bowen, 2022).

Practically, trauma-informed meditation in substance use treatment can assist participants to become more aware of urges sooner, disrupt automaticity, and react to discomfort in more feasible manners (Joss et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Such gains are more likely when contemplative practices are presented at a slow pace, with robust facilitator support, and with awareness of the trauma history (Bautista et al., 2024; Garfin et al., 2024 Garfin et al., , 2025)). Mindfulness in addiction care is therefore most persuasive when it is adapted to trauma burden rather than delivered as a standard protocol alone (Garfin et al., 2024; Somohano & Bowen, 2022).

Women With Trauma Histories

Women with trauma histories represent an important application area because trauma exposure in this group often intersects with gendered violence, shame, housing instability, caregiving strain, and substance-related vulnerability (Bautista et al., 2024; Davies et al., 2025; Garfin et al., 2024 Garfin et al., , 2025;; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Gender-responsive care is therefore essential, as the specific forms of trauma common in women's lives shape both the content of contemplative experience and the relational conditions under which practice can be safely introduced. Recent research in women who are unhoused and exposed to trauma and women with PTSD and substance use disorder indicates that contemplative interventions may be beneficial, although only when delivered in a clearly trauma-adapted and gender-responsive manner (Bautista et al., 2024; Garfin et al., 2024 Garfin et al., , 2025;; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). In such contexts, relational safety is not secondary to technique but a condition that makes the technique usable (Bautista et al., 2024; Davies et al., 2025; Garfin et al., 2024).

Practices that intensify internal awareness too quickly may feel exposing rather than restorative, especially when trauma histories involve bodily violation or chronic fear (Garfin et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). A gender-responsive approach should therefore emphasize voice, pacing, sensitivity to shame, and supportive group process (Bautista et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Garfin et al., 2024). Women-centered research indicates that participants may not initially be able to focus or tolerate, but they can still benefit when interventions are tailored to engagement barriers and instructor support (Bautista et al., 2024; Garfin et al., 2025). In this population, contemplative practice is most useful when it strengthens dignity, self-trust, and relational safety rather than simply asking participants to observe distress (Davies et al., 2025; Garfin et al., 2024).

Community and Re-Entry Settings

Community and re-entry settings require a different application logic. Whether meditation can be introduced is often not the primary concern; rather, the focus is on whether it can be maintained beyond formal services (Daros et al., 2025; Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025). People who have experienced trauma and are released from correctional, residential, or acute treatment facilities tend to revert to unstable environments characterized by stress, loneliness, poor service connection, and risk of relapse (Barnert et al., 2024; Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025). In these contexts, peer-supported and culturally responsive contemplative models may be more realistic than highly formalized programs (Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025; Harerimana et al., 2025).

The Mandela Yoga case study is especially instructive because it situated contemplative practice within a community re-entry setting and emphasized peer connection, consistency, and the relevance of recovery (Gawande et al., 2025). Online or hybrid solutions can also help ensure continuity when access barriers exist (Daros et al., 2025). Digital meditation applications may support continuity of practice, although their effectiveness and safety among individuals with substantial trauma exposure require further investigation (Britton et al., 2021; Daros et al., 2025).

Peer support, facilitator contact, and pathways back to human care remain important (Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025; Harerimana et al., 2025). Meditation in re-entry contexts will likely be most effective when it is part of a broader support ecology that also encompasses recovery services, social connection, and trauma-informed case support (Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025).

Overall Implications

Across settings, the same conclusion emerges that trauma-informed meditation is most useful when it is matched to trauma burden, embedded in context, and aligned with functional recovery goals (Coelho et al., 2025; Dobischok et al., 2026; Garfin et al., 2024; Gawande et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). This translates to regulation and reintegration in the correctional setting (Coelho et al., 2025; Georgiadis et al., 2026). In substance use care, it means craving management and relapse prevention (Joss et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). In trauma services for women, it implies relational safety and shame-sensitive provision (Bautista et al., 2024; Garfin et al., 2024). In community re-entry, it means continuity, peer support, and transfer of self-regulation skills into everyday life (Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025).

Clinical, Ethical, and Future Directions

Clinically meaningful informed consent is the essential starting point for trauma-informed meditation. Participants must be clearly informed that contemplative practice may be helpful, but that difficult experiences can also occur, including anxiety, dysphoric affect, re-experiencing, dissociation, or temporary worsening before benefit becomes clear (Binda et al., 2022; Britton et al., 2021; Matko & Van Dam, 2026; Pauly et al., 2022). Equally important, participants should be informed that some difficulty during practice is not necessarily a sign that something is wrong. In the framework of meditative development, productive confrontation of discomfort, including trauma-related material, in a supported and titrated context may itself be part of the recovery mechanism (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022 , 2025a). Informed consent must therefore communicate both the reality of potential MRPAEs and the possibility that these, when appropriately held, can contribute to therapeutic growth (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, Grabovac, et al., 2026; Ehmann, Sparby, Naughton, & Sacchet, 2026; Galante et al., 2023; Hugemark et al., 2026; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b). Recent safety literature therefore supports screening, expectation-setting, psychoeducation, and ongoing monitoring rather than assuming meditation is risk-free (Bowles et al., 2025; Britton et al., 2021; Matko & Van Dam, 2026). Instead, matching and adaptation should be achieved through screening, not unjustifiable exclusion (Goldstein et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Patients with significant dissociation, intense panic reactions to breathing or body signals, or recent destabilization might need safer entry points, including eyes-open practice, movement-based work, externally anchored attention, shorter length, and more facilitator support (Leech et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). These factors suggest that ethical entry into practice is an issue of fit rather than easy access or rejection (Goldstein et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).

Implementation also depends on instructor competence and cultural sensitivity. Facilitators need more than a technical understanding of meditation. They must also be able to recognize dissociation, dysregulation, and escalating distress, and know when modification or referral is required (Seitanidou et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Recent studies on implementing trauma-informed care indicate that consistent training, workforce development, and organizational support are critical to safe delivery (Goldstein et al., 2024; Keesler et al., 2025). Cultural and spiritual sensitivity are equally important. Meditation cannot be positioned in a manner that imposes alien assumptions or that stillness, silence, and introspection are universally safe (Davies et al., 2025; Harerimana et al., 2025). Contemplative practices in rehabilitation environments must thus be culturally, in relation to identity, and contextually modified, and provided with cultural humility rather than spiritual authority (Davies et al., 2025; Goldstein et al., 2024; Harerimana et al., 2025).

Another ethical concern involves distinguishing potentially constructive psychological difficulty from meditation-related harm. Although meditative development may involve encountering uncomfortable thoughts, emotions, or bodily sensations, such experiences should not automatically be interpreted as therapeutically beneficial. Trauma-related distress or reactivation may occur during contemplative practice, but it is neither a necessary component of meditative development nor equivalent to the structured processing of traumatic memories undertaken in evidence-based trauma-focused psychotherapy. The distinction between manageable discomfort and clinically significant deterioration requires careful attention to symptom severity, duration, functional consequences, and individual vulnerability (Sparby & Sacchet, 2022 , 2025a).

Facilitators should therefore be trained to recognize signs of psychological destabilization, adapt or discontinue practices when indicated, and seek appropriate clinical support. Institutions should establish clear supervision and referral procedures to ensure that meditation-related difficulties are managed safely and within the facilitator's professional competence.

Four research priorities now emerge from this review. First, the field needs to prioritize improved measurement, as trauma-informed meditation has not been adequately operationalized.

Validated instruments are needed to measure contemplative readiness, MRPAEs during a session, interoceptive tolerance, and ACE-stratified response patterns (Binda et al., 2022; Bowles et al., 2025; Britton et al., 2021; Leech et al., 2024). Second, the study designs must go beyond one-arm and fixed-dose designs. More appropriate methods, such as stepped-care designs, dose-finding studies, subgroup analyses, and longitudinal models, will be more suitable for trauma-exposed rehabilitation populations (Bowles et al., 2025). Third, mechanism studies need to move beyond identifying relevant processes toward testing their specific contributions to outcomes in traumaexposed rehabilitation populations. Autonomic regulation, interoception, dissociation, self-critical rumination, shame, guilt, and broader self-regulation are well-established as relevant (Arslan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Fox et al., 2024; Joss et al., 2025; Leech et al., 2024; Sezgin, 2025; Treves et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). What is needed are study designs that can isolate and disentangle these contributions using mediation analyses, component studies, or mechanistically-stratified trials specifically in rehabilitation-relevant samples.

These processes require investigation using both subjective and objective indicators where methodologically feasible. Fourth, implementation science remains essential. Research needs to test staff training, fidelity frameworks, contextual adaptation, and service integration across correctional, rehabilitation, and low-resource settings (Davies et al., 2025; Goldstein et al., 2024; Keesler et al., 2025; Roberts et al., 2026; Seitanidou et al., 2024), using frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) or the Exploration-Preparation-Implementation-Sustainment (EPIS) model to map barriers systematically (Aarons et al., 2011; Damschroder et al., 2009).

Beyond the scope of this review, an important future direction is the study of advanced meditation in trauma-exposed populations (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026; Sparby et al., 2026). Emerging research suggests that as regulatory capacity grows, contemplative practice may become a vehicle for deeper developmental change engaging and resolving trauma-related material rather than simply managing it (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a , 2025b). This developmental trajectory remains understudied in rehabilitation and clinical contexts and represents a promising direction for next generation of meditation science research (Lieberman & Sacchet, 2026; Sacchet & Lieberman, 2026).

Clinical Implications

The evidence reviewed suggests several implications for practitioners and services that deliver meditation-based interventions to trauma-exposed people. First, ACE exposure and trauma symptoms should be considered before delivery, because trauma burden may influence tolerability, meditation-related adverse effects, and dropout (Britton et al., 2021; Canby et al., 2025). A brief intake covering trauma symptoms, dissociative vulnerability, and interoceptive tolerance may support decisions to proceed, adapt, or pause practice (Binda et al., 2022; Britton et al., 2021; Leech et al., 2024; Matko & Van Dam, 2026). Second, practice should be adapted rather than delivered generically; shorter sessions, eyes-open or externally anchored attention, movementbased options, and choice over participation can improve tolerability (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Richard et al., 2024; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024). Third, progression should be staged, beginning with stabilization and increasing training volume and intensity only as regulatory capacity grows (Ehmann, Sparby, et al., 2025; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a). Fourth, facilitators need trauma-informed training, clinical supervision, and clear referral pathways, and services should monitor for meditation-related adverse effects and distinguish productive challenge from harm (Britton et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2024; Hugemark et al., 2026; Keesler et al., 2025; Pauly et al., 2022; Sparby & Sacchet, 2025a; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024; Treleaven, 2018). Finally, outcomes should be evaluated beyond symptom change to include emotional regulation, treatment engagement, and functional recovery (Dobischok et al., 2026; Gawande et al., 2025; Georgiadis et al., 2026; Joss et al., 2025; Melillo et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022). Because these implications draw largely on pilot, feasibility, and conceptual work, and the staged framework has not yet been formally validated, they should be applied with clinical judgment and not be overstated.

Conclusion

Meditation in trauma-exposed rehabilitation populations cannot be treated as a one-sizefits-all intervention. ACE history shapes regulation, embodiment, shame, dissociation, and contemplative tolerability in ways that directly affect engagement, benefit, and risk. Clinical presentations also vary considerably PTSD, psychotic disorders, dissociative disorders, and substance use disorders each carry distinct implications for how meditation should be delivered, paced, and monitored and these differences call for more diagnosis-specific guidance than any single review can provide. Trauma-informed meditation therefore requires structured entry assessment, staged delivery, adapted practice selection, safety monitoring, trained facilitators, and outcomes that extend beyond symptom reduction to include regulation, functioning, and reintegration. Critically, herein we also adopt a developmental lens within meditation, such that challenging experiences, including trauma-related reactivation, are not merely side effects to be minimized, but may be integral to the mechanism of growth. When delivered within a traumainformed structure that ensures adequate safety, titration, and relational support, the productive confrontation of difficult experience may itself constitute part of the mechanism of therapeutic benefit.

An ACEs-sensitive model of meditative development offers a more realistic and clinically useful framework than generic contemplative delivery because it holds both dimensions in view the imperative to adapt practice to individual trauma burden, and the developmental potential of meditation to transform the relationship to trauma and to suffering more broadly across time.

Future studies should examine whether PTSD severity, dissociative vulnerability, and co-occurring substance use disorders influence meditation tolerability, practice selection, and rehabilitation outcomes (Canby et al., 2025; Joss et al., 2025; Somohano & Bowen, 2022; To & Schuman-Olivier, 2024).
