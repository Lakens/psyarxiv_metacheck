Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Little is known about which adults actually turn to generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots for mental health advice, and whether the profile of these users changes with age. The aim of this study was to examine the sociodemographic and psychological correlates of actual chatbot consultation for mental health advice, and of its frequency. In this cross-sectional online survey, 843 Greek adults aged 18 to 50 years reported whether, and how often, they had consulted an AI chatbot about their thoughts and feelings during the previous three months. Validated instruments captured anxiety, depression, loneliness, sleep quality, self-esteem and problematic social media use. Logistic and proportional odds models, extended with age-bypredictor product terms, were fitted. Seven in ten respondents (71.3%) reported such use, and 37.5% did so weekly or more often. Independent correlates of any use were younger age, recent consultation with a mental health professional, greater problematic social media use and lower self-esteem. Among users, worse sleep, lower self-esteem and greater problematic social media use were associated with more frequent consultation of AI chatbots for mental health advice. Age moderated the role of loneliness, which was related to chatbot use only among the youngest respondents, and a comparable but weaker gradient emerged for depressive symptoms. Chatbotbased mental health advice is now widespread among Greek adults up to midlife.

Clinicians should ask patients of every age about it, and preventive efforts should recognize that its psychological drivers are not uniform across adulthood.

These systems were not built as clinical tools, yet survey and qualitative data indicate that many people bring them their worries, low mood and interpersonal difficulties, and treat them as a confidant or an informal advisor [8] [9] [10]. The reasons are easy to understand. A chatbot answers instantly, at any hour, at no cost, and without the embarrassment that may accompany a conversation with a relative, a friend or a therapist [9, 11]. Adults in the United States who sought psychological help from LLMs explicitly cited cost, convenience and availability, as well as difficulties in obtaining conventional treatment [9], and health-related chatbot use more broadly already extends to self-diagnosis, self-medication and lifestyle coaching [12].

Whether chatbots help or harm people who use them in this way is still unresolved.

Mental health applications that were specifically designed and tested as interventions yield modest improvements in symptoms [13], and a recent trial of a clinically finetuned generative model reported meaningful reductions in depression and anxiety [14]. None of this evidence, however, applies to general-purpose assistants, which have never been validated as treatments and fall outside medical device regulation [15]. Evaluations of their behavior are worrying: they discriminate poorly between intermediate levels of suicide risk [16], their safeguards differ according to the interface through which they are accessed [17], and they may reinforce, rather than question, distorted beliefs [18, 19]. Concerns also exist about substitution for professional care, since more than one in four US adults with a history of professional care reported seeing clinicians less often after they began using artificial intelligence (AI) for the same purpose [20], and about dependence, given links between escapemotivated chatbot use and addictive patterns [21] and evidence that reliance on companion chatbots can deepen loneliness over time [22].

1.2. Who uses AI chatbots for mental health advice?

Estimates of how many people consult chatbots about their mental health have risen steeply. Only about one in twenty US university students reported doing so in early 2024 [11], compared with roughly one in four US adults in a stratified sample surveyed later that year [9]. Two nationally representative US surveys of 12-to 21-year-olds found that the proportion grew from 13.1% to 19.2% within 2025 [23, 24], and more than a third of US adults aged 18 to 49 years described weekly reliance on AI tools for psychological support in late 2025 [20]. Yet studies that quantified actual behavior, rather than willingness or intention, remain few, and they have largely sampled North American adolescents, students and young adults [23] [24] [25] [26], with one recent exception from Japan [27].

Across these studies, a fairly coherent clinical profile emerges. Users report more depressive and anxious symptoms [20, 25], greater overall distress [27, 28] and a heavier clinical burden [26] than non-users, and they are more often in contact with mental health services [20, [24] [25] [26]. Weaker social support [26] and stronger feelings of loneliness [9, 21, 29] have also been described. By contrast, constructs such as sleep quality, self-esteem and problematic social media use have received almost no attention, even though each is plausibly connected to digital coping.

Findings on age are puzzling. Among adolescents and young adults, the likelihood of use increases with age [23, 24]. Among adults, however, younger Israeli users of generative AI were more likely to seek psychological support from it [28], whereas in the United States respondents in their thirties reported more use than those in their twenties [20]. A very large US survey further showed that intensive generative AI use was concentrated among younger adults, while its correlation with depressive symptoms was strongest in middle age [30]. Taken together, these results raise the possibility that age shapes not only how common chatbot use is, but also which psychological states lead people to it. To our knowledge, this possibility has not been tested.

1.3. Why might age matter?

Several lines of theory predict that the psychological correlates of chatbot use should weaken from early adulthood to midlife. Research on technology acceptance shows that younger people adopt new tools largely on the basis of their own attitudes, whereas older people rely more on perceived control and on the expectations of others [31]; a distressed young adult may therefore reach for a chatbot almost reflexively.

Socioemotional selectivity theory holds that, as perceived time horizons shorten, adults invest in a smaller circle of emotionally close relationships and regulate their emotions more efficiently within it [32]. Loneliness itself follows a non-linear course, with a peak in young adulthood and age-specific sources thereafter [33], and favorable attitudes toward professional help become more common with age [7]. It follows that feeling lonely or depressed should translate into chatbot consultation more readily in the early twenties than in the forties, when existing relationships and formal services offer alternative routes to support.

1.4. The present study

Our hypotheses were derived from two frameworks. Andersen's model of health care utilization distinguishes predisposing characteristics, enabling resources and need [34]. Treating chatbot consultation as an informal analogue of help-seeking, we considered sex and age as predisposing characteristics and perceived financial situation as an enabling resource, recent contact with a mental health professional as an indicator of evaluated need and help-seeking readiness, and anxiety, depression, loneliness, poor sleep and low self-esteem as indicators of perceived need.

Complementary accounts of compensatory internet use [35], social compensation through chatbots [29] and the Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model [36] propose that people with unmet psychosocial needs, or with an established habit of regulating mood online, gravitate toward digital sources of relief.

Against this background, we investigated the sociodemographic and psychological correlates of actual chatbot consultation for mental health advice, and of its frequency, in Greek adults, and we asked whether these correlates varied with age. We expected that: • H1: sex, age and perceived financial situation would be related to chatbot consultation (no direction specified); • H2: respondents who had recently consulted a mental health professional would report more chatbot use; • H3: anxiety and depressive symptoms would be positively related to chatbot use; • H4: loneliness would be positively related to chatbot use; • H5: worse sleep would be related to more chatbot use; • H6: lower self-esteem would be related to more chatbot use; • H7: greater problematic social media use would be related to more chatbot use; and • H8: the links between psychological variables and chatbot use would be stronger in younger respondents.

2. Materials and Methods

>Materials and Methods

2.1. Design, setting and sampling

This was a cross-sectional survey carried out in Greece from June to November 2026.

Respondents completed an anonymous questionnaire hosted on Google Forms, which was promoted through posts on Facebook, Instagram, TikTok and LinkedIn; recruitment therefore relied on convenience sampling. Because the behavior of interest takes place online, an internet survey reached the relevant population efficiently, and completing the questionnaire privately and at one's own pace was expected to encourage honest reporting of symptoms and help-seeking.

Adults were eligible if they were 18 to 50 years old, resided in Greece, could read

Greek and held at least one active social media account. The upper limit of 50 years was set because engagement with generative AI chatbots drops markedly after midlife.

In a national survey of US adults conducted in February 2026, about six in ten respondents younger than 50 years used AI chatbots, compared with roughly four in ten of those aged 50 to 64 years and fewer still among those aged 65 years or older, and daily use was concentrated among adults under 50 [37]; intensive generative AI use has likewise been shown to be most common among younger adults [30].

Extending recruitment beyond 50 years would therefore have added few users and yielded unstable estimates for the frequency of use, while social media recruitment was expected to reach comparatively few older adults. For descriptive comparisons and supplementary analyses, respondents were grouped into young adults (18-29 years) and adults aged 30-50 years. The boundary at 29 years follows the age span of 15 to 29 years that European Union youth policy and statistics use to define young people [38, 39], restricted here to its adult part, and coincides with the end of emerging adulthood, the developmental stage covering the late teens and the twenties, which is characterized by identity exploration and instability in work and relationships [40].

Apart from these group comparisons, age was analyzed as a continuous variable.

Reporting follows the STROBE recommendations for cross-sectional studies [41].

The analytic sample comprised 843 respondents. Sample adequacy was judged in two ways. With ten predictors, or eleven once a product term was added, the commonly used threshold of ten outcome events per predictor [42] required at least 110 cases in the smaller outcome category. This was comfortably exceeded in the binary analysis (242 non-users; 22.0 events per predictor) and met in the ordinal analysis among users (smallest category, 139 respondents; 12.6 per predictor). In addition, using the approach of Hsieh and colleagues [43] with an expected percentage of users of 71.3%, two-sided α = 0.05, an odds ratio of 1.5 per standard deviation and R² = 0.30 between a given predictor and the remaining covariates, 334 respondents would provide 80% power; the available sample yielded power above 99% for main effects.

2.2. Instruments Predictors

Respondents reported their sex, their age in years and their financial situation on a 0 (very poor) to 10 (excellent) rating, which was analyzed as a continuous score. They also stated whether they had seen a psychiatrist, psychologist or other mental health professional during the last three months (no/yes).

Anxiety and depression over the preceding fortnight were screened with the four-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-4) [44], validated in the Greek general population [45]. Each two-item subscale yields 0 to 6 points, and scores of 3 or more indicate a positive screen [44]; Cronbach's α in the present data was 0.833 (anxiety) and 0.781 (depression).

Loneliness was assessed with the three-item short form of the UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA LS-3) [46] in its Greek version [47]. Items concern lack of companionship, feeling left out and feeling isolated, each rated 1 (hardly ever) to 3 (often), giving totals of 3 to 9 (α = 0.807).

Sleep quality over the previous week was rated on the Single-Item Sleep Quality Scale (SQS) [48], available in Greek [49], from 0 (very poor) to 10 (excellent).

Global self-esteem was measured with the Single-Item Self-Esteem Scale (SISE) [50], on which respondents rate how true the statement "I have high self-esteem" is for them from 1 to 7.

Problematic social media use during the last year was assessed with the six-item Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) [51] in its Greek adaptation [52]. The items correspond to salience, tolerance, mood modification, relapse, withdrawal and conflict, are rated 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often) and sum to 6-30 (α = 0.850). The BSMAS is a screening measure and was not used to assign diagnoses.

Outcome

Two questions adapted from McBain and colleagues [23] assessed chatbot consultation. Respondents first indicated (no/yes) whether, during the last three months, they had turned to an AI chatbot such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude for advice concerning their mental health, which was explained as their thoughts and feelings, for example sadness, anger, nervousness or stress. Those who answered affirmatively then reported how often they did so (hardly ever; at least monthly; at least weekly; daily or almost daily). The three-month window was chosen to reflect current behavior, to limit recall error and to match the time frame of the question on professional consultation. The resulting variables were any use (yes/no) in the full sample and frequency of use (four ordered levels) among users.

2.3. Ethics

The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Nursing, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens, approved the protocol (No. 01, 16 September 2025), and the study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki [53]. The opening page of the survey explained its purpose, the voluntary and anonymous nature of participation and the possibility of stopping at any point; only respondents who ticked a consent box could proceed. Contact details for mental health helplines and support services were displayed at the end. No names, e-mail addresses, IP addresses or other identifiers were recorded, files were password-protected and results are reported only at group level.

2.4. Data analysis

Descriptive statistics comprise counts with percentages and means with SD, medians and IQR. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests and Q-Q plots indicated non-normal distributions for every continuous variable, so group differences (18-29 vs. 30-50 years; users vs. non-users) were tested with Mann-Whitney U tests, and with Pearson's χ² tests for categorical variables.

Any use was modelled with binary logistic regression in all 843 respondents, and frequency of use with a cumulative logit (proportional odds) model in the 601 users.

The same ten predictors entered both models: sex, age, financial situation, recent professional consultation, positive anxiety screen, positive depression screen (both PHQ-4 subscales dichotomized at ≥ 3), loneliness, sleep quality, self-esteem and BSMAS score, the latter four as continuous terms. Crude estimates were followed by fully adjusted models, and results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Model diagnostics included variance inflation factors (VIF > 5 taken as problematic) for both models. For the binary model we additionally examined linearity in the logit with Box-Tidwell terms, screened for outlying and influential observations (standardized residuals beyond ±3, leverage above 3(k + 1)/n, Cook's distance above 1), assessed calibration with the Hosmer-Lemeshow statistic, the unweighted sum-ofsquares statistic [54] and a decile-based calibration plot, and quantified discrimination with the area under the ROC curve (AUC). For the ordinal model, proportionality of odds was checked with the SPSS test of parallel lines and with the Brant procedure [55]. Overall model fit is summarized by the likelihood ratio χ² and Nagelkerke R².

Moderation by age (H8) was tested by entering, in separate models, the product of mean-centered age (centered at 26.52 years) with each of the remaining nine predictors. Each product term was evaluated with a 1-df likelihood ratio test, and all nine were also evaluated together (9 df). Because only nine pre-specified interactions were examined for each outcome, no adjustment for multiple testing was applied.

Significant interactions were decomposed by computing conditional ORs at 20, 30 and 40 years and by locating regions of significance with the Johnson-Neyman method [56]. As a supplementary check, moderation was re-examined with a binary age variable (30-50 vs. 18-29 years), and crude and adjusted ORs were estimated within each age band.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1. Principal findings

In this Greek survey of adults aged 18 to 50 years, consulting generative AI chatbots about one's mental health emerged as a mainstream behavior: almost three-quarters of respondents had done so in the last three months, and well over a third did so every week.

The strongest independent correlates of any use were younger age, recent professional mental health care, problematic social media use and low self-esteem, while the frequency of consultation was additionally tied to poor sleep. The central new observation concerns age as a moderator. Loneliness, and less clearly depressive symptoms, were associated with chatbot use among respondents in their late teens and early twenties, but these associations faded progressively with age. Measured against our hypotheses, H2, H6 and H7 were supported; H1 held for age only; H5 held for frequency only; H3 (for depression) and H4 were supported only in the youngest respondents; and H8 was supported for loneliness and depression, but not for the other psychological predictors.

4.2. How widespread is chatbot consultation?

The percentage of respondents who had consulted a chatbot for mental health advice in the previous three months (71.3%) is far above earlier figures, which range from roughly a fifth of US college students [25] and a quarter of US adults [9] to 29.3% of Israeli generative AI users [28] and 41.0% of Japanese young adults over one month [27]; our weekly rate (37.5%), by contrast, is close to that reported for US adults under 50 (35.2%) [20]. The gap probably reflects a combination of factors. Our fieldwork took place later than any previous survey, during a period of very rapid growth [9, 11, 23, 24]. Recruitment through social media inevitably favored digitally engaged people and, perhaps, those already curious about AI or psychology. Women and distressed individuals, groups known to use these tools more [20, 25, 27, 28], were strongly represented. Finally, our question covered everyday emotional difficulties as well as mental health problems. This percentage should therefore be regarded as specific to our sample rather than as a national estimate.

Use was less common in the 30-50 band, and age remained an independent negative correlate after adjustment, in keeping with the concentration of intensive generative AI use [30] and of AI-based psychological support [28] among younger adults, although one US survey found more use in the fourth than in the third decade of life [20]. The more striking point, however, is that nearly two-thirds of respondents aged 30 to 50 years had consulted a chatbot, and almost one-third did so weekly. Chatbotbased mental health advice is therefore not a youth phenomenon. The fact that half of all users consulted chatbots weekly and a quarter daily also deserves attention, given that frequent emotional disclosure to chatbots has been linked to dependence [10, 27].

4.3. An age-dependent role for loneliness and depressive symptoms

Our main contribution is the demonstration that loneliness predicted chatbot consultation only in the youngest part of the age range, with a similar but statistically weaker gradient for depressive symptoms. This helps reconcile our pooled results, in which neither variable was significant after adjustment, with the consistent associations reported in younger samples [25, 27, 29, 57]. Put simply, the psychological route into chatbot use appears to change across adulthood.

Each of the theoretical accounts outlined in the Introduction offers part of the explanation. Younger adults decide whether to use a technology mainly on the basis of their own attitudes toward it [31], so that a chatbot may be the first thing a lonely or low young person turns to. With advancing age, people prune their social networks toward emotionally significant ties and become more skilled at managing negative affect within them [32]; older respondents in our sample also used professional services more often, and attitudes toward such help improve with age [7]. Moreover, loneliness is most prevalent in young adulthood and stems from different sources at different life stages [33]. In early adulthood it often reflects unsettled friendships and romantic relationships, a deficit that an ever-present, responsive and non-judgmental conversational partner may seem to fill [29, 35]; in midlife it is more likely to arise from the strains of work, parenting and caregiving [4], which a chatbot conversation is unlikely to relieve.

The finding carries a warning. Lonely young people who come to depend on chatbots for emotional support may become caught in a self-reinforcing cycle: longitudinal data show that weak social connection precedes increased companion chatbot use, which in turn predicts greater loneliness [22], and loneliness was the sole psychosocial correlate of emotional dependence on chatbots in Japanese young adults [27]. Short-term comfort may thus come at the cost of withdrawal from offline relationships [58].

These moderation results nonetheless need confirmation. Respondents over 30 years made up under a third of the sample, no moderation was detected for frequency of use, and a US study reported the reverse age pattern for the link between general generative AI use and depression [30], although that study did not focus on mental health-related use.

4.4. Correlates that did not vary with age

Low self-esteem was the only variable related to both any use and frequency of use, and its association was similar across ages. People who think poorly of themselves tend to anticipate criticism and may hesitate to reveal their problems to others; fear of stigma and embarrassment is a recognized obstacle to seeking help [6], and users repeatedly describe chatbots as a confidential space free of judgment [8, 59]. In the terms of compensatory internet use theory [35], the reassurance a chatbot provides may offset a negative self-image. Because these systems tend to agree with their users, however, they may also validate maladaptive cognitions instead of correcting them [18, 19]. That self-esteem was not an independent correlate in a Japanese sample [27] may reflect cultural, instrumental or analytical differences.

Problematic social media use was likewise related to both outcomes irrespective of age. Within the I-PACE framework [36], online behaviors persist because they gratify and relieve, and because users come to expect that a given application will lift their mood. People who already turn to social media to escape unpleasant feelings may simply extend this strategy to chatbots, a view consistent with the association between escape motives and addictive chatbot use [21]. Problematic social media use may thus mark a broader tendency toward heavy, emotionally driven technology use, and adults with high BSMAS scores represent an obvious target for preventive and digital literacy initiatives.

Recent contact with a mental health professional was also an age-invariant correlate of use, mirroring previous reports [20, [24] [25] [26]. In Andersen's terms, it signals both an assessed need and a disposition to seek help [34], and patients may use chatbots between sessions as an adjunct to treatment. Because both behaviors were recorded over the same three months, we cannot say whether chatbots steer people toward professionals [60] or away from them [20]; that psychiatric inpatients rarely tell their clinicians about such use [59] makes this question practically important.

Finally, the bivariate links of anxiety and depression with use lost significance after adjustment in the full sample. These symptoms overlap with loneliness, low selfesteem and poor sleep, which probably absorbed part of their explanatory power, and, as shown above, the depression association was largely confined to the youngest respondents.

An unexpected interaction also emerged for frequency of use: from about 37 years of age onward, users who rated their financial situation more favorably consulted chatbots more often, whereas no such association was present among younger users.

Older adults with greater financial resources may have readier access to paid versions of chatbots or to occupations and devices that embed generative AI in everyday routines, which could facilitate regular use. Because the joint test of interactions for frequency of use was not significant, this finding should be regarded as exploratory.

4.5. Sleep and frequency of consultation

Worse sleep was associated with consulting chatbots more often, but not with consulting them at all. One plausible explanation is nocturnal use: people lying awake with worries may turn to a chatbot when no one else is available. The relationship could also run in the opposite direction, since screen use around bedtime impairs sleep in young people [61] and in adults [62], or it could reflect the fact that poor sleep both signals and contributes to psychological distress [63]. Without information on when consultations took place, these explanations cannot be distinguished.

4.6. Practical implications

Several practical lessons follow. Clinicians should not assume that chatbot use for mental health advice concerns only young patients; asking about it should become part of routine assessment at every age, particularly because most users do not volunteer this information [24, 59]. Prevention can be partly age-specific: in young adults, strengthening social connection and detecting depressive symptoms early may lessen the pull toward compensatory chatbot use, whereas low self-esteem and problematic social media use are relevant targets throughout adulthood. Adults who consult chatbots frequently, especially those who sleep badly, have low self-esteem or use social media problematically, may be most exposed to over-reliance, and AI literacy programs should explain what general-purpose chatbots can and cannot offer and encourage use that complements rather than replaces human contact [10, 58]. At the system level, developers and regulators should adopt minimum standards for psychologically sensitive conversations, including crisis recognition and active signposting to professional and emergency services [15] [16] [17] 19].

4.7. Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this work include the measurement of actual rather than intended use, the joint modelling of a broad set of psychological correlates assessed with validated instruments, the examination of both any use and frequency of use, and the explicit test of age as a moderator.

Several limitations must also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal conclusions. Loneliness, for instance, may be both an antecedent and a consequence of chatbot use [22], and a similar bidirectionality is plausible for poor sleep, low self-esteem and problematic social media use, since intensive interaction with conversational agents, particularly late in the evening, may itself displace sleep and offline social contact. The ordering of professional consultation and chatbot use within the same three months is also unknown; advice obtained from a chatbot may have prompted a visit to a professional, or a professional encounter may have stimulated further information seeking. Moreover, because age was recorded at a single time point, the age-dependent associations cannot be attributed unequivocally to developmental stage. They may equally reflect cohort differences in digital socialization, in that adults who grew up with smartphones and social media may relate to conversational agents differently from those who adopted these technologies later in life, irrespective of age itself.

Second, the convenience sample, recruited online, was predominantly female and may over-represent people interested in AI or mental health, so generalization to the Greek adult population is not warranted. Because participation required internet access and an active social media account, adults with limited digital engagement, who are probably less likely to use chatbots, were excluded by design, and self-selection into a survey on this topic may have inflated the percentage of users. The number of people who saw the invitation is unknown, so a response rate could not be calculated and the characteristics of non-respondents could not be compared with those of respondents.

Third, all information was self-reported and may be affected by recall and social desirability bias; the stigma that still surrounds mental health may have led some participants to under-report psychological distress or the use of chatbots for emotional concerns. Sleep, self-esteem and chatbot use were each measured with a single item, and the PHQ-4 identifies symptoms rather than disorders. Single items cannot capture the multidimensional nature of these constructs. The question on chatbot use deliberately covered everyday emotional difficulties as well as mental health problems; it may therefore have grouped together forms of use that differ in purpose and in their psychological correlates, such as occasional emotional support and consultation about a specific psychological condition, and respondents of different ages may have interpreted the term mental health advice differently. In addition, frequency of use does not reflect the duration or depth of conversations, the degree of reliance on the advice received, or whether chatbots served as a substitute for or a complement to professional care.

Fourth, we lacked information on the type of chatbot, distinguishing general-purpose assistants from companion or mental health-specific applications, on the content, timing and perceived usefulness of conversations, and on whether the advice was followed or resulted in any benefit or harm. Consequently, the present findings describe who turns to chatbots for mental health advice, not whether this practice is helpful or harmful. Potentially relevant factors such as personality, mental health literacy, social support, trust in and attitudes toward AI, digital literacy, current psychiatric diagnosis or treatment, and barriers to professional mental health care, including cost and waiting times, were not assessed and might partly account for the observed age differences.

Fifth, data were collected between June and November 2026, a period of rapid development and diffusion of generative AI. New functionalities, such as voice interaction, persistent memory and personalized companionship features, together with evolving regulation and public debate, may alter both the extent and the correlates of chatbot use, so our results represent a snapshot that may soon become outdated. Adults older than 50 years were not included, because chatbot use is considerably less common after midlife [37]; our findings, including the agedependent associations, therefore cannot be extended to older adults, among whom chatbot use is rising. Finally, cultural features of help-seeking and of the digital environment in Greece may limit transferability to other countries.

4.8. Directions for future research Prospective cohorts are needed to establish whether loneliness and depressive symptoms precede chatbot consultation, and whether this sequence differs by age. Representative samples spanning the full adult lifespan, including older adults, should test whether the age gradient we observed can be replicated. Mechanistic studies could examine the contribution of digital familiarity, emotion regulation, network composition and help-seeking attitudes, while outcome studies should weigh benefits against harms in different age groups. Qualitative work with young and middle-aged users would help clarify why they turn to chatbots and what they gain or lose by doing so. 5. Conclusions Among Greek adults up to 50 years of age, asking AI chatbots for mental health advice was the norm rather than the exception, and more than a third of respondents did so weekly. Younger age, recent professional mental health care, problematic social media use and low self-esteem characterized users, and poor sleep, low self-esteem and problematic social media use characterized frequent users. Crucially, loneliness, and to a lesser extent depressive symptoms, were linked to chatbot use only in young adulthood, indicating that the psychological pathways into this behavior shift with age. Health professionals should routinely explore chatbot use with patients of all ages, and AI literacy and prevention efforts should be tailored to the needs of different age groups. Longitudinal and representative studies are required to verify these findings and to clarify the balance of benefits and risks of chatbot-based mental health advice across adulthood.
